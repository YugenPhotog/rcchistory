Names across Jewish History

Hebrews

A traditional name for the ancestors during the patriarchal and Exodus narratives.

Israelites

The people associated with Jacob/Israel, the tribes, judges, monarchy, and First Temple.

Jews

From Judah—Jacob’s son, his tribe, and later the southern kingdom—increasingly used after the Babylonian exile.

Judaism

The evolving religious and communal tradition of the Jewish people.

Names change as political and communal identities change.

Understanding This Slide

The labels belong to different historical settings and should not be treated as interchangeable in every period. “Hebrews” appears in the ancestral and Exodus setting; “Israelites” fits the tribal and monarchic periods; “Jews” derives from Judah and becomes common after exile.

Judaism names more than a set of doctrines. It is a religious civilization joining peoplehood, sacred texts, law, worship, memory, land, and diaspora communities.

Understanding This Slide

Genesis 1 presents an ordered creation declared good. Genesis 2 offers a second, more intimate account focused on human relationship, work, moral limits, and companionship.

Understanding This Slide

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “Abram was seventy-five when God told him to leave everything: his country, his relatives, his father’s house, and go to a land God would show him. So he went. Years later he was still childless, and he asked God how he could know the promises were real. God told him to bring a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove, and a pigeon. Abram cut the animals in two and laid the halves facing each other; the birds he left whole. When birds of prey swooped down on the carcasses, he drove them away. As the sun went down, a deep sleep and a terrifying darkness fell on him. Then, in the dark, a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between the pieces. ‘On that day the Lord made a covenant with Abram.’”

Why walk between cut animals? Jeremiah 34:18 explains the ceremony: covenant-breakers will be made “like the calf when they cut it in two and passed between its parts.” Walking between the pieces says, in effect: may I become like these animals if I break my word (a paraphrase, not a quotation). In Genesis 15, only God’s fire passes through: God binds himself to the promise. That is why Hebrew speaks of “cutting” a covenant.

The call: when Abram was ninety-nine, the Lord appeared to him again. This is a theophany, a visible manifestation of God to humankind. God’s first words set the terms: “I am God Almighty; walk before me, and be blameless. And I will make my covenant between me and you” (Genesis 17:1–2). AK unpacks those terms.

A two-way promise, with a sign carried into every generation.

Understanding This Slide

Tell it as a story (about 1–2 minutes): “Abram is ninety-nine. God appears and lays out the covenant like the terms of an agreement. Abram’s side: walk before God and be blameless. God’s side: ‘I will be their God.’ Then God changes his name. No longer Abram, but Abraham, ‘for I have made you the ancestor of a multitude of nations.’ His wife Sarai becomes Sarah. And the covenant gets a sign that every family will carry: every baby boy is to be circumcised on his eighth day.”

Terms (Genesis 17:1–8): Abraham and his posterity are to worship, believe, and obey, and to be blameless (older translations such as the KJV read “be thou perfect”). God, in turn, will be their God. The name change marks Abram’s new status: a new name for a new role.

Don’t skip Sarah: God renames her too and promises, “I will bless her, and she shall give rise to nations” (Genesis 17:16). The covenant line runs through her son Isaac (17:19), which sets up J.

Token (Genesis 17:11–12): circumcision is the physical sign of the covenant, done on the eighth day. Jews still mark it today at a brit milah (“covenant of circumcision”); Lecture 3 covers the ceremony.

Firstborn (Primogeniture)

Primogeniture is the custom that the firstborn son inherits family leadership and the larger share of the inheritance. Genesis repeatedly complicates it: younger sons such as Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph are the ones the story follows.

Understanding This Slide

The ancestral narratives move through Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, then Jacob, Leah, and Rachel. Jacob is also named Israel; his sons become the remembered ancestors of the tribes.

Use “patriarchal” descriptively, but do not erase the matriarchs. Sarah, Rebekah, Leah, and Rachel make decisive choices that shape the covenant line. Joseph’s story explains the family’s movement into Egypt.

Bottom row: Jacob’s children grouped by their four mothers, as in Genesis 35:22–26; the births are told in Genesis 29–30, with Benjamin born last (Genesis 35). Dinah is Leah’s daughter (Genesis 30:21); she is not counted as a tribe.

Next: AG and AH tell the story of Joseph, which brings Jacob’s family to Egypt.

Firstborn point: Reuben is the firstborn, yet Judah leads and the birthright passes to Joseph’s sons. Reuben is “Jacob’s firstborn,” but 1 Chronicles 5:1–2 says his birthright “was given to the sons of Joseph,” while “Judah became prominent among his brothers and a ruler came from him.” The covenant line again does not simply follow the eldest son.

Joseph’s sons Ephraim and Manasseh: Jacob adopts them as his own (“Ephraim and Manasseh shall be mine, just as Reuben and Simeon are,” Genesis 48:5). In the later tribal lists they count as two tribes, while Levi receives towns rather than a share of land (Joshua 14:4).

The Exodus Narrative

  • Enslavement: Pharaoh subjects the Israelites to forced labor in Egypt
  • Calling: God commissions Moses to confront Pharaoh and lead the people out
  • Deliverance: plagues and escape through the Sea of Reeds / Red Sea break imperial control
  • Covenant: wilderness testing leads to Sinai, Torah, and a new communal identity

Understanding This Slide

Exodus is the central liberation narrative of Jewish collective memory: enslavement, Moses’ call, confrontation with Pharaoh, escape, wilderness, and covenant at Sinai.

Separate theological importance from historical certainty. Archaeology and Egyptian records do not verify the biblical account as narrated, and scholars debate its scale and chronology. The narrative’s formative power for Jewish identity is not dependent on resolving every historical question.

Transition from Joseph (AG–AH): Jacob’s family came to Egypt as welcome guests in Goshen; under a new king “who did not know Joseph” (Exodus 1:8) they are enslaved. Start the Exodus story there.

Understanding This Slide

Understanding This Slide

The exile is a political disaster and a theological crisis. Babylon defeats the Davidic monarchy, destroys the sanctuary, and deports portions of the population.

Prophetic and priestly traditions interpret the defeat without concluding that Babylon’s gods defeated YHWH. Exile becomes punishment for covenant failure, while hope shifts toward restoration and renewed fidelity.

Hellenism & the Road to Revolt

A Greek World

332 BCE: Alexander’s conquest brings Judea into a Greek-speaking world. Many Jews learn Greek ways and remain fully Jewish.

Jews Disagree

In Jerusalem the high priest Jason opens a Greek gymnasium. Some Jews welcome it; others see it as a betrayal of the covenant.

Understanding This Slide

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “After Alexander the Great, Judea sits inside a Greek-speaking world. Greek language, schools, and city life are attractive, and many Jews adopt them while staying Jewish—think of Jews in Alexandria reading the Torah in Greek. In Jerusalem, though, a fight breaks out over how far to go. The high priest Jason gets permission from the Seleucid king, Antiochus IV, to open a gymnasium, a Greek-style school and athletic club. Some Jews love it. Others think the priests are abandoning the covenant. Then the king himself steps in: around 167 BCE he outlaws Sabbath, circumcision, and Torah scrolls, and a pagan altar goes up in the Temple. Now it is no longer a debate about culture. It is a question of whether Jews may live as Jews at all.”

Careful framing: Hellenization was not simply forced assimilation. Greek language, education, and ideas could be adapted in many ways, and Jews who embraced them still saw themselves as Jews. 1 and 2 Maccabees are written from the rebels’ side and call the Hellenizers “renegades” and “ungodly”; historians read that as one side of an inner-Jewish argument.

Earlier, Antiochus had already plundered the Temple treasures (1 Maccabees 1:20–24 dates this to the 143rd Seleucid year, about 169 BCE; the textbook gives 168 BCE). The decrees and the “desolating sacrilege” follow in 167 BCE.

Hanukkah

Hebrew for “dedication.” The festival commemorates the rededication of the Temple in 164 BCE after the Maccabean victory. The story of one day’s oil lasting eight days comes from later rabbinic tradition (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 21b).

Understanding This Slide

Understanding This Slide

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): sixty years after the Temple burns, Rome still faces Jewish resistance. First, around 115–117, while Emperor Trajan is fighting in Mesopotamia, Jewish communities in Egypt, Cyrene (Libya), and Cyprus rise up. The fighting is aimed as much at their Greek neighbors as at Rome, and Roman armies crush it so hard that these old communities almost disappear. Then, in 132, Simeon bar Kosiba, called Bar Kokhba, “son of the star,” leads Judea in a second great war. Rome needs more than three years to win it. Careful framing. Steven’s original said “most of Jewish population killed, sold into slavery, or forced to flee.” That fits Judea proper, which was devastated. Cassius Dio’s figure of 580,000 killed is an ancient estimate that historians treat with caution. Galilee remained heavily Jewish, and it became the home of the rabbis and the Mishnah (AB).

Places. Steven’s original listed “Egypt, Iberia, and Central Asia.” Babylonia (Mesopotamia), not Central Asia, was the great eastern center. Egypt’s communities were centuries old, though the revolt of 115–117 nearly destroyed them. Jews are attested in Spain (Iberia) by about 300 CE, at the Council of Elvira, so Iberia is “later.” The diaspora did not begin with Rome; it began with the Assyrian and Babylonian exiles (Q–S).

Dates. Hadrian founded Aelia Capitolina around 130, and Cassius Dio says this provoked the war. The name Syria Palaestina appears after the revolt; the earliest clear evidence is a military diploma of 139 CE. Steven’s “136” is therefore rounded. The revolt is usually dated 132–135, and some scholars extend it to 136.

Key concept

Understanding Diaspora

Diaspora: the movement, migration, or scattering of a people away from an established or ancestral homeland.

Diaspora is not identical to exile: belonging abroad may coexist with memory, adaptation, and multiple homelands.

Understanding This Slide

Define diaspora analytically before applying it to Jewish history. The term originally referred especially to Jewish dispersion, but scholars now use it for many populations whose communities span an ancestral homeland and multiple host societies.

Distinguish diaspora from exile. Exile emphasizes forced removal and loss; diaspora is broader and may result from conquest, enslavement, trade, study, work, family migration, or choice. A diaspora does not require every member to desire permanent return.

The card is Steven’s original definition, verbatim except for capitalization. The fuller scholarly definition used on this slide before 2026-10-23: “a population dispersed from an ancestral homeland that sustains a shared identity and connections across multiple communities.” The word comes from the Greek for “scattering” (see the S pop-up).

Three wording changes, and why. (1) “Pogroms” moved here from the slide. It is a Russian word for the anti-Jewish riots of 1881–1884 and later, so on a slide about antiquity it would be an anachronism. Use it when you reach medieval and modern persecution. (2) “Unable to blend” became “Often unable, or not allowed, to blend,” since exclusion was often imposed by host societies. (3) “Hostile” became “Often hostile,” because many host societies were not. Jews in Babylonia and Alexandria built large, thriving communities, and Jeremiah told the first exiles to “seek the welfare of the city” (R).

Four features of a diaspora (moved here from the retired slide Z, 2026-10-23): Ancestral Homeland: a real or remembered place of origin remains significant to collective identity. Dispersion: people live in more than one host society through forced or voluntary movement. Shared Identity: memory, religion, language, institutions, family, or customs sustain group continuity. Ongoing Connections: communities maintain ties to the homeland and to one another across borders.

The four elements are recurring features rather than a rigid checklist. Their intensity changes across time: homeland orientation may be religious, cultural, political, familial, or symbolic, while communities also develop genuine belonging in their places of residence.

Compare the criteria in diaspora studies, for example William Safran, “Diasporas in Modern Societies: Myths of Homeland and Return,” Diaspora 1, no. 1 (1991), which uses the Jewish case as its model.

Diaspora: Dispersion without Disappearance

Before Rome

Large Jewish communities already lived in Babylonia, Alexandria, Antioch, and other cities.

After Revolt

War, enslavement, migration, and opportunity expand communities across the Mediterranean and beyond.

Many Centers

Jerusalem remains sacred while communal life develops in numerous homelands.

Shared Identity

Texts, worship, law, memory, family, and networks connect dispersed communities.

Diaspora becomes a durable form of Jewish civilization—not merely an interruption.

Understanding This Slide

Diaspora means dispersion, not the disappearance of a people or a single forced migration. Jewish communities outside Judea were already ancient and often thriving before the Roman wars.

After 70 and 135, dispersion increases, but the relationship between homeland and diaspora remains complex. Judaism develops as a multi-centered tradition rather than a community simply waiting in one uniform way to return.

Looking ahead (not taught in this lecture): in the Middle Ages the dispersed communities develop into large cultural families. Sephardi Jews trace their roots to Iberia, Ashkenazi Jews to the Rhineland and later Central and Eastern Europe, and Mizrahi Jews to the Middle East and North Africa. Significance of the Diaspora: From Temple to Torah

  • Synagogue replaces Temple ritual: prayer, study, and community become the center of worship
  • Rabbis replace priests: teachers, heirs of the Pharisees, become spiritual leaders in the synagogues
  • Oral traditions written down: the Mishnah (c. 200 CE), then the Talmud
  • Decentralized worship: Judaism can be lived wherever Jews are

Adaptation preserves continuity: Temple-centered worship becomes a portable way of life.

Mishnah

Understanding This Slide

Rabbinic Judaism does not appear fully formed in 70 CE. Teachers associated with Pharisaic and scribal traditions gradually gain communal authority over several centuries.

The Mishnah, compiled around 200 CE, organizes oral legal traditions. Palestinian and Babylonian discussions produce the Talmuds. This portable, text-centered, debate-centered tradition becomes the foundation on which later Jewish movements build or against which they react.

Steven’s original slide, “Significance of The Diaspora”: “Temple rituals replaced by synagogue; Authority of priest tribe (Levites) replaced by teachers (Rabbi); Rabbis: Successors of the Pharisees who preserved lay-led Judaism; Became spiritual leaders in the synagogues; Oral traditions written down; Decentralized form of Worship.” The bullets follow his order.

Corrections. (1) Synagogues existed before 70 CE (the Theodotos inscription in Jerusalem; Gamla; synagogues in Egypt and across the diaspora). After 70 they became the center rather than a new invention. (2) The priests were the kohanim, Aaron’s descendants within the tribe of Levi. The other Levites assisted them, so “priest tribe (Levites)” became “priests.” (3) The rabbis’ authority grew over about two centuries; it was not handed over in 70 (see textbook caution 2 in the instructor note).

Yavneh (story, about 1 minute): Talmudic tradition (Gittin 56a–b) says that during the siege Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai had himself carried out of Jerusalem in a coffin and asked Vespasian for “Yavneh and its sages.” Treat it as a later rabbinic story rather than an eyewitness report. What is solid is that Yavneh became an early center where the sages kept the calendar, law, and festivals going (Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 4:1). Full telling: the From Temple to Synagogue mini-lecture, slide O, linked by the button.

The Talmuds: the Jerusalem Talmud was completed roughly around 400 CE and the Babylonian roughly 500–600 CE; both dates are approximate.

Origins of Antisemitism

  • Outsiders: a minority in every land, Jews were often treated as outsiders, even before Christianity
  • Land & work: in much of medieval Europe, barred from land and guilds, many turned to non-farming occupations
  • Scapegoating: blamed for crises, disease, debt, or political instability
  • Code of Justinian (529–534 CE): anti-Jewish laws that shaped European legal systems for centuries

Explain the institutions and myths that produce prejudice—never treat prejudice as inevitable.

Understanding This Slide

Antisemitism is a modern term applied to a long and changing history of anti-Jewish hostility. Ancient ethnic and political prejudice, Christian anti-Judaism, economic myths, racial ideology, nationalism, and conspiracy theories are related but not identical.

Avoid reproducing stereotypes when explaining persecution. Jews did not enter particular occupations because of an innate group trait; law, exclusion from land or guilds, urban networks, communal needs, and local opportunity varied enormously.

Steven’s original slide, “Origins of Anti-Semitism”: “In Diaspora, Jews were always outsiders; In agrarian societies, Jews were prevented from owning land; Turned to non-farming occupations; Targets of scape-goating; Code of Justinian (527 C.E); Contained discriminatory legislation against the Jews; Influenced European legal systems for centuries and contributed to anti-Semitism.”

Corrections. (1) “Always outsiders” became “often treated as outsiders”: in some places and periods Jews were well integrated. (2) The land and guild bans belong mainly to medieval Christian Europe. In late antiquity Jews owned and farmed land in Galilee and Babylonia. (3) 527 is the year Justinian became emperor. His Code was issued in 529 and revised in 534; the revised version is the one that survives. (4) The word “antisemitism” is modern: it was popularized in Germany around 1879 for a racial hatred of Jews. Historians apply it backward to a long history of anti-Jewish hostility. The slide uses the unhyphenated spelling most scholars now prefer.

What the Code actually said (verified provisions only): (a) no Jew may hold public office or municipal honors, and no new synagogue may be built, though old ones may be repaired (C. 1.9.18/19, a law of 439 carried into the Code); (b) marriage between Jews and Christians is punished as adultery (C. 1.9.6, from 388); (c) Jews and heretics may not testify against orthodox Christians (C. 1.5.21, Justinian, 531); (d) Jews may not own or circumcise Christian slaves (C. 1.10). Justinian’s later Novels, especially Novel 146 (553), reached into synagogue worship itself. Procopius (Secret History 28) says Justinian kept Jews from celebrating Passover before the Christian Easter. That is a hostile historian’s report, not a law in the Code.

Dropped as unverified: the “forced conversion of Jewish children” and “property confiscation for a Christian woman who married a Jew” listed in the notes of Steven’s later PowerPoint. Neither was confirmed in the Code.

About the image (real photograph): the apse mosaic in San Vitale, Ravenna, dedicated in 547, shows Justinian with Bishop Maximian, clergy, officials, and guards. It presents imperial and church power side by side, the partnership behind laws like these.

## Smoke and Gunboats: Opium, Silver, and the First Unequal Treaty

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch18/ch18-l1-smoke-and-gunboats.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q2

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The court debate and Lin Zexu

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q2 Opium became a crisis because a mass habit, fed by a price war among British and Indian suppliers, seemed to be draining China's silver at the same time as rising silver prices raised every farmer's taxes. The court debated legalization and chose suppression. Lin Zexu struck at the supply: he arrested Chinese dealers, blockaded the foreign merchants until they surrendered their opium, and destroyed it in public at Humen.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q3

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — After the war

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q3 The war grew out of the opium crisis, Lin's seizure of British opium, and Britain's demand for open trade and diplomatic equality. British steam warships, artillery and disciplined infantry won it. The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) and the treaties of 1843–1844 opened five ports, ceded Hong Kong, imposed a 21-million-dollar indemnity, fixed a low tariff, and added extraterritoriality and most-favored-nation treatment. They were "unequal" because they were imposed at gunpoint and every obligation ran from China to the foreign powers.

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Pierian Spring: Too Many People, Too Little Silver (Review Question 1)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q1 Population growth shrank farms, lowered wages and living standards, and produced a floating population of poor, unmarried men. The state's tax base and its number of officials did not grow, so revenue fell short of the cost of water control, relief and the banners. The rising price of silver then raised everyone's real taxes. Daoguang's frugality could slow the decline but not reverse it.

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q4

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The cost

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q4 The rebellion began when Hong Xiuquan, a Hakka who repeatedly failed the examinations, read his feverish visions through a Christian tract and founded the God Worshippers among poor Hakka communities in Guangxi. Armed clashes with Qing troops turned the sect into an army and then a state, the Heavenly Kingdom, with its capital at Nanjing from 1853. It became so destructive because it lasted fourteen years in China's richest region, both sides massacred civilians, and war brought famine and disease to tens of millions.

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Regionalism

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q5 Zeng Guofan raised the Hunan Army through gentry officers who recruited men from their own districts, paid them from local taxes such as lijin, and built a river fleet to retake the Yangzi. His protégés Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang raised similar armies, and at Shanghai Western-officered forces helped. Their methods saved the dynasty but moved armies, money and patronage into the hands of Han regional leaders, a shift that weakened central control in the long run.

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The Black Flags

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q6 The Nian rebellion in the north grew from poverty, banditry and the Yellow River disaster of 1855. Muslim-led risings broke out in Yunnan (1856–1873) and the northwest (1862–1873), and Yaqub Beg's state rose in Xinjiang. They came from competition for land and mines, Han–Muslim feuds, new religious movements and a state whose armies were tied down fighting the Taiping. ## Large and Yet Weak: The Second Opium War and Self-Strengthening

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch18/ch18-l3-large-and-yet-weak.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q7

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The Conventions of Peking and their meaning

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q7 Britain and France wanted the 1842 treaties revised and used the Arrow incident and a missionary's execution as pretexts; Russia exploited the crisis on the Amur. The allies took Guangzhou and the Dagu forts, imposed the Treaties of Tianjin (1858), and after the Qing resisted in 1859 marched on Beijing and burned the Yuanmingyuan (1860). The outcomes were envoys in Beijing, new ports including the Yangzi, travel and preaching in the interior, legal opium, Kowloon for Britain, new indemnities, and the Amur and Ussuri lands for Russia. Each clause narrowed what the Qing state could decide for itself.

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Li Hongzhang's projects

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q8 Feng Guifen and the self-strengtheners wanted to make China strong enough to resist the West while keeping its Confucian order. Feng proposed hiring foreign experts to build shipyards and arsenals, rewarding successful managers with degrees, translating Western science, and reforming how officials were chosen. Prince Gong, Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang built a foreign office, language schools, arsenals and shipyards, sent students abroad, and founded mines, telegraphs, shipping lines and a modern navy.

## The Empress and the Boxers: Cixi, Lost Wars, and the Crisis of 1898–1901

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch18/ch18-l4-empress-and-the-boxers.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q10

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The hard-liners and the French war

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q10 Cixi dominated the court from 1861 to 1908, as co-regent for her son and then for her nephew, whom she chose as a three-year-old so that she could keep ruling. She defended Manchu privilege, used modernizers such as Li Hongzhang and Prince Gong, and checked them by encouraging their conservative critics. This balance let self-strengthening continue but never let it go deep. When she sided with the hard-liners in 1884, the Fujian fleet was destroyed. In 1898 she ended the emperor's radical reforms, and in 1900 she backed the Boxers (Act IV).

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q11

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Shimonoseki and the scramble

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q11 The Qing lost its tributaries one by one: the Ryukyus to Japan in the 1870s, Vietnam to France in 1885, and Korea after the war of 1894–1895. Each loss came by force, through powers that treated tribute as a sign of weakness, not of a legitimate order. Defeat by Japan, a former tributary-system neighbor, cost Taiwan and a huge indemnity, led to foreign loans, and set off the scramble of 1898. China's international position had fallen from the center of an East Asian order to a target for partition.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q9

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: Why Reform Fell Short (Review Question 9)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q9 Self-strengthening fell short because the court and much of the elite rejected anything that looked like giving in to the foreigners, so reform stayed limited to arsenals, ships and schools and never reached the examinations, the bureaucracy or the structure of the state. Conservatives such as Woren blocked even mathematics; Guo Songtao, who praised Western government, was disgraced. The projects depended on a few regional patrons, revenue was divided, and Cixi balanced reformers against their critics. Meiji Japan, by contrast, changed its political system first.

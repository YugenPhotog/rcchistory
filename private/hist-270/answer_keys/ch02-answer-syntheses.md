## When Birth Stopped Mattering: Hegemons, Total War, and the Rise of the Shi

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch02/ch2-l1-hegemons-war-shi.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q1

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act I: A King in Name Only (Review Question 1)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q1 In the Western Zhou the king, backed by his own armies, ruled through kinsmen and allies he had enfeoffed. In the Eastern Zhou the king kept his title and ritual primacy but lost the power to compel, and the fiefs functioned as independent states in a multistate system of rivals and allies. The hegemon was the ruler of the strongest state, recognized as leader of the alliance: he called meetings where rulers swore to uphold the Zhou order (in 657, not to dam waters, withhold grain, replace heirs or promote concubines), led coalitions against outsiders and expanding rivals such as Chu, and arbitrated disputes like successions. Duke Huan of Qi, guided by Guan Zhong, was the first. The system kept order without a ruling king, but its edge states kept growing until only seven remained.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q2

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act II: War Without Rules (Review Question 2)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q2 Spring and Autumn warfare was an aristocratic contest: chariot-borne nobles, armies of up to ten thousand, battles by appointment hedged with ritual, and wars lasting a season. In the Warring States, technology (the crossbow, iron weapons and helmets, cavalry adopted from the steppe), tactics (drilled infantry, sieges, tunnels, walls and forts, generals who used deception) and organization (near-universal conscription, elite corps paid in tax exemptions, generals chosen for ability) made war vast: armies of a couple hundred thousand by 300 BCE, campaigns of years on several fronts, and in 260 BCE Qin’s mobilization of every man over fifteen. The social impact was that war now reached every household, and states had to count, feed, train and tax their whole populations to fight it.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q3

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: Iron, Coins and Counted People (Review Question 3)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q3 The Eastern Zhou economy grew and changed shape. Rulers expanded farmland by draining marshes and digging irrigation, cast iron spread to farm tools and weapons, cities grew outer walls for artisans and merchants, a class of rich traders appeared, and rulers cast spade-shaped coins. Demographically, states sought larger populations and began registering households, which spread family names to commoners. Serfdom declined because rulers, needing soldiers and revenue for constant war, surveyed land and taxed peasants directly instead of leaving them under local lords, and governed through appointed, dismissible officials instead of hereditary ones. Agriculture supplied the surplus, administration made each farmer the state’s own taxpayer, and iron made both farming and war more productive.

### 4. Answer synthesis · Q4

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act IV: Two Applicants (Review Question 4)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q4 The shi were the lowest rank of the old aristocracy, educated men with knightly values of honor and loyal service but little land or inherited office. Their numbers grew because every conquest pushed a defeated state’s nobles down into their ranks. The political changes of the Eastern Zhou created demand for them: rulers at war needed advisers, generals and officials they could appoint, control through documents and dismiss, rather than hereditary lords; and rulers competed for able men and for the prestige of attracting them. Shi travelled from court to court offering strategy and policy, and their competition produced the Hundred Schools, including Confucius, the first and most important of them. Their rise marks the shift from a society ranked by birth to one with real opportunity for talent.

## The Sage and His Challengers: Confucius, Mozi, Mencius and Xunzi

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch02/ch2-l2-sage-and-challengers.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q5

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act I: Rule by Example (Review Question 5)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q5 Confucius’s ethics centered on ren , humane care for others, expressed through li , the rituals and proprieties that give each relationship its proper form, with filial piety as the root and the five relationships as the frame of a well-ordered society. Politically, he held that rulers should lead by moral example and ritual rather than by laws and punishments, because example makes people good while punishment only makes them evasive; his model was the early Zhou of the Duke of Zhou. He redefined the junzi from a nobleman by birth into a gentleman by cultivation, someone who studies, practices ritual and pursues what is right rather than what is profitable, and he made such men the proper officials of the state. He respected the spirits but kept the focus on human conduct.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q6

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act II: Does It Help? (Review Question 6)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q6 Confucius built his ethics outward from the family and valued ritual and music as the forms through which people become humane; Mozi judged every practice by its usefulness to the people as a whole. By that test, Confucian graded love was partial and bred conflict, so he urged universal love, caring for others’ families as for one’s own. Elaborate funerals, long mourning and ritual music wasted resources and labor that should feed and clothe the poor, so he called for frugality. Offensive war was the greatest waste of all, destroying lives and wealth for no common benefit, so the Mohists condemned it and organized to defend besieged cities. Confucians saw Mozi’s love without distinctions as unnatural, but his emphasis on merit and thrift outlived his school.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q7

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: Is Human Nature Good? (Review Question 7)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q7 Mencius held that human nature is good: everyone has the sprouts of compassion, shame, deference and a sense of right, shown by the instant alarm anyone feels at a child about to fall into a well. Moral development is therefore a matter of nurturing what is already there, and good government means a benevolent ruler who reduces taxes and punishments, avoids war for profit and secures the people’s livelihood so their goodness can grow; a ruler who fails loses Heaven’s favor, which speaks through the people. Xunzi held that people are born selfish and that goodness comes from conscious effort, like straightening warped wood. Moral development therefore requires teachers, study and ritual imposed from outside, and government requires clear rules, rewards and punishments and a strict hierarchy, though he still valued ritual and the people’s support.

## The Way and the Law: Daoists and Legalists

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch02/ch2-l3-way-and-law.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q8, for instructor preparation only

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act I: The Laozi, Do Less (Review Question 8)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q8, for instructor preparation only The Daoists responded to Confucian striving by rejecting its premise: purposeful efforts to improve society, including honoring the worthy, cultivating virtue, ritual and study, generally make things worse, because they breed competition, desire and artificial distinctions. Their alternative society was small, simple and content, ruled by a sage who governs by non-action, fills bellies and empties ambitions, so that order arises by itself. For the individual they offered freedom within the natural order: like water, yield and serve; like Zhuangzi, refuse office, accept that useful and useless or life and death are relative distinctions, and treat death as a change like the seasons. Skill and wisdom live in practice, not in books. Yet many later Chinese combined Confucian public service with Daoist private perspective.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q9, for instructor preparation only

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: The Legalists, Trust No One (Review Question 9)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q9, for instructor preparation only Legalism held that strong government depends on effective laws and procedures rather than on the virtue of rulers and officials. Han Feizi articulated its tenets: assume that subordinates, even wives and concubines, pursue their own interests; trust no one; keep intentions secret and manipulate competition among officials; make laws and prohibitions clear and rewards and punishments automatic; allow no debate that questions the laws. Qin implemented them under Lord Shang: ranks by enemy heads instead of inherited titles, counties under appointed officials instead of fiefs, migrants recruited with land, land made saleable, serf obligations ended but taxes and labor service heavy, and statutes that bound officials with fines. Legalists thought virtue insufficient because it is rare and because even parents calculate advantage; rulers will be no more selfless, so only clear laws can make people work hard and fight wars.

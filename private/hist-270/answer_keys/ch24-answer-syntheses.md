## Cutting the Queue: The Last Qing Reforms, the First Nationalists, and the Revolution of 1911

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch24/ch24-l1-cutting-the-queue.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q1

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The court's reforms

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q1 The Boxer indemnity of 450 million taels, paid over thirty-nine years, swallowed the customs revenue and left the court unable to fund reform, so it pushed costs onto the provinces. Local elites filled the gap themselves, founding schools, newspapers, chambers of commerce and railway companies, and they turned nationalist in the boycott of 1905 and the rights-recovery movement. The court, too, reformed: new schools, the end of the examinations in 1905, new armies, provincial assemblies in 1909 and a promised constitution. But every reform created groups, from assemblymen to New Army officers, that wanted real power, and the court answered with a cabinet dominated by Manchu princes. The reforms were doomed because they strengthened the court's critics without satisfying them.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q2

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Zou Rong and anti-Manchu nationalism

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q2 Early twentieth-century intellectuals read Western and Japanese power as the product of whole political systems, not just technology, and many saw the world through Social Darwinism as a struggle in which weak nations perished. Japan, where most Chinese students went, proved an Asian state could modernize. Yan Fu argued that Western governments freed individual energy for national goals and that China's troubles were mostly its own fault. Liang Qichao called for "democracy" in the sense of a state drawing strength from the people, but after 1903 he preferred the strong states of Japan and Germany. Zou Rong blamed Manchu rule and called for a race war against the Manchus.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q3

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: Sun Yatsen (Review Question 3)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q3 Sun's strategies shifted over time. First he tried to use south China's secret societies as the muscle for a rising, through the Revive China Society; its plots failed. In 1905 he joined radical students in Tokyo to form the Revolutionary Alliance, which sponsored seven or eight failed risings while Sun raised money among overseas Chinese and looked for foreign backers. His framework was the Three People's Principles: nationalism against the Manchus and the foreign powers, democracy as elections and a constitution, and the people's livelihood as a mild socialism, with a period of political tutelage before full democracy. The program united very different groups, but the rising that toppled the Qing came from New Army soldiers, not from Sun's own plans.

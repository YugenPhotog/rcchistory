## The Shang King Ruled Through His Ancestors

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch01/ch1-l1-shang-sacred-state.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q2

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act II: The Sacred King (Review Question 2)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q2 Ancestor worship made the royal dead powerful intermediaries with Di; divination was the technique by which the king consulted them on rain, harvest, war and family; and the king’s role as high priest made him the person best qualified to perform the offerings. Together they established his authority (descent from the most powerful ancestors) and maintained it (each recorded divination and each good outcome confirmed his ritual competence, while failures could be blamed on the offering).

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q3

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: The Tombs (Review Question 3)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q3 Hierarchy: royal tombs packed with bronze and jade contrast with commoners’ pit houses and farmers’ stone tools, and even the dead were ranked (followers versus victims). Military leadership: Lady Hao’s 130 weapons, yue axes and the 13,000-troop campaign show that royal rank and command went together, even for a royal woman. Wealth: 1.6 tons of bronze and tribute from distant regions show the reach of royal power. Ritual sacrifice: human and animal offerings in tombs and pits, fed by war captives, tie war directly to ancestor worship.

## Rare Skills Became Power, and the Shang Were Not Alone

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch01/ch1-l2-writing-bronze-wider-world.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q5

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act I: Writing as Power (Review Question 5)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q5 Administration: scribes recorded war results, booty, hunts and tribute and dated them on a shared sixty-day calendar, so the court could store and compare information. Communication: written orders could travel across an expanding realm without distortion and outlast the person who gave them. Political control: the script took years to master, so literacy stayed with the king’s trained specialists, and controlling the record meant controlling the state’s information. The records also preserved the authority of royal women such as Lady Hao. The surviving bones are only the durable part of a larger written culture.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q7

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act II: Bronze for the Ancestors (Review Question 7)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q7 Organization: a single 830–875 kg cauldron needed two to three hundred craftsmen and many furnaces pouring on a signal; piece-mold casting divided the work among model makers, mold makers, casters and finishers under managers; and behind the workshops stood miners, refiners, porters and charcoal producers. Beliefs: most bronze went into ritual vessels for feeding ancestors and gods rather than weapons, showing that the Shang located real power in the spirit world. Hierarchy: kings owned the most bronze, and inscribed vessels of Lady Hao and Queen Mother Wu show royal women holding wealth and ritual authority. Uncertainty: the meaning of the taotie and other animal decoration is still debated.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q8

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: A Bronze Age Bigger Than the Shang (Review Question 8)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q8 The traditional view made the Shang the single source of early Chinese civilization, spreading outward from the Yellow River. Sanxingdui shows a walled city with bronze casting equal to Anyang’s but an art and religion of its own (giant masks, gold-foil heads, a monumental figure, pits without human victims). The middle Yangzi, Jiangxi and the Northern Zone add regional styles, Shang-scale elite wealth and links to the steppe. Since the Shang king’s safe zone was small and shared technology does not prove shared rule, early China looks like several interacting centers. The Shang seem central partly because they alone left writing.

## Heaven’s Mandate: How the Zhou Turned Conquest into Legitimacy

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch01/ch1-l3-heavens-mandate-zhou.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q10

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act I: Conquest and Mandate (Review Question 10)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q10 The Mandate of Heaven held that Heaven gives the right to rule to a king who governs for the good of the people and takes it away from one who does not. The Zhou used it to describe their conquest as Heaven’s judgment: the last Shang king was cruel and drunken, so Heaven transferred the Mandate to King Wen and his son Wu. The Book of Documents and inscriptions such as the Da Yu ding repeat the claim, and the king’s title “Son of Heaven” built it into the monarchy. Because the idea had no place in Shang religion, some scholars see it as Zhou propaganda; either way, it made the conquest look just and later legitimated every new dynasty.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q11

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act II: Lords and Garrisons (Review Question 11)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q11 Instead of governing everything from the capital, the Western Zhou kings granted territories to relatives and trusted followers, who built walled garrisons and ruled them as lords. The king was both head of the royal lineage and supreme lord, and he held the lords through ancestral rituals, court visits, written appointments read at court ceremonies, and rewards of land and wealth for service, as Yu’s inscription shows. Fiefs and the offices beneath them became hereditary, so by 800 BCE some two hundred noble houses ruled their own domains, and Rong and Yi chiefs could join by using Chinese writing. The system is “quasi-feudal” because it resembles European land-for-loyalty arrangements but rested on kinship and ritual.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q12

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act III: What Changed (Review Question 12)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q12 The Zhou continued the Shang worship of ancestors, Shang-style bronze ritual vessels (now also used for weapons and tools), and the Shang writing system, which became central to elite life and spread to new peoples. Two important Shang practices declined gradually: divining by heating oracle bones, which had largely disappeared by 771 BCE, and human sacrifice, including the burial of followers, which became rarer but continued sporadically at rulers’ burials. The Zhou also added sacrifices to Heaven. The change was gradual, more continuity than rupture.

### 4. Answer synthesis · Q13

Context (nearest preceding heading): h2 — Act IV: Voices of the Songs (Review Question 13)

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q13 Political ideas: the Book of Documents shows the Zhou court’s claim that Heaven chose the Zhou and punished the Shang, and its idea of the king as Son of Heaven ruling for the people. Social hierarchy: the Book of Songs reflects a society ranked by birth, with lords, officials and farmers, and Poem 189 shows sons raised to rule and daughters to serve the household. Everyday life: farm songs describe crops such as millet and hemp, hunting, gathering rushes and a year paced by the seasons. Both are transmitted texts, edited over centuries, so historians check them against excavated bronze inscriptions.

## The Gate in the Wall: The Banner State, the Conquest, and the Loyalists

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch16/ch16-l1-the-gate-in-the-wall.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q1

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Names, and the state Hong Taiji built

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q1 Nurhaci turned the Jurchens into a state by replacing tribal loyalty with an organization. The banners enrolled soldiers with their families and slaves in hereditary companies, split defeated groups among different units, and made the khan and his lineage the source of rank and land. His son Hong Taiji renamed the people Manchu and the state Qing, added a Chinese-style bureaucracy, and extended the banners to Mongols and Chinese. By 1644 there were twenty-four banners, eight of each kind. The result was a military and social organization that could absorb other peoples, feed and arm a professional army, and fight on equal terms with the Ming.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q2

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Loyalism of another kind

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q2 The Ming fell to Chinese rebels, and the Qing entered Beijing as allies of a Ming general, Wu Sangui, who opened the pass at Shanhaiguan in 1644. The Qing then refused to stop at the north. Many Chinese generals and officials joined them; others resisted, and the response varied from region to region. Militant loyalists such as Shi Kefa at Yangzhou, the delta cities that rebelled over the head-shaving order, and Koxinga on the coast fought and often died. Intellectual loyalists such as Huang Zongxi, Gu Yanwu and Wang Fuzhi survived, refused office and asked why the Ming had failed. They blamed not only bad emperors and eunuchs but the imperial system, empty philosophizing and the neglect of practical knowledge. The fighters tried to restore the dynasty; the thinkers tried to understand what had gone wrong.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q14 and Q4

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Why the banners stayed loyal

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q14 and Q4 After 1644 the banners were settled as a hereditary garrison army in Beijing and the provincial cities, supported by land expropriated around the capital and by stipends in silver and grain. Because bannermen could only be soldiers or officials, the system that guaranteed their privilege also made them dependent. Their numbers grew faster than the paid posts, they fell into debt, and banner land passed to Chinese owners. Qianlong responded by expelling many Chinese bannermen and trying to resettle Manchus in Manchuria. A system designed to keep a conquest elite apart and ready for war ended by producing a large, idle and impoverished population that still remained loyal to the dynasty that fed it.

## Grandfather and Grandson: Kangxi, Qianlong, and the Making of the High Qing

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch16/ch16-l2-grandfather-and-grandson.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q3

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The Jesuits at court

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q3 Kangxi secured Manchu rule in three ways. He won over Chinese elites by ruling as a Confucian monarch: keeping the examinations, recruiting loyalists through the special examination of 1679 and the Ming History project, issuing the Sacred Edict and touring the south. He destroyed the greatest internal threat, the revolt of the Three Feudatories (1673–1681), by using the banner armies and the loyalty of Chinese generals and officials who now preferred the Qing to Wu Sangui; Taiwan followed in 1683. On the frontiers he made peace with Russia at Nerchinsk (1689), accepted the Khalkha Mongols' submission (1691), defeated Galdan (1696) and brought Tibet under Qing protection (1720). Firearms and logistics gave the Qing the edge on the steppe, though the Zunghar threat was ended only under Qianlong.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q5

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The banners, Heshen and the end of the reign

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q5 Qianlong ruled a multiethnic empire by presenting himself differently to each of its peoples: as a Confucian sage, scholar and filial son to the Chinese; as a Buddhist cakravartin and patron of the lamas to Tibetans and Mongols; as a khan to the steppe; and as a conqueror who let the Muslims of Xinjiang keep their own leaders. He sponsored scholarship, poetry, painting and the Four Treasuries. Yet he never trusted that the Chinese accepted Manchu rule. He hunted sedition in the queue-clipping scare of 1768 and in a literary inquisition that destroyed more than two thousand titles. Patronage and suppression were not contradictions; both were tools for holding an empire together under a minority dynasty.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q13

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The rites controversy

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q13 The rites controversy was a dispute among Catholic missionaries over whether Chinese converts could keep ancestral and Confucian rites. The Jesuits said the rites were civil; the Dominicans and Franciscans said they were idolatry, and the pope agreed with them. Kangxi had valued the Jesuits as scientists and had tolerated Christianity in 1692, but he would not accept a foreign pontiff's authority over how his subjects behaved. He required missionaries to accept Ricci's position or leave, and he later declared that they should no longer preach. His son Yongzheng made the ban formal in 1724, while Jesuit scientists and painters stayed on at court.

### 4. Answer synthesis · Q12

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Yongzheng

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q12 Yongzheng strengthened central control by working through secret memorials that let him hear directly from officials, and by creating the small council that became the Grand Council, a fast, confidential channel for decisions. He rationalized local finance by turning the irregular meltdown surcharge into a regular tax and paying officials "nourishing honesty" allowances from it. These reforms reduced corruption for a time, filled the treasury and left his son Qianlong with surpluses that paid for the high Qing.

## The Kowtow and the Crowd: The Macartney Embassy and the Doubling of China

Source: `public/hist-270/background_essays/essays/ch16/ch16-l3-the-kowtow-and-the-crowd.html`

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q6

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The answer

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q6 The Qing regulated European trade by confining it after 1757 to Guangzhou, where foreigners lived outside the walls, dealt only with licensed Chinese merchants and paid in silver for tea, silk and porcelain. Britain wanted something else: Macartney was sent in 1793 to obtain a trading base near the tea districts, a commercial treaty, a market for British goods, a resident envoy in Beijing and access to Japan and Southeast Asia. Qianlong refused every request. The court saw no merit in them, valued the existing system of controlled trade, and treated Britain as one more distant state that should follow Qing rules; the emperor also feared British power in India and ordered coastal defenses strengthened. Neither side changed its view, and the questions Macartney raised were settled by war fifty years later.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q7

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Consequences in the village

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q7 China's population roughly doubled in the eighteenth century, to about 300 million. Ebrey names four causes: a milder climate that lengthened growing seasons, the spread of New World crops that made hills and poor soils farmable, a slowing of new epidemics, and effective state famine relief; long internal peace also mattered. The consequences were mixed. Partible inheritance shrank farms; a shortage of women, caused by concubinage and female infanticide, left many men unable to marry; and landless bachelors drifted into hauling, portering and mining, where officials feared them. Settlers moved into the hills and frontiers, causing environmental damage and conflict with non-Han peoples. Growth made the high Qing prosperous, but it also created the pressures behind the rebellions of Lecture 4.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q11

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The uprising of 1795–1797

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q11 As the Han population grew, settlers moved into the hills of the south and southwest, the homes of the Miao, Yao, Zhuang and other peoples. They often took indigenous land through debt, purchase or force, and Qing officials, who had extended direct rule into these areas in the 1720s and 1730s, treated the local peoples with contempt. The Miao resisted from the 1720s on. Officials tried to separate Miao and Han by law and by fortified borders, with little success. In 1795 the Miao of the Hunan–Guizhou border rose to drive out the Han "guests" and recover their fields, and the Qing needed two years and its best generals to suppress them.

## Inside the Garden, Outside the Law: The Dream of Red Mansions, Brotherhoods, and Sects

### 1. Answer synthesis · Q9

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The novel

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q9 After the conquest, moralists blamed the Ming's fall on laxity, attacked fiction and drama, and the state banned novels and restricted theaters. Yet creative and individualistic culture continued, because both the court and the elite left room for it and because rich patrons paid for it. Yuan Mei taught women poetry, the Eight Eccentrics of Yangzhou painted in personal styles and still sold, and theater thrived. The greatest example is The Dream of Red Mansions , written by Cao Xueqin, a member of a bondservant family ruined under Yongzheng. Through the Jia family and its garden, it explores love and desire, wealth and decline, and illusion and truth, with talented young women at its center, and it circulated widely despite the bans on novels.

### 2. Answer synthesis · Q10

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — The White Lotus rebellion, 1796–1804

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q10 Population growth, shrinking farms and a shortage of brides left many men without land, wives or families, moving between hard jobs far from home. Secret societies such as the Heaven and Earth Society offered them security, mutual aid and power: sworn brothers, loans and funeral help, passwords that worked across provinces, and sometimes control of gambling, smuggling and other rackets, with an anti-Qing rhetoric of restoring the Ming. Religious sects, which officials lumped together as the White Lotus, offered salvation through the Eternal Mother, healing, martial arts and a community that welcomed women as equals. The state saw both as threats and suppressed them, and persecution, together with the pressures of migration, helped turn the sects into the rebellion of 1796–1804.

### 3. Answer synthesis · Q8

Context (nearest preceding heading): h3 — Evidential scholarship

Plain text:

Answer synthesis · Q8 The fall of the Ming and the conquest convinced many scholars that moral laxity, encouraged by Wang Yangming's emphasis on feeling and spontaneity, had destroyed the dynasty. They turned back to Zhu Xi's orthodoxy, ritual and duty. In society the turn showed in an intensified cult of widow chastity, including faithful maidens who never married; in harsher laws on sexuality; and in attacks on fiction and drama. In scholarship it produced evidential research, which tested the classics by careful philology. Yan Ruoju's proof that part of the Book of Documents was forged shows the result: a conservative movement that ended by questioning some of its own foundations.

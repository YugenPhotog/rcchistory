Boston, 14 August 1765

  • At dawn, a stuffed and labeled effigy hangs from an elm at Essex and Orange Streets
  • It hangs all day. No officer in Boston dares take it down
  • By night, thousands parade it through town and pull apart a building believed to be the stamp office
  • They burn it on Fort Hill, then go to Andrew Oliver's house. His family escapes minutes ahead
  • The next morning, Oliver resigns
Remember the sheriff who would not touch it. We come back to that.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture opens as a story, not a lecture about method. Slides 2 and 3 are the only real narration in the whole deck — everything after is a callback to this. Get the events straight now and the rest of the hour is about explaining them.

Breaking It Down

The elm at Essex and Orange Streets became known as the Liberty Tree. The effigy hanging from it represented Andrew Oliver, newly named the stamp distributor for Massachusetts. Read more: Liberty Tree.

The sheriff would not take it down. Hold onto that detail — it comes back later as a sign of how weak royal authority in Boston already was.

By nightfall a crowd of thousands paraded the effigy, tore apart a building believed to be the stamp office, burned the effigy on Fort Hill, and went to Oliver's house. The next morning Oliver resigned.

Why This Matters

One night, one crowd, and an act of the British Parliament became unenforceable in an entire province. But notice you have a sequence of events, not yet an explanation of them — that gap is what the whole lecture is about.

Twelve Days Later — and Then Repeal

  • 26 August: a crowd moves on three houses, ending at Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson's mansion — doors axed, wainscoting torn out, the cupola pulled down
  • His manuscript history of Massachusetts is scattered in the mud. Some pages survive, still stained
  • 1 November: the Stamp Act takes effect. In Massachusetts it is never enforced. Nobody is left willing
  • March 1766: Parliament repeals it. Boston hangs lanterns in the same elm and toasts the king
  • The same month, the Declaratory Act asserts Parliament's authority in all cases whatsoever
Independence is eleven years away. Nobody in this story knows that.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide finishes the story fast: a second, more violent night twelve days later, the tax's official failure, and its eventual repeal. Read it as one chain of consequences, not four separate facts.

Breaking It Down

26 August 1765: a crowd destroyed the mansion of Thomas Hutchinson, the lieutenant governor — doors axed, walls torn out, his manuscript history of Massachusetts scattered in the mud. Read more: Thomas Hutchinson.

1 November 1765: the Stamp Act took effect — and was never enforced in Massachusetts, because no one was left willing to do it.

March 1766: Parliament repealed the tax. Boston celebrated by hanging lanterns in the same elm and toasting King George. The same month, the Declaratory Act asserted Parliament's authority “in all cases whatsoever.” The tax was gone; the claim of authority was not.

Why This Matters

The detail to hold: independence is eleven years away, and nobody in this story knows that. The people celebrating in 1766 are toasting the king, not planning a revolution. It's tempting to read 1765 as “the start of the Revolution,” but that's hindsight — and resisting that hindsight is one of the lecture's central lessons.

Causation

You now have a sequence: an act, an effigy, a crowd, a resignation, a second night, a repeal.

A sequence is not a claim about causation.

“After this” is not “because of this.” A causal claim names the mechanism — what operated, on whom, with what weight, and why the effect was not automatic.
The question it lets you ask: which of these causes actually produced the outcome — and how?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the first concept slide, and the most important idea in the hour. It has its own title — Causation — because everything else depends on it.

Breaking It Down

You have a sequence: act, effigy, crowd, resignation, second night, repeal. A sequence only says what came after what.

Causation is a stronger claim — that one thing actually produced another. The classic error is assuming that because B followed A, A caused B (its Latin name is post hoc ergo propter hoc).

A real causal claim names the mechanism: the actual pathway from cause to effect — who did what, through what, in what order — and it says which causes carried the most weight.

Why This Matters

This slide sets the standard every later slide has to meet. From here on, when the lecture offers a cause, ask yourself: has it named a mechanism, or just pointed at something that happened first? The question causation lets you ask: which of these causes actually produced the outcome, and how?

The Question for Today

Why did Boston explode in 1765 — and who made it happen?
Every answer rests on an assumption about what usually makes history happen. You will answer twice — now, and when you know more.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture's central question, plus the key idea that answering it depends on an assumption you might not notice you're making.

Breaking It Down

“Why did Boston explode in 1765, and who made it happen?” is really two questions folded together: a causal one (why) and an agency one (who).

The deeper point: historical disagreement often starts before anyone looks at the facts. It starts with different assumptions about what usually makes history happen — people, larger forces, or a direction history is heading.

You'll vote on this in a moment, and again at the very end. The interesting question is whether your answer changes.

Why This Matters

Don't try to settle the essential question now. The point of the hour is to give you three different tools for answering it — agency, structure, and progress — and to notice that which tool you reach for shapes the answer you get.

Why This Matters

This section teaches the first of the lecture's three explanatory frameworks: human agency.

Human Agency

Agency is the idea that history changes because people make choices — they decide, persuade, organize, resist, and take risks.

Whose agency?

Leaders — but also crowds, workers, women, enslaved people, Native nations, local organizers, and people whose names were never written down.

The question it asks

What did the people in this story actually choose — and could they have chosen otherwise?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the concept slide for the first framework. Its title is the concept itself — Human Agency — so there's no mistaking what's being taught here.

Breaking It Down

Agency is the idea that history changes because people make choices: they decide, persuade, organize, resist, and take risks.

Crucially, agency isn't only for the famous. It belongs to leaders and to crowds, workers, women, enslaved people, Native nations, local organizers, and people whose names were never written down.

Agency isn't the same as total freedom — everyone acts under constraints. It just means the choice was real and could have gone another way.

Why This Matters

The question agency lets you ask: what did the people in this story actually choose, and could they have chosen otherwise? Keep that question in mind — the next slide puts it to work on the real people behind 14 August.

Nine Men and a Shoemaker

The Loyal Nine

  • Boston tradesmen — a distiller, a printer, a jeweler
  • Meeting at the Boston Gazette office
  • They chose the tree, made the effigies, kept their names out of it

Ebenezer Mackintosh

  • Twenty-eight, a cordwainer, not wealthy
  • Captain of the South End company on Pope's Day
  • Already commanded hundreds — annually, by tradition
Nine shopkeepers cannot fill a street. Witnesses saw gentlemen among the crowd in workmen's clothes — one man's trouser leg rode up over silk stockings. Agency runs up and down that street — not just the famous, and not just the crowd.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now the concept gets demonstrated on real people. The surprise here is that the planners and the crowd were almost entirely different sets of people.

Breaking It Down

The Loyal Nine were nine Boston tradesmen — a distiller, a printer, a jeweler — who met secretly at a newspaper office, chose the tree, built the effigies, and kept their names hidden.

Ebenezer Mackintosh was the shoemaker (a “cordwainer”) they recruited, because once a year on Pope's Day he already commanded hundreds of men in the street. He was the one person who came with a crowd. Read more: Ebenezer Mackintosh.

The silk stockings: witnesses saw well-dressed men disguised in workmen's clothes. Elite Bostonians weren't just watching — some were in the street.

Why This Matters

Agency ran in every direction that night: elite planners, a working-class organizer, thousands of ordinary people, and disguised gentlemen. No single group is “the cause” by itself — which is exactly the breadth the concept slide promised.

Great Man Theory

One narrow version of agency — history through exceptional individuals. So where is Samuel Adams?

Popular accounts put Adams at the center of that night. The evidence placing him in the planning is thin — he becomes prominent in the days after.

Great Man history is seductive because famous names are the best-documented thing in the archive. The error is not that leaders never matter — it is mistaking visibility for causality.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide names a specific, narrow version of agency — Great Man Theory — and uses a famous name to show its limits.

Breaking It Down

Great Man Theory explains big change mainly through exceptional individuals. Its strength is real: leaders do sometimes make consequential decisions.

But watch what happens with Samuel Adams. Popular accounts put him at the center of 14 August — yet the evidence for his role in the actual planning is thin. He becomes prominent in the days after. Read more: Samuel Adams.

Why does he end up at the center anyway? Because famous people leave the most records. Adams wrote constantly; Mackintosh left almost nothing.

Why This Matters

That gap in the paper trail quietly becomes a gap in the story. The trap in Great Man Theory isn't that leaders never matter — it's mistaking visibility in the archive for causality in the events.

What an Agency Claim Must Show

Capacity → Decision → Mechanism → Consequence

Mackintosh could raise a crowd. He agreed to raise this one. He raised it through a company that already existed. Oliver resigned.

Skip a link and you have a character sketch, not a cause.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A four-part checklist for proving that a person actually caused something, run directly on Mackintosh so you can see it work.

Breaking It Down

Capacity — could he act? Yes; he already commanded a company. Decision — did he choose to? Yes; he agreed when the Loyal Nine asked.

Mechanism — how did the choice travel into the world? Through an organization that already existed. Consequence — what changed? Oliver resigned.

Why This Matters

Skip any one of those four links and you have a character sketch, not a cause. This is the same mechanism requirement from the causation slide, now applied to a person — and you can use this test on any historical figure for the rest of the course.

Why This Matters

Historical Structure

A structure is a durable condition that shapes and constrains the choices people can make — economies, institutions, geography, demography, labor systems, custom.

People still act

But, in Marx's phrase, not in circumstances of their own choosing. The circumstances came first.

The question it asks

What conditions made this choice available — or likely — before anyone chose?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The concept slide for the second framework, titled with the concept itself — Historical Structure.

Breaking It Down

A structure is a durable condition that shapes and constrains the choices people can make: economies, institutions, geography, demography, labor systems, custom.

People still act — but, in Marx's phrase, not in circumstances of their own choosing. The circumstances came first and set the range of what was possible.

Structures aren't invisible forces acting on their own; they're patterns that last because people keep enacting them.

Why This Matters

The question structure lets you ask: what conditions made this choice available, or likely, before anyone chose? The next slide shows a structure that had been quietly shaping Boston crowds for decades.

The Crowd Already Knew How

Every 5 November, Boston's North and South End companies paraded effigies, fought each other, and burned the pope in a bonfire.

Effigy. Procession. Bonfire. The form of 14 August was not invented that morning.

Britain after 1763

Enormous war debt, new territory to garrison, and a doctrine that Parliament's authority was total.

Boston in 1765

Assemblies long used to taxing themselves, postwar depression, and a town that could organize itself.

Pope's Day is a cultural structure — a pattern people already knew how to use. Likely and repeatable, but not necessary, and not on that date.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Structure demonstrated. The surprise is that the form of the 14 August protest wasn't invented that day — it was borrowed from a yearly tradition.

Breaking It Down

Pope's Day (5 November): every year Boston's North and South End companies paraded effigies, fought each other, and burned the pope in a bonfire. Read more: Pope Day.

Effigy, procession, bonfire — the exact form of 14 August. The Loyal Nine took a ritual Boston already knew, united two rival companies, and aimed it at an imperial official. A cultural structure is as real as an economic one.

Other structures standing in 1765: Britain's postwar debt and doctrine of total parliamentary authority; Boston's self-taxing assemblies and postwar depression.

Why This Matters

These conditions made a confrontation likely and repeatable — but they didn't make it happen on that date, in that form. That still took people choosing to act, which is why you need both frameworks, not one.

Follow the Money

Ask a different question: who was actually in that street, and what did they have to lose?

  • Postwar depression; unemployment among sailors and laborers
  • The stamp tax fell hardest on printers, merchants, and lawyers — the literate and the organized
  • Mackintosh's men were artisans and mariners with grievances of their own
A materialist reading — Marx's legacy — asks who bears the cost and who gains, and it gets you a crowd that principle alone does not. Its limit is determinism: if structures decided everything, the choices on the last three slides would not matter.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide covers a specific tradition within structural explanation — the one associated with Karl Marx — and names its most famous danger.

Breaking It Down

A materialist reading asks who bears the cost and who captures the gain. Applied here: Boston was in a depression, sailors and laborers were out of work, and the tax hit the literate, organized classes hardest. That gets you a crowd that constitutional principle alone doesn't explain.

This is Marx's real contribution — look beneath the political language at interests and conditions. The outline treats it as a productive but partial example of structural thinking.

Its limit is determinism: if structures decided everything, the human choices from the last three slides wouldn't matter at all.

Why This Matters

Notice the balance the lecture is keeping: material conditions explain a great deal, but treating them as the whole story erases agency. Neither framework alone is enough — which is exactly where the next slide goes.

Why This Matters

This section teaches the lecture's third framework: the progress narrative, and the trap that comes with it.

Progress Narrative

History told as steady movement toward the present. Was 1766 progress?

Something real was won. A tax was defeated by organized colonial opposition — and ordinary people had done the organizing.

A progress narrative is a claim, and claims get tested. Progress in what respect? For whom? At what cost? Compared with which alternative?
Boston celebrated. Hutchinson lost his house and his papers. Massachusetts still held thousands of enslaved people, for whom nothing changed. The corrective is precision, not cynicism.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The concept slide for the third framework, titled with the concept — Progress Narrative — and tested on the 1766 repeal.

Breaking It Down

A progress narrative organizes history as steady movement toward a better present: tyranny giving way to liberty, monarchy to democracy, and so on.

It's a claim, and claims get tested. Four questions make it precise: progress in what respect? For whom? At what cost? Compared with which alternative?

Run them on 1766: Boston celebrated, but Hutchinson lost his home and papers, and Massachusetts still held thousands of enslaved people for whom nothing changed.

Why This Matters

The point isn't that repeal wasn't a real win — it was. The point is that “progress” has to be earned with specifics, not assumed. As the professor puts it, the corrective is precision, not cynicism.

Whig History — What I Did Not Tell You

Trumbull, 1818 — the Revolution arranged, fifty-three years later, as a settled achievement.
  • Samuel Adams and the Loyal Nine condemned the 26th
  • Mackintosh was arrested — then released without charge
  • The Sons of Liberty commemorated the 14th every year. Never the 26th
One night became a founding. The other became an embarrassment. The participants edited the story first.
Teleology — explaining earlier events by the ending they produced. The outcome cannot be the cause of its own arrival.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide delivers information withheld since the opening, and it demonstrates Whig history — the danger built into progress narratives. Read it carefully; it's the payoff of the section.

Breaking It Down

The organizers of 14 August were horrified by 26 August. Samuel Adams condemned the Hutchinson attack; the Loyal Nine distanced themselves from Mackintosh, who was arrested and quietly released.

And then this: the Sons of Liberty commemorated the fourteenth of August every year afterward. They never commemorated the twenty-sixth.

Whig history tells the past as a tidy march toward the present, keeping the events that point that way and dropping the ones that don't. Its underlying error is teleology — explaining events by the ending they eventually produced, as if the outcome caused its own arrival. Read more: The Whig Interpretation of History.

Why This Matters

The participants themselves edited the story before any historian touched it, sorting their own recent past into the part that pointed toward the future they got and the part that didn't. That should make you wonder how often the same editing happens invisibly — including in essays you write.

Necessary, Sufficient, Neither

Necessary

Without it, this outcome does not occur in this form.

Sufficient

By itself, it produces the outcome. In history, genuinely rare.

The Stamp Act may be necessary for this crisis. It cannot explain independence alone — many taxed populations never rebelled, and Boston was toasting the king in 1766.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A precise way to talk about how strong a cause actually is — useful whenever you're tempted to say one thing “caused” a big outcome by itself.

Breaking It Down

Necessary: without it, the outcome doesn't happen in this form. Sufficient: by itself, it produces the outcome — genuinely rare in history.

The Stamp Act may have been necessary for this crisis, but it can't explain independence by itself: plenty of taxed populations never rebelled, and Boston was toasting the king in 1766.

Why This Matters

Keeping the two apart stops a common mistake — showing that something was required, then writing as if you'd shown it was the whole story.

The Disciplined “What If”

A counterfactual cannot be verified like a date. Used carefully, it exposes what an argument assumes.

  • Without Pope's Day, is there a crowd that knows how to do this?
  • Without Mackintosh, does someone else answer the Loyal Nine?
  • Without the war debt, does Parliament reach for a colonial stamp tax at all?
Change one variable and reason carefully. Change five and you are writing fiction.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Counterfactual reasoning — imagining history without one factor — used as a real analytical tool rather than idle speculation.

Breaking It Down

A counterfactual tests how much weight a cause carried: if removing it wouldn't have changed much, it was probably background, not a driver.

The three questions here: without Pope's Day, is there a crowd that knows how to do this? Without Mackintosh, does someone else answer the call? Without the war debt, does Parliament reach for a stamp tax at all?

Why This Matters

The discipline is restraint: change one variable and reason carefully; change five and you've left history for fiction.

The Structures We Passed Over

  • Print. Boston's newspapers carried the crisis between colonies — and the Loyal Nine met at a newspaper office
  • The post. A functioning postal network let committees coordinate across hundreds of miles
  • Distance. Three thousand miles of ocean meant every imperial order arrived weeks late
  • Money. Scarce hard currency made a tax payable only in specie feel confiscatory
None of these caused the riot. All of them shaped what the riot could become.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The structural factors the classroom hour didn't have time for — kept here rather than lost.

Breaking It Down

Print carried the crisis between colonies (and the Loyal Nine met at a newspaper). The post let committees coordinate across distance. Distance itself meant London's orders arrived weeks late. Scarce hard currency made a coin-only tax feel punishing.

Why This Matters

Match the Scale to the Evidence

One night in Boston

Explained by local rumor, neighborhood companies, personal grievance, and official decisions that evening.

Riots in a dozen ports

Needs imperial policy, print networks, shared political language, comparable urban conditions.

A continental outcome can emerge from local actions no participant intended. Do not use evidence from one scale to prove a claim at another.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A point about matching the size of a claim to the size of the evidence behind it.

Breaking It Down

Scale is the level a claim operates at — a person, a town, a colony, an empire. Different scales need different evidence.

One night in Boston is explained by local rumor, neighborhood companies, and official decisions that evening. Riots across a dozen ports need imperial policy, print networks, and shared political language.

Why This Matters

Don't use evidence from one scale to prove a claim at another — and remember that a large outcome can emerge from many small local actions no one coordinated.

When Events Change the Structures

Structures usually shape events. Occasionally an event rearranges the structure itself.

  • Pope's Day companies existed to fight each other — after 1765 they were a standing political instrument
  • The committees and correspondence built for one tax outlived the tax
  • Enslaved people and Native nations navigated systems built to restrict them, and found room in wartime disorder to bargain, escape, serve, or relocate
Structures hand people both the walls and the instruments — and sometimes the using remakes the wall.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The less obvious direction of the agency/structure relationship: sometimes an event reshapes the very structure that produced it.

Breaking It Down

Pope's Day companies existed to fight each other; after 1765 they were a standing political instrument. The committees built to fight one tax outlived the tax.

Enslaved people and Native nations navigated systems built to restrict them — and wartime disorder sometimes opened narrow room to bargain, escape, or relocate. Not liberation, but real agency inside a constraining structure.

Why This Matters

Structures hand people both walls and tools — and sometimes using the tools permanently changes the wall. The relationship runs both ways.

  • Bernard Bailyn — a political language of conspiracy against liberty had real causal force
  • Gordon Wood — republican ideas reshaped society more radically than intended
  • T. H. Breen — consumer practice built the networks resistance ran on
  • Woody Holton — pressure from debtors, enslaved people, and Native resistance pushed Virginia's elite; strongly contested

A Name for Reading Backward

The classroom hour gave you teleology. There is also a name for the tradition of history writing built on it.

Causation

A claim that one thing produced another — not merely that it came earlier.

Chronology is necessary but not sufficient. Establishing that the Stamp Act passed before the riot is the beginning of the work, not the end of it. A causal claim has to say how the one produced the other.

Historians distinguish kinds of causes: long-running background conditions, immediate triggers, and the mechanism connecting them. A strong explanation also assigns weight — saying which causes mattered more, and why.

The error this guards against has a Latin name: post hoc ergo propter hoc, “after this, therefore because of this.”

×
Mechanism

The actual pathway by which a cause produced an effect — who did what, through which institution, with what resources, and in what order.

It matters because “taxes caused the Revolution” names a cause without naming a mechanism. Did the tax bite financially? Did it insult a constitutional principle? Did it hit every colony at once and so create a reason to coordinate? Those are three different mechanisms supported by different evidence.

If you cannot state the mechanism, you have named a correlation and called it a cause.

×
Agency

The capacity of people — individually or collectively — to make choices that shape outcomes.

Agency is not the same as freedom. Everyone acts under constraint: law, resources, distance, custom, bad information, and no map of the future. Agency means only that the choice was real and could have gone otherwise.

It also is not limited to the famous. Ministers and governors have agency; so do printers, clergy, merchants, crowds, households, soldiers, enslaved people, and Native diplomats. Action from below is not automatically more authentic, and elite action is not automatically more decisive. Both have to prove the mechanism.

×
Great Man History

An approach that explains major historical change primarily through the decisions and character of prominent individuals.

Its appeal is real. Leaders do make consequential decisions, individual choice is easy to narrate, and famous people are by far the best-documented thing in any archive — which quietly makes them look like the best-supported cause.

Its limit: it mistakes visibility for causality. A leader's capacity to act usually depends on institutions, resources, and other people's cooperation that the leader did not create. The corrective is not to deny that leaders matter, but to ask what made their action possible and effective.

×
Historical Structure

A durable pattern of rules, relationships, resources, or meanings that shapes what people can do — constitutions, markets, property systems, bureaucracies, labor systems, religious institutions, settlement patterns, even language.

Structures last because people repeatedly act through them, institutions enforce them, and resources are distributed so that some actions are easy and others expensive.

Cultural structures count. A recurring ritual like Pope's Day is as real a structure as a tax code: it organized people, assigned roles, and told them what to do in the street.

What to avoid: talking about structures as if they act on their own. A legislature is rules, precedents, a building, clerks, constituencies, and money. Someone is always doing it.

×
Materialist Explanation

A family of approaches treating economic structures — production, property, labor, class relations, who bears costs and who captures gains — as central to explaining historical change.

Its distinctive questions are genuinely productive: who controlled land and capital, who paid the tax, whose livelihood was at stake, who stood to benefit. Applied to August 1765, it asks who was actually in the street and what they had to lose.

Its recurrent risks: treating stated belief as merely a mask for interest, assuming a whole class wanted one thing, and naming an interest without showing the mechanism.

These are not special defects of materialism. Every explanatory tradition has a matching failure mode, and all are held to the same standard.

×
Agency and Structure

“Do individuals make history, or do larger forces?” is a useful provocation and a bad answer. Historians treat agency and structure as mutually constitutive — each one exists through the other.

People act through structures: the institutions, resources, and shared expectations available to them are what make an action possible and effective. And structures persist only because people keep enacting them — a rule nobody enforces stops being a rule.

This is why the same institution can be a constraint and an instrument depending on who is using it and when. Pope's Day divided Boston every November and organized it in August.

×
Progress Narrative

An account that organizes the past as steady improvement toward a better present.

Progress is a legitimate historical claim, not an automatic error. Real gains happen and can be documented: institutions change, rights arguments intensify, participation widens.

But it is a claim, and claims get tested. Four questions make it precise: progress in what respect, for whom, at what cost, and compared with which alternative? An account that cannot answer those has asserted improvement rather than shown it.

The corrective is precision, not cynicism.

×
Teleology

Explaining earlier events by the endpoint they eventually produced — as though the past were traveling toward a destination it already knew.

The problem is logical: the outcome cannot be the cause of its own arrival. People in 1765 could not act on knowledge of 1776, and treating them as early participants in a revolution they had not imagined explains nothing.

It also distorts selection. Once you know how the story ends, the events that point toward the ending look important and the ones that do not look like noise — which is exactly how the second night in August became forgettable.

The warning is narrow. You may still judge the past, and you may still describe genuine improvement. What you may not do is let the outcome quietly become the explanation.

×
Contingency

The idea that an outcome depended on a particular convergence of conditions, choices, and timing — and so was not settled in advance.

Contingency is not the claim that events have no causes, and not the claim that anything could have happened. An outcome can be heavily constrained and still not inevitable.

Its practical use is to give historical actors back their uncertain future. Colonial leaders spent years seeking rights within the empire, loyalties stayed divided, and nobody in Boston in 1765 was working toward independence.

×
Path Dependence

The pattern by which earlier steps make some later steps easier and others more costly — so that a sequence narrows the range of live possibilities without ever determining the ending.

A catalyst is an event that accelerates a process already underway rather than creating it. The Stamp Act did not manufacture Anglo-colonial tension; it gave existing tension an occasion, a target, and an organization.

This is why repeal did not restore the old world. The grievance was withdrawn; the committees, printers, correspondence, and a population that had now done this once all remained. Each step made the next cheaper and retreat costlier.

×
Counterfactual Reasoning

Asking whether an outcome would probably have changed if one factor had been absent or different.

Historians use it as a test of causal weight, not as storytelling. If removing a factor changes nothing, it was probably background rather than a driving cause.

The discipline is in the restraint: alter one variable and reason carefully about the consequences. Alter several and the exercise stops being history.

×
Necessary and Sufficient

A necessary cause is one without which the outcome does not occur in this form. A sufficient cause is one that produces the outcome by itself.

In history, genuinely sufficient causes are rare. Most historical outcomes require a combination of conditions, none of which would have produced the result alone.

Keeping the two apart prevents a common error: showing that something was necessary and then writing as though it were the whole explanation.

×
Scale

The level at which a historical claim operates — an individual, a town, a colony, an empire, a continent, a century.

Different scales require different evidence and support different kinds of explanation. One riot can be explained by local rumor and neighborhood ties. A pattern of riots across a dozen ports requires imperial policy, communication networks, and shared political language.

The discipline: do not use evidence gathered at one scale to prove a claim at another. And remember that a large outcome can emerge from many local actions that no participant intended.

×
Whig History

Herbert Butterfield's name, from The Whig Interpretation of History (1931), for history written backward from the present — treating the past as a story of steady advance toward current arrangements.

Butterfield's specific target was a school of English constitutional historians who narrated the past as the progressive triumph of liberty and Parliament. His general point outlived that quarrel.

Three habits identify it: sorting historical actors into friends and enemies of the present; praising whoever most resembles us; and explaining events by the outcome they eventually produced.

This is teleology operating as a whole way of writing history rather than as a single mistaken sentence.

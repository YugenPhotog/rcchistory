Boston, 14 August 1765

  • At dawn, a stuffed and labeled effigy hangs from an elm at Essex and Orange Streets
  • It hangs all day. No officer in Boston dares take it down
  • By night, thousands parade it through town and pull apart a building believed to be the stamp office
  • They burn it on Fort Hill, then go to Andrew Oliver's house. His family escapes minutes ahead
  • The next morning, Oliver resigns
Remember the sheriff who would not touch it. We come back to that.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the story the whole lecture runs on, and it opens with almost no interpretation attached — just what happened, in order. That's deliberate. Hold off on deciding who's to blame until you have more to go on.

Breaking It Down

The elm at Essex and Orange Streets stood in Boston's South End. After this night it became known as the Liberty Tree and stayed a gathering place for protest for the next decade. Read more: Liberty Tree.

The effigy represented Andrew Oliver, newly appointed as Massachusetts's stamp distributor — the official job of collecting the new tax. A boot hung beside it, a visual pun on Lord Bute, a former royal minister widely blamed for unpopular policy.

"No officer dares take it down": notice what this tells you before any crowd even gathers. Royal authority in Boston was already fragile enough that a sheriff wouldn't touch an effigy hanging in public view for an entire day. Hold onto that detail — it becomes important later when the lecture explains why authority failed here.

The stamp office was a building near Oliver's dock that the crowd believed would be used to sell the stamps. Tearing it down before it ever opened was a way of making the tax physically impossible to administer, not just unpopular.

Andrew Oliver resigns the next morning — forced to resign again, publicly, under the same tree four months later on 17 December.

Why This Matters

You now have a sequence of events, but notice you don't yet have an explanation. Why did this particular crowd form, on this night, over this tax? Who actually decided to do this? Those are the questions the rest of the lecture answers. For now, just hold the story: an effigy, a crowd, a resignation. The professor's note at the bottom — "remember the sheriff who wouldn't touch it" — is a hint that this detail will matter again.

Twelve Days Later — and Then Repeal

  • 26 August: a crowd moves on three houses, ending at Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson's mansion — doors axed, wainscoting torn out, the cupola pulled down
  • His manuscript history of Massachusetts is scattered in the mud. Some pages survive, still stained
  • 1 November: the Stamp Act takes effect. In Massachusetts it is never enforced. Nobody is left willing

📖 Understanding This Slide

The story doesn't end with Oliver's resignation, and this slide covers a lot of ground fast: a second, much more violent night: twelve days later, the tax's official start date, and its eventual repeal five months after that. Read it as one continuous chain of consequences, not four separate events.

Breaking It Down

26 August 1765: a crowd attacked three houses, ending at the mansion of Thomas Hutchinson, the lieutenant governor and chief justice of Massachusetts — and Andrew Oliver's brother-in-law. This was far more destructive than the fourteenth: doors axed open, walls torn apart, the roof's cupola pulled down, furniture and silver carried off. Read more: Thomas Hutchinson.

Hutchinson had been writing a history of Massachusetts. His manuscript notes were scattered in the street and trampled into mud that night — some of those pages, still stained, survive today in the Massachusetts Archives. That's a striking irony worth sitting with: a historian's own primary-source evidence was nearly destroyed by the event he would have wanted to write about.

1 November 1765: the date the Stamp Act was supposed to take effect. In Massachusetts, it never did. There was no one left willing to enforce it — a striking outcome for an act of Parliament.

March 1766: Parliament repealed the Stamp Act. Boston celebrated by hanging lanterns in the very same elm that had carried Oliver's effigy eight months earlier, and crowds drank toasts to King George III. The same month, Parliament passed the Declaratory Act, asserting its authority to bind the colonies "in all cases whatsoever." The tax was gone; the underlying claim to authority was not.

Why This Matters

Here's the detail to really absorb: independence is eleven years away from this moment, and nobody in this story knows that. The crowds celebrating repeal in 1766 are toasting the king, not planning a revolution. Keep that firmly in mind as you read the rest of this lecture — it's very easy to look back at 1765 and read it as "the start of the Revolution," but that's not how anyone experienced it at the time. That gap between how it felt then and how we tell it now becomes the lecture's central puzzle.

What Causation Actually Requires

You now have a sequence: an act, an effigy, a crowd, a resignation, a second night, a repeal.

But knowing what happened in what order is not the same as knowing what produced what. That stronger claim — that one thing actually caused another, not merely came before it — is what historians call causation. A sequence, by itself, is not a causal claim.

“After this” is not “because of this.” A causal claim has to show what actually operated, on whom, with what weight, and why the effect was not automatic — historians call that pathway the mechanism.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide is where the lecture stops telling the story and starts asking what it would even mean to explain it. This is arguably the single most important idea in the whole hour, so take it slowly.

Breaking It Down

You now have a sequence: act, effigy, crowd, resignation, second night, repeal. A sequence just says what came after what.

Causation is a much stronger claim — that one thing actually produced another, not just preceded it. The classic trap here has a Latin name, post hoc ergo propter hoc ("after this, therefore because of this"), and it's one of the most common reasoning errors people make, in history and everywhere else.

A real causal claim has to do three things: name the mechanism (the actual pathway from cause to effect — who did what, through which institution, with what resources), assign weight (which causes mattered more than others), and explain why the effect wasn't automatic — because plenty of colonies got taxed and nothing like this happened.

Why This Matters

This slide sets the standard the rest of the lecture has to meet. Every time you see a claim about agency, structure, or contingency from here forward, ask yourself: has this actually named a mechanism, or has it just described a pattern and called it a cause? That question is the difference between historical thinking and just listing facts in order.

The Question for Today

Why did Boston explode in 1765 — and who made it happen?
Historians call this the problem of agency and structure. You will answer twice — now, and when you know more.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture's central question, plus a name for the deeper problem hiding inside it. This is the question you'll be asked to answer twice — once now, with almost no information, and once at the end of the hour.

Breaking It Down

"Why did Boston explode in 1765, and who made it happen?" Notice this is really two questions folded together: a causal question (why) and an agency question (who).

Agency and structure: historians have a name for the tension in that second question. Did specific people make a choice that caused this (agency), or did larger forces — economic pressure, institutions, tradition — make it happen regardless of who was involved (structure)? That framing is going to organize the entire lecture, and it gets resolved — not by picking a side — on slide 14.

Why This Matters

Don't try to answer the essential question definitively right now. The whole point of the hour is to give you better tools before you commit to an answer. What matters at this stage is noticing that you're being asked to hold two different kinds of questions apart: what caused this, and who is responsible for it. They're related, but they're not the same question.

Somebody had to choose that particular tree, on that particular morning, and decide what the effigy's labels would say. That's not an accident, and the next few slides go looking for who made those specific choices.

Why This Matters

Nine Men and a Shoemaker

The Loyal Nine

  • Boston tradesmen — a distiller, a printer, a jeweler
  • Meeting at the Boston Gazette office
  • They chose the tree, made the effigies, kept their names out of it

Ebenezer Mackintosh

  • Twenty-eight, a cordwainer (shoemaker), not wealthy
  • Captain of the South End company on Pope’s Day — Boston’s annual crowd tradition each 5 November, explained ahead
  • Already commanded hundreds — annually, by tradition
Nine shopkeepers cannot fill a street. Witnesses saw gentlemen among the crowd in workmen's clothes — one man's trouser leg rode up over silk stockings. The capacity to make a choice that actually shapes an outcome is what historians call agency — and here it runs up and down that street.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide introduces the actual organizers of that night, and it's built around a genuine surprise: the people who planned it and the people who carried it out were almost entirely different from each other.

Breaking It Down

The Loyal Nine were nine Boston tradesmen and businessmen — a distiller, a printer, a jeweler among them — who met secretly at the office of the Boston Gazette. Notice that connection to a newspaper; it matters again later when the lecture talks about the communication networks that let resistance spread. They chose the tree, built the effigies, and deliberately kept their own names out of it.

Agency is the capacity to make a choice that shapes an outcome. But agency isn't just something famous or powerful people have — everyone in this story exercised it, under different constraints.

Ebenezer Mackintosh was the person the Loyal Nine actually needed: a twenty-eight-year-old shoemaker (the period term is cordwainer), not wealthy, not formally educated. But once a year, on Pope's Day (5 November, Boston's local version of Guy Fawkes Night), Mackintosh commanded the South End's several hundred men by long tradition. He was the one person in Boston who already had a crowd. Read more: Ebenezer Mackintosh.

The silk stockings detail: witnesses that night reported seeing well-dressed men in the crowd, disguised in workmen's clothes. One observer watched a rioter's trouser leg ride up — revealing silk stockings underneath, a fabric only the wealthy could afford. This is physical evidence that Boston's elite weren't just watching from a distance; some of them were in the street.

Why This Matters

Nine shopkeepers alone couldn't have filled a street with thousands of people — they needed Mackintosh, and Mackintosh needed the crowd tradition Pope's Day had already built. So agency here runs in every direction at once: elite planners at the top, a working-class organizer in the middle, and thousands of ordinary Bostonians — some of them possibly disguised gentlemen — in the street itself. No single one of those groups is "the cause" by itself.

So Where Is Samuel Adams?

Popular accounts put Adams at the center of that night. The evidence placing him in the planning is thin — he becomes prominent in the days after.

It is tempting to put one famous leader at the center of a story like this — historians have a name for that habit: Great Man history. It is seductive because famous names are the best-documented thing in the archive. The error is not that leaders never matter — it is mistaking visibility for causality.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide does something a little unusual: it introduces a famous name mainly to explain why he shouldn't be at the center of this particular story — even though he usually is in popular retellings.

Breaking It Down

Samuel Adams is the figure most people associate with early Boston resistance, and he did later write about 14 August as a day "which ought to be forever remembered in America." Read more: Samuel Adams.

But look closely at the evidence: his role in planning that night is thin. He becomes visibly prominent in the days after it, not in the room where the Loyal Nine made their decisions.

Great Man history is the name for an approach that explains major change chiefly through famous individuals' decisions and character. It's appealing for an understandable reason: famous people are, by far, the best-documented figures in any archive. Adams wrote constantly, and people wrote about him constantly. Mackintosh, the actual organizer of the crowd, left almost no paper trail at all.

Why This Matters

That gap in the evidence quietly turns into a gap in the story we tell. It's not that leaders never matter — they obviously can. The trap is mistaking visibility in the historical record for causality in the actual events. Ask yourself: is a name showing up in this story because of what that person did, or because that person happened to write things down?

What an Agency Claim Must Show

Capacity → Decision → Mechanism → Consequence

Mackintosh could raise a crowd. He agreed to raise this one. He raised it through a company that already existed. Oliver resigned.

Skip a link and you have a character sketch, not a cause.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A four-part checklist for actually proving an agency claim, applied directly to Mackintosh so you can see it work on a real case rather than in the abstract.

Breaking It Down

Capacity: could this person actually act? Mackintosh could, because he already commanded a company through the Pope's Day tradition.

Decision: did they choose to? Yes — the Loyal Nine asked, and he agreed.

Mechanism: how did the choice actually travel into the world? Through an organization — the South End company — that already existed and already knew how to move as a crowd.

Consequence: what changed? Oliver resigned within a day.

Why This Matters

Skip any one of those four links and what you have left is just a character sketch — an interesting biographical fact, not an actual explanation of why something happened. This four-part test is something you can apply to any historical figure you're asked to evaluate for the rest of the semester, not just Mackintosh.

Nine men had a plan. A plan is cheap — the real puzzle is why three thousand people were willing to show up and act on it.

Why This Matters

The Crowd Already Knew How

Every 5 November, Boston's North and South End companies paraded effigies, fought each other, and burned the pope in a bonfire.

Effigy. Procession. Bonfire. The form of 14 August was not invented that morning.

Britain after 1763

Enormous war debt, new territory to garrison, and a doctrine that Parliament's authority was total.

Boston in 1765

Assemblies long used to taxing themselves, postwar depression, and a town that could organize itself.

Pope's Day is a cultural structure — a pattern people already know how to use. Likely and repeatable, but not necessary, and not on that date.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide answers the "why three thousand people" question with an idea that might surprise you: the form of that night's protest wasn't invented on the spot. It was borrowed from an existing tradition.

Breaking It Down

Pope's Day: every 5 November, Boston's North End and South End companies each built effigies, paraded them through the streets, fought each other over the honor of it, and burned an effigy of the pope in a bonfire — a local, anti-Catholic version of England's Guy Fawkes Night. Read more: Pope Day.

Look at the pattern: effigy, procession, bonfire. Now look back at 14 August: effigy, procession, bonfire. The Loyal Nine didn't invent a new form of protest — they took a ritual Boston had rehearsed annually for decades, united two companies that normally fought each other, and pointed the whole thing at an imperial official instead of a religious figure.

Structure, in the historian's sense, is exactly this: a durable pattern that shapes what people know how to do. Pope's Day is a cultural structure — not an economy or a law code, but a tradition just as real and just as capable of organizing behavior.

Other structures standing in 1765: Britain was carrying enormous debt from the Seven Years' War and needed to govern vast new territory, all under a doctrine that Parliament's authority was total. Boston, meanwhile, had assemblies long used to taxing themselves, a postwar depression, and the organizational capacity to mobilize quickly.

Why This Matters

None of these structural conditions made 14 August happen on that exact date, in that exact form — that still required people to choose to act, which is what the last section covered. But without Pope's Day's ready-made ritual and without the deeper tensions between Britain and Boston, there's no crowd for the Loyal Nine to mobilize in the first place. Remember the sheriff who wouldn't take the effigy down? That's a structure — royal authority in Boston — visibly failing to hold.

Follow the Money

Ask a different question: who was actually in that street, and what did they have to lose?

  • Postwar depression; unemployment among sailors and laborers
  • The stamp tax fell hardest on printers, merchants, and lawyers — the literate and the organized
  • Mackintosh's men were artisans and mariners with grievances of their own
A materialist reading asks who bears the cost and who gains — and it gets you a crowd that principle alone does not.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A different lens entirely: instead of asking who chose to act or what traditions were available, this slide asks who actually had something material at stake.

Breaking It Down

Materialist explanation is a family of approaches that treats economic conditions — who bears costs, who captures gains, whose livelihood is threatened — as central to why people act.

Applied here: Boston in 1765 was in a postwar economic depression, with sailors and laborers out of work. The stamp tax itself fell hardest on printers, merchants, and lawyers — the literate, organized people best equipped to protest publicly. And Mackintosh's own crowd was largely artisans and mariners who had economic grievances that had nothing specifically to do with constitutional theory.

Why This Matters

This lens earns its place because it explains something the earlier slides don't: constitutional principle alone doesn't obviously get you three thousand people willing to spend a August night destroying property. Economic hardship does a lot of that explanatory work. But notice this is one lens among several — the next slide is going to ask what this approach might miss.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the slide that ties the whole lecture's method together, and it does two things at once: it holds every explanatory approach to the same standard, and it resolves the agency-versus-structure question from slide 5.

Breaking It Down

Equal standards, no exceptions: an interests-based explanation (like slide 13's) risks treating what people said they believed as just a cover story for what they wanted. An ideas-based explanation risks floating free of who actually benefited from an outcome. An institutions-based explanation risks mistaking the rules written down somewhere for how power actually worked in the street. Every single tradition has to show its mechanism and its evidence, or it doesn't count — no framework gets a pass for being traditional, radical, or currently fashionable.

The false choice: remember the question from slide 5 — do people make history, or do forces? Look at Pope's Day one more time. It was a cage: every November it set the North and South Ends against each other. And it was a tool, the moment somebody united it and pointed it outward in August. Agency and structure are mutually constitutive — a fancy phrase that just means they make each other possible. People act through structures; structures survive only because people keep acting them out.

Why This Matters

This is probably the single most important idea to take away from the entire lecture. "Individuals versus forces" isn't a real choice you have to make — it's a false dichotomy. Real historical explanation almost always requires both, working together, and the analytical job is showing exactly how they interact rather than picking a side.

⏸ Find the Flaw

“The riots of August 1765 show that Bostonians had already decided on independence.”

This sentence is wrong. Find two things wrong with it.

One flaw is about evidence. One is about time.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This activity hands you a plausible-sounding claim and asks you to find what's wrong with it. It's harder than it looks, because the sentence isn't obviously false on first read.

Breaking It Down

The claim: "The riots of August 1765 show that Bostonians had already decided on independence." You're told there are two separate flaws — one about evidence, one about time.

The evidence flaw: what did the crowds actually demand? Oliver's resignation and the tax's repeal — not separation from Britain. When repeal came in 1766, they toasted the king. That's not the behavior of people who'd already decided to leave the empire.

The time flaw: independence is eleven years away from this moment. Reading the events of 1765 as if they were already building toward 1776 assumes an outcome that nobody living through 1765 could possibly have known about.

Why This Matters

This exercise is training you to notice a mistake historians work hard to avoid: reading the past as if it were always headed toward whatever eventually happened. The time flaw you just identified is going to come back with a formal name two slides from now — keep it in mind.

Was 1766 Progress?

Something real was won. A tax was defeated by organized colonial opposition — and ordinary people had done the organizing.

Calling this progress makes a claim — that things moved toward something better. Historians call that framing a progress narrative, and like any claim, it gets tested: progress in what respect? for whom? at what cost? compared with which alternative?
Boston celebrated. Hutchinson lost his house and his papers. Massachusetts still held thousands of enslaved people, for whom nothing changed. The corrective is precision, not cynicism.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The repeal of the Stamp Act looks like an obvious win. This slide asks you to slow down and test that judgment rather than just accepting it.

Breaking It Down

What was genuinely won: organized colonial resistance defeated a tax, and ordinary people — not just elites — had done much of the organizing. That's a real, meaningful outcome, not nothing.

A progress narrative is a claim that events moved toward something better — and like any claim, it can be tested rather than just asserted. Four questions make it precise: progress in what respect? For whom? At what cost? Compared with which alternative?

Run those questions on 1766: Boston celebrated. But Thomas Hutchinson lost his house, his family's possessions, and years of his own historical research. And Massachusetts in 1766 still held thousands of enslaved people, for whom this entire crisis changed absolutely nothing.

Why This Matters

The point here isn't to conclude that repeal wasn't a real accomplishment — it was. The point is that "progress" is a claim you have to earn with specifics, not a label you can apply automatically to any outcome you approve of. As the professor puts it, the corrective for a vague progress narrative is precision, not cynicism — you don't have to become cynical about the past to be careful about it.

What I Did Not Tell You

Trumbull, 1818 — the Revolution arranged, fifty-three years later, as a settled achievement.
  • Samuel Adams and the Loyal Nine condemned the 26th
  • Mackintosh was arrested — then released without charge
  • The Sons of Liberty commemorated the 14th every year. Never the 26th
One night became a founding. The other became an embarrassment. The participants edited the story first.
Teleology — explaining earlier events by the ending they produced. The outcome cannot be the cause of its own arrival.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here's the information withheld since slide 3, and it changes how you should understand the entire story. This is probably the single most important slide in the lecture — read it carefully.

Breaking It Down

The organizers of 14 August were horrified by 26 August. Samuel Adams condemned the Hutchinson attack as a lawless assault on property. The Loyal Nine distanced themselves from Mackintosh immediately. He was arrested afterward — and then released without charge, because prosecuting him risked causing more unrest than letting him go.

And here's the detail that really lands it: every year afterward, the Sons of Liberty gathered under the same elm to commemorate the fourteenth of August. John Adams records dining with 350 of them at a Dorchester tavern on the fourth anniversary. They never commemorated the twenty-sixth.

Two crowd actions, twelve days apart, in the same town, over the same grievance. One became an annual founding celebration. The other quietly disappeared from how people talked about that summer.

Teleology is the name for explaining earlier events by the ending they eventually produced — as if the past were traveling toward a destination it already knew. It's a logical problem, not just a bad habit: the outcome can't actually be the cause of its own arrival. Nobody in 1765 could act on knowledge of events in 1776.

Why This Matters

The participants themselves edited this story before any historian ever touched it. They sorted their own recent past into the part that pointed toward the future they eventually got, and the part that didn't — and quietly let the second part fade. That's teleology in action, done by the people who lived it, which should make you wonder how often historians (and you, writing essays) do the same thing without noticing.

Agency

📖 Understanding This Slide

A direct payoff of everything the lecture has built so far: the same single night, examined through three different explanatory approaches, each of which shows you something real and hides something else.

Breaking It Down

Agency reveals the Loyal Nine, Mackintosh, and the specific decisions they made — and it obscures why thousands of ordinary people were willing to follow along.

Structure reveals the debt, the assemblies, Pope's Day, and the depression — and it obscures why the crisis broke out on this particular night rather than some other one.

Contingency (the unpredictable, path-dependent unfolding of events after the fact) reveals the twenty-sixth, the eventual repeal, and the political network that outlasted the tax itself — and it obscures the deeper pressures that had been building regardless of how any single night went.

Why This Matters

Every explanatory framework reveals some causes and hides others — that's not a flaw you fix by finding the one "correct" lens. It's exactly why historians learn to use more than one framework, and why disagreement between historians using different lenses isn't automatically a sign that someone is getting it wrong. This idea sets up next lecture directly: if every framework is partial, how do you judge which explanation is actually stronger?

Back to the Elm

Structures made it likely → Choices made it this → Contingency decided what it became
Nobody planned the twenty-sixth, or the repeal, or the network that outlasted the tax. That unscripted way conditions and choices happened to converge — not fixed in advance, not random either — is what contingency names.

Breaking It Down

Structures made it likely: Boston had the debt, the assemblies, the depression, and a crowd tradition built for exactly this kind of protest.

Path dependence is the pattern where earlier steps make later ones cheaper, and reversing course more costly. Repeal removed the tax — but not the committees, not the printers, not a population that had now organized like this once and discovered it worked. Each step made the next one easier and retreat harder.

Why This Matters

Necessary, Sufficient, Neither

Necessary

Without it, this outcome does not occur in this form.

Sufficient

By itself, it produces the outcome. In history, genuinely rare.

The Stamp Act may be necessary for this crisis. It cannot explain independence alone — many taxed populations never rebelled, and Boston was toasting the king in 1766.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A precise distinction for talking about how strong a cause actually is — useful anytime you're tempted to say one thing "caused" a big historical outcome all by itself.

Breaking It Down

Necessary: without this cause, the outcome doesn't happen in this particular form. Sufficient: this cause, by itself, produces the outcome. In real history, genuinely sufficient causes are rare — most big outcomes need a combination of factors, none of which would have been enough alone.

Applied here: the Stamp Act may well have been necessary for this specific crisis. But it can't explain American independence by itself — plenty of other populations got taxed without rebelling, and Boston itself was toasting the king just months later in 1766.

Why This Matters

Keeping necessary and sufficient apart stops you from making a common mistake: showing that something was required for an outcome, and then writing as if you'd shown it was the whole explanation. Those are very different claims.

The Disciplined “What If”

One way to test how much a factor actually mattered is to imagine the story without it. Historians call that a counterfactual — it cannot be verified like a date, but used carefully, it exposes what an argument assumes.

  • Without Pope's Day, is there a crowd that knows how to do this?
  • Without Mackintosh, does someone else answer the Loyal Nine?
  • Without the war debt, does Parliament reach for a colonial stamp tax at all?
Change one variable and reason carefully. Change five and you are writing fiction.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Counterfactual reasoning — imagining history without one particular factor — sounds like idle speculation, but historians use it as a genuine analytical tool, with real discipline attached.

Breaking It Down

A counterfactual can't be verified the way a date can. Used carefully, it's a way of testing how much causal weight a factor actually carried: if removing it wouldn't have changed much, it was probably background noise rather than a real driver.

The three questions on this slide: without Pope's Day, is there a crowd that already knows how to do this? Without Mackintosh specifically, does someone else answer the Loyal Nine's call? Without Britain's massive war debt, does Parliament reach for a colonial stamp tax at all?

Why This Matters

The discipline is entirely in the restraint. Change one variable at a time and reason carefully about what plausibly follows — change five variables at once and you've stopped doing history and started writing speculative fiction.

The Structures We Passed Over

  • Print. Boston's newspapers carried the crisis between colonies — and the Loyal Nine met at a newspaper office
  • The post. A functioning postal network let committees coordinate across hundreds of miles
  • Distance. Three thousand miles of ocean meant every imperial order arrived weeks late
  • Money. Scarce hard currency made a tax payable only in specie feel confiscatory
None of these caused the riot. All of them shaped what the riot could become.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The classroom lecture didn't have time to cover every structural factor at play in 1765 — this slide picks up four that got cut for time, without losing them entirely.

Breaking It Down

Print: Boston's newspapers carried news of the crisis between colonies rapidly — and remember, the Loyal Nine themselves met at a newspaper office.

The postal network let committees of correspondence coordinate resistance across hundreds of miles.

Distance: three thousand miles of ocean meant every order from London arrived weeks out of date by the time it reached Boston.

Scarce hard currency made a tax payable only in coin feel especially punishing in a cash-strapped colonial economy.

Why This Matters

None of these four factors caused the riot by itself — but all of them shaped what the riot could grow into and how far its consequences could spread. This is a good reminder that "structure" in history usually means several overlapping conditions working together, not one single dominant force.

Match the Scale to the Evidence

Some claims cover one night in one town. Others cover an empire. The level a claim operates at is its scale — and the evidence it needs changes with it.

One night in Boston

Explained by local rumor, neighborhood companies, personal grievance, and official decisions that evening.

Riots in a dozen ports

Needs imperial policy, print networks, shared political language, comparable urban conditions.

A continental outcome can emerge from local actions no participant intended. Do not use evidence from one scale to prove a claim at another.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A methodological point about matching the size of a historical claim to the size of the evidence actually available to support it.

Breaking It Down

Scale is the level at which a historical claim operates — an individual, a town, a colony, a whole empire. Different scales need different kinds and amounts of evidence.

One night's riot in Boston can be explained by local rumor, existing neighborhood companies, personal grievances, and what officials chose to do that specific evening. A pattern of riots repeating across a dozen different ports needs a much bigger explanatory apparatus — imperial policy, print networks, shared political vocabulary, comparable urban conditions across colonies.

Why This Matters

The rule to remember: don't use evidence gathered at one scale to prove a claim operating at a different scale. And keep in mind that a large-scale outcome — like colony-wide resistance — can emerge from thousands of small local actions that no single participant ever intended or planned.

When Events Change the Structures

Structures usually shape events. Occasionally an event rearranges the structure itself.

  • Pope's Day companies existed to fight each other — after 1765 they were a standing political instrument
  • The committees and correspondence built for one tax outlived the tax
  • Enslaved people and Native nations navigated systems built to restrict them, and found room in wartime disorder to bargain, escape, serve, or relocate
Structures hand people both the walls and the instruments — and sometimes the using remakes the wall.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Usually structures shape events. This slide covers the less intuitive reverse case: sometimes a single event reshapes the structure that produced it in the first place.

Breaking It Down

Pope's Day companies existed for years as rivals whose whole point was fighting each other every November. After 1765, that same organizational structure became a standing political instrument — something that could be called on again for future resistance.

The committees and correspondence networks built to fight one specific tax didn't disband once that tax was repealed. They outlived the crisis that created them.

Enslaved people and Native nations navigated legal and military systems that were built specifically to restrict them — but the disorder of wartime sometimes opened real, if narrow, room to bargain, escape, serve in exchange for freedom, or relocate. That's not liberation, but it's a meaningful example of people finding agency inside a structure designed to deny it to them.

Why This Matters

Structures hand people both walls (limits on what they can do) and instruments (tools they can use) — and sometimes the act of using those instruments permanently changes the wall itself. That's a genuinely two-directional relationship between people and the structures around them, not a one-way street.

And the Historians Disagree

  • Charles Beard — property and material interest shaped political choice
  • Bernard Bailyn — a political language of conspiracy against liberty had real causal force
  • Gordon Wood — republican ideas reshaped society more radically than intended
  • T. H. Breen — consumer practice built the networks resistance ran on
  • Woody Holton — pressure from debtors, enslaved people, and Native resistance pushed Virginia's elite; strongly contested
  • Alan Taylor — a continental story of empires, Native nations, and Loyalists

📖 Understanding This Slide

A preview of the actual professional debate over what caused the American Revolution — and a direct setup for next lecture, which is entirely about how to judge between disagreements like these.

Breaking It Down

Charles Beard argued that property and material economic interest shaped political choices during the founding era.

Bernard Bailyn argued that a specific political language — a real fear of conspiracy against liberty — had genuine causal force, not just economic interest.

Gordon Wood argued that republican ideas reshaped colonial society far more radically than anyone actually intended when the crisis began.

T. H. Breen argued that everyday consumer practices — what people bought and boycotted — built the actual networks that organized resistance.

Woody Holton argued that pressure from debtors, enslaved people, and Native resistance pushed Virginia's elite toward revolution; this reading remains strongly contested among historians.

Alan Taylor tells the story on a continental scale, including empires, Native nations, and Loyalists that a Boston-focused account leaves out entirely.

Why This Matters

Notice that these six historians aren't marching from earlier error toward later truth — they're offering genuinely competing, evidence-based arguments that remain revisable. That's normal, healthy scholarly disagreement, not a sign that history is unreliable. How to actually judge between arguments like these is exactly what Lecture 3 is about.

A Name for Reading Backward

The classroom hour gave you teleology. There is also a name for the tradition of history writing built on it.

  • In 1931 the British historian Herbert Butterfield published a short book attacking what he called Whig history

  • Its effect: compressing a messy, contested past into a story of steady advance toward the present arrangement
  • Butterfield's target was a specific school of English constitutional historians — but the habit is general
You already saw it done by the participants themselves: the Sons of Liberty commemorated 14 August every year and never the 26th.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide gives a formal name to something you already met on slide 18: the habit of explaining the past by reading the ending back into it.

Breaking It Down

You already have the concept — teleology, from slide 18. This slide adds the name of the specific tradition of history writing built on that habit.

In 1931, British historian Herbert Butterfield published a short, influential book attacking what he called Whig history — the practice of sorting past actors into friends and enemies of the present, praising whoever most resembles "us," and compressing a genuinely messy, contested past into a single tidy story of steady advance. Read more: The Whig Interpretation of History.

Butterfield's original target was a specific school of English constitutional historians who told England's story as the steady triumph of liberty and Parliament. But the habit he identified turned out to be far more general — it shows up anywhere a story gets tidied up to point toward wherever it eventually ended.

Why This Matters

You already saw Whig history in action, done by the actual historical participants themselves: the Sons of Liberty commemorated 14 August every single year and never once commemorated 26 August. They edited their own recent past into a tidy story before any professional historian ever touched it.

Causation

A claim that one thing produced another — not merely that it came earlier.

Chronology is necessary but not sufficient. Establishing that the Stamp Act passed before the riot is the beginning of the work, not the end of it. A causal claim has to say how the one produced the other.

Historians distinguish kinds of causes: long-running background conditions, immediate triggers, and the mechanism connecting them. A strong explanation also assigns weight — saying which causes mattered more, and why.

The error this guards against has a Latin name: post hoc ergo propter hoc, “after this, therefore because of this.”

×
Mechanism

The actual pathway by which a cause produced an effect — who did what, through which institution, with what resources, and in what order.

It matters because “taxes caused the Revolution” names a cause without naming a mechanism. Did the tax bite financially? Did it insult a constitutional principle? Did it hit every colony at once and so create a reason to coordinate? Those are three different mechanisms supported by different evidence.

If you cannot state the mechanism, you have named a correlation and called it a cause.

×
Agency

The capacity of people — individually or collectively — to make choices that shape outcomes.

Agency is not the same as freedom. Everyone acts under constraint: law, resources, distance, custom, bad information, and no map of the future. Agency means only that the choice was real and could have gone otherwise.

It also is not limited to the famous. Ministers and governors have agency; so do printers, clergy, merchants, crowds, households, soldiers, enslaved people, and Native diplomats. Action from below is not automatically more authentic, and elite action is not automatically more decisive. Both have to prove the mechanism.

×
Great Man History

An approach that explains major historical change primarily through the decisions and character of prominent individuals.

Its appeal is real. Leaders do make consequential decisions, individual choice is easy to narrate, and famous people are by far the best-documented thing in any archive — which quietly makes them look like the best-supported cause.

Its limit: it mistakes visibility for causality. A leader's capacity to act usually depends on institutions, resources, and other people's cooperation that the leader did not create. The corrective is not to deny that leaders matter, but to ask what made their action possible and effective.

×
Historical Structure

A durable pattern of rules, relationships, resources, or meanings that shapes what people can do — constitutions, markets, property systems, bureaucracies, labor systems, religious institutions, settlement patterns, even language.

Structures last because people repeatedly act through them, institutions enforce them, and resources are distributed so that some actions are easy and others expensive.

Cultural structures count. A recurring ritual like Pope's Day is as real a structure as a tax code: it organized people, assigned roles, and told them what to do in the street.

What to avoid: talking about structures as if they act on their own. A legislature is rules, precedents, a building, clerks, constituencies, and money. Someone is always doing it.

×
Materialist Explanation

A family of approaches treating economic structures — production, property, labor, class relations, who bears costs and who captures gains — as central to explaining historical change.

Its distinctive questions are genuinely productive: who controlled land and capital, who paid the tax, whose livelihood was at stake, who stood to benefit. Applied to August 1765, it asks who was actually in the street and what they had to lose.

Its recurrent risks: treating stated belief as merely a mask for interest, assuming a whole class wanted one thing, and naming an interest without showing the mechanism.

These are not special defects of materialism. Every explanatory tradition has a matching failure mode, and all are held to the same standard.

×
Agency and Structure

“Do individuals make history, or do larger forces?” is a useful provocation and a bad answer. Historians treat agency and structure as mutually constitutive — each one exists through the other.

People act through structures: the institutions, resources, and shared expectations available to them are what make an action possible and effective. And structures persist only because people keep enacting them — a rule nobody enforces stops being a rule.

This is why the same institution can be a constraint and an instrument depending on who is using it and when. Pope's Day divided Boston every November and organized it in August.

×
Progress Narrative

An account that organizes the past as steady improvement toward a better present.

Progress is a legitimate historical claim, not an automatic error. Real gains happen and can be documented: institutions change, rights arguments intensify, participation widens.

But it is a claim, and claims get tested. Four questions make it precise: progress in what respect, for whom, at what cost, and compared with which alternative? An account that cannot answer those has asserted improvement rather than shown it.

The corrective is precision, not cynicism.

×
Teleology

Explaining earlier events by the endpoint they eventually produced — as though the past were traveling toward a destination it already knew.

The problem is logical: the outcome cannot be the cause of its own arrival. People in 1765 could not act on knowledge of 1776, and treating them as early participants in a revolution they had not imagined explains nothing.

It also distorts selection. Once you know how the story ends, the events that point toward the ending look important and the ones that do not look like noise — which is exactly how the second night in August became forgettable.

The warning is narrow. You may still judge the past, and you may still describe genuine improvement. What you may not do is let the outcome quietly become the explanation.

×
Contingency

The idea that an outcome depended on a particular convergence of conditions, choices, and timing — and so was not settled in advance.

Contingency is not the claim that events have no causes, and not the claim that anything could have happened. An outcome can be heavily constrained and still not inevitable.

Its practical use is to give historical actors back their uncertain future. Colonial leaders spent years seeking rights within the empire, loyalties stayed divided, and nobody in Boston in 1765 was working toward independence.

×
Path Dependence

The pattern by which earlier steps make some later steps easier and others more costly — so that a sequence narrows the range of live possibilities without ever determining the ending.

A catalyst is an event that accelerates a process already underway rather than creating it. The Stamp Act did not manufacture Anglo-colonial tension; it gave existing tension an occasion, a target, and an organization.

This is why repeal did not restore the old world. The grievance was withdrawn; the committees, printers, correspondence, and a population that had now done this once all remained. Each step made the next cheaper and retreat costlier.

×
Counterfactual Reasoning

Asking whether an outcome would probably have changed if one factor had been absent or different.

Historians use it as a test of causal weight, not as storytelling. If removing a factor changes nothing, it was probably background rather than a driving cause.

The discipline is in the restraint: alter one variable and reason carefully about the consequences. Alter several and the exercise stops being history.

×
Necessary and Sufficient

A necessary cause is one without which the outcome does not occur in this form. A sufficient cause is one that produces the outcome by itself.

In history, genuinely sufficient causes are rare. Most historical outcomes require a combination of conditions, none of which would have produced the result alone.

Keeping the two apart prevents a common error: showing that something was necessary and then writing as though it were the whole explanation.

×
Scale

The level at which a historical claim operates — an individual, a town, a colony, an empire, a continent, a century.

Different scales require different evidence and support different kinds of explanation. One riot can be explained by local rumor and neighborhood ties. A pattern of riots across a dozen ports requires imperial policy, communication networks, and shared political language.

The discipline: do not use evidence gathered at one scale to prove a claim at another. And remember that a large outcome can emerge from many local actions that no participant intended.

×
Whig History

Herbert Butterfield's name, from The Whig Interpretation of History (1931), for history written backward from the present — treating the past as a story of steady advance toward current arrangements.

Butterfield's specific target was a school of English constitutional historians who narrated the past as the progressive triumph of liberty and Parliament. His general point outlived that quarrel.

Three habits identify it: sorting historical actors into friends and enemies of the present; praising whoever most resembles us; and explaining events by the outcome they eventually produced.

This is teleology operating as a whole way of writing history rather than as a single mistaken sentence.

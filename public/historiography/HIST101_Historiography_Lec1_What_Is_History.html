King Street, Boston

Eleven people shot. Five die.

Within a week, two accounts are written down.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide does something unusual for a history course: it gives you an event without telling you what it means. You get a street, a date, and a body count. That is deliberate. Everything you already "know" about this night arrived through somebody else's interpretation, and the lecture is going to take that interpretation apart before handing it back to you.

Breaking It Down

King Street, Boston, 5 March 1770: British troops had been stationed in Boston since 1768, sent to enforce customs collection and to back up royal officials after years of resistance to parliamentary taxation. Soldiers and townspeople had been rubbing against each other for eighteen months. Read more: Boston Massacre.

Eleven shot, five dead: Samuel Gray, Crispus Attucks, and James Caldwell died where they fell. Samuel Maverick, seventeen years old, died the next morning. Patrick Carr died on 14 March.

"Two accounts are written down": Notice the verb. Nobody filmed this. Nobody recorded it. Everything anyone will ever know about that night has to pass through a person who decided to write something.

Why This Matters

Your professor is deliberately not calling this "the Boston Massacre" yet. That name is not a neutral description — it was a political claim made by one side within weeks of the shooting, and it won so completely that it is now the event's official title in your textbook. Sitting with the unnamed version for a few minutes is the whole point.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eric Hinderaker – Boston's Massacre (2017)

Hinderaker argues that the most interesting historical question about that night is not "what happened?" but "why has the meaning of what happened never been settled?" From the first week, rival groups were producing rival versions for rival audiences — and those versions, not the twenty minutes of violence, are what shaped the coming Revolution.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here are the two accounts, in the writers' own words. Read them slowly. The temptation is to skim to the end and pick a side; resist it, because the real lesson is in how similar these documents are in kind even though they conflict in content.

Breaking It Down

Captain Thomas Preston commanded the soldiers that night. He wrote his account on 12 March 1770 — one week after the shooting — while in jail, awaiting trial for murder. His two sentences do very specific work: the first establishes that the crowd was threatening, and the second denies that he gave the order to fire. Read more: Thomas Preston.

The anonymous Boston narrative was published in Boston that same spring by a committee of the town. Its two sentences do the mirror-image work: the first minimizes the provocation to a few snowballs, and the second says Preston ordered the shooting and then repeated the order.

Notice the hedge: the Boston account says Preston "is said to have ordered them to fire." Even the document making the accusation is reporting what other people claimed. That small phrase is the kind of thing historians live on.

Why This Matters

Both of these are what your course will call primary sources — made inside the event by people who were part of it. Neither one is "the truth," and neither one is worthless. Each is a person under pressure, writing for an audience, trying to be believed. Learning to read documents that way, rather than as containers of fact you pour out, is most of what this course will teach you.

Not in dispute

  • The date and the street

  • Who provoked whom

The disagreement is not about what the facts are. It is about what the facts mean.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the slide that breaks the easy answer. If you assumed the two accounts were wildly different stories, look again: they line up on nearly every checkable detail. The conflict is concentrated in two places, and those two places are exactly the ones where interpretation lives.

Breaking It Down

What is not in dispute: the date, the street, Preston's command of the party, the gathering crowd at the Custom House, the throwing of snowballs and clubs, and the death of Samuel Gray. Both sides say all of it — and the fact that two hostile witnesses agree is precisely why historians treat these details as solid.

What is in dispute: who provoked whom, and whether Preston gave the order to fire. Both are questions about intention and responsibility — and intention is never sitting on the surface of a document.

The ropewalk: both accounts also mention a brawl a few days earlier at John Gray's ropewalk, where soldiers and rope-makers fought. Each side blames the other. Samuel Gray, killed on King Street, worked there.

Why This Matters

Here is the crucial shift: the disagreement is not about what the facts are. It is about what the facts mean. That distinction is the engine of this entire lecture, and it explains something that otherwise looks like a scandal — how professional historians, all of them honest, all of them looking at the same documents, can reach conclusions that genuinely conflict.

The Question for Today

If history is simply what happened, why do accounts of the same event disagree?
Two easy answers we are going to refuse: that one side is simply lying, and that nobody can really know anything.
Historical knowledge is inferential, provisional, and corrigible — but not arbitrary.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The essential question of the day, plus two escape hatches your professor is welding shut. Both escapes feel reasonable. Both let you stop thinking. The position this course takes is harder to hold than either, which is why it needs a whole lecture.

Breaking It Down

"One side is simply lying": sometimes people do lie. But if that is your explanation every time two accounts conflict, you will be wrong most of the time — and you will never have to examine a document closely, because you will have decided in advance which one to throw away.

"Nobody can really know anything": this one sounds worldly and sophisticated. It is not. If no account can be better supported than another, then there is no difference between a historian and a liar, and no reason for you to be in this room.

The third position: historical knowledge is inferential (built by reasoning from traces), provisional (open to revision), and correctable (it can be shown to be wrong) — but it is not arbitrary. Some accounts really are better than others, and there are standards for saying so.

Why This Matters

Almost every bad argument you will encounter about history — online, in politics, in family arguments — is one of those two escape hatches wearing a costume. Either "they're lying to you" or "it's all just perspective anyway." By the end of today you should have a third thing to say, and by the end of the third lecture you should be able to defend it.

The rest of the lecture gives your instincts their formal names and sharpens them.

Section I

The Past Is Not the Same as History

Why two honest accounts of one night can both survive.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A section marker. The lecture is turning from the puzzle to the equipment needed to solve it, and the first piece of equipment is a distinction between two words you have probably used interchangeably your whole life.

Breaking It Down

The past and history are not synonyms. For about ninety seconds that will sound like word games. Then it will turn out to be the entire discipline.

Why This Matters

Once you can hold this distinction, the puzzle from the opening stops being a scandal. Two honest accounts of one night can both survive because neither one is the night itself — each is somebody's account of it, built for a purpose, from a position, out of what that person could see.

Two Different Things

The Past

  • Everything that happened
  • Gone, and not recoverable
  • Most of it left no trace

History

  • An account of part of the past
  • Built from surviving traces
  • Answers a particular question
A house is built from selected materials, chosen and arranged for a purpose. A history is constructed the same way — built from selected traces, arranged to answer a question.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the distinction. The past is everything that ever happened. History is the account somebody built out of the small fraction of it that left traces. Confusing the two is the single most common mistake beginning students make, and it is the source of most bad arguments about "what really happened."

Breaking It Down

The past: everything that has already occurred — not just the important parts, not just the recorded parts, not just the remembered parts. Most human action left no durable trace at all. Nearly every conversation ever held vanished the moment it ended.

History: the account. Books, articles, lectures, documentaries, museum exhibits, maps, arguments. A history is always partial, because it answers a particular question, works at a particular scale, and selects from whatever evidence survives.

Traces: documents, objects, buildings, landscapes, images, numbers — anything the past left behind that made it to the present. The pop-up on this slide adds provenance, which is a trace's own history: who made it, when, why, and how it got to where you found it.

Built from selected materials: a house is built from lumber, brick, and glass, chosen and arranged for a purpose — you would not accuse a builder of "inventing" a house because they selected the materials rather than using every material that exists. A history works the same way: it is constructed from selected traces, arranged to answer a question. That is what "constructed" means for the rest of this course — not fabricated, but built.

Why This Matters

This is why "partial" does not mean "distorted." Every history selects, and selection is what makes an account intelligible instead of an undifferentiated pile. The question is never "did this historian leave things out?" — of course they did. The question is whether the selection was governed by a real question and honest evidence, or by a conclusion decided in advance.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Marc Bloch – The Historian's Craft

Bloch, a French medievalist who was executed by the Gestapo in 1944 for his work in the Resistance, insisted that history is not the collecting of facts but the disciplined questioning of evidence. The historian does not receive the past; the historian interrogates what is left of it.

R. G. Collingwood – The Idea of History

Collingwood's contribution is a single sharp idea: sources do not volunteer meaning. A document sits there mutely until someone asks it something. Change the question and the same document tells you something different — which is exactly what happens on slide 19 of this lecture.

History Is Significant, True, and Storied

Significant
Someone asked a question and selected what bears on it
True
Constrained by evidence — claims can be wrong
Storied
Organized across time so it explains, not just lists
Remember: constructed does not mean invented. Selection gives an account focus. Evidence decides what it is allowed to say.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three words that together define what history is. Each is doing precise work, and each is easy to misread. Take them one at a time, then read the red caution box twice, because that sentence is the one most likely to be misheard for the rest of the semester.

Breaking It Down

Significant: significance is not a property a fact carries around by itself. It comes from the relationship between a fact and a question. A minor tax regulation is a footnote in an artist's biography and the central document in a study of imperial administration. Same fact, different weight, because different question.

True: historians establish many things with very high confidence — a law was passed, a battle was fought, a census reported a number. But bigger claims about motive, cause, and significance are arguments, and arguments are judged by how well they handle the evidence, including the evidence against them.

Storied: narrative is not decoration on top of the facts. It is a form of explanation. Telling a story establishes sequence, connects actions to consequences, and separates ordinary background conditions from genuine turning points. Deciding where to begin is already an interpretive act.

The danger of story: narrative can also lie. It can make outcomes look inevitable when they were not, flatten complicated people into heroes and villains, and impose a tidy shape on events that were a mess. The most compelling story is not automatically the best history.

Why This Matters

Constructed does not mean invented. When your professor says history is constructed, that is a claim about labor — somebody built this, out of evidence, by making choices you are allowed to inspect. It is not a claim that history is made up. Selection gives an account its focus. Evidence decides what the account is permitted to say.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard J. Evans – In Defence of History

Evans wrote his book against critics who argued that because historians select, interpret, and write in language, history is essentially indistinguishable from fiction. His answer concedes all the premises and rejects the conclusion: yes, historians select and interpret — and no, that does not mean they are free to say anything, because the evidence keeps saying no.

Section II

The Equipment

One workflow for producing history. One toolkit for analyzing it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The second section introduces two lists that you will use for the rest of the semester. They are easy to confuse with each other, so notice from the start that they do different jobs.

Breaking It Down

One list describes how a historian works from the beginning of a project to the end. The other describes what a historian asks the evidence at any point along the way.

Why This Matters

These two frameworks are the intellectual operating system for this course. Every source analysis, every exam question, and every discussion post you write this term will be running one or both of them. Learn them now and the rest of the semester gets noticeably easier.

The Five Moves Historians Make

The workflow — how an account gets produced.

Ask a question → Find sources → Evaluate them → Interpret the evidence → Construct and defend
The last move is public. An argument nobody can inspect is not yet scholarship.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the workflow — the sequence a historian moves through to produce an account. It looks like a straight line on the slide. In practice it loops: what you find sends you back to revise what you asked.

Breaking It Down

Ask a question: strong questions cannot be looked up. "When did the Stamp Act pass?" is a lookup. "Why did the Stamp Act provoke an imperial crisis?" requires explanation, and the answer can be argued.

Find sources: archives, newspapers, court records, letters, account books, objects, landscapes, datasets. Historians face two opposite problems here. Scarcity forces cautious reconstruction. Abundance — and some topics generate mountains of paper — forces principled sampling.

Evaluate them: the pop-up on this slide covers the three core habits: sourcing (who made this, and why), contextualizing (what world did it come from), and corroborating (does independent evidence agree). Crucially, reliability is not a property of a whole source: a document can be excellent evidence for one claim and useless for another.

Interpret the evidence: connecting what a source says to a larger claim. This requires distinguishing what a document states directly from what you can reasonably infer — and resisting the temptation to treat a vivid example as a typical one.

Construct and defend: this last move is public. Citation lets a reader find your evidence; exposition shows your reasoning; other scholars then try to knock it down. A private conviction about the past does not become history until other people can inspect how you got there.

Why This Matters

Notice that the discipline's credibility does not rest on historians being unusually honest people. It rests on the last move — the requirement that the reasoning be exposed where others can attack it. That is the same reason you are required to cite your sources: not as a formality, but so someone can check you.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Sam Wineburg – Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts

Wineburg tested how expert historians and bright high-school students read the same documents. The students went straight to what the document said. The historians looked first at who wrote it, when, and why — often before reading the body at all. His conclusion is in his title: this way of reading is unnatural. It has to be learned deliberately, which is why your professor is teaching it in week one rather than assuming it.

The Five Historical-Thinking Questions

The toolkit — what you ask the evidence.

  • Cause — why did this happen, and by what mechanism?
  • Comparison — how do two cases differ, judged on the same basis?
  • Context — what world made this action make sense?
  • Consequence — what changed, for whom, over how long?
  • Evidence — what supports the claim, and is it enough?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the toolkit — five questions historians ask over and over, inside any stage of the workflow. If the five moves are the recipe, these five questions are the knives you use throughout the cooking.

Breaking It Down

Cause: why did this happen, and by what mechanism? Good causal work separates long-running background conditions from immediate triggers, and says how one thing actually produced another instead of just listing what came before.

Comparison: how are two cases similar or different, judged on the same basis? The word "consistent" matters — comparing one case's ideals with another's practice is the most common way comparison goes wrong.

Context: what world made this action make sense to the person doing it? Context is the antidote to sneering at the past for not sharing your assumptions.

Consequence: what changed, for whom, over how long? Note the "for whom" — an event can be liberating for one group and catastrophic for another at the same moment.

Evidence: what supports this claim, and is it enough? Not just "is there evidence" but is it reliable and representative enough to carry the size of the claim being made.

Why This Matters

Copy these five down somewhere you will find them again. They are the rubric behind your exams and source analyses this semester, and in the third lecture they become the standard for judging between competing interpretations by professional historians. They are not decoration — they are the tools.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A short slide with one job: keeping the two lists from collapsing into one blurry list of ten things. They are different kinds of thing, and the sentence in the gold box is worth memorizing word for word.

Breaking It Down

Historians follow five moves to produce an account, and within that process they ask five recurring questions. That is the whole relationship in one sentence.

Moves = workflow: a project from beginning to end. You run through them once per piece of research, in rough order, with loops backward.

Questions = toolkit: used repeatedly inside that workflow, at any stage, on assignments far smaller than a book. You will use these on a single document in a fifty-minute class.

Why "evidence" is on both lists: this is not sloppiness. Evidence is a stage of the work — you go find it and evaluate it — and it is also the standing test applied to every claim the work eventually produces. It does double duty because the discipline makes it do double duty.

Why This Matters

If you take one organizational thing from this lecture, take this. Students who merge the two lists end up with ten vaguely remembered words and no idea what to do with them. Students who keep them separate have a procedure for doing research and a set of questions for testing any claim — including claims made by textbooks, documentaries, and politicians.

Run King Street Through It

  • Ask: did Preston order his men to fire?
  • Find: depositions, trial testimony, printed narratives, engravings
  • Evaluate: who wrote it, when, for whom, facing what stakes?
  • Interpret: what does the pattern of accounts support?
  • Defend: show the sources; invite the objection
Preston wrote his deposition while facing a charge of murder. That does not make it false. It tells you what to check.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now the equipment gets used on the actual case. Watch what happens: the abstractions from the last three slides turn into concrete moves on a real problem — and the list you wrote at the beginning of class starts paying off.

Breaking It Down

Ask: did Preston order his men to fire? This is a genuine historical question. It cannot be looked up, honest people have disagreed about it for two and a half centuries, and the answer changes what the whole night means.

Find: Preston's deposition, the Boston committee's pamphlet, dozens of other depositions taken that spring, the trial testimony from that autumn, printed engravings, and newspaper coverage on both sides of the Atlantic. The evidence base is much larger than the two documents you saw.

Evaluate: who wrote it, when, for whom, and facing what stakes. If you wrote any of those on your paper earlier, that was not luck — those are the questions the discipline actually uses.

The deposition point: Preston wrote his sworn statement while facing a charge of murder. Read that carefully, because the conclusion most students jump to is the wrong one. It does not mean he lied. People tell the truth under pressure constantly. What it tells you is precisely what to check: does anybody with nothing to gain say the same thing? That check has a name — corroboration.

Interpret and defend: build the best-supported account, then publish it where people who disagree with you can take it apart.

Why This Matters

There is a deeper idea hiding in this slide. Evidence is a relationship, not a thing. Preston's deposition is a source. It becomes evidence only when a historian uses it to support a specific inference — and the same document can be evidence for wildly different things: what happened on King Street, how British officers understood crowds, how legal depositions were written, or how an officer expected to be believed. The document does not change. The question does.

Section III

What Kind of Source Is It?

The answer changes depending on what you are trying to find out.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The final section of the lecture, and the most immediately practical one. You are about to get the vocabulary you will use on nearly every assignment in this course.

Breaking It Down

The section title is a question, and its phrasing is a hint: "what kind of source is it?" turns out to have no permanent answer. It depends on what you are trying to find out.

Why This Matters

Most courses hand you "primary and secondary sources" as a pair of boxes to sort things into and move on. This lecture is going to hand you the boxes and then, on slide 19, show you an object that changes boxes depending on the question. That flip is the intellectual high point of the day.

Two Kinds of Source

Primary

  • Made inside the period studied
  • A trace left by the process itself
  • Preston's deposition, 1770

Secondary

  • Made later, about that period
  • Interprets primary evidence
  • Your textbook chapter
Classifying a source is the entry point. Interrogating it is the actual work.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The basic distinction, stated plainly. Learn it — it is genuinely indispensable. Then notice the line at the bottom, which is the part most students skip and the part your professor actually cares about.

Breaking It Down

Primary source: created during the period or process being studied. A letter, a deposition, a ledger, a newspaper, a tool, a photograph. It is a trace left by the process itself. Example on the slide: Preston's 1770 deposition. Read more: primary source.

Secondary source: created later, interpreting primary evidence and earlier scholarship. A monograph, a journal article, a documentary — or the textbook chapter you were assigned this week. Read more: secondary source.

"Classifying is the entry point; interrogating is the actual work": sorting a document into the right box earns you nothing by itself. It is the first thirty seconds of the job. What follows — who made this, why, for whom, what can it actually establish — is the job.

Why This Matters

Secondary does not mean "just somebody's opinion." A good secondary work formulates a problem, organizes a body of evidence, tests claims it inherited, and shows its reasoning so you can check it. That is why your textbook has citations. And a secondary source becomes a primary source the moment your question shifts to the era in which it was written — hold that thought for three slides.

Primary Does Not Mean Unbiased

A primary source gives you proximity, not transparency.

Witnesses misjudge distance, mishear orders, and remember what confirms what they already believed.

Both of tonight's accounts are primary. Both are also arguments, written by people who wanted to be believed.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide exists because of a specific, predictable mistake: students hear "primary source" and think "direct, unfiltered, therefore more trustworthy." It is the most common error of the first week, and correcting it early saves you from a semester of bad analysis.

Breaking It Down

Proximity, not transparency: a primary source puts you close to the event. It does not give you a clean window onto it. Being present is not the same as being neutral — in fact, the people closest to an event usually have the most at stake in how it is remembered.

What witnesses actually do: misjudge distance and time, mishear words in chaos, forget, exaggerate, conceal, and reshape memory toward what they already believed. None of this requires dishonesty. It is how human perception works, especially at night, in a crowd, with gunfire.

Institutional versions of the same problem: census records flatten complicated identities into simple categories; court testimony is produced under legal pressure in answer to somebody else's questions; newspapers pursue political and commercial goals.

So why value primary sources at all? Because they were embedded in the process being studied. That embeddedness is the source of their value — not any escape from perspective.

Why This Matters

Bring this back to King Street. Both accounts you read are primary. Both were written within days by people who were there. And both are arguments, composed by men who wanted very much to be believed by specific audiences. Recognizing that does not make the documents useless. It tells you what kind of use is warranted — which is the whole skill.

What Is This Evidence For?

Revere, 1770 — note the orderly firing line and the sign reading “Butcher's Hall.”
  • Printed within weeks of the shooting
  • Copied from Henry Pelham's design
  • Sold to shape opinion, not to record
Weak evidence for what happened. Excellent evidence for what Bostonians were being asked to believe.

📖 Understanding This Slide

You saw this image behind the title slide and probably did not look hard at it. That was on purpose. This is Paul Revere's 1770 engraving, almost certainly the picture in your head when you hear "Boston Massacre" — and it is a piece of political persuasion, not a record.

Breaking It Down

Printed within weeks: Revere got his version to market in late March 1770, roughly three weeks after the shooting, while depositions were still being collected. Read more: Paul Revere.

Copied from Henry Pelham: the composition was not Revere's invention. He took it from a drawing by another Boston engraver, Henry Pelham, and published first.

What the picture asserts: soldiers in a disciplined firing line with an officer behind them — that is a commanded volley, not a panic. A crowd that is well dressed and entirely unarmed. No snowballs. No clubs. No ropewalk brawl. And a sign reading "Butcher's Hall" over the building, which was actually the Custom House.

The title is an argument: the word "massacre" is not a neutral description of eleven people shot during a street confrontation. It is a claim about what kind of event this was — and it is the claim that won so thoroughly that it is now the entry in your textbook's index.

Why This Matters

So is this evidence? Yes — but you have to finish the sentence. It is terrible evidence for what happened on King Street, because almost every detail contradicts what both written accounts agree on. It is outstanding evidence for something else: what Bostonians were being asked to believe, three weeks later, by people pursuing a political outcome. The image did not become useless. The question changed.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eric Hinderaker – Boston's Massacre (2017)

Hinderaker treats the fight over the event's meaning — the prints, the pamphlets, the annual commemorative orations — as the real historical story. On his reading, Revere's engraving is not a failed attempt at accuracy. It is a successful act of political construction, and it worked.

Hiller B. Zobel – The Boston Massacre (1970)

Zobel rebuilt the night from the trial record rather than the propaganda, and produced a considerably messier picture: a genuinely threatening crowd, frightened soldiers, and confused shouting. His account is a useful check on the image, though other historians argue he leans too far toward the soldiers' side.

The Same Night, Drawn in 1856

Bufford after Champney, 1856 — the first known print to place Crispus Attucks at the center.
  • Made 86 years after the event
  • No artist present; no new testimony
  • Published as the slavery crisis deepened
Secondary for 1770. Primary for how the 1850s wanted to remember 1770.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the intellectual high point of the lecture. Same event, different picture, made eighty-six years later — and the reason it looks different has nothing to do with new evidence. Everything about this slide turns on one idea: change the question and a source changes category.

Breaking It Down

Bufford after Champney, 1856: a color lithograph published in Boston by J. H. Bufford, based on a drawing by William Champney. Nobody involved was alive in 1770. No witness had come forward. Nothing had been discovered between 1770 and 1856 that told anyone more about King Street.

Crispus Attucks at the center: Attucks, a man of African and Wampanoag descent who worked as a sailor and whaler, was killed that night. This is, as far as anyone has found, the first print to place him at the center of the scene. Revere's engraving does not depict him as a Black man at all. Read more: Crispus Attucks.

What changed was the country: this print appears in Boston in the 1850s, as the conflict over slavery moves toward war, and while abolitionists are arguing that Black Americans have been present at the nation's founding from its very first night.

So which is it? Secondary for 1770 — it is a later interpretation, based on no new evidence. Primary for the 1850s — it is a genuine artifact of how that decade wanted to remember the Revolution, made inside the process being studied.

Why This Matters

Primary and secondary are not permanent labels glued to objects. They describe a relationship between a source and a question. Your professor's other example: a documentary from 1990 is a secondary source about the civil rights movement, and a first-rate primary source about how Americans in 1990 chose to remember it. Once you can perform this flip on your own, you are doing real historical thinking.

⏸ Student Activity

1. Primary or secondary?   a diary  ·  a textbook  ·  a photograph  ·  a documentary

2. Now name a question that would make the documentary a primary source.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Your second activity, in two parts. Part one is easy and you will get it in seconds. Part two is where the actual learning happens.

Breaking It Down

Part 1 — sort them: a diary, a textbook, a photograph, a documentary. Most students land on primary, secondary, primary, secondary. That is a defensible first pass.

Part 2 — break your own answer: name a question that would make the documentary a primary source. Hints: when was it made, who made it, and what did they assume their audience already believed? A 1985 documentary is primary evidence for what filmmakers in 1985 thought worth showing.

A wrinkle worth noticing: "diary" is not automatically simple. A diary kept the same evening is one kind of source. A memoir written forty years later from memory is a very different one, even though both are authored by a participant.

Why This Matters

The point of part two is that you cannot classify a source without knowing the question. Any student can memorize two definitions. What separates historical thinking from vocabulary recall is being able to say "that depends — what are you trying to find out?" and mean it.

Back to King Street

  • Established beyond serious doubt: the date, the place, the dead
  • Contested and testable: who provoked, and the order to fire
  • A jury heard the evidence and acquitted Preston in 1770
Facts are the starting point. Explanation is the goal.
Compare this to your list of three. How much of it did you already know to ask?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture closes where it opened, but you are not the same reader you were thirty minutes ago. The two accounts have not changed. What has changed is that you now have a procedure for handling them — and a way to say what is settled and what is not.

Breaking It Down

Established beyond serious doubt: the date, the place, the names of the dead, the presence of a crowd, the ropewalk fight days earlier. Notice why these are solid — two hostile accounts, written for opposed purposes, agree on them. That is corroboration doing its work.

Contested and testable: who provoked whom, and whether Preston gave the order. "Contested" is not the same as "unknowable." It means the claim has to be argued from evidence and survive the strongest objection anyone can raise.

And it was argued: in the autumn of 1770 a Boston jury heard the witnesses and acquitted Captain Preston — not out of sympathy, but because the testimony on the single decisive point contradicted itself. Six soldiers were also acquitted; two were convicted of manslaughter.

Your list of three: compare it to what the lecture supplied. Most students find they had four or five of the professional moves already, without the names.

Why This Matters

Facts are the starting point. Explanation is the goal. That is the takeaway of the entire lecture and the answer to the question you opened with. Accounts of the same event disagree not because facts are unavailable, but because explaining what facts mean requires argument — and arguments can be better or worse, tested against evidence rather than against your preferences.

Before You Go

In one sentence: what is the difference between the past and history?
Next: if historians build evidence-based accounts, what kinds of causes are they allowed to put in them?

📖 Understanding This Slide

One exit question and a bridge to the next lecture. The exit question is short on purpose — if you can state the distinction in one sentence, you have the foundation the rest of the unit is built on.

Breaking It Down

The exit response: what is the difference between the past and history? A workable answer: the past is everything that happened and is gone; history is an evidence-based account of a selected part of it, built to answer a question.

The bridge: if historians build evidence-based accounts, what kinds of causes are they allowed to put in them? That is the next lecture's essential question, and it is a live disagreement, not a topic.

Come with an opinion: do people make history, or do forces bigger than people make history? Both answers have serious defenders. You will be asked to sort real causes of the American Revolution into competing frameworks.

Why This Matters

This lecture established that history is a disciplined argument rather than either a fixed list of facts or a pile of opinions. The next one asks what belongs inside those arguments — individuals, structures, ideas, accidents — and the third asks how to judge between arguments when honest historians reach different conclusions. The three fit together as one unit.

Independent Study

For the online hour

📖 Understanding This Slide

Everything from here is the Independent Study portion — the asynchronous online hour. These slides were not delivered in class. Work through them on your own; they deepen and complicate what the lecture established rather than repeating it.

Breaking It Down

Five extensions: how the Boston case was actually decided in court, why archives are not neutral, how historians read records against their intended purpose, whether objectivity is possible, and the difference between having a perspective and distorting the evidence.

Why This Matters

The classroom hour gave you a working model. These slides show you where the model gets complicated — which is exactly the movement your professor wants: from "I can see the pattern" to "I can explain where the pattern holds and where it breaks down."

How the Case Was Actually Decided

  • John Adams — a Patriot — defended the soldiers
  • Preston was tried separately and acquitted, October 1770
  • Six soldiers acquitted; two convicted of manslaughter
  • Witnesses contradicted each other on the single decisive point
A court and a historian face the same problem: an unrepeatable event, reachable only through people who wanted something from the telling.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture told you Preston was acquitted. This slide tells you how that happened, and the details are more interesting than the verdict — because a courtroom and a history seminar are trying to solve the same structural problem.

Breaking It Down

John Adams defended the soldiers: Adams was a committed Patriot who would later help draft the Declaration of Independence and serve as the second president. He took the soldiers' defense because he believed everyone was entitled to counsel, and it cost him popularity in Boston. Read more: John Adams.

Preston tried separately, acquitted October 1770: his defense turned entirely on whether he gave the order to fire — the same question that divides the two documents you read in class.

The soldiers' trial: six were acquitted; two were convicted of manslaughter rather than murder.

Why the contradictions mattered: witnesses swore incompatible things about the decisive moment. Some said they heard Preston give the order; others said the word "fire" came from the crowd; others could not tell who spoke. In a dark, shouting street, that is exactly what you would expect.

Why This Matters

A court and a historian face the same problem: an unrepeatable event, reachable only through people who wanted something from the telling. Both develop procedures for deciding what testimony can carry — rules of evidence in one case, source criticism in the other. Neither achieves certainty. Both produce judgments better than a coin flip, and both are willing to say when the evidence will not support a conclusion.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Hiller B. Zobel – The Boston Massacre (1970)

Zobel built his account from the trial transcripts rather than the pamphlets and prints, which is why his version reads so differently from the famous one. It is a good demonstration of a lesson from the lecture: the sources a historian chooses to privilege shape the story that results.

The Archive Has a History Too

Silence

  • Never recorded
  • Destroyed or discarded
  • Named only by an enemy

Abundance

  • Courts, armies, churches, firms
  • Procedure recorded in detail
  • Motive rarely recorded at all
Both directions mislead. Ask not only what is missing, but why so much of one particular kind survives.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Historians work in archives, and it is tempting to picture an archive as a neutral warehouse where the past is stored. It is nothing of the kind. Every collection is the product of decisions — about what to write down, what to keep, how to file it, and what to throw away.

Breaking It Down

Silence: some things were never recorded at all. Some records were deliberately destroyed or quietly discarded. Some people appear only through the descriptions of people who despised them.

Abundance: the opposite problem, and it is just as distorting. Governments, courts, armies, churches, and businesses generated enormous quantities of paper. Files preserve procedure in extraordinary detail while barely touching motive, emotion, or anything that happened outside the institution's field of vision.

Digitization adds a new layer: what has been scanned and made searchable feels more representative than it is, simply because it is easier to find.

The caution that matters: it is tempting to reduce all of this to "the powerful left the records." Power certainly shaped what was created and preserved — but powerful actors also destroyed evidence, communities without power maintained rich oral and material traditions, and institutional files often document the daily lives of ordinary people in great detail.

Why This Matters

The practical upshot: ask not only what is missing from an archive, but why so much of one particular kind survives. Both directions mislead. A historian who only notices silence will overcorrect; a historian who mistakes abundance for completeness will write the institution's own view of itself and call it history.

Reading Against the Grain

  • A merchant's ledger reveals credit, trust, and mobility
  • Church minutes reveal conflict and community governance
  • Court files preserve traces of people who wrote nothing themselves
With restraint. This widens what can be recovered; it does not abolish uncertainty. Distinguish what is probable from what is merely possible, and say where the evidence ends.

📖 Understanding This Slide

If institutions created most surviving records for their own purposes, how do historians recover people who never wrote anything? The answer is a method: read records for what they reveal accidentally, beyond what they were made to document.

Breaking It Down

Merchant ledgers: made to track prices and debts. They also reveal who was trusted with credit, who moved between towns, what ordinary households bought, and how commercial relationships actually worked.

Church minutes: made to record membership and discipline. They also expose belief, community conflict, expectations about men and women, and how a congregation governed itself.

Court files and runaway advertisements: produced by authorities, often hostile ones. They nonetheless preserve descriptions, names, skills, relationships, and acts of resistance belonging to enslaved people, laborers, and migrants who left no letters or diaries of their own.

The restraint clause: this method widens what can be recovered; it does not abolish uncertainty. A responsible historian distinguishes what is probable from what is merely possible, and states plainly where the evidence runs out.

Why This Matters

Two temptations sit on either side of this method. One is to conclude that people absent from the records are unrecoverable, which is too pessimistic. The other is to let moral urgency — a real desire to give silenced people a voice — slide into filling gaps with invention. Neither large quantities of data nor good intentions substitute for evidence.

Can Historians Be Objective?

  • Marc Bloch — history as disciplined questioning of evidence
  • R. G. Collingwood — sources answer questions; they do not volunteer meaning
  • E. H. Carr — history as interaction between the historian and the facts
  • Richard J. Evans — a defense of truthful historical knowledge
Objectivity is best understood as accountable practice — citation, corroboration, fair treatment of contrary evidence, willingness to be corrected — not as having no point of view.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The oldest argument in the discipline, and one you should be able to discuss carefully rather than settle in a sentence. Four major thinkers, real disagreements among them — and one thing none of them is willing to give up.

Breaking It Down

The two bad answers: that a historian can become a viewless recording instrument with no assumptions, and that standards vanish because every historian has a perspective. The first is impossible; the second does not follow.

What historians actually inherit: accurate citation, source criticism, chronological discipline, contextualization, corroboration, fair representation of evidence that cuts against them, and a willingness to be corrected. These are professional obligations, and they are checkable.

The reframe: objectivity is best understood as accountable practice rather than as political innocence or total detachment. The question is not whether a historian has a point of view. It is whether the reasoning has been exposed where others can test it.

Why This Matters

Notice what unites these four despite their real differences: none of them treats evidence as optional, and none of them accepts a historian's background or politics as a substitute for argument. You can point out where a scholar is standing. You still have to engage what they actually claimed and how well the evidence supports it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Marc Bloch – The Historian's Craft

For Bloch, the discipline's integrity lives in the critical questioning of evidence — the habit of asking of every document how it came to exist and what it can honestly be made to say.

E. H. Carr – What Is History?

Carr's famous emphasis is on the interaction between the historian and the facts: facts do not select themselves, and the historian who chooses them is shaped by their own era. Useful against naive fact-collecting — though his confidence in historical progress reflects his own mid-century moment.

Richard J. Evans – In Defence of History

Evans wrote as a direct answer to strong postmodern skepticism, arguing that acknowledging selection, interpretation, and the mediation of language does not collapse the difference between history and fiction. Evidence still constrains what a historian is allowed to claim.

Bias, Standpoint, Distortion

Standpoint is unavoidable. Distortion is not. Students who call everything “bias” lose the ability to tell them apart.

Present concerns can open genuinely new inquiry. They become presentism when today's categories are treated as timeless, or when moral judgment replaces explanation.

Conclusions are provisional because they remain open to revision — not because every claim is equally shaky.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The last slide gives you better vocabulary. Students tend to use "bias" as a universal solvent — a single word that dissolves any account they dislike. Four sharper words will serve you far better, in this course and outside it.

Breaking It Down

Standpoint: everyone has one. Where you stand shapes the questions that occur to you. Unavoidable, and often productive.

Limitation: what a particular approach cannot see. Every framework illuminates some things and leaves others dark — which is the subject of the next lecture.

Distortion: misrepresenting evidence, suppressing what cuts against you, ignoring chronology. This is a failure, and it is demonstrable.

Bad faith: knowingly arguing something you believe to be false. Rare in professional scholarship, common in public argument.

Presentism: present-day concerns can open genuinely new inquiry — historians ask about things earlier scholars ignored, and find real evidence. It becomes presentism when today's categories are treated as timeless, when historical actors are reduced to positions in current political fights, or when moral judgment substitutes for explanation. Read more: presentism.

Revision runs in every direction: quantitative evidence has overturned ideological narratives; attention to religious belief has challenged purely material explanations; institutional research has complicated identity-centered accounts. No school of interpretation gets automatic authority for being traditional, or for being new.

Why This Matters

Historical conclusions are provisional because they remain open to revision — not because every claim is equally shaky. That distinction is the difference between a discipline and a shrug, and it is what the next two lectures are built to demonstrate: first by comparing frameworks for explaining change, then by judging between rival interpretations of the Revolution itself.

Constructed

Built from selected materials, chosen and arranged for a purpose — the way a house is built from selected lumber and brick, not from every material that exists.

Constructed does not mean invented. A history is built from surviving traces, the way a house is built from selected materials. The builder chooses and arranges; the builder does not fabricate the materials themselves. Evidence still governs what a constructed account is allowed to say.

Inferential

Historical knowledge is not observed directly — nobody today watched Preston's men on King Street. It is built by reasoning from surviving traces: depositions, artifacts, records, images.

This is not a weakness unique to history. Much of what you know with confidence — that the sun will rise, that a doctor's diagnosis is sound — is also inference from evidence, not direct observation of the conclusion itself.

Provisional

A historical claim is held with the confidence the current evidence supports — open to revision if new evidence or a better argument appears.

Provisional does not mean weak or temporary in a dismissive sense. A well-supported claim can stand for a century. It only means the claim was never declared immune to being overturned.

Corrigible

Capable of being shown to be wrong. A corrigible claim is one that makes a real commitment — specific enough that evidence could, in principle, prove it false.

This is what separates a historical claim from a claim that simply cannot lose. If nothing could ever count as evidence against a position, that position is not disciplined historical thinking — it is closer to the “nobody can really know anything” escape hatch this slide already refused.

Trace / Provenance

A trace is anything the past left behind that survives into the present: a document, an object, a building, a landscape, a photograph, a set of numbers.

Provenance is the trace's own history — who made it, when, why, and how it reached the place you found it. Provenance is the first thing a historian establishes, because a source that cannot be located in time and purpose cannot be used with confidence.

Sourcing, Contextualizing, Corroborating

Three habits that make up the evaluation move:

Sourcing — who produced this, under what conditions, for whom?
Contextualizing — what world did it come out of?
Corroborating — what independent evidence agrees or disagrees?

Sam Wineburg's research found that these are not intuitive. Untrained readers go straight to what a document says before asking who wrote it or why — which is exactly backward.

Deposition

A sworn written statement, taken outside a courtroom, that can be used as testimony in a legal proceeding.

Depositions are among the richest sources for early American history, because ordinary people who left no letters or diaries still ended up in court. But the genre shapes the content: a deposition is produced under legal pressure, often in answer to questions someone else chose, by a person who knows the stakes.

Preston's was written in the week after the shooting, before his trial for murder.

Primary Source

A source created within the period or process being studied — a letter, a deposition, a ledger, a newspaper, a tool, a photograph.

It matters here because primary sources are what give a historical claim traction. But proximity is not neutrality: the people who made these sources were inside the events, with interests, limited sightlines, and reasons to be believed.

×
Secondary Source

A later work that interprets primary evidence and earlier scholarship — a monograph, a journal article, a textbook chapter, a documentary.

Secondary does not mean “just somebody's opinion.” A good secondary work organizes evidence, tests inherited claims, and shows its reasoning so you can check it.

And a secondary source becomes a primary source the moment your question shifts to the time when it was written.

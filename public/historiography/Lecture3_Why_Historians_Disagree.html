HIST 101 · Lecture 3

Why Do Historians
Disagree?

How honest scholars examine the same evidence
and still reach different conclusions

Introduction to Historiography First-Year College Students

Essential Question

How can honest historians examine much of the same evidence and still reach different conclusions?

This is not a problem to be solved.
It is a feature of the discipline to be understood.

Today’s Goal

Understand why disagreement is normal
— and how to judge it.

Historians may ask different questions,
but their answers remain accountable to evidence.

Opening Hook

Three Historians.
Three Revolutions.

Same event. Different stories.

The American Revolution

Charles Beard
The Revolution reflected struggles over power, property, and economic interest.
Bernard Bailyn
The Revolution grew from colonists’ sincere fear that British power threatened liberty.
Alan Taylor
The Revolution was not one shared experience. Patriots, Loyalists, enslaved people, and Native nations faced different struggles and choices.

Pause & Consider

Ask yourself:

  • Why do these historians explain the same event differently?
  • Does one of them have to be wrong?
  • Are they even answering the same question?
  • What evidence would help us judge their arguments?

Idea 1

Historians Ask
Different Questions

Explainer

The question determines
what counts as evidence.

The past does not change.
The questions we ask of it do.

Different questions pull different pieces of evidence into view.

Same Event · Three Questions

1. Why did the colonies rebel?
Focuses on taxation, political ideas, pamphlets, resistance.
2. Whose interests did the Revolution serve?
Focuses on property, wealth, trade, voting rights, economic conflict.
3. What did the Revolution mean to different peoples?
Focuses on enslaved people, Native nations, Loyalists, women, frontier settlers.

The event has not changed.
The question has changed.
Therefore the relevant evidence changes.

This is the first reason honest historians disagree.

Idea 2

Three Interpretations
of the Revolution

Material · Ideological · Continental

Interpretation 1

Charles Beard · Material Interest

Guiding question: Who held power, and whose interests did political change protect?

Political language about liberty cannot be separated from questions of property, taxation, commerce, debt, and power.

Strength → explains elite conflict and why new systems protected certain forms of property.
Limit → can underplay sincerely held beliefs and non-elite experiences.

Interpretation 2

Bernard Bailyn · Revolutionary Ideology

Guiding question: Why did colonists believe British policies threatened their freedom?

Colonists inherited a political language of liberty, corruption, power, and conspiracy. Many genuinely believed British actions formed a larger threat.

Strength → explains the language of resistance and why people accepted extraordinary risks.
Limit → can underplay material interests and people excluded from formal political discourse.

Interpretation 3

Alan Taylor · Continental Revolution

Guiding question: Was there one American Revolution, or several overlapping struggles?

The conflict involved Patriots, Loyalists, enslaved people, Native nations, frontier settlers, and competing empires. For different groups it meant independence, civil war, opportunity, or intensified danger.

Strength → shows why outcomes differed by group and why the Revolution cannot be understood only from Philadelphia or Boston.
Limit → resists a single concise explanation covering everyone.

These are not three random opinions.
They are three disciplined arguments
answering different questions.

Disagreement is not proof that history is unreliable.
It is proof that history is a conversation.

Individual Activity · 4 minutes

Compare the Three

On a piece of paper or in your notes, answer these three questions about any one of the interpretations:

  1. What question is this historian trying to answer?
  2. What kind of evidence would matter most to this argument?
  3. What does this interpretation leave out or explain less well?

Work alone. You will not turn this in. The goal is to practice the habit of judging arguments by their questions and evidence.

Idea 3

How Do We Judge
Historical Arguments?

Not all interpretations are equally strong.

Four Tests for Stronger Arguments

1
Evidentiary Support

Does the evidence actually support the claim? Finding some supporting evidence is not enough.

2
Contrary Evidence

Does the historian confront evidence that complicates the argument? Strong work does not hide inconvenient facts.

3
Explanatory Power

How much does the interpretation explain? Narrow arguments can succeed in one area and fail when applied more broadly.

4
Openness to Revision

Could another historian challenge the argument with evidence? Scholarship advances because interpretations remain testable.

Critical Distinction

Revision vs. Denial

Revision

  • New evidence appears
  • New questions are asked
  • Weaknesses are exposed
  • Better explanations emerge

This is how the field improves.

Denial

  • Starts with a required conclusion
  • Ignores or distorts evidence
  • Refuses contrary findings
  • Makes the claim unfalsifiable

This is not historical reasoning.

Individual Activity · One-Minute Write

What makes one historical interpretation better than another?

Write for 60 seconds. No right answer required. Just put your current thinking into words.

Use the four tests if they help. Or simply finish the sentence: “A stronger interpretation is one that…”

Final Takeaway

Historians disagree because they ask different questions and construct different explanations.

The strongest interpretations explain the evidence,
confront complications,
and survive informed criticism.

Course Principle

History is neither a fixed list of facts
nor a collection of arbitrary opinions.

It is a disciplined argument about the past,
constructed and tested through evidence.

Opening Hook

Three Historians.
Three Revolutions.

Charles Beard
The Revolution reflected struggles over power, property, and economic interest.
Bernard Bailyn
The Revolution grew from colonists’ sincere fear that British power threatened liberty.
Alan Taylor
The Revolution was not one shared experience. Patriots, Loyalists, enslaved people, and Native nations faced different struggles and choices.

The Problem

How can honest historians examine much of the same evidence and still reach different conclusions?

Neither is lying. Neither is careless.
So what is going on?

Concept 1

Historians Ask Different Questions

The question a historian asks determines which pieces of evidence become relevant.

The tool’s question: What problem is this historian trying to solve?

Related term: historiography

Demonstrate · Same Event, Different Questions

Why did the colonies rebel?
Taxation · political ideas · pamphlets · organized resistance
Whose interests did the Revolution serve?
Property · wealth · trade · voting rights · economic conflict
What did the Revolution mean to different peoples?
Enslaved people · Native nations · Loyalists · women · frontier settlers

The event has not changed. The question has. Therefore the relevant evidence changes.

Different questions pull different evidence into view.

This is the first reason honest historians disagree.

Concept 2

Competing Interpretations

When historians ask different questions, they construct different explanations of the same past.

The tool’s question: What does this interpretation explain especially well — and what does it leave out?

Interpretation 1

Charles Beard · Material Interest

Guiding question: Who held power, and whose interests did political change protect?

Political language about liberty cannot be separated from property, taxation, commerce, debt, and power.

Explains well: elite conflict and why new systems protected certain forms of property.
Explains less well: sincerely held beliefs and non-elite experiences.

Interpretation 2

Bernard Bailyn · Revolutionary Ideology

Guiding question: Why did colonists believe British policies threatened their freedom?

Colonists inherited a political language of liberty, corruption, power, and conspiracy. Many genuinely believed British actions formed a larger threat.

Explains well: the language of resistance and why people accepted extraordinary risks.
Explains less well: material interests and people excluded from formal political discourse.

Interpretation 3

Alan Taylor · Continental Revolution

Guiding question: Was there one American Revolution, or several overlapping struggles?

The conflict involved Patriots, Loyalists, enslaved people, Native nations, frontier settlers, and competing empires. For different groups it meant independence, civil war, opportunity, or intensified danger.

Explains well: why outcomes differed by group and why the Revolution cannot be understood only from Philadelphia or Boston.
Explains less well: a single concise cause covering everyone.

⏸ Judge & Defend · 3 minutes · Individual

Which interpretation explains its evidence most successfully?

Choose one of the three historians. On paper, answer:

  1. What question is this historian trying to answer?
  2. What kind of evidence would matter most?
  3. What does this interpretation leave unexplained?

You will not turn this in. The goal is to practice judging an argument by its question and its evidence.

Concept 3

How Do We Judge
Historical Arguments?

Interpretations can differ without being equally valid. There are standards.

The tool’s question: Does this argument survive the four tests?

Four Tests for Stronger Arguments

1
Evidentiary Support

Does the evidence actually support the claim? Some supporting evidence is not enough.

2
Contrary Evidence

Does the historian confront evidence that complicates the argument? Strong work does not hide inconvenient facts.

3
Explanatory Power

How much does the interpretation explain? Narrow arguments can succeed in one area and fail more broadly.

4
Openness to Revision

Could another historian challenge the argument with evidence? Scholarship advances because interpretations remain testable.

Critical Distinction

Revision versus Denial

Revision

  • New evidence appears
  • New questions are asked
  • Weaknesses are exposed
  • Better explanations emerge

This is how the field improves.

Denial

  • Starts with a required conclusion
  • Ignores or distorts evidence
  • Refuses contrary findings
  • Makes the claim unfalsifiable

This is not historical reasoning.

⏸ One-Sentence Write · 60 seconds · Individual

What makes one historical interpretation better than another?

Write one clear sentence. Use the four tests if they help.

Starter: “A stronger interpretation is one that…”

This is the consolidation step. You are applying the standard, not just watching it.

Final Takeaway

Historians disagree because they ask different questions and construct different explanations.

The strongest interpretations explain the evidence,
confront complications,
and survive informed criticism.

Course Principle

History is neither a fixed list of facts
nor a collection of arbitrary opinions.

It is a disciplined argument about the past,
constructed and tested through evidence.

Independent Study · Historiographical Texture

Beard in Context

Charles Beard’s An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution (1913) was part of the Progressive-era challenge to “heroic” founding narratives. He argued that the Constitution reflected the economic interests of its framers.

Later scholars complicated or rejected pure economic determinism, but the question he forced — whose interests does political change serve? — remains productive.

Independent Study · Historiographical Texture

Bailyn and the Ideological Turn

Bernard Bailyn’s The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution (1967) recovered the radical Whig and classical-republican language that shaped colonial resistance. Ideas, he insisted, were not mere cover for material interest.

The “ideological” school shifted attention to pamphlets, sermons, and the political culture of conspiracy and liberty. It remains one of the most influential reinterpretations of the period.

Independent Study · Historiographical Texture

Taylor and the Continental Frame

Alan Taylor’s work (including American Revolutions, 2016) places the Revolution inside a larger continental and imperial contest. Native nations, the West, slavery, and competing empires are not side stories; they redefine what “the Revolution” was.

This approach makes a single, unified cause harder to sustain — and that is the point.

Vocabulary · Study Sheet

Terms from This Lecture

Historiography — the study of how historians construct and evaluate accounts of the past
Interpretation — an evidence-based explanation of part of the past
Evidentiary support — whether the evidence actually warrants the claim
Contrary evidence — material that complicates or challenges an argument
Explanatory power — how much of the relevant past an interpretation accounts for
Revision — improving explanations through new evidence, questions, or methods
Denial — protecting a required conclusion by ignoring or distorting evidence
Material interest (IS) — economic and property concerns as drivers of political action
Ideological origins (IS) — the political language and ideas that shaped colonial resistance
Continental frame (IS) — viewing the Revolution as overlapping struggles across peoples and empires

(IS) = introduced in Independent Study only

Historiography

The study of how historians construct, explain, and evaluate accounts of the past. Not the past itself, but the arguments we make about it.

Revision

Historical revision occurs when new evidence, new questions, or better methods produce explanations that account for the evidence more effectively. It is how the discipline improves.

Denial

Denial begins with a required conclusion and protects it by ignoring evidence, distorting sources, or making the claim impossible to disprove. It is not historical reasoning.

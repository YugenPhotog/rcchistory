The real course

How did China become China?

Delivery: Put this on the board and leave it there. Every unit answers some piece of it.

The questions we keep coming back to

  • Why did China repeatedly divide and reunify?
  • What made a ruler legitimate?
  • How did Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism shape society?
  • What happened when non-Chinese peoples ruled China?
  • Why did the imperial system last so long?
  • Why did it finally collapse?
  • How did revolution produce modern China?
We are not memorizing dynasties. Dynasties give us the chronology. The course is about explaining historical change.

Delivery: Read these fast — they are a preview, not a lesson. The callout at the bottom is the line that matters. Say it twice if you have to.

The quiz system

Two kinds of questions

Anchor Questions

An anchor points you toward an important sentence or passage in the textbook. It helps you recognize where the evidence lives.

Review Questions

The review question asks you to explain the larger historical meaning of that evidence.

Find the evidence. Then say what it means.

Delivery: The anchor/lightbulb pairing is the mental model students should walk out with. Anchor = location. Review = meaning.

The “Three Teachings”

Confucianism — a tradition centered on ethics, family obligation, ritual propriety, and the moral cultivation of the person who governs.

Daoism — a tradition emphasizing the natural order (the Dao), spontaneity, and skepticism toward heavy-handed rule.

Buddhism — arrived from India along the trade routes and became one of the most powerful institutional and intellectual forces in Chinese history.

You do not need to know these yet. You will meet all three in depth.

×
The Imperial System

The form of centralized rule under an emperor — with a bureaucracy, a law code, a tax system, and an official ideology — that governed China in some version from 221 BCE until 1912 CE.

That is over two thousand years. Explaining that durability is one of the central problems of this course.

×
Primary Source

A document, object, or image produced by people who lived through the events in question — an edict, a poem, a memorial to the throne, a painting, a bronze vessel, a photograph.

A secondary source, by contrast, is a later historian writing about those events. Your textbook is a secondary source.

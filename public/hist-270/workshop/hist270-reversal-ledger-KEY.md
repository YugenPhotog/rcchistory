# The Reversal Ledger — Instructor Key and Grading Specification

**Course:** HIST 270, History of China — Richland Community College
**Source:** "The Examination System," in *Chinese Civilization: A Sourcebook*, trans. Clara Yu
**Assignment:** Four-anecdote reversal ledger plus three synthesis questions
**Audience for this file:** an AI grading assistant, not the student

---

## 1. Role and operating instructions

You are grading a first-year undergraduate assignment on a Tang dynasty primary source. Work from this key alone. Do not consult outside knowledge of Tang history to judge a student's answer — the assignment tests whether a student read *this selection*, and an answer that is historically true but not present in the text earns no credit.

Hold these rules throughout:

1. **Grade evidence, not eloquence.** A blunt, correct cell outranks a polished, vague one.
2. **Accept paraphrase freely.** Students will not use the key's wording. Match on substance.
3. **Never invent a deduction the rubric does not list.** If a cell is defensible from the text, credit it even if it is not the target answer.
4. **Flag, do not accuse.** Where integrity concerns arise (§6), report observations to the instructor with the evidence attached. Never state a conclusion about academic dishonesty to the student or in student-facing feedback.

**What the students were asked to do.** They read all eight anecdotes in the selection. They chart **four** of them — the 1st, 2nd, 6th, and 8th — chosen because each contains an unambiguous status reversal and because together they run from the opening of the selection to its final paragraph. The three synthesis questions ask about the whole reading, not only the charted four, so an answer there may legitimately cite anecdotes 3, 4, 5, or 7 and should be credited when it does.

**The two traps worth knowing before you start.** In the 2nd anecdote the humiliated party is the prefect, not the losing rival. In the 8th the reversal is caused by chance announced in advance, not by anyone's effort. Students fail these two cells more than any others, and the student-facing instructions warned them about both in general terms.

## 3. The ledger key

Each anecdote is worth **4 points** — one per column — for **16 points total**.

Per cell: **1 point** for a substantively correct answer, **0.5** for a partially correct or imprecise one, **0** for incorrect, absent, or untraceable to the text.

---

### Anecdote 1 of 8 — Xiao Yingshi and the old man in the purple robe

*Opens the selection, immediately after the editor's introduction.*

| Column | Target | Also accept | Do not credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starts high | Xiao Yingshi — passed the examination in 735, famous for literary talent, "unequaled in conceit and arrogance" | "The jinshi"; "the poet drinking at the inn" | Naming the old man here without explanation |
| Starts low | The old man in the purple robe, arriving on foot with a page boy to shelter from the storm; treated insolently because of their informality | "The stranger at the inn"; "the man Xiao insulted" | "The page boy" alone |
| What causes the flip | The storm ends and the retinue arrives, revealing the old man as the minister of personnel — the very official Xiao had repeatedly tried and failed to see. Nothing changes but Xiao's knowledge of whom he insulted | Any answer centered on the revelation of the minister's identity | "Xiao was punished for being rude" (states the outcome, not the trigger) — **half credit** |
| Who is humiliated | Xiao. Scolded in the minister's hallway, told he deserves "family discipline," told he is fit to remain a mere jinshi. Permanent: he dies a chief clerk in Yang prefecture | Credit either the scolding or the ruined career; full credit for both | The minister; "nobody" |

**Grader's note.** The purple robe is a rank marker. A student who observes that status was visible on the man's body and Xiao still missed it has read well — give that a positive comment, not extra points.

### Anecdote 2 of 8 — Lu Zhao and Huang Po

*Follows the first set of asterisks. Ends with the dragon-boat poem.*

| Column | Target | Also accept | Do not credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starts high | Huang Po — wealthy, the only one of the two invited to the prefect's farewell dinner at the Pavilion of Departure | "The rich friend from the same prefecture" | — |
| Starts low | Lu Zhao — very poor, uninvited, riding past the banquet on an old, weak horse | "The poor candidate who waited outside the city" | — |
| What causes the flip | Lu Zhao sits the examination and returns the next year as "number one" | — | "He was smarter than Huang Po" — not in the text |
| Who is humiliated | **The prefect of Yuanzhou**, not Huang Po. Officials from the regional commander down turn out to welcome Lu Zhao and the prefect is "greatly embarrassed"; Lu Zhao then presses it in the dragon-boat poem — he had said it was a dragon and was not believed | Full credit for naming the prefect with either the welcome or the poem | Huang Po alone. **Half credit** if the student names Huang Po but correctly describes the prefect's embarrassment |

**Grader's note.** This is the most-missed cell in the assignment. The rival is never punished; the *judge who misjudged* is. Comment on it in every submission that gets it wrong — the distinction is the whole point of the column.

---

### Anecdote 6 of 8 — Xu Tang and Wang Zun

*The first clean reversal after the three middle anecdotes. Contains the "lowly clerk" insult.*

| Column | Target | Also accept | Do not credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starts high | Xu Tang — a candidate of long standing, more than twenty attempts, contemptuous of a "lowly clerk" | "The man who had taken the exam twenty times" | — |
| Starts low | Wang Zun — a minor government functionary from the same village who wrote good poetry but kept it secret | "The low clerk"; "the functionary nobody knew wrote poetry" | — |
| What causes the flip | Wang Zun resigns his post and sets out for the capital to sit the examination — and passes | "He quit his job and took the exam" | "He was talented all along" (true, but it is the precondition, not the trigger) — **half credit** |
| Who is humiliated | Xu Tang, and the punishment is dated. Wang Zun passes and becomes very famous; Xu Tang does not pass for another five years. The text records the gap deliberately | Full credit for naming Xu Tang with either the reversal of fame or the five-year delay | Wang Zun (he is insulted, but he is not the one humiliated by the outcome) — **half credit** if the student explains both directions |

**Grader's note.** Note for students who get the flip column right here: Wang Zun's secrecy about his poetry is the detail that makes the story work. He had the talent the whole time; what he lacked was the willingness to stake anything on it. Worth a comment, not points.

---

### Anecdote 8 of 8 — Zhang Shu and Cui Zhaowei

*The final anecdote. Begins with a visit to a fortune-teller.*

| Column | Target | Also accept | Do not credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starts high | Zhang Shu — reputed for literary talent, generally known as the "number-one-to-be"; even Cui was regarded as inferior to him | "The favorite"; "the one everyone expected to win" | — |
| Starts low | Cui Zhaowei — the other candidate sent up from Xichuan, thought the lesser of the two | "Cui, who was regarded as inferior" | A cell left blank because the text says little — **half credit** if the student says the text gives Cui almost no characterization, which is accurate and shows close reading |
| What causes the flip | **Chance, and it is announced in advance.** The fortune-teller ignores Zhang and tells Cui he will come out on top. A death in Zhang's family forces him to withdraw from that year's examination; Cui becomes "number one," and chief minister seven years later | Either the family death or the prediction; full credit for both. Also accept any answer identifying that nothing about either man's ability changed | "Cui worked harder" or "Cui was better than people thought" — **zero**, not in the text |
| Who is humiliated | Zhang, repeatedly: passed over by the fortune-teller, forced out by the family death, pressed at the drinking party to accept the top rank as Cui's future gift, and finally obliged to pay homage to Cui exactly as predicted | Any two of the four | Cui; "nobody" |

**Grader's note.** This anecdote is the collection's consolation story and it matters for question 3. Zhang loses to fate, not to inferiority. A student who notices that the fortune-teller's prediction comes true in every particular — and that this makes the outcome a matter of destiny rather than merit — is already most of the way to the payoff question.

## 4. The synthesis questions

These ask about the whole selection. Credit evidence drawn from the four uncharted anecdotes (Lu Hui, Liu Xubo, Bai Minzhong, Peng Kan and Zhan Bi) exactly as you would credit the charted four.

### Question 1 — What behavior gets punished? (6 points)

Target elements, with where they appear:

- **Arrogance about one's own talent** — Xiao Yingshi (1st), Xu Tang (6th), Zhang Shu (8th).
- **Contempt for social inferiors** — Xiao toward the man in the purple robe, Xu Tang toward the clerk, Peng Kan toward his own brother-in-law (7th, uncharted).
- **Judging by wealth and station rather than ability** — the prefect of Yuanzhou (2nd).
- **Shortcuts, and the trading of friends for rank** — both are offered and both refused, by Lu Hui (3rd) and Bai Minzhong (5th), and the refusal is what gets rewarded.

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 6 | Names at least two of the above and supports each with a specific anecdote |
| 4–5 | Names one or two clearly, with at least one specific anecdote |
| 2–3 | Gestures at the right territory ("being mean," "pride") with no anecdote attached |
| 0–1 | Retells a plot, or is blank |

### Question 2 — What behavior gets rewarded? (6 points)

Target elements:

- **Persistence over decades** — Lu Hui waits twenty years (3rd), Zhan Bi several (7th), Wang Zun succeeds only after resigning (6th). Nobody in this collection succeeds quickly.
- **Loyalty, including to the dead** — Lu Hui keeps an agreement with an uncle who can no longer hold him to it (3rd); Bai Minzhong keeps a friend at the cost of the top rank (5th).
- **Talent that does not advertise itself** — Wang Zun keeps his poetry secret (6th); Lu Zhao rides past the banquet without complaint and waits ten li outside the city for the man who was invited (2nd).
- **Shame converted into work** — Zhan Bi, after his wife's scolding (7th).

Same scale as Question 1.

### Question 3 — Who benefits? (10 points)

This is the question the exercise exists for. The target: these stories were collected and circulated by and among men who held the degree or wanted it. A credential is only worth having if it measures something real. If the examinations largely ratified families that were already powerful — which the editor's introduction concedes — then the jinshi title is inherited status with extra steps. Stories in which the poor man takes first place, the clerk outpaces the twenty-year candidate, and the arrogant jinshi dies a minor official are an *argument that the credential means something*. They are what a nervous new elite tells itself.

Three functions a strong answer may identify, in any combination:

- **Legitimation** — merit visibly defeats wealth and connection, over and over.
- **Policing** — the humiliations are instructions aimed at degree-holders. Do not be Xiao Yingshi. Do not be Peng Kan.
- **Consolation** — Zhang Shu loses to a death in the family and a fortune-teller's prediction, that is, to fate rather than to inferiority. For a readership that mostly failed, this is a usable explanation.

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 9–10 | Identifies the gap between the introduction's concession and the stories' content, and names a beneficiary with a reason. Any of the three functions above, argued |
| 7–8 | Names the beneficiary correctly (degree-holders, the scholar-official class, candidates) but thinly argued, or argued without reference to the gap |
| 5–6 | Recognizes the stories are doing persuasive work but cannot say for whom |
| 3–4 | Treats the anecdotes as straightforward evidence that the system was fair |
| 0–2 | Does not engage the question, or is blank |

**Do not require the vocabulary.** A student who writes "people who passed wanted to feel like they earned it" has the argument. Credit it at the 9–10 level if it is tied to the text.

**Total: 16 (ledger) + 22 (questions) = 38 points.** Report as a percentage.

## 5. Written feedback

Return to each student, in this order and no longer than roughly 150 words:

1. One sentence naming the strongest thing in the submission, specifically.
2. The two or three cells most worth correcting, each with the correction stated plainly and the place in the text where it could have been found.
3. One sentence on whichever of the two traps they hit — the prefect in the 2nd anecdote, or the role of chance in the 8th — with a short explanation if they missed it and credit if they caught it.
4. The score.

Address the student directly. Do not pad with encouragement that is not tied to something they actually wrote, and do not quote this key's phrasing back at them as though it were the only acceptable answer.

---

## 6. Flags for the instructor

Report these separately from the student-facing feedback, as observations with evidence attached, never as conclusions:

- **Fabricated causation in the 8th anecdote** — "Cui studied harder," "Cui's talent was finally recognized," or any account in which Cui earns the reversal. The text gives a fortune-teller's prediction and a death in the family. This is the assignment's clearest tell: it is what a reader who did not open the book would produce from the assignment's own framing, which promises a reversal and implies someone earned it.
- **Content from an uncharted anecdote placed in a charted row** — most often the Peng Kan donkey fall (7th) appearing under the 6th, since both involve a humiliated jinshi. Indicates the student worked from a summary of the selection rather than the selection.
- **Fabricated detail** generally — names, outcomes, or motives not in the text.
- **Outcome-for-cause substitution across the whole flip column** ("he passed the exam," three or four times over) despite the instructions warning against it. Usually weak reading rather than anything worse; note it as a teaching problem.
- **Uniform register across all sixteen cells** with no textual specificity, paired with synthesis answers markedly more sophisticated than the ledger beneath them.
- **Near-identical phrasing between submissions**, which the individual-work instruction forbids.

Flag only what you can point to. A strong submission is not suspicious for being strong.

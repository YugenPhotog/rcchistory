Historical Context

During the Eastern Zhou period, warfare weakened the old political order based on hereditary land and office. The shi had originally occupied the lower ranks of the aristocracy, serving as warriors, retainers, minor officials, and advisers. As states expanded, rulers increasingly needed literate administrators, diplomats, strategists, and teachers. The meaning of shi therefore began to shift toward an educated person whose standing could rest on cultivated character and useful ability.

Both excerpts below come from the Confucian tradition. They are arguments about how people and rulers should behave, not neutral descriptions of everything that happened. Nevertheless, they provide evidence about the values and political opportunities emerging in this period.

Primary Sources

Source A: Confucius, Analects 4.9

About the source: The Analects preserves sayings attributed to Confucius and material transmitted by his followers. In this passage, the translated word “scholar” represents shi.

“A scholar (shi), whose mind is set on truth, and who is ashamed of bad clothes and bad food, is not fit to be discoursed with.”
Confucius, Analects 4.9, translated by James Legge.

Source B: Mencius, Gong Sun Chou I 2A.5

About the source: Mencius was a traveling Confucian teacher and adviser during the Warring States period. Here he tells rulers how to attract talented people to their courts.

“If a ruler give honour to men of talents and virtue and employ the able, so that offices shall all be filled by individuals of distinction and mark, then all the scholars (shi) of the kingdom will be pleased, and wish to stand in his court.”
Mencius, Gong Sun Chou I 2A.5, translated by James Legge.

Cutting the Queue: The Last Qing Reforms, the First Nationalists, and the Revolution of 1911

Central interpretation

The Boxer Protocol of 1901 left the Qing bankrupt, and everything that followed grew out of that weakness. Local elites, forced to fend for themselves, built schools, newspapers and nationalist campaigns. The court, under Cixi and then the regents for the boy emperor Puyi, undertook the most sweeping reforms in its history: it abolished the examinations, built new armies, and created elected assemblies and the promise of a constitution. Each reform empowered people who wanted more than the court would give. Meanwhile students in Japan forged a new nationalism, and Sun Yatsen built a revolutionary movement abroad. When a bomb went off by accident in Wuchang in October 1911, the New Army and the provincial assemblies, both creations of the court, brought the dynasty down. The revolution ended two thousand years of monarchy with a negotiated abdication that spared the imperial family, but in several garrison cities anti-Manchu violence killed thousands of ordinary Manchu men, women and children.

The story in four acts, matching Review Questions 1, 2 and 3, plus 1911

  1. The Bankrupt Court and the Local Elites (Q1): the indemnity, local activism, the boycott, and the court's reforms from the schools to the princes' cabinet.
  2. New Ideas from Abroad (Q2): the students in Tokyo, Yan Fu, Social Darwinism, Liang Qichao, and Zou Rong.
  3. Sun Yatsen (Q3): Hawaii and Hong Kong, the secret societies, the Revolutionary Alliance, the Three People's Principles and tutelage.
  4. 1911: Wuchang, the secession of the provinces, the killings of Manchus, and the abdication.
  5. Pierian Spring: the reformers in exile, Yuan Shikai's army, and the abdication terms.

Act I: The Bankrupt Court and the Local Elites (Review Question 1)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 416, 418. Reading-guide sentence (p. 416): "Forced to look after their own interests, local elites increasingly took on modernization projects."

The indemnity

The Boxer Protocol, signed on 7 September 1901, set an indemnity of 450 million taels, to be paid over thirty-nine years at four percent interest; with interest the total came to about 982 million taels. Ebrey calls the sum "450 million silver dollars" (p. 416), but the treaty set it in Haikwan taels, the customs unit of silver, so slides c and d say taels. Payments were secured on the Maritime Customs, the salt revenue and the native customs, and together with the service on the loans taken out to pay Japan after 1895 they absorbed the customs revenue entirely. Beijing therefore assigned quotas to each province, and the provinces raised them through surcharges on land, salt, liquor, pawnshops and trade. The new taxes fell on the same people the court was about to ask to support reform.1

Local activism

Ebrey's point (slide e) is that fiscal weakness pushed initiative down to local society. Gentry and merchants founded schools, study societies, chambers of commerce (authorized in 1904), police forces, and railway and mining companies, and the number of periodicals rose roughly tenfold between 1901 and 1910. These men were what historians call the "reformist elite": examination-degree holders and merchants who wanted a stronger China and a larger say in running their own provinces. Esherick's study of Hunan and Hubei shows how the same men later dominated the provincial assemblies and, in 1911, steered the revolution in their provinces.

Their nationalism showed most clearly in the anti-American boycott of 1905 (slide f). The Gresham–Yang treaty of 1894, which accepted American exclusion of Chinese laborers, expired in 1904, and Washington refused Chinese demands for better treatment of merchants and students. Shanghai merchants called a boycott of American goods in May 1905; it spread to Guangzhou, Xiamen and the overseas communities and lasted, unevenly, into 1906. Ebrey also mentions the mistreatment of Chinese visitors at the St Louis fair of 1904, where the Qing had an official pavilion. Guanhua Wang argues that the boycott marks the first nationwide urban protest movement in modern China.2 The rights-recovery movement, which raised money to buy back railway concessions from foreign companies, came from the same circles and would matter in 1911, when the court's plan to nationalize the Sichuan and Hubei railways set off the Sichuan railway protection movement in the months before Wuchang.

The court's reforms

The court's program of "New Policies" (xinzheng), begun with an edict of January 1901 issued from exile in Xi'an, went further than the reformers of 1898 had dared (slide g). It ordered new schools in 1901, added questions on foreign government and science to the examinations, and in September 1905 abolished the examinations outright. It reorganized ministries, created a ministry of education and a ministry of police, trained "New Army" divisions, and sent thousands of students to Japan. Reynolds has called the period a "Xinzheng revolution" because so much of the later Chinese state, from schools to police to law codes, was built in these years, largely on Japanese models.

The constitutional plan (slides h, i) followed Japan's defeat of Russia in 1905, which many Chinese read as a victory of constitutional over autocratic government. A commission of five officials went abroad in late 1905 (a revolutionary's bomb at the Beijing railway station delayed its departure), and on its return recommended the Japanese model of 1889, in which the monarch granted the constitution and kept sovereignty. In 1908 the court published an "Outline of a Constitution" and a nine-year timetable; Ebrey's "full constitution to be in place by 1917" (p. 418) refers to that schedule.

Then the leaders died (slide j). The Guangxu emperor died on 14 November 1908, aged thirty-seven, and Cixi the next day. Ebrey's "thirty-three-year-old" is an error; she correctly calls the death suspicious, and a forensic study announced in 2008 reported arsenic in his remains. Cixi had chosen Puyi, the son of Guangxu's brother Zaifeng, Prince Chun; Ebrey's "three-year-old" uses the Chinese count, and he was two. Prince Chun, as regent, dismissed Yuan Shikai in January 1909, officially because of a "foot ailment."

The provincial assemblies of 1909 (slide k) were elected on a narrow franchise: men over twenty-five with a degree, official experience, or property of at least 5,000 yuan. Fewer than one percent of the population qualified, but the assemblies quickly became platforms for the reformist elite. Their petitions for an early parliament, the third claiming 25 million signatures, pushed the court to shorten its timetable. The court's answer in May 1911 was the "princes' cabinet" (slide l) under Yikuang, Prince Qing: eight Manchus (five of them imperial clansmen), one Mongol bannerman and four Han Chinese. Edward Rhoads shows that this was part of a long effort by the regency to concentrate power, especially military power, in Manchu and imperial hands, and that it convinced many constitutionalists that the court would never share power.3

Answer synthesis · Q1

The Boxer indemnity of 450 million taels, paid over thirty-nine years, swallowed the customs revenue and left the court unable to fund reform, so it pushed costs onto the provinces. Local elites filled the gap themselves, founding schools, newspapers, chambers of commerce and railway companies, and they turned nationalist in the boycott of 1905 and the rights-recovery movement. The court, too, reformed: new schools, the end of the examinations in 1905, new armies, provincial assemblies in 1909 and a promised constitution. But every reform created groups, from assemblymen to New Army officers, that wanted real power, and the court answered with a cabinet dominated by Manchu princes. The reforms were doomed because they strengthened the court's critics without satisfying them.

Act II: New Ideas from Abroad (Review Question 2)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 416–417. Reading-guide sentence: "Yan Fu argued that the Western form of government freed the energy of the individual…" The guide places this sentence on p. 417, but it is on p. 416; the deck cites p. 416.

The students in Japan

The number of Chinese students in Japan rose from a few hundred in 1901 to perhaps eight to ten thousand by 1905–1906, the largest movement of students abroad in history to that time (slide n). Most took short courses in law, education, police or military science. Japan was cheap, close and culturally familiar, and it offered a model of an Asian state that had modernized without being colonized. In Tokyo the students formed provincial associations and published magazines with titles like Hubei Students' Circle and Zhejiang Tide. Many cut their queues there. One of them, photographed in 1903 on slide n, was Zhou Shuren, who studied at the Kōbun Gakuin and then medicine at Sendai before turning to literature as Lu Xun. His story, and his "Diary of a Madman," return in Lecture 3.

Yan Fu and Social Darwinism

Yan Fu (1854–1921) studied at the Royal Naval College, Greenwich, in 1877–1879 (slides o, p). His Tianyan lun (1898), a free rendering of T. H. Huxley's Evolution and Ethics with commentary drawn from Herbert Spencer, put the vocabulary of "natural selection" (tianze) and "survival of the fittest" (shizhe shengcun) into circulation. He went on to translate Adam Smith, Mill and Montesquieu. Benjamin Schwartz's classic argument is that Yan read Western liberalism as a recipe for national "wealth and power": individual liberty mattered because it released energies the state could use.4 Ebrey's account (p. 416) follows this view.

Liang Qichao

From Japan, Liang Qichao (1873–1929) edited Qingyi bao (from 1898) and Xinmin congbao ("New Citizen," 1902–1907), the most widely read Chinese periodicals of the decade (slide q). His essays on the "new citizen" urged Chinese to think of themselves as members of a nation rather than subjects of a dynasty. His tour of North America in 1903 (he entered from Canada and spent several months in the United States) left him disillusioned with American politics and with the Chinese communities he met, and he turned toward "enlightened despotism" and the statism of Germany and Japan. Ebrey's "five months" is plausible but the deck avoids the exact figure. Hao Chang's study traces this intellectual turn.5

Zou Rong and anti-Manchu nationalism

Zou Rong (1885–1905) returned from Japan to publish The Revolutionary Army (Gemingjun) in Shanghai in 1903, with a preface by the classical scholar Zhang Binglin (slide r). Ebrey's "nineteen-year-old" is the Chinese count. The tract fused Social Darwinism with race: the Han were the "sacred" descendants of the Yellow Emperor, the Manchus an alien race to be expelled or exterminated. When the radical newspaper Subao promoted it, the Qing demanded the arrest of Zou and Zhang. Because they were in the International Settlement, they were tried in the Mixed Court; Zou was sentenced to two years and died in prison on 3 April 1905. The case made him a martyr, and his tract circulated in huge numbers. Rhoads argues that the anti-Manchu language of Zou, Zhang and the Revolutionary Alliance shaped what revolutionaries did in 1911 (Act IV).

Answer synthesis · Q2

Early twentieth-century intellectuals read Western and Japanese power as the product of whole political systems, not just technology, and many saw the world through Social Darwinism as a struggle in which weak nations perished. Japan, where most Chinese students went, proved an Asian state could modernize. Yan Fu argued that Western governments freed individual energy for national goals and that China's troubles were mostly its own fault. Liang Qichao called for "democracy" in the sense of a state drawing strength from the people, but after 1903 he preferred the strong states of Japan and Germany. Zou Rong blamed Manchu rule and called for a race war against the Manchus.

Act III: Sun Yatsen (Review Question 3)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 417–418. Reading-guide sentence (p. 417): "Like Hong Xiuquan, Kang Youwei, and Liang Qichao before him, Sun came from Guangdong province."

Sun Yatsen (1866–1925) was born in Cuiheng village, Xiangshan county (now Zhongshan), to a farming family (slide t). In 1879 he joined his elder brother Sun Mei in Hawaii and attended the Iolani School and Oahu College; he was baptized in Hong Kong in 1883–1884. He graduated from the Hong Kong College of Medicine for Chinese in 1892. With friends there, the so-called "Four Bandits," he talked of overthrowing the Qing (slide u). In November 1894 he founded the Revive China Society (Xingzhonghui) in Honolulu, and a Hong Kong branch followed in 1895. A plot to seize Guangzhou in October 1895 was discovered, and Sun fled to Japan, where, according to most biographers, he cut his queue and adopted Western dress late that year; Ebrey dates this to 1896. In October 1896 the Qing legation in London kidnapped him; his release after British press and government pressure made him internationally famous.6

The Revolutionary Alliance (Tongmenghui), founded in Tokyo on 20 August 1905, merged Sun's following with student groups such as Huang Xing's China Revival Society from Hunan (slide v). Its oath pledged to "expel the Tartar caitiffs, restore China, establish a republic, and equalize land rights." Its journal Minbao debated Liang's Xinmin congbao over reform or revolution. The Alliance sponsored a series of risings in the south, culminating in the Guangzhou rising of 27 April 1911, whose dead were later honored as the "Seventy-Two Martyrs" (slide y). Funding came from overseas Chinese in Southeast Asia and the Americas. Bergère stresses that Sun's real talent was as a fundraiser and publicist; the Alliance's organization inside China was weak, and the decisive work of recruiting New Army soldiers in Hubei was done by local groups such as the Literary Society and the Progressive Association, only loosely tied to Sun.

The Three People's Principles (slide w) and the theory of tutelage (slide x) appear in outline in the Alliance's program of 1905–1906, which laid out three stages: military government, a period of provisional constitutional rule, and full constitutional government. Sun later developed both ideas at length in the 1920s. Ebrey's definitions are accurate. The deck invites students to ask the obvious question about tutelage, who decides when the people are ready; the Nationalist government after 1928 used the doctrine to justify one-party rule.

Answer synthesis · Q3

Sun's strategies shifted over time. First he tried to use south China's secret societies as the muscle for a rising, through the Revive China Society; its plots failed. In 1905 he joined radical students in Tokyo to form the Revolutionary Alliance, which sponsored seven or eight failed risings while Sun raised money among overseas Chinese and looked for foreign backers. His framework was the Three People's Principles: nationalism against the Manchus and the foreign powers, democracy as elections and a constitution, and the people's livelihood as a mild socialism, with a period of political tutelage before full democracy. The program united very different groups, but the rising that toppled the Qing came from New Army soldiers, not from Sun's own plans.

Act IV: 1911

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 418–419.

Wuchang and the provinces

On 9 October 1911 a bomb exploded by accident in a revolutionary workshop in the Russian concession of Hankou, apparently while Sun Wu of the Progressive Association was assembling explosives (slide b). Police seized membership lists, and three men were executed the next morning. On the evening of 10 October soldiers of the Eighth Engineer Battalion in Wuchang mutinied, other units joined, and the governor-general, Ruicheng, fled. The rebels pressed the brigade commander Li Yuanhong to head the Hubei Military Government. Over the following weeks, provincial assemblies and New Army officers led one province after another to declare independence (slide aa). Esherick's argument is that in Hunan and Hubei the reformist elite quickly took control and kept social order, so that 1911 was less a popular revolution than a transfer of power to provincial elites. Sun Yatsen learned of the rising from a newspaper in Denver, Colorado.

The killings of Manchus

This section is fuller than the slide (slide bb), because the subject is not in Ebrey and is not widely taught.

Who the victims were. In 1911 most Manchus outside Beijing and Manchuria lived as hereditary members of the Eight Banners in walled garrison quarters ("Manchu cities") inside a dozen or so provincial cities, a pattern set after the conquest of the 1640s (Chapter 16, Lecture 2). They lived on dwindling state stipends, were barred from most trades, and could be identified by where they lived, by their Beijing-style speech, by their names, and in the case of women by dress, hairstyle and unbound feet.

Why it happened. Revolutionary propaganda since 1903 had cast the Manchus as an alien race. Zou Rong called for wiping out "the five million barbarian Manchus"; the Alliance's oath spoke of expelling the "Tartar caitiffs." Rhoads concludes that this rhetoric was "no mere rhetorical flourish": in several cities revolutionary soldiers acted on it. Fear also played a part, since garrisons were armed and could resist, and in some places the banner troops did fight.

Wuchang, 10–13 October. According to Rhoads, the rallying cry of the mutiny was "Slay the Manchu officials and the banner people!" and the Military Government's early proclamations spoke of "elevating the Han and exterminating the Manchus." On 12 October soldiers searched the city for Manchus, using a shibboleth: suspects had to say "six hundred sixty-six" (liubailiushiliu), which Beijing-speaking Manchus pronounced differently from local people (the account comes from the memoir of Li Lianfang, cited by Rhoads). A representative of the Military Government put the number of banner people killed in the first three days at 400 to 500; a Reuters correspondent estimated 800; the revolutionary participant Cao Yabo recorded 400 banner soldiers killed and 300 detained. The killing stopped on 13 October, after the foreign consuls in Hankou protested. Esherick writes that the violence in Wuchang "approached racial slaughter."

Xi'an, 22–25 October. The worst violence came in Xi'an, which had one of the largest garrisons, perhaps 20,000 people. Revolutionary troops rose on 22 October; the walled Manchu quarter resisted and fell on the 24th, after which there was indiscriminate killing. Many banner families killed themselves; the garrison general, Wenrui, drowned himself in a well. The British Baptist missionary J. C. Keyte, who was in the city, wrote of "not less than ten thousand" Manchus "who were either killed or took their own lives." Keyte's account is an outsider's report, written for a Western readership, and should be weighed with others: Japanese consular intelligence put the dead at about 4,000 to 5,000 out of some 6,000 in the quarter, and a later study by the Academia Sinica gave figures of 2,000 or 5,000. Rhoads notes that if the garrison numbered 20,000, Keyte's figure would mean half perished. Surviving girls were taken as servants and young women as wives.

Other cities. Rhoads lists violence on a smaller scale at Taiyuan (29 October, some twenty to twenty-five killed), at Zhenjiang (after 7 November, twenty to thirty killed, then expulsions), at Fuzhou (8–10 November, heavy fighting and suicides, followed by leniency), and at Nanjing (after its capture on 2–3 December, an "incalculable number" killed, much of it by troops under Su Liangbi). Jingzhou was besieged for about a month with heavy losses but surrendered without a massacre. Hangzhou, Guangzhou, Chengdu and Yili came through with little bloodshed, often because local gentry negotiated a surrender. Beijing and Manchuria, where perhaps two-thirds of all banner people lived, were spared. The Manchu official Duanfang, leading troops into Sichuan, was killed by his own mutinous soldiers at Zizhou on 27 November.

How historians treat it. Rhoads observes that the anti-Manchu violence "has been minimized in nearly all the retrospective accounts," both in Chinese histories that celebrate 1911 and in Western ones that stress its moderation. He concludes that in the places where it occurred, banner people "were essentially victims of genocide." Not every historian uses that word, and the death tolls are uncertain; but the killings themselves are well documented in Chinese, Japanese and Western sources. Monica Kin Ian Chang's recent doctoral research on "ethnoracial violence" in 1911 extends the picture. Pamela Crossley's Orphan Warriors follows one Hangzhou banner family through the end of the garrisons. After 1912 the republic adopted the slogan "five races in one republic" (Han, Manchu, Mongol, Hui and Tibetan), and many Manchus hid their identity for decades.7

The notes connect the killings back to Zou Rong's words on slide r and forward to the abdication terms on slide hh, which promised the Manchus equal rights only months later. A good discussion question: how could the same revolution be celebrated as bloodless and remembered by Manchus as a massacre?

The abdication

Sun Yatsen returned via Europe and was inaugurated provisional president at Nanjing on 1 January 1912 (slide cc). The court recalled Yuan Shikai, whose Beiyang troops retook and burned Hankou in early November and took Hanyang, then halted. Yuan negotiated with both sides. The revolutionaries, short of money and afraid of foreign intervention, agreed that Yuan would become president if he secured the abdication. On 12 February 1912 the Empress Dowager Longyu, Guangxu's widow, issued the edict in the name of the six-year-old Xuantong emperor, Puyi. Sun resigned, and Yuan was inaugurated as provisional president in Beijing on 10 March. In the cities, queue-cutting became the sign of the new order; Ebrey's photo caption notes that "soldiers often forced men to cut off their queues" (p. 419), and some men resisted or kept their queues for years.

Pierian Spring: Exiles, Armies, and the Boy Emperor

Reformers in exile (slide ff). Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao founded the Protect the Emperor Society (Baohuanghui) at Victoria, British Columbia, in July 1899. It built branches, schools and businesses across the Americas and Southeast Asia and competed with Sun for the same overseas Chinese money. Eve Armentrout Ma's study of the Chinatowns shows how bitter that competition became.8

Yuan Shikai's army (slide gg). As governor-general of Zhili (1901–1907) Yuan built the Beiyang Army to six divisions by 1905 and ran model reforms in police, schools and local self-government in Tianjin. Stephen MacKinnon shows how his network of officers and officials made him indispensable, which is why the court had to recall him in 1911.9

The abdication terms (slide hh). The Articles of Favorable Treatment let Puyi keep his title, an annual subsidy of four million dollars, his private property and the inner Forbidden City (with a promise to move later to the Summer Palace). Separate articles promised Manchus, Mongols, Muslims and Tibetans equality, protection of property and freedom of religion, and an eventual end to the banner stipends with provision for the banner poor. Puyi was briefly restored in July 1917 and was expelled from the palace by Feng Yuxiang in November 1924. Rhoads shows how unevenly the promises to ordinary Manchus were kept.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • The indemnity was 450 million taels, not "silver dollars" (Ebrey, p. 416). Slides c and d say taels.
  • The Guangxu emperor was 37 when he died in 1908, not 33 (p. 418). Slide j says 37.
  • Puyi was two by Western count; Ebrey's "three-year-old" is the Chinese count (p. 418). Slide j says two.
  • Zou Rong was eighteen by Western count in 1903; Ebrey's "nineteen" is the Chinese count (p. 417). Slide r says eighteen.
  • Sun's queue. Ebrey dates it to 1896 (p. 418); most biographers place it in Japan in late 1895. The slide gives no year; the notes explain.
  • Liang's American tour. Ebrey's "five months" (p. 417) is not on the slide; the length depends on whether the Canadian leg is counted.
  • Reading guide anchor. The Q2 anchor "Yan Fu argued" is on p. 416, not p. 417 as the guide says.
  • The violence of 1911. Ebrey's "neither executions nor humiliations" (p. 419) is true of the royal family only. Slide bb covers the killings of ordinary Manchus.

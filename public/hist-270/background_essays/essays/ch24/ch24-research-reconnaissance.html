Audit disposition. Chapter 24 had no FA26 background essays, so this reconnaissance covers four new essays and an audit of the four FA26 decks (Cutting the Queue, The President Who Would Be Emperor, Youth Is Like Early Spring and The Day the Students Marched), built from the approved arc plan in October 2026. The decks pass. Every textbook detail that the decks correct was checked against a named source; where no reliable source confirmed a correction, the deck keeps or softens Ebrey's wording. Section 8 lists every such point, by lecture and slide.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. Ebrey's Chapter 24, "Remaking China (1900–1927)," pp. 415–429, is the course baseline, not its ceiling. Dates, names, numbers and quotations in the decks were checked against the textbook (from an OCR of the course PDF), against standard monographs, and against primary records where they exist, above all the State Department's Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS).

The rule for every correction is the instructor's: contradict Ebrey only when a reliable source confirms the correction; otherwise keep her wording or soften it. When a deck corrects her, the visible slide teaches the corrected history, the speaker notes quote Ebrey and explain the difference, and the source is named in the notes and on the deck's Sources slide. No slide carries a visible "outside the textbook" tag.

Audience: the instructor's lecture preparation and advanced students. The essays keep plain language and define terms, but cite real scholarship in footnotes and end with annotated reading lists.

2. Textbook baseline

Ebrey's chapter supplies the spine: the Boxer indemnity and the New Policies (pp. 416–417); Yan Fu, Liang Qichao, Zou Rong and the Tokyo students (pp. 416–417); Sun Yatsen and the 1911 revolution (pp. 417–419); Yuan Shikai, the warlords and the wartime economy (pp. 419–421); the New Culture Movement, Lu Xun and the women's movement (pp. 420–427); May Fourth (p. 425); and reunification under the Nationalists (pp. 428–429), with Map 24.1 (p. 416), the Biography of Sophia Chen and H. C. Zen (p. 424), and the Documents box "Sudden Notions" (p. 423).

The chapter is clear and covers a crowded quarter-century in fifteen pages, so dates are compressed and some numbers are rounded or dated. Statements needing correction or caution:

  • The Boxer indemnity in "silver dollars" (p. 416): it was set in taels.
  • The "thirty-three-year-old" Guangxu emperor and "three-year-old" Puyi (p. 418): Guangxu was 37; Puyi was two by Western count.
  • Zou Rong at "nineteen" (p. 417): eighteen by Western count.
  • Sun's queue cut in "1896" (p. 418): most biographers say late 1895.
  • Yuan's monarchy announced in "August 1915" (p. 419): he accepted the throne in December.
  • Yuan's acceptance of the Twenty-One Demands on "May 7" (p. 425): 9 May.
  • "The first issue of New Youth in 1915" (p. 420): it was Youth Magazine until 1916.
  • Vernacular textbooks ordered in "1921" (p. 422): January 1920.
  • Lu Xun's "newsreel" (p. 422): a lantern slide, by his own account.
  • Einstein as a visitor (p. 426): invited, but the visit was cancelled.
  • The Science Society founded "while still in China" (p. 424): founded at Cornell.
  • Chinese delegates "not even admitted" at Versailles (p. 425): they were seated, and Koo spoke before the Council of Ten.
  • The Comintern alliance "in 1920" and the united front "in 1922" (p. 428): formalized in 1923–1924.
  • "About one thousand Russian military advisers" by 1925 and Chiang's "four months' training" (p. 428): dozens of advisers; a three-month mission.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

The last Qing decade and 1911

Douglas Reynolds, China, 1898–1912: The Xinzheng Revolution and Japan (1993), for the New Policies; Mary Rankin, Elite Activism and Political Transformation in China (1986), and Roger Thompson, China's Local Councils in the Age of Constitutional Reform (1995), for local elites; Benjamin Schwartz, In Search of Wealth and Power (1964), for Yan Fu; Hao Chang (1971) and Joseph Levenson (1953) for Liang; Joseph Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976), for 1911 in the provinces; Edward Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000), for the anti-Manchu violence. Primary sources: Zou Rong's Revolutionary Army (1903), trans. Lust (1968); the abdication edict and the Articles of Favorable Treatment (1912).

Yuan Shikai and the warlords

Ernest Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977), remains the standard account; Edward McCord, The Power of the Gun (1993), and Arthur Waldron, From War to Nationalism (1995), for the warlord system; Marie-Claire Bergère, The Golden Age of the Chinese Bourgeoisie (1989), and Sherman Cochran, Encountering Chinese Networks (2000), for the wartime economy and the Rong brothers. Primary sources: FRUS 1915 on the Twenty-One Demands and the monarchy.

The New Culture Movement, Lu Xun and women

Chow Tse-tsung (1960) is still the reference; Vera Schwarcz (1986), Jerome Grieder (1970), Timothy Weston (2004), Leo Ou-fan Lee (1987), Lydia Liu (1995), Dorothy Ko (2005), Joan Judge (2008) and Christina Gilmartin (1995) carry the subject forward. Hu Danian (2005) settles the Einstein question; Peter Buck (1975) and Zuoyue Wang (2002) the Science Society. Primary source: Lu Xun, "Diary of a Madman" (1918), the chapter's assigned reading.

May Fourth and the First United Front

For 1919: Chow (1960); Xu Guoqi, China and the Great War (2005) and Strangers on the Western Front (2011); Erez Manela, The Wilsonian Moment (2007); Margaret MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001); Jeffrey Wasserstrom (1991) for Shanghai. For the alliance: C. Martin Wilbur and Julie Lien-ying How, Missionaries of Revolution (1989); Hans van de Ven, From Friend to Comrade (1991); Alexander Pantsov (2000); Jon Jacobson (1994); Eugene Wu (1990) on Chiang's mission; Marie-Claire Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998); Jay Taylor (2009). For the campaign: Donald Jordan (1976) and C. Martin Wilbur, The Nationalist Revolution in China (1983); Richard Rigby (1980) on 30 May; Chan Lau Kit-ching (1990) on the Hong Kong strike; William Duiker (2000) and Sophie Quinn-Judge (2002) on Ho Chi Minh. Primary sources: FRUS, Paris Peace Conference 1919, vol. 3, doc. 50 (Koo before the Council of Ten); the Sun–Joffe statement (26 January 1923); Sun's political will (1925).

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

The arc plan assigns RQ1–3 to Lecture 1; RQ4–5 to Lecture 2, with RQ8 in its required Pierian Spring; RQ6, RQ7 and RQ10 to Lecture 3; and RQ9 and RQ11 to Lecture 4. All eleven questions are taught, ten in the main lectures and one in a required Pierian Spring, and each checkpoint follows the slides that teach it. The roadmap passes. It keeps the chapter's chronology while giving each lecture one narrative problem: why the dynasty fell, why the republic failed, why a generation turned on its culture, and how nationalism became a mass movement and an army. Two seams need care. The Twenty-One Demands are taught in Lecture 2 (slide j) and recapped in Lecture 4 (slide e), so the 9 May correction must stay identical in both. The magazine's name appears in Lecture 2 (slide v) and Lecture 3 (slides a, b) and is now consistent.

6. Individual background essay audits

Essay 1 — Cutting the Queue

Passes. Follows the deck's corrected history (taels, Guangxu at 37, Puyi at two, Zou Rong at eighteen) and gives the killings of Manchus in 1911 the full treatment the instructor requested, from Rhoads.

Essay 2 — The President Who Would Be Emperor

Passes. Dates the throne to December 1915 and the acceptance of the demands to 9 May, explains the Reorganisation Loan in sterling, and qualifies "state religion."

Essay 3 — Youth Is Like Early Spring

Passes. Corrects the magazine's name, the 1920 order and the lantern slide; treats Chen Duxiu in France as disputed; keeps Einstein out; places the Science Society at Cornell; sets up "Diary of a Madman" without summarizing it.

Essay 4 — The Day the Students Marched

Passes. Corrects Paris, the alliance dates, Chiang's mission and the number of advisers; gives the range of deaths for 30 May and Shamian; confirms the 1,150 arrests and the laborers; and adds the Wilsonian context (Manela) and the united-front historiography.

8. Deck issues and fixes applied

Each row names the permanent slide letter, the point checked, its type and the result. "Corrected" means the visible slide gives the corrected history and the notes quote Ebrey and name the source. "Softened" means no reliable source confirmed a correction, so the slide keeps or hedges Ebrey's wording. "Confirmed" means a reliable source supports Ebrey. No slide was added or relettered after its first build.

Audit conclusion

The four Chapter 24 decks and essays are fit for use. They follow Ebrey's chapter closely, correct her where named sources require it, and soften rather than contradict where the evidence is mixed. Two follow-up items remain: the Lecture 3 "next" slide and the course landing page should point to the FA26 Lecture 4 when it is published, and the Chapter 25 link on the last slide of Lecture 4 points to the SU26 Chapter 25 deck until that chapter is rebuilt.

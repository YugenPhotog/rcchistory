The Kowtow and the Crowd: The Macartney Embassy and the Doubling of China

Background for the lecture "The Kowtow and the Crowd": the Canton system, Macartney's embassy and Qianlong's answer, the population boom and its costs, and the Miao frontier · 1757–1797

Central interpretation

The Macartney embassy of 1793 is often told as a parable: a modern, trading Britain met a proud, closed China that refused the future. The better story is of two confident powers, each with good reasons for its position. The Canton system worked for the Qing. It brought in silver for tea and kept foreigners at the edge of the empire. Britain wanted to change it, and the court saw the danger clearly enough to say no and quietly prepare its coasts. Meanwhile the real transformation of eighteenth-century China was internal. The population roughly doubled, to about 300 million. The growth filled the hills with settlers and the roads with landless, unmarried men, and it drove Han farmers into the lands of the Miao and other peoples. Those pressures, not British demands, were the main problems the Qing carried into the nineteenth century.

The story in two acts, matching Review Questions 6 and 7, plus the Pierian Spring (Review Question 11)

  1. The Kowtow (Q6): the tribute model, the tea trade, the Canton system, the balance of trade, and Macartney's goals, gifts, audience and answer.
  2. The Crowd (Q7): the doubling of the population, its causes, partible inheritance, missing brides, the bare sticks, and the move into the hills.
  3. Pierian Spring, The Southern Hills (Q11): settlers and sweet potatoes, the Miao and their neighbors, the failure of separation, and the Miao uprising of 1795–1797; then the embassy's books and the standard-of-living debate.

Act I: The Kowtow (Review Question 6)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, "Contacts with Europe," pp. 280–281. Reading-guide sentence (p. 280): "Macartney was instructed to secure a place for British traders near the tea-producing areas, negotiate a commercial treaty, create a desire for British products, arrange for diplomatic representation in Beijing, and open Japan and Southeast Asia to British commerce as well."

A world of tribute

The deck begins with the Ming inheritance (slide B). The Qing, like the Ming, treated foreign relations as a matter of hierarchy: foreign rulers sent envoys with "tribute," received gifts and calendars in return, and traded at set places and times. Ebrey lists Korea, Ryukyu, Japan, Vietnam and much of Southeast Asia as states that "sent envoys to the court at Beijing" (p. 280). Japan should come off that list, and the deck's notes now leave it off. The Tokugawa shogunate sent no embassies to the Qing; Chinese merchants traded at Nagasaki under Japanese licenses, on Japan's terms. Korea, Ryukyu and Vietnam were the model tributaries. Historians also warn against treating the "tribute system" as a single, rigid machine. It was a set of rituals and practices the court applied flexibly, and much trade, especially on the coast and the inner Asian frontier, happened outside it.1

Tea, Canton and silver

Britain came for tea (slide C). In the eighteenth century the East India Company's China trade grew from a sideline into one of its largest businesses, and tea became a British national habit. After 1757 the Qing confined European trade to Guangzhou (Canton) (slide D). Foreign merchants lived in the "factories" outside the city walls during the trading season, could not enter the city, dealt only with a licensed guild of Chinese firms (the Cohong), and were forbidden to hire Chinese teachers. When the English trader James Flint sailed north to Ningbo and then to Tianjin in 1759 to protest, he was jailed for three years, and the Chinese who had helped him write his petition was executed.

Slide E's concept, a lopsided balance of trade, explains why the system frustrated the British. China bought little that Britain made, so the Company paid for tea largely in silver. By the end of the century, Ebrey notes, "tea made up 80 percent of Chinese exports to Europe" (p. 280). The Canton system was not a refusal to trade. It was a way of managing a very profitable trade under the control of a few accountable people, far from the capital, and it brought the Qing a steady inflow of silver.2

Macartney's goals and gifts

The British government was also suspicious of the Company itself, which "had made great fortunes from its trade with China" (Ebrey, p. 280), and wanted direct relations with the Qing (slide F). George III sent George, Lord Macartney (1737–1806), an experienced diplomat who had negotiated a commercial treaty with Russia in the 1760s and governed Madras in 1781–1785. His instructions are the reading-guide sentence: a trading base near the tea districts, a commercial treaty, a market for British manufactures, a resident envoy in Beijing, and access to Japan and Southeast Asia. Macartney also hoped for a small island where British traders could live under their own rules, and for a fixed, published tariff.

The embassy sailed in September 1792 in HMS Lion and the Company ship Hindostan, with an entourage of eighty-four and six hundred cases of gifts: clocks, a planetarium, telescopes, globes, Wedgwood, glass, carpets and woolens (slide G). Ebrey says that "the only member of the British party able to speak Chinese" was a twelve-year-old boy (p. 280). The boy was George Thomas Staunton, son of the embassy's secretary, who learned Chinese on the voyage and later became Britain's leading Sinologist. But he was not the embassy's only Chinese speaker. Macartney had recruited two Chinese Catholic priests trained at the Chinese College in Naples to serve as interpreters. One left the embassy at Macao. The other, Li Zibiao (known to the British as Jacobus Ly or Plumb), did the interpreting all the way to Rehe, at considerable risk to himself. The boy's role was to copy Chinese documents, and at the audience he spoke a few words to the emperor. Slide G's headline now says that he was the only Briton who could speak Chinese, and the slide names Li Zibiao.3

The audience

The embassy did not land at Guangzhou. It sailed north and reached the mouth of the Beihe, near Tianjin, in late July and August 1793, and went up to Beijing by river and road. Ebrey's "After Lord Macartney arrived in Guangzhou in 1793" (p. 280) is a slip; Macartney left China through Guangzhou, after traveling overland from Beijing, and sailed from Macao in March 1794. Ebrey's "letter to him from George II" is a misprint for George III. The deck gets both right (slide H).

The kowtow, three kneelings and nine knockings of the head on the ground, became the most famous issue. Macartney refused to perform it unless a Qing official of equal rank kowtowed to a portrait of George III. In the end, on 14 September 1793, he was received by Qianlong, then eighty-one, in a great tent at the summer palace at Rehe (Chengde), beyond the Great Wall, and knelt on one knee, as he would to his own king. Some Chinese records and later accounts say he did more. James Hevia argues that the quarrel was not a misunderstanding but a clash of two coherent ritual systems, each of which expressed a theory of how sovereigns related.4 The deck's slide H image is William Alexander's watercolor of the emperor's arrival at the tent. Alexander, the embassy's draftsman, did not go to Rehe and worked from a fellow officer's sketches; the slide says so.

The answer

Ebrey writes: "No negotiations followed this meeting, however, because the Qing court saw no merit in Macartney's requests. It was as interested in maintaining its existing system of regulated trade as Britain was in doing away with it" (p. 280; slide I). The deck's notes now quote the sentence with "however" restored.

Qianlong replied in two edicts to George III. The first answered the king's letter. The second, written after Macartney set out his specific requests, rejected them one by one: no resident envoy, no new ports, no island, no special tariff. The most quoted line, "I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures," comes from the 1914 translation by Edmund Backhouse and J. O. P. Bland, and it is freer than the Chinese. Henrietta Harrison has shown that the edicts were part of a larger response. While the court wrote with calm superiority, Qianlong privately ordered coastal officials to strengthen defenses and watch the British closely. He knew that Britain ruled much of India, and the court suspected the British of having helped the Gurkhas of Nepal, against whom the Qing had just fought a war in Tibet (1788–1792). The edict's tone of indifference was diplomatic, not ignorant. Harrison also shows that the image of a China uninterested in the outside world owes much to how the edict was translated and read in the early twentieth century.5

Ebrey's conclusion, that "the Chinese did not learn much about Europe or Britain from this encounter" (p. 281), is fair for the court as a whole. It is less true of the emperor and his frontier officials, who learned enough to worry.

Answer synthesis · Q6

The Qing regulated European trade by confining it after 1757 to Guangzhou, where foreigners lived outside the walls, dealt only with licensed Chinese merchants and paid in silver for tea, silk and porcelain. Britain wanted something else: Macartney was sent in 1793 to obtain a trading base near the tea districts, a commercial treaty, a market for British goods, a resident envoy in Beijing and access to Japan and Southeast Asia. Qianlong refused every request. The court saw no merit in them, valued the existing system of controlled trade, and treated Britain as one more distant state that should follow Qing rules; the emperor also feared British power in India and ordered coastal defenses strengthened. Neither side changed its view, and the questions Macartney raised were settled by war fifty years later.

Act II: The Crowd (Review Question 7)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, "The Qing at Its Height," pp. 273–274, and "The Less Advantaged and the Disaffected," pp. 283–284. Reading-guide sentence (p. 273): "Population growth during the course of the eighteenth century has been attributed to many factors: global warming that extended the growing season; expanded use of New World crops; slowing of the spread of new diseases that had accompanied the sixteenth-century expansion of global traffic; and the efficiency of the Qing government in providing relief in times of famine."

How many people?

China's population rose from about 150–175 million in 1700 to about 300–325 million by 1800, by Ebrey's estimate (p. 273; slide K). The figures rest on Qing registration data, which improved after the 1740s, and on modern reconstructions. They are estimates, but the doubling is not in doubt. By 1800 something like one person in three on earth lived under the Qing.6

Why?

Slide L presents Ebrey's four causes, with the first now worded as a milder climate. Each needs a sentence of explanation.

  • Climate. Ebrey's "global warming" does not mean modern climate change. The seventeenth century was one of the coldest periods of the Little Ice Age, with droughts and famines that helped bring down the Ming. The eighteenth century was milder, with longer growing seasons. "A milder climate" is the safer phrase.7
  • American crops. Maize, sweet potatoes, white potatoes and peanuts, all brought from the Americas in the sixteenth century, let farmers cultivate hills and sandy soils "previously rejected as too hilly or infertile" (p. 283).
  • Disease. The waves of new epidemics that accompanied the first age of global contact slowed, and Qing elites practiced smallpox inoculation.
  • Famine relief. The eighteenth-century state ran a large network of "ever-normal" granaries and relief campaigns. Pierre-Étienne Will's study of the great drought in Zhili in 1743–1744 shows a bureaucracy capable of feeding millions.8

To these, add the long peace after 1683, the opening of frontier land in the southwest, Taiwan and Manchuria, and commercial growth. James Lee and Wang Feng add a demographic point. Chinese families limited births, through late weaning, low marital fertility and, above all, female infanticide, so the growth was not a Malthusian explosion of uncontrolled breeding. It was the product of families making choices within limits.9

Consequences in the village

Slide M's concept is partible inheritance: land was divided equally among sons. With more sons surviving, farms shrank in each generation, until many were "too small to support a family" (Ebrey, p. 284). Ebrey adds that Chinese villages were "open": they held no common land and could not control who bought or sold there, so land passed quickly between families.

The second consequence was a shortage of brides (slide N). The rich took concubines, and poor families practiced "sex-selective infanticide" (Ebrey, p. 284), letting newborn girls die. The result was a large surplus of men who would never marry; by some estimates, as many as one man in five. These men, the "bare sticks" (guanggun), worked as boat haulers, porters, chair bearers and miners, moved from job to job and were viewed with fear by officials (slide O). Matthew Sommer's studies of Qing legal cases show how the state tried to police them, through harsher laws against rape and male homosexuality, and how poor families sometimes shared or sold wives to survive.10

The third consequence was movement (slide P). Settlers pushed into the hills of the Yangzi highlands, the southwest, Taiwan and, against official prohibitions, Manchuria. Hillside farming of maize and sweet potatoes caused erosion and silting downstream. And the hills were not empty. They were the homes of the Miao, Yao, Zhuang and other peoples, the subject of the Pierian Spring.

Answer synthesis · Q7

China's population roughly doubled in the eighteenth century, to about 300 million. Ebrey names four causes: a milder climate that lengthened growing seasons, the spread of New World crops that made hills and poor soils farmable, a slowing of new epidemics, and effective state famine relief; long internal peace also mattered. The consequences were mixed. Partible inheritance shrank farms; a shortage of women, caused by concubinage and female infanticide, left many men unable to marry; and landless bachelors drifted into hauling, portering and mining, where officials feared them. Settlers moved into the hills and frontiers, causing environmental damage and conflict with non-Han peoples. Growth made the high Qing prosperous, but it also created the pressures behind the rebellions of Lecture 4.

Pierian Spring: The Southern Hills (Review Question 11)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 284–286. Reading-guide sentence (p. 286): "As the Han Chinese population swelled, more and more settlers moved into Miao territories, often expropriating Miao land."

Peoples of the hills

Ebrey describes the indigenous peoples of the south as "called Miao, Yao, Zhuang, and many other names" (p. 284; slides T–U). The Qing state knew them through "Miao albums," illustrated guides that sorted the peoples of the southwest into dozens of categories by dress and custom (slide U image). Laura Hostetler reads these albums as a form of early modern colonial ethnography, comparable to European works of the same period.11

Under Yongzheng, the governor-general Ortai carried out a large campaign in the late 1720s and early 1730s to replace hereditary native chieftains with regular Qing officials (gaitu guiliu), opening Miao country in Guizhou to administration, taxation and settlement. That campaign provoked major Miao risings in the 1730s (slide V, "from the 1720s"). Officials then tried to separate the Miao and Han, forbidding land sales and intermarriage and building walls and forts on the Hunan–Guizhou border (slide W). The rules were not enforced. Settlers lent money at high interest, took land for debts, and bought it outright.

The uprising of 1795–1797

The great rising of 1795 began in the Miao districts on the Hunan–Guizhou border (slide X). Donald Sutton's reexamination, based on the rebels' confessions, shows that their leaders were acculturated men, some literate, and that their grievance was land. The one known handbill said: "The Miao fields are now completely occupied by the Han people. If you help us to burn and kill the guests we can recover our lands." The "guests" were kemin, Han settlers of every kind. The Qing sent its best commanders; two of them, Fuk'anggan and Heliin, died during the campaign, and the main fighting lasted into 1797.12

Ebrey says the revolt broke out where "a Han ethnic group called the Hakka had recently been moving in" (p. 286). This seems to be a misreading of the sources' term for the settlers. "Guest people" can be written in Chinese both as kemin and as kejia, and the second word is the name of the Hakka, a distinct Han group with its own dialect, concentrated in Guangdong, Fujian and Jiangxi. But the newcomers in western Hunan were Han migrants in general, not the Hakka in particular. Slide U's names box and slide X now speak of Han settlers, the "guest people," and keep the textbook's "Hakka" for the instructor notes. The Hakka belong to a later chapter: they were central to the Hakka–Punti wars and the Taiping movement of the nineteenth century.13

Answer synthesis · Q11

As the Han population grew, settlers moved into the hills of the south and southwest, the homes of the Miao, Yao, Zhuang and other peoples. They often took indigenous land through debt, purchase or force, and Qing officials, who had extended direct rule into these areas in the 1720s and 1730s, treated the local peoples with contempt. The Miao resisted from the 1720s on. Officials tried to separate Miao and Han by law and by fortified borders, with little success. In 1795 the Miao of the Hunan–Guizhou border rose to drive out the Han "guests" and recover their fields, and the Qing needed two years and its best generals to suppress them.

More to explore: the embassy's books and the living-standard debate

Slide Z returns to the embassy. Several of its members wrote illustrated books on their return. George Staunton's official account appeared in 1797, and William Alexander's Costume of China in 1805. Ebrey calls them "less rosy than the reports of the Jesuits" (p. 280). They shaped British views of China for decades, and almost none of their authors could read Chinese.

Slide AA raises Kenneth Pomeranz's question. Ebrey reports the argument that China's "overall standard of living in the mid-eighteenth century was comparable to Europe's," and that Jiangnan compared well with England and the Netherlands (pp. 273–274). Pomeranz argues that the "great divergence" between Europe and China came only after 1800, and that it owed much to England's coal and its access to the land and resources of the Americas. The thesis remains debated; wage studies by economic historians have found Jiangnan living standards closer to those of central and southern Europe than to England's.14

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Japan. Tokugawa Japan sent no envoys to the Qing; Ebrey includes it in the tribute list (p. 280).
  • The only Chinese speaker. The embassy's interpreters were Chinese Catholic priests, chiefly Li Zibiao. The twelve-year-old Staunton was the only Briton who spoke Chinese.
  • Guangzhou and George II. Macartney arrived near Tianjin and left via Guangzhou; the letter was from George III. Both are slips in Ebrey (p. 280).
  • The kowtow. Macartney knelt on one knee at Rehe on 14 September 1793; Chinese accounts differ.
  • The edict. The famous line is Backhouse and Bland's 1914 translation. The court also ordered coastal defenses and feared British India.
  • "Global warming." Means a milder phase after the cold seventeenth century, not modern climate change.
  • The Hakka. The settlers of 1795 were Han "guest people" (kemin) in general, not the Hakka.
  • Population figures. Estimates, with registration data improving only after the 1740s.

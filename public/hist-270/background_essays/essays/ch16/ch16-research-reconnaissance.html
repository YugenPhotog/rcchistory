Audit disposition. Chapter 16 had no HIST 270 background essays, so this reconnaissance covers four new essays and a retroactive audit of the four published FA26 decks (The Gate in the Wall, Grandfather and Grandson, The Kowtow and the Crowd and Inside the Garden, Outside the Law). The four-lecture roadmap passes, and the decks are accurate on most points. Quotations attributed to Ebrey were checked against the printed text and are faithful, with three small exceptions of form. The main problems were textbook errors repeated in visible text: Koxinga "holding out" on Taiwan until 1683, Kangxi jailing Oboi "at fourteen," the Mongols with "bows and arrows only," the Macartney embassy's "only Chinese speaker," the "Hakka" of 1795 and Baoyu passing the jinshi. There were also several cross-deck inconsistencies. Steven approved the fixes, and on 25 October 2026 they were applied to the four published decks; Section 8 lists each issue and the fix applied. Every item was applied except one, the Lec2 F link, which has no stable target yet; Section 8 explains.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. Ebrey's Chapter 16, "The Creation of the Manchu Empire (1600–1800)," pp. 270–287, is the course baseline, not its ceiling. Dates, names, numbers and quotations in the decks were checked against the textbook (from an OCR of the course PDF, page by page), against standard monographs and the Cambridge History of China, volume 9, and, where the decks quote primary sources, against published translations. Every quotation that a deck attributes to Ebrey was compared with the printed text by script, and the flagged cases were read by hand. Where Ebrey's statements conflict with current scholarship, the essays follow the scholarship and flag the textbook. Where a deck followed the textbook into error, the essays give the corrected history and this audit lists the slide and the fix.

The rule for the fixes is the course rule: visible slides teach the correct history; the textbook's discrepancy is explained only in an instructor note; outside works are cited in a brief notes line and on the Sources slide; no visible "outside the textbook" tags; slide letters are permanent and no slide is added or relettered.

Audience: the instructor's lecture preparation and advanced students. The essays keep plain language and define terms, but cite real scholarship in footnotes and end with annotated reading lists.

2. Textbook baseline

Ebrey's chapter supplies the spine: the Jurchen background, Nurhaci and the banners (pp. 270–271); Hong Taiji, Dorgon and the conquest (pp. 271–273); Ming loyalism and three loyalist thinkers (pp. 272–273); population and living standards (pp. 273–274); Kangxi, the Three Feudatories, Mongolia and Tibet (p. 274); the Biography of Jiang Chun (p. 275); the Jesuits and the rites controversy, Yongzheng and Qianlong (pp. 276–277); the banner system (pp. 277–278); the Documents feature on Fang Bao (pp. 278–279); contacts with Europe and the Macartney embassy (pp. 280–281); the conservative turn, evidential scholarship, Yuan Mei, the Eight Eccentrics and The Dream of Red Mansions (pp. 281–283); the less advantaged, secret societies, sects and the Miao (pp. 283–286); and the summary (pp. 286–287). The reading guide sets eleven review questions and three review-only questions (RQ12–RQ14).

The chapter is clear and well proportioned. Statements needing correction or caution:

  • Nurhaci's offer against Japan "the next year" after 1590 (p. 271): the invasion and the offer were in 1592.
  • Nurhaci in 1616 "declared war ... by calling himself khan ... and listing his grievances" (p. 271): the khanate was 1616, the Seven Grievances and war 1618.
  • Hong Taiji died "at age forty-six" (p. 271): he was fifty.
  • "Dorgon proposed to Wu that they join forces" (p. 272): most accounts have Wu appealing to Dorgon; the battle of Shanhaiguan is omitted.
  • Huang Zongxi "served the Ming resistance court at Nanjing" (p. 273): he was persecuted at Nanjing and served the Prince of Lu in Zhejiang.
  • Koxinga "was able to hold out in Taiwan until 1683" (p. 273): he died in 1662; his heirs held out.
  • Yangzhou, "slaughtering hundreds of thousands" (p. 273): the scale is unverifiable.
  • "Global warming" (p. 273): a milder climate after the cold seventeenth century.
  • Kangxi jailed his regent "at age fourteen" (p. 274): personal rule 1667, Oboi's arrest 1669.
  • Mongols "armed with bows and arrows only"; "ending two thousand years of northern border defense problems" (p. 274): overstated.
  • Qing army "occupied Lhasa in 1718" (p. 274): 1720.
  • Kangxi "outlawed Christian missionaries" (p. 276): permits from 1706; formal ban 1724 under Yongzheng.
  • Yongzheng "forty-five"; Qianlong died "at age eighty-nine" (pp. 276–277): Chinese count; Western 44 and 87.
  • "Most of the Chinese bannermen removed" (p. 277): overstated.
  • Japan among the states that sent envoys (p. 280): Tokugawa Japan did not.
  • The embassy's only Chinese speaker "a twelve-year-old boy"; Macartney "arrived in Guangzhou"; a letter "from George II" (p. 280): interpreters, Tianjin, George III.
  • Theaters "were closed" (p. 281): repeated, partial bans.
  • "Qian Daxian" (p. 281): Qian Daxin.
  • Baoyu passes "the jinshi examinations" (p. 283): the provincial examination, in the continuation.
  • The Luo sect "an offshoot of the White Lotus" (p. 285): a distinct tradition.
  • "A Han ethnic group called the Hakka" in western Hunan in 1795 (p. 286): Han settlers generally ("guest people," kemin).

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

The Manchu state and the conquest

The field rests on Frederic Wakeman's The Great Enterprise (1985) for the conquest narrative and on the "New Qing History" of the 1990s and 2000s for the Manchus themselves: Pamela Crossley (Orphan Warriors, 1990; The Manchus, 1997; A Translucent Mirror, 1999), Evelyn Rawski (The Last Emperors, 1998) and Mark Elliott (The Manchu Way, 2001). These scholars argue that the Qing was not simply "sinicized" but ruled as an Inner Asian empire with a distinct Manchu identity, maintained above all through the banners. The essays adopt that frame without overstating it. For the Southern Ming and the loyalists, Lynn Struve's The Southern Ming (1984) and Voices from the Ming-Qing Cataclysm (1993) are standard; for Taiwan, Tonio Andrade's Lost Colony (2011); for the queue, Philip Kuhn's Soulstealers (1990).

The high Qing emperors and the frontiers

Spence's Emperor of China (1974) and Ts'ao Yin (1966), Oxnam on the Oboi regency (1975) and Kessler on Kangxi's consolidation (1976) cover the early reign. Peter Perdue's China Marches West (2005) has reshaped the frontier story, and Millward's Beyond the Pass (1998) covers Xinjiang. On Yongzheng, Zelin (1984), Bartlett (1991) and Pei Huang (1974); on Qianlong, Elliott's biography (2009), Berger on Buddhist imagery (2003), Guy on the Four Treasuries (1987) and Kuhn on 1768. On the Jesuits and the rites controversy, Mungello's edited volume (1994), Jami (2012) and Cams (2017).

Macartney, population and the frontiers of settlement

The Macartney embassy has been reinterpreted twice: by Hevia's Cherishing Men from Afar (1995), which read it as a clash of ritual systems, and by Henrietta Harrison (2017; 2021), who shows that the Qing court was better informed and more worried than the edict's tone suggests and recovers the role of the Chinese interpreter Li Zibiao. Population history rests on Ho Ping-ti (1959) and Lee and Wang (1999); the living-standards debate on Pomeranz (2000) and its critics, such as Allen and colleagues (2011). For the poor and unmarried, Sommer (2000; 2015); for the southwest, Hostetler (2001) and Sutton (2003), whose reexamination of the 1795 Miao uprising underlies the Hakka correction.

Culture, sects and the conservative turn

For women and the chastity cult, Mann (1987; 1997), Theiss (2004) and Weijing Lu (2008); for evidential scholarship, Elman (1984) and for ritualism Chow (1994); for theater, Goldman (2012); for the novel, the Hawkes–Minford translation, Levy (1999) and Yu (1997). For the sects, ter Haar (1992) and Overmyer (1976) on the "White Lotus" label; Naquin (1976; 1981) on rebellions; Murray and Qin (1994) and ter Haar (1998) on the Triads; Kelley (1982) on the Luo sect boatmen; and Dai (2019) on the White Lotus war.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

The arc plan assigns RQ1–2 to Lecture 1 with RQ14 and RQ4 in its Pierian Spring; RQ3 and RQ5 to Lecture 2 with RQ13 and RQ12 in its Pierian Spring; RQ6 and RQ7 to Lecture 3 with RQ11 in its Pierian Spring; and RQ9 and RQ10 to Lecture 4 with RQ8 in its Pierian Spring. The roadmap passes. It follows the chapter's logic rather than its page order (population, on p. 273, is taught in Lecture 3 with its consequences), and every review question has a home and a Reading Guide slide whose text matches the guide verbatim.

Seams that need care:

  • The banners are taught in Lecture 1's Pierian Spring and recapped in Lecture 2 (slides V–W); the page cite for debts and land sales differed between the two (Lec1 FF p. 278; Lec2 W "pp. 277, 279"); Lec2 W now cites pp. 277–278.
  • The Macartney audience picture is captioned correctly in Lecture 3 (slide H: Alexander did not attend) but was "Alexander's 1793 sketch of the audience" in Lecture 2 (slide Y); slide Y now agrees with Lecture 3.
  • Theater was "closed" in Lecture 4 (slide C) but thriving in Lecture 2 (slide KK, Jiang Chun's two troupes); slide C now speaks of bans on novels and plays, not closed theaters.
  • The abdication was "1795" in Lecture 2 (slide X) and "1796" in Lecture 4 (slides S, U); slide X now gives the announcement of 1795 and the handover of 1796.
  • Gu Yanwu's mother (Lec1 T) and the faithful maidens (Lec4 Z) are the same phenomenon and are now linked both ways.
  • Ages. Lecture 2 mixes Western and Chinese counts (Kangxi "fourteen," Yongzheng "forty-five," Qianlong "eighty-nine"). Chapter 18 uses Western ages, and Lecture 2 now does too (thirteen and fifteen, forty-four, eighty-seven).

6. Individual background essay audits

After the deck fixes of 25 October 2026, each essay was revised so that its references to slides describe the fixed decks rather than the decks as audited.

Essay 1 — The Gate in the Wall

Passes. Gives the corrected Nurhaci chronology (1592; 1616/1618), the Manchu name decree of 1635, Hong Taiji's age, the battle of Shanhaiguan, the two queue orders, Koxinga's heirs and Huang Zongxi's actual service; adds Gu Yanwu's adoptive mother and the limits of the Chinese-banner expulsions.

Essay 2 — Grandfather and Grandson

Passes. Separates 1667 and 1669, Taiwan and the feudatories, and the stages of the missionary ban; corrects the Zunghar firearms point, Lhasa 1720 and the ages; adds Changkya Rolpe Dorje, Guy on the Four Treasuries and Zelin and Bartlett on Yongzheng.

Essay 3 — The Kowtow and the Crowd

Passes. Corrects Japan's place in the tribute list, the embassy's interpreters, its landing place and George III; frames the edict through Harrison; explains "global warming," Lee and Wang's demography and Sutton's reading of the 1795 rising; treats the Hakka label as a misreading.

Essay 4 — Inside the Garden, Outside the Law

Passes. Qualifies the theater bans, gives Cao Xueqin's uncertain dates, assigns the ending to the 1791 continuation and corrects Baoyu's examination; adds ter Haar on the "White Lotus" label and the Luo sect, Murray and Qin on the Triads, Dai on the White Lotus war and Weijing Lu on faithful maidens.

7. Primary-source and narrative shortlist

  • Wang Xiuchu, Record of Ten Days in Yangzhou (1645), in Struve, Voices from the Ming-Qing Cataclysm. The most powerful eyewitness source for Lecture 1; excerpt with care.
  • Huang Zongxi, Waiting for the Dawn, "On the Prince" (de Bary translation). Two pages that show loyalist political thought at its most radical.
  • Kangxi's own words, in Spence, Emperor of China. Short passages on hunting, illness, government and the Jesuits.
  • Fang Bao, "Random Notes from Prison" (Ebrey's Documents feature, pp. 278–279). Already in the textbook; links the literary inquisition (Lec2 S) to the lives of ordinary prisoners.
  • Qianlong's edicts to George III (1793), read in a modern translation beside the Backhouse–Bland version, with Harrison (2017).
  • Macartney's journal, ed. Cranmer-Byng (1962): the kowtow negotiations and his famous image of China as "an old, crazy, first-rate man-of-war."
  • Cao Xueqin, The Story of the Stone, chapters 1, 17–18 and 42 (Hawkes): the stone myth, the garden and Baochai's confession (Lec4 J).
  • The 1795 Miao handbill and confessions, quoted in Sutton (2003): the rebels' own statement of grievance.
  • The official's description of a White Lotus woman leader (Ebrey, p. 285, from Kwang-ching Liu).
  • Miao albums (Hostetler): images for Lec3 U–W.

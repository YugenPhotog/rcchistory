Audit disposition. Chapter 25 had no FA26 background essays, so this reconnaissance covers four new essays and an audit of the four FA26 decks (The Barrel of a Gun, Paris of the East, Eight Thousand Arrived and Two Capitals), built from the approved arc plan in October 2026. The decks pass. Every textbook detail that the decks correct was checked against a named source; where no reliable source confirmed a correction, the deck keeps or softens Ebrey's wording. Section 8 lists every such point, by lecture and slide.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. Ebrey's Chapter 25, "War and Revolution, China (1927–1949)," pp. 439–454, is the course baseline, not its ceiling. Dates, names, numbers and quotations in the decks were checked against the textbook (from an OCR of the course PDF, with doubtful readings checked against the page images), against standard monographs, and against party and government chronologies where they exist.

The rule for every correction is the instructor's: contradict Ebrey only when a reliable source confirms the correction; otherwise keep her wording or soften it. When a deck corrects her, the visible slide teaches the corrected history, the speaker notes quote Ebrey and explain the difference, and the source is named in the notes and on the deck's Sources slide. No slide carries a visible "outside the textbook" tag.

Two instructor decisions shape the chapter. There is no primary-source reading for Chapter 25; students watch Bertolucci's The Last Emperor (1987), to which Lecture 3 points. And the Nanjing massacre is taught in the main lecture, on one restrained slide with no photographs of violence, with a documentary to be embedded from Canvas Studio.

2. Textbook baseline

Ebrey's chapter supplies the spine: the founding of the Communist Party and the First United Front (pp. 439–440); the April 1927 purge (p. 440); Mao in Hunan and Jiangxi (pp. 440–442); the Nationalist government in Nanjing (pp. 442–444); Shanghai (pp. 444–445); the Long March and Zunyi (pp. 445–446); Yan'an and Mao's rivals (pp. 446–448); Japan, Xi'an and the war of 1937–1938 (pp. 448–450); Chongqing and Yan'an at war (pp. 450–452); and the civil war (pp. 452–454). It includes the Biography of Yuetsim (p. 443), Material Culture: Qipao (p. 448), the Documents box with Wang Shiwei's "Wild Lilies" (pp. 446–447), and Map 25.1 (p. 449).

The chapter covers twenty-two crowded years in sixteen pages, so some dates are compressed and some numbers are dated. Statements needing correction or caution:

  • Li Dazhao's "six years" of law "in Japan" (p. 441): six years in Tianjin, then about two at Waseda.
  • Mao at the "Fourth Normal School… (1913–1918)" (p. 441): the Fourth merged into the First Normal in 1914.
  • Changsha assaulted "in October 1930" (p. 442): July and September 1930.
  • Nanjing "not used as a capital since the early Ming" (p. 442): the Taiping capital and Sun Yatsen's 1912 capital.
  • A marriage law "outlawing concubinage" (p. 442): the Civil Code did not criminalize it.
  • The Bund park signs (p. 445): the exclusion was real, the single sign a legend.
  • The Central Committee to Jiangxi in "1932" (p. 445): January 1933.
  • "Seventeen" leaders at Zunyi (p. 446): about twenty.
  • "Almost 10,000 kilometers" (p. 445): the official figure; a retracing measured about 6,000.
  • Snow "visited Yan'an in 1936" and the base "from 1936 to 1946" (pp. 446–447): Bao'an in 1936; Yan'an from January 1937 to March 1947.
  • The Russian-trained group "arrived in late 1935" (p. 447): Wang Ming arrived in November 1937.
  • The Marco Polo Bridge incident "staged" (p. 450): probably not planned.
  • The Yellow River flood "drowned some three hundred thousand" (p. 450): at least 400,000–500,000 deaths.
  • Soviet aid ending "in 1939" and aid flown "from Burma" (p. 451): it ended in 1941; the Hump flights left from India.
  • Chiang at "Cairo and Yalta" (p. 451): Cairo only.
  • The civil war "broke out in 1947" (p. 454) and Chiang to Taiwan "in early 1949" (p. 453): June 1946; December 1949.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

The Communist Party to 1930

Arif Dirlik, The Origins of Chinese Communism (1989), and Hans van de Ven, From Friend to Comrade (1991), for the party's founding; Elizabeth Perry, Shanghai on Strike (1993), for 1927; Stuart Schram, ed., Mao's Road to Power, vols. 1–3, for Mao's writings, including the Hunan Report (1927) and the Xunwu investigation (1930); Stephen Averill, Revolution in the Highlands (2006), for Jinggangshan.

The Nanjing Decade and Shanghai

Lloyd Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974), and the China Quarterly special issue "Reappraising Republican China" (no. 150, 1997), with William Kirby and Frederic Wakeman, for the debate over the regime; Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009), for Chiang; Marie-Claire Bergère, Shanghai (2009), Emily Honig, Sisters and Strangers (1986), and Robert Bickers and Jeffrey Wasserstrom (1995) for Shanghai; Kathryn Bernhardt (1999) and Lisa Tran (2015) for the Civil Code.

The Long March and Japan

Benjamin Yang (1986) on Zunyi; Ed Jocelyn and Andrew McEwen, The Long March (2006); Helen Praeger Young, Choosing Revolution (2001); Louise Young, Japan's Total Empire (1998); Parks Coble, Facing Japan (1991); Rana Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Joshua Fogel, ed., The Nanjing Massacre in History and Historiography (2000); Diana Lary (2001) and Micah Muscolino (2015) on the Yellow River flood.

Wartime and civil war

Lloyd Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984); Mark Selden, China in Revolution (1995); Chen Yung-fa, Making Revolution (1986); Gao Hua, How the Red Sun Rose (2018); Odd Arne Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Suzanne Pepper, Civil War in China (1978); Steven Levine, Anvil of Victory (1987); Tony Saich, From Rebel to Ruler (2021).

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

The arc plan assigns RQ1–2 to Lecture 1; RQ3–5 to Lecture 2, with RQ11 in its required Pierian Spring; RQ6–7 to Lecture 3; and RQ8–10 to Lecture 4. All eleven questions are taught, ten in the main lectures and one in a required Pierian Spring, and each checkpoint follows the slides that teach it. The roadmap passes. It follows Ebrey's order while giving each lecture one narrative problem: how a small urban party became a peasant army; what the Nationalists built and whom they left out; how a disastrous retreat and a foreign invasion saved the Communists; and why the war decided the civil war. Two seams need care. The fifth encirclement campaign is taught in Lecture 2 (slide m) and finished in Lecture 3 (slide c), so "German-planned" is softened identically in both. Yan'an's dates appear in Lecture 3 (slides o, y, z) and are consistent with Lecture 4.

6. Individual background essay audits

Essay 1 — The Barrel of a Gun

Passes. Follows the deck's corrected history for Li Dazhao's schooling, Mao's normal school and the Changsha attacks, and softens the 12 April death toll to "hundreds, then thousands."

Essay 2 — Paris of the East

Passes. Restores Nanjing's Taiping and 1912 capitals, softens concubinage and "German-planned," treats the park sign as a legend with a real rule behind it, and keeps unconfirmed figures as Ebrey's.

Essay 3 — Eight Thousand Arrived

Passes. Dates the Central Committee's move to January 1933, gives about twenty at Zunyi, treats the distance and the Luding battle with caution, distinguishes the Marco Polo Bridge from Mukden, gives the Nanjing figures and their dispute, raises the flood toll, and places Snow at Bao'an.

Essay 4 — Two Capitals

Passes. Corrects the Hump, Cairo and Yalta, Wang Ming's arrival, the start of the civil war and Chiang's departure; softens the assassinations and "moderate" land reform; keeps Ebrey's party-membership figure for 1940 (an OCR misreading of "1949" was caught against the page image) and continues it to 1949.

8. Deck issues and fixes applied

Each row names the permanent slide letter, the point checked, its type and the result. "Corrected" means the visible slide gives the corrected history and the notes quote Ebrey and name the source. "Softened" means no reliable source confirmed a correction, so the slide keeps or hedges Ebrey's wording. "Kept" means the deck quotes Ebrey, attributed. No slide was added or relettered after its first build.

Audit conclusion

The four Chapter 25 decks and essays are fit for use. They follow Ebrey's chapter closely, correct her where named sources require it, and soften rather than contradict where the evidence is mixed.

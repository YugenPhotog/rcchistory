Heaven’s Mandate: How the Zhou Turned Conquest into Legitimacy

Central interpretation

The Zhou won their kingdom with chariots, but they kept it with an idea and a family. The idea was the Mandate of Heaven: Heaven grants the right to rule to a virtuous king and withdraws it from a cruel one, so the conquest of 1045 BCE could be told as justice rather than seizure. The family was the royal lineage: kings sent brothers, sons and trusted followers out to walled garrisons, bound them by ancestral ritual, court visits and written appointments, and let their fiefs and offices pass to their sons. That arrangement kept Shang writing, bronze and ancestor worship alive while the bloodiest Shang practices faded, and it drew outsiders into a widening Zhou world. It also carried the seeds of its own failure: by 771 BCE the king’s distant kinsmen were strong enough to join the Rong in killing him. The Book of Documents and the Book of Songs let us hear both the court’s claim and the voices beneath it.

The story in four acts, matching Review Questions 10–13

  1. Conquest and Mandate (Q10, slides E–L): the Zhou frontier state, Kings Wen and Wu and the Duke of Zhou, the Mandate concept, Heaven versus the Shang high god, the Book of Documents, and the propaganda question.
  2. Lords and Garrisons (Q11, slides M–U): feudal vocabulary, walled garrisons, appointment ceremonies, Yu’s reward, the founding of Zheng, hereditary lords and the Rong and Yi.
  3. What Changed (Q12, slides V–Y): continuity in ancestor rites, bronze and writing; the gradual fading of oracle-bone divination and human sacrifice.
  4. Voices of the Songs (Q13, slides Z–AF): transmitted versus excavated texts, the two books compared, the aristocratic hierarchy, the farming year and Poem 189 on sons and daughters.

The lecture closes with the fall of 771 (slide AG), the widening circle of people “we can call Chinese” (AH) and the move to Luoyang (AI). The Pierian Spring (AJ–AM) deepens Q10, Q13 and Q11.

Act I: Conquest and Mandate (Review Question 10)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 1, “The Western Zhou Dynasty (1045–771 BCE)” (pp. 15–17 in the course PDF), especially the paragraph on Heaven and the Mandate on p. 17 that the Reading Guide asks students to copy (“Because the theory…”).

The deck opens (slide B) not with the Mandate but with an object, the Li gui, found at Lintong in 1976. Its 32-character inscription records that King Wu attacked the Shang on a jiazi morning, prevailed, and seven days later rewarded the official Li with metal, which Li cast into the vessel. The inscription is the only contemporary record of the conquest, and its jiazi date matches the transmitted tradition; both points come from beyond the textbook and the notes flag them. Opening there lets the deck tie Lecture 2 (writing, bronze, the sixty-day calendar) directly to the Zhou and pose the lecture’s question: a battle could make Wu a conqueror, but what would make him a legitimate king?

Slides F and G give the textbook’s narrative. The Zhou were a frontier state in the Wei valley that shared most of Shang material culture, perhaps allied with the Qiang, and the textbook’s three founders are carefully distinguished: King Wen, the “cultured” or “lettered” king who built Zhou power; King Wu, the “martial” king who won the battle; and the Duke of Zhou, Wu’s brother, who consolidated the conquest and served as regent for Wu’s young son. The founders composite on slide G uses late imperial album portraits; the caption is explicit that these are imagined likenesses made nearly three thousand years later.

Slide H is the concept slide for the Mandate. It defines the term in the textbook’s conditional form (Heaven grants the right to rule only as long as the ruler governs in the people’s interest) and adds the two corollaries students need: a bad last ruler loses the Mandate, and success itself becomes evidence of Heaven’s favor. Slide I places the idea in its religious setting. The Zhou kept sacrificing to their ancestors, as the Shang had, but they also sacrificed to Heaven (tian), and the Zhou king took the title “Son of Heaven.” The He zun (Baoji, 1963), whose inscription has King Cheng recalling that King Wu reported to Heaven after taking the Shang “central lands” (zhongguo), and the Da Yu ding, which says that King Wen “received Heaven’s great mandate” and that the Shang lost it through drunkenness, show the idea in near-contemporary bronze. Both inscriptions are beyond the textbook and are cited after Shaughnessy.

Slides J and K turn to the source problem. The Book of Documents, which the textbook calls the earliest transmitted Chinese text, presents the conquest as just warriors backed by Heaven against a decadent Shang and an evil last king. The textbook adds that the Mandate had no place in Shang cosmology, which is why “some scholars think” it was Zhou propaganda. The deck keeps that hedge (the earlier version presented the Mandate as fact on one slide and as “likely propaganda” on another) and asks students to weigh both possibilities: a self-serving justification invented after the fact, and a sincere religious conviction that also happened to be useful. The textbook’s larger point closes the act: the theory would legitimate every later change of dynasty, because any successful rebel could claim Heaven had shifted its favor.

Source caution · the Book of Documents

The “Documents” (Shangshu) is a layered compilation. A core of chapters is probably Western Zhou in origin, but others were written or rewritten in the Eastern Zhou, and a block of the received text is a later forgery. Students do not need the textual history, but answers should treat the book as the Zhou court’s version of events, not as a neutral record. The bronze inscriptions are useful precisely because they are excavated and roughly contemporary.

Answer synthesis · Q10

The Mandate of Heaven held that Heaven gives the right to rule to a king who governs for the good of the people and takes it away from one who does not. The Zhou used it to describe their conquest as Heaven’s judgment: the last Shang king was cruel and drunken, so Heaven transferred the Mandate to King Wen and his son Wu. The Book of Documents and inscriptions such as the Da Yu ding repeat the claim, and the king’s title “Son of Heaven” built it into the monarchy. Because the idea had no place in Shang religion, some scholars see it as Zhou propaganda; either way, it made the conquest look just and later legitimated every new dynasty.

Act II: Lords and Garrisons (Review Question 11)

Textbook anchor: pp. 16–18, from the appointment-document paragraph on p. 16 through the political-structure paragraph that begins “Rather than attempting…” on p. 17 and the passages on hereditary lords, Yu’s reward, and the Rong and Yi on p. 18.

The act opens with Albert Herrmann’s 1935 atlas map (slide M), chosen because its legend distinguishes fiefs held by sons of King Wen, sons of King Wu, sons of the Duke of Zhou, other royal kin, the Jiang clan and other families. That legend is itself an argument for the lecture’s claim that Zhou rule was family rule. The map reflects 1930s scholarship and old Wade-Giles spellings, and the caption says so.

Slide N is the concept slide for feudalism, fief, vassal, decentralized and quasi, because Q11 uses the textbook’s phrase “decentralized, quasi-feudal” and few first-year students can define either term. Slide O then gives the textbook’s core description: rather than ruling directly, the early kings sent relatives and trusted subordinates out to found walled garrisons; the king was both ritual head of the royal lineage and supreme lord of the nobility; ancestor rituals and court visits kept the lords loyal. The course-made schematic map from the previous deck is retained with a caption that its arrows show directions, not routes.

Slides P and Q put human faces on the system. The textbook describes how appointments to offices or fiefs were written on bamboo or wood, read aloud by an official on the king’s behalf at a court ceremony, handed to the appointee, and later reproduced in bronze for the family. The Song ding, a late Western Zhou appointment inscription that narrates the ceremony step by step (the scribe reading the command, the gifts of robes and chariot fittings, Song bowing and leaving with the document), illustrates the passage from beyond the textbook. Slide Q follows the textbook’s Yu inscription closely: an expedition against the Southern Huai, 100 heads and 40 manacled prisoners, a reward of two bronze vessels, fifty strands of cowries and one hundred fields given in the ancestral temple, and Yu’s prayer that his descendants treasure the vessel. The textbook does not name the vessel; it is probably the Yu gui (敔簋) known from the Song-dynasty catalogues, but that identification is not verified here and the deck does not make it.

Slide R uses the textbook’s dated example, the founding of Zheng in 806 BCE, and corrects its genealogy (see Corrections below). Slide S follows the change over time: vassal positions became hereditary, by 800 BCE there were about two hundred lords of whom some twenty-five mattered, and offices beneath the lords became hereditary too. The Jin lords’ family cemetery at Beizhao (Tianma-Qucun), where successive marquises and their consorts were buried in pairs, illustrates a lineage turning a fief into a dynasty of its own. Slide T covers the Rong and Yi as the textbook does: outsiders with distinct languages but not more primitive technology, whose chiefs could be recognized as lords if they used Chinese writing for matters of state. The deck notes that “barbarian” labels were Zhou labels.

Source caution · “feudal”

Li Feng’s Landscape and Power in Early China and Bureaucracy and the State in Early China argue that “feudalism” misdescribes the Western Zhou. European feudalism rested on contractual bonds between often unrelated lords and vassals under a weak monarchy; Zhou regional states were run by royal kinsmen under a lineage-based order Li calls “delegatory,” and the central court developed a real bureaucracy. Creel, by contrast, used “feudal” deliberately. The textbook’s “quasi-feudal” is a reasonable middle course; the deck teaches the word and puts the debate in the Pierian Spring (slide AM).

Answer synthesis · Q11

Instead of governing everything from the capital, the Western Zhou kings granted territories to relatives and trusted followers, who built walled garrisons and ruled them as lords. The king was both head of the royal lineage and supreme lord, and he held the lords through ancestral rituals, court visits, written appointments read at court ceremonies, and rewards of land and wealth for service, as Yu’s inscription shows. Fiefs and the offices beneath them became hereditary, so by 800 BCE some two hundred noble houses ruled their own domains, and Rong and Yi chiefs could join by using Chinese writing. The system is “quasi-feudal” because it resembles European land-for-loyalty arrangements but rested on kinship and ritual.

Act III: What Changed (Review Question 12)

Textbook anchor: p. 16 (“Divining by heating…”, the sentence the Reading Guide asks students to copy), with the end-of-chapter summary on p. 19.

Act III is short by design: two teaching slides and a checkpoint. Slide W gathers continuity: the Zhou shared Shang material culture before the conquest, the period shows no abrupt break, ancestor sacrifice continued, bronze vessels kept their shapes and spread to new uses, and the Shang script became central to elite life. The rectangular ding chosen for the slide is catalogued as “late Shang to early Western Zhou,” which makes the point visually. Slide X gathers decline, and its wording follows the textbook exactly: oracle-bone divination and human sacrifice became “less common,” interred followers “gradually declined,” and by 771 divination had largely disappeared while human offerings continued “somewhat sporadically” at rulers’ burials. The earlier deck’s “dropped sharply” overstated this. The Zhouyuan oracle bones on the slide remind students that the Zhou did divine with bones, at least early; the textbook’s claim is about frequency.

The textbook does not explain the decline. The deck offers one hypothesis, clearly marked as a question to test: a dynasty whose legitimacy rested on Heaven’s approval of good government had less need to display power through mass sacrifice. Other explanations in the literature include changing ritual specialists, the growth of written documents as a political tool, and regional variation; none is settled.

Answer synthesis · Q12

The Zhou continued the Shang worship of ancestors, Shang-style bronze ritual vessels (now also used for weapons and tools), and the Shang writing system, which became central to elite life and spread to new peoples. Two important Shang practices declined gradually: divining by heating oracle bones, which had largely disappeared by 771 BCE, and human sacrifice, including the burial of followers, which became rarer but continued sporadically at rulers’ burials. The Zhou also added sacrifices to Heaven. The change was gradual, more continuity than rupture.

Act IV: Voices of the Songs (Review Question 13)

Textbook anchor: the Book of Songs box (pp. 16–17), the paragraph on aristocratic society and the farming life (pp. 18–19, “Glimpses of what…”), and the remarks on the Book of Documents on pp. 16 and 19.

Slide AA is the concept slide for transmitted and excavated texts. It reuses the textbook’s own vocabulary (p. 8) and links back to Lecture 1’s discussion of the Xia, so that Q13 becomes a source question rather than a list. Slide AB compares the two books: the Documents for political ideas about Heaven and the Son of Heaven; the Songs, the earliest Chinese poetry, for many voices, with sacrificial hymns, praise of rulers and folk songs. It also introduces allegorical reading, which the textbook mentions in the Songs box. The Ma Hezhi handscrolls in the Metropolitan Museum are used on slides Z, AB, AD and AL because they pair each song with its text; every caption dates them to the twelfth century CE and the notes warn against reading them as evidence of Zhou life.

Slide AC lays out the textbook’s aristocratic ladder (king, dukes and marquises, hereditary great officials, shi, ordinary subjects), patrilineal descent and the serf–lord bargain, with a pop-up on the shi that anticipates Chapter 2. Slide AD uses the textbook’s farming-life details (millet, hemp, beans, vegetables, small game, grasses and rushes, the seasons marked by grasshoppers and crickets), illustrated by “The Seventh Month.” Slide AE uses Poem 189, which the textbook prints: sons on beds with scepters, daughters on the floor with pottery. The illustration pairs a Western Zhou jade zhang with a spindle whorl; linking the poem’s pottery to spinning follows traditional commentary and is flagged as beyond the textbook. The slide links back to Lady Hao so students can test how far one poem describes all women.

Answer synthesis · Q13

Political ideas: the Book of Documents shows the Zhou court’s claim that Heaven chose the Zhou and punished the Shang, and its idea of the king as Son of Heaven ruling for the people. Social hierarchy: the Book of Songs reflects a society ranked by birth, with lords, officials and farmers, and Poem 189 shows sons raised to rule and daughters to serve the household. Everyday life: farm songs describe crops such as millet and hemp, hunting, gathering rushes and a year paced by the seasons. Both are transmitted texts, edited over centuries, so historians check them against excavated bronze inscriptions.

The Fall of 771 and the Bridge to Chapter 2

Slide AG brings the narrative to 771 BCE as the textbook tells it: weakened ties of kinship and loyalty, a king killed by Rong warriors allied with Zhou vassals, nobles burying bronzes as they fled, and the 1976 Zhuangbai hoard of 103 vessels made for several generations of the Wei family. The Shi Qiang pan from that hoard closes the deck’s bronze bookend: its 284 characters list the Zhou kings from Wen onward before praising the scribe’s own ancestors, so the lecture ends with a buried record of the order whose rise it began with. Slide AH uses the textbook’s summary claim that by 771 there were many more people “we can call Chinese,” and stresses that the label is cultural. Slide AI covers the move to Luoyang and the division into Western and Eastern Zhou, and links forward to Chapter 2.

The Pierian Spring: Bao Si, Allegory, “Feudalism”

The three independent-study slides each end with a review question to answer. Slide AK sets the Bao Si legend from Sima Qian’s Shiji (King You lighting the beacon fires to make his consort laugh) against the Xinian, a bamboo-slip chronicle in the Tsinghua University collection acquired in 2008, which says King You attacked the lord of Shen and was killed by Shen, Zeng and the Western Rong. The Xinian fits the textbook’s alliance of Rong and Zhou vassals better than the legend does. The slide asks students to see the legend as the Mandate at work and extends Q10. Slide AL lets students practice the allegorical reading the textbook describes, using Ode 187, “Yellow Bird” (黃鳥), whose Mao preface reads it as criticism of King Xuan; it extends Q13. Slide AM presents the “feudal” debate and the graded ding sets of the Guo lords as a material marker of rank; it extends Q11.

How the Four Questions Connect

The deck’s unifying question, how a conqueror became a legitimate dynasty, gives the four questions an order. Q10 is the claim (Heaven chose us). Q11 is the machinery (family, ritual, written appointment and reward). Q12 is the cultural cost and benefit of the transition (keep the Shang toolkit, let its bloodiest practices fade). Q13 is the evidence (what the court said about itself in the Documents and what the Songs reveal beneath it). The fall of 771 ties them back together: the Mandate’s logic would explain the Zhou’s own decline, and the hereditary lords who had held the realm became its rivals. Reading Guide order (12, 10, 11, 13) follows the textbook’s page order; the deck teaches them in narrative order and labels each checkpoint by its guide number, with no renumbering.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Zheng’s founder (textbook p. 17). The textbook says that in 806 BCE “a younger son of King You” was made a duke and founded Zheng. In Sima Qian’s account (Shiji 42) the founder, Duke Huan of Zheng (personal name You 友), was a younger son of King Li and half-brother of King Xuan, who enfeoffed him in 806; King You (r. 781–771) was his nephew, and Duke Huan died with him in 771. The textbook appears to have confused the duke’s personal name with the king’s. The deck teaches the Shiji version on slide R and notes the textbook wording on the Sources slide.
  • Zheng’s “swampy area.” The textbook places the new state in a swampy area that had to be drained. Zheng was first located in the west (in present Hua County, Shaanxi); its move east to marshy land near the Yellow River came around 773–770, in the last years of the Western Zhou. The deck dates the marshland episode to that later move.
  • Chariot dates. The textbook says chariots were in use by about 1200 BCE (p. 10), but its chariot-burial caption (p. 18) says the type spread around 1100 BCE. The deck avoids the caption date.
  • Conquest date. The deck uses the textbook’s 1045 BCE throughout; the PRC Chronology Project’s 1046 is common in other books.
  • The Yu inscription. The vessel is not named in the textbook. It is probably the Yu gui (敔簋), but this was not verified; the deck calls it simply “Yu’s inscription.”
  • Mandate as fact or propaganda. The previous deck stated the Mandate as fact on one slide and as “likely propaganda” on another. The textbook says only that “some scholars think” it was propaganda; the revised deck keeps that hedge.
  • “Fifteen hundred years before Europe.” The previous deck said Zhou feudalism emerged “about 1,500 years before” European feudalism. Medieval European feudalism (9th–12th centuries CE) is roughly 1,800–2,000 years later than the Western Zhou. The revised deck says only “more than fifteen centuries later” and treats the comparison as a debate.
  • “Dropped sharply.” The previous deck said human sacrifice “dropped sharply.” The textbook says it became less common and continued sporadically at rulers’ burials; the revised deck uses that wording.
  • Answer keys removed. The previous deck’s answer slides are gone; checkpoints give the exact Reading Guide wording, and their notes point to evidence without writing the answer. The syntheses above are for instructor preparation only.
  • Images. No image in the deck is AI-generated. Late imperial portraits (slide G), the Qing print of Bao Si (AK), the Song-dynasty handscrolls (Z, AB, AD, AL) and the 1935 map (M) are later interpretations, and their captions say so. Some identifications rest on museum or uploader labels: the title-slide zun as the He zun, the Luoyang fangyi lid (possibly the Ling fangyi), and the Li gui photograph’s location.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Edward L. Shaughnessy, Sources of Western Zhou History: Inscribed Bronze Vessels (1991). How to read and date the bronzes; translations of key inscriptions.

Edward L. Shaughnessy, “Western Zhou History,” in Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy, eds., The Cambridge History of Ancient China (1999). The standard narrative of the period, from the conquest to 771.

Li Feng, Landscape and Power in Early China: The Crisis and Fall of the Western Zhou, 1045–771 BC (2006). Geography, the regional states and the fall; the critique of “feudalism.”

Li Feng, Bureaucracy and the State in Early China: Governing the Western Zhou (2008). The case that the Zhou court built a real bureaucracy.

Li Feng, Early China: A Social and Cultural History (2013). The clearest recent survey.

Herrlee G. Creel, The Origins of Statecraft in China, vol. 1: The Western Chou Empire (1970). The classic argument for a Zhou “feudal” order and the Mandate as political theory.

Lothar von Falkenhausen, Chinese Society in the Age of Confucius (1000–250 BC): The Archaeological Evidence (2006). Lineages, ritual reform and graded vessel sets, from tombs.

Martin Kern, “Bronze Inscriptions, the Shijing and the Shangshu,” in John Lagerwey and Marc Kalinowski, eds., Early Chinese Religion, Part One (2009). How the two transmitted books relate to inscriptions and ritual performance.

Rare Skills Became Power, and the Shang Were Not Alone

Background for the lecture “Writing, Bronze, and the Wider World”: the king’s records, the bronze industry and royal women, and the Bronze Age cultures beyond Anyang · ca. 1200–1045 BCE

Central interpretation

Writing and bronze were the two rare skills of the Shang court, and both turned into political power. The written archive let the king record, date, compare and command across a growing realm, while years of training kept literacy in a few hands. The bronze industry mobilized miners, fuel makers, mold makers, casters and managers, and it spent its finest metal on feeding the ancestors rather than on war. Royal women such as Lady Hao and Queen Mother Wu stood at the center of both systems. Yet the same evidence that makes the Shang look central is also what makes them visible: beyond the Shang core, Sanxingdui, the Yangzi valley, Jiangxi and the Northern Zone show other Bronze Age societies with their own wealth, gods and claims to be the center.

The story in three acts, matching Review Questions 5, 7 and 8

  1. Writing as power (Q5 and Q6, slides E–K, with the concept slide AM and checkpoints M and AN): what oracle-bone records contained, the sixty-day calendar, lost bamboo books, literacy as an ally of political control, how characters work (Q6) and why the script unified China.
  2. Bronze for the ancestors (Q7, slides N–U): the Houmuwu ding, piece-mold casting, the supply chain, bronze and royal women, ritual over war, and the taotie puzzle.
  3. A wider world (Q8, slides V–AE): the small zone of safe royal travel, the middle Yangzi, Jiangxi, the Northern Zone, Sanxingdui’s pits and city, and the case for decentering the Shang.

Act I: Writing as Power (Review Question 5)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 1, “Writing” (pp. 12–13 in the course PDF), the oracle-bone paragraphs on p. 10, and the Lady Hao passage on p. 11.

The deck opens (slide B) with the 1939 discovery of the Houmuwu ding because it binds the two halves of the lecture together: a cauldron so large that only an organized state could cast it, carrying three cast characters that name a royal woman. The story of the villagers who hid it from Japanese troops comes from Chinese press accounts and museum histories, not from the textbook, and the deck labels it that way. The 1948 photograph of the vessel on display in Nanjing is a modern primary source about the discovery.

Act I then asks students to look at oracle bones a second time. In Lecture 1 the bones were religious tools; here they are documents. The textbook’s evidence is specific enough to support a strong answer: by 1200 BCE writing was already “a major element” of Shang culture, and the same inscriptions that asked about rain also recorded enemies slain, booty, hunting bags and tribute (slide F). Slide F reuses the Lady Hao campaign against the Qiang with 13,000 troops so that students see writing preserving a woman’s command. Slide G adds the sexagenary calendar, illustrated with Heji 37986, an ox scapula carrying the complete table of sixty day names. It is not a divination, and the notes say so; it is the closest thing in the Shang record to a reference chart, which makes it a good image for administration.

Slide H handles the textbook’s argument from absence. Writing appears on the bones fully formed, so it must be older; its early stages are lost because everyday writing was probably done on bamboo, wood or silk. The graph 冊 (ce), which pictures tied strips, is the best single piece of supporting evidence, since it implies bound bamboo documents in Shang times even though none survive. The textbook figures belong here too: roughly five thousand graphs on divination texts, only about 40 percent deciphered.

Slide I turns the evidence into the causal claim the question needs. The textbook’s sentence, “Literacy is an ally of political control, facilitating communication across an expanding realm,” is quoted directly. The deck unpacks it in three functions (memory beyond human recall, exact orders at a distance, and a basis for checking claims) and then adds the restriction argument: because the script took years to learn, literacy stayed with a trained few, so control of writing meant control of the state’s information. That parallels Lecture 1’s argument that the king monopolized the most powerful channel to the ancestors. David Keightley’s description of the oracle-bone corpus as a dated, formulaic, king-centered archive is the scholarly basis for treating divination records as government records.

Source caution · what the bones can and cannot show

Students easily slide from “the Shang kept records” to “the Shang had a bureaucracy.” The oracle-bone record is a divination archive, not tax registers or law codes, and it overrepresents a few reigns, especially Wu Ding’s. The inference that the court also kept everyday records on perishable materials is reasonable but unprovable. Encourage the phrasing “the surviving records show” rather than “the Shang government recorded everything.”

Question 6 now has its own checkpoint. The concept slide AM defines a logographic script, slides J and K teach it, and checkpoint AN closes the sequence; the Q5 checkpoint (M) now comes straight after the writing-and-control slide (I). The textbook’s points are the logographic principle, the comparison with Egyptian and Sumerian writing, the three ways characters were formed (pictures, sound loans, and the meaning-plus-sound compounds that already make up more than half of oracle-bone graphs), and the cost: two to three thousand characters for basic literacy. Slide K gives the payoff: unlike western Eurasia, China never went phonetic, and the script let speakers of mutually unintelligible varieties read the same texts and read texts from long before. The cost-benefit question in social terms (who paid for the years of study, who gained), which brings Q6 back to Q5’s theme of power, now sits in slide K’s callout and notes. The former thinking slide L was retired on 2026-10-24; its letter is never reused.

Answer synthesis · Q5

Administration: scribes recorded war results, booty, hunts and tribute and dated them on a shared sixty-day calendar, so the court could store and compare information. Communication: written orders could travel across an expanding realm without distortion and outlast the person who gave them. Political control: the script took years to master, so literacy stayed with the king’s trained specialists, and controlling the record meant controlling the state’s information. The records also preserved the authority of royal women such as Lady Hao. The surviving bones are only the durable part of a larger written culture.

Act II: Bronze for the Ancestors (Review Question 7)

Textbook anchor: “Bronze Metallurgy” and the art passage that follows (pp. 13–14 in the course PDF), Figure 1.4 on the piece-mold, and the Lady Hao tomb passage on p. 11.

Act II is organized around the two halves of the question. The first half, social organization, rests on three slides. Slide O uses the textbook’s estimate that two to three hundred craftsmen were needed for the largest surviving vessel; slide P walks through piece-mold casting (model, mold sections, core, pour, break-out, finishing) and the textbook’s inference that precision and standardization imply division of labor and managers; slide Q adds the invisible supply chain of miners, refiners, porters and charcoal makers, plus the textbook’s observation that city workshops specialized in single products such as hairpins or arrowheads. Robert Bagley’s argument that the piece-mold method shaped everything about Chinese bronzes, and that the foundries demonstrate managerial capacity as impressive as the art, is the main scholarly frame.

Slide R is where the deck deliberately counters the assumption that Shang women were powerless. Lady Hao’s tomb held bronzes weighing 1.6 metric tons, about 60 of them inscribed with her name, and the oracle bones record her as a commander. The Houmuwu ding itself carries the three graphs usually read “Queen Mother Wu” (后母戊, once read 司母戊, “Simuwu”), and most scholars identify the honoree as Fu Jing, another consort of Wu Ding, commemorated by a son who reigned after him. That identification and the reading of the first graph are scholarship beyond the textbook; the deck flags both. The point for students is not that every Shang woman was powerful, but that elite rank, ritual authority and wealth could and did belong to women.

The second half of the question, beliefs and culture, rests on slides S and T. The textbook’s sentence “In Shang times, bronze was used more for ritual than for war” is the key evidence: most surviving bronzes are food and wine vessels for offerings. Slide S illustrates it with Lady Hao’s triple steamer, a royal woman’s banquet equipment for the ancestors. Slide T presents the textbook’s contrast with Egyptian, Assyrian and Babylonian art, in which crops, kings, priests and slaves appear, while Shang bronzes favor wild animals. Cicadas plausibly suggest rebirth and birds messengers to the sky, but the taotie remains contested (apotropaic monster, ritual mask, sacrificial animal, totem, shamanic helper, or nearly meaningless design). Sarah Allan reads it through Shang myth and sacrifice; Bagley has argued that the motif developed from pattern and should not be forced into iconography. The textbook’s own moral, that such disagreement “reminds us of the huge gaps in our understanding,” is the honest conclusion.

Answer synthesis · Q7

Organization: a single 830–875 kg cauldron needed two to three hundred craftsmen and many furnaces pouring on a signal; piece-mold casting divided the work among model makers, mold makers, casters and finishers under managers; and behind the workshops stood miners, refiners, porters and charcoal producers. Beliefs: most bronze went into ritual vessels for feeding ancestors and gods rather than weapons, showing that the Shang located real power in the spirit world. Hierarchy: kings owned the most bronze, and inscribed vessels of Lady Hao and Queen Mother Wu show royal women holding wealth and ritual authority. Uncertainty: the meaning of the taotie and other animal decoration is still debated.

Act III: A Bronze Age Bigger Than the Shang (Review Question 8)

Textbook anchor: “Neighbors of the Shang” and the Sanxingdui passage (pp. 14–15 in the course PDF), including the sentence beginning “The existence of sites like Sanxingdui…”.

Act III starts from a fact students rarely notice: the textbook says the zone where the Shang king could travel safely was limited to northern Henan and western Shandong (slide W). The governing distinction for the whole act is between sharing a technology and sharing a government. Slides X–Z then visit three regions. In the middle Yangzi, bronzes share Shang technique but differ in design, sometimes replacing the taotie with human faces, and bells are buried in groups in mountain sides; the Ningxiang human-face ding is the illustration. In Jiangxi, tombs such as Dayangzhou at Xin’gan (1989) show elite wealth on a Shang scale and a regional taste for tigers on vessel handles; the textbook leaves open whether this is provincial Shang or a separate culture. In the Northern Zone, knives, axes and mirrors resemble Central Asian and Siberian metalwork, yet oracle-bone divination links the region to the Shang, and its people may have brought the chariot. Slide Z also returns to Lady Hao: Northern Zone knives and mirrors, along with southern bronzes, appear in her tomb as probable tribute.

Slides AA–AC give Sanxingdui the weight the textbook gives it. The two 1986 pits held elephant tusks, gold-foil-covered heads, protruding-eye masks and the 2.6-meter standing figure. The pits were filled in ordered layers, and many objects were burned or deliberately broken. The textbook offers interpretations without settling them: a great sacrifice without human victims, bronze figures as substitutes for humans, or a decision to burn and bury the images of gods. The walled city of nearly two square kilometers, with some fifty building foundations, proves a political center, abandoned around 1000 BCE. The textbook’s conclusion (slide AD) is the thesis of the act: such sites have “forced archaeologists to reconsider the political landscape,” and because the other cultures left no writing, there is no reason to assume their elites saw themselves as less central than the Shang did. Bagley and Li Feng are the most useful voices here; both argue that the Shang-centered picture reflects where archaeologists dug first and which society left texts.

Source caution · absence of writing cuts both ways

The lack of texts at Sanxingdui and in Jiangxi means we cannot know what languages these peoples spoke or how they related to Anyang. That uncertainty should not be turned into a confident claim of total independence any more than into a claim of Shang rule. The textbook’s own careful phrasing (“provincial version” or “a different culture that borrowed extensively”) is a model for student answers.

Answer synthesis · Q8

The traditional view made the Shang the single source of early Chinese civilization, spreading outward from the Yellow River. Sanxingdui shows a walled city with bronze casting equal to Anyang’s but an art and religion of its own (giant masks, gold-foil heads, a monumental figure, pits without human victims). The middle Yangzi, Jiangxi and the Northern Zone add regional styles, Shang-scale elite wealth and links to the steppe. Since the Shang king’s safe zone was small and shared technology does not prove shared rule, early China looks like several interacting centers. The Shang seem central partly because they alone left writing.

The Pierian Spring: Diffusion, New Finds, Historians

Slide AI covers Question 9, and the Pierian Spring checkpoint AO (ps-rg-q9) follows it. The textbook’s argument for the chariot is an argument from missing antecedents: no draft animals, no wheels of any kind and no simpler carts precede the spoke-wheeled chariot, which appears around 1200 BCE, so it most likely arrived by diffusion across Asia, probably through the Northern Zone. The domesticated horse and wheat also came from the west. The textbook reports Shang silk found in an Egyptian tomb; the identification is debated, and slide AI now states it as disputed on its face. Slide AJ updates the textbook on Sanxingdui: pits 3–8, excavated 2020–2022, yielded nearly 13,000 numbered finds including a gold mask of about 280 grams from Pit 5, and radiocarbon dates announced in 2022 placed several pits at about 1131–1012 BCE. Jinsha in Chengdu, found in 2001, is widely treated as Sanxingdui’s successor, which complicates the textbook statement that no later sites of the culture are known. Slide AK summarizes Keightley, Boltz, Bagley, Allan and Li Feng.

How the Three Questions Connect

The deck’s unifying question is how rare skills became political power, and whether the Shang were the only place where that happened. Q5 and Q7 are parallel answers: writing and bronze each required long training and organized specialists, and each served the king’s relationship with the ancestors. Q8 then turns the lens on the evidence itself. Writing, the subject of Act I, is exactly why the Shang dominate the historical record; once students see that, decentering becomes an argument about sources rather than a slogan. Slide AF uses Lady Hao’s tomb as a single body of evidence for all three questions (her inscribed bronzes, her 1.6 tons of metal and her tribute from the south and north), and slide AG uses the Li gui, with its jiazi-day date, to show writing and bronze recording the Shang’s fall and to bridge to the Zhou and the Mandate of Heaven.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Houmuwu weight. The textbook gives 875 kg; the National Museum of China lists 832.84 kg. Slide O shows “about 830 kg” with the textbook’s 875 kg beside it, and the notes give both. Lecture 1, slide Z, was aligned on 2026-10-24.
  • The Shang fall. The deck follows the textbook’s 1045 BCE (Act II kicker corrected from 1046). The notes for slide N mention that some books give 1046 BCE, the date of the Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project.
  • Piece-mold figure. The textbook’s piece-mold illustration is Figure 1.1; earlier notes for slide P said Figure 1.4.
  • Sanxingdui dates. Captions on slides AA and AD now say about 1200–1100 BCE rather than about 1200 BCE, closer to the 2022 radiocarbon results for the pits.
  • Chariot dates. The textbook says chariots were in use by about 1200 BCE (p. 10), but its later figure caption on a chariot burial says about 1100 BCE. The previous deck put the 1100 date under a Shang (Yinxu) chariot burial; the revised deck uses about 1200 BCE.
  • Sanxingdui masks. The previous deck described “gold-foil masks with protruding eyes.” The famous protruding-eye masks are plain bronze; the gold foil is on separate heads. The revised deck keeps them apart (slides AA and AD).
  • Sanxingdui after the textbook. The textbook’s “two pits” and “no later sites” statements predate pits 3–8 (2020–2022) and the wide acceptance of Jinsha (2001) as a successor. The deck reports these in the Pierian Spring as beyond the textbook.
  • Silk in Egypt. The textbook’s claim of Shang silk in an Egyptian tomb rests on a disputed fiber identification. Present it as reported, not settled.
  • Answer keys removed. The previous deck’s “Teaching the Answer” slides gave model answers to Q5–Q8. The revised Reading Guide checkpoint slides use the exact Reading Guide wording and give no suggested answer; the syntheses above are for instructor preparation only.
  • AI image. Only the Act III opener (slide V) is AI-generated, carried over from the earlier deck and labeled in its notes. Its parasol chariot resembles later Chinese chariots, not Shang ones. Do not cite it as evidence.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

David N. Keightley, Sources of Shang History: The Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of Bronze Age China (1978). How the bones were made, read and dated, and why they work as an archive.

William G. Boltz, The Origin and Early Development of the Chinese Writing System (1994). The standard account of how characters were formed and why the script never became phonetic.

Robert Bagley, “Shang Archaeology,” in Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy, eds., The Cambridge History of Ancient China (1999). Piece-mold casting, the foundries, and the regional Bronze Age cultures beyond Anyang.

Robert Bagley, ed., Ancient Sichuan: Treasures from a Lost Civilization (2001). The best English introduction to Sanxingdui and its art.

Sarah Allan, The Shape of the Turtle: Myth, Art, and Cosmos in Early China (1991). A reading of Shang cosmology and decoration, including the taotie.

Li Feng, Early China: A Social and Cultural History (2013). The clearest recent survey, strong on regional cultures and the steppe connection.

Lothar von Falkenhausen, “On the Historiographical Orientation of Chinese Archaeology,” Antiquity 67 (1993). How text-centered expectations shaped Chinese archaeology and the Shang-centered narrative.

The Sui Tragedy: The Dynasty That Reunited China—and Broke Itself

A tragedy in three acts: seizing the realm, building the machine, and breaking the empire · 581–618

Central interpretation

The Sui did not collapse because it failed to build a powerful state. It collapsed because its rulers used that power without accepting the limits of the households, soldiers, and officials who sustained it.

The tragedy in three acts

  1. Seizing the Realm: Yang Jian takes a throne, crosses the Yangzi, and reunifies China.
  2. Building the Machine: officials, law, canals, registers, taxes, soldiers, and religion bind the empire together.
  3. Breaking the Empire: Yangdi pushes that machinery beyond what households and elites will bear.

Epilogue: the dynasty dies, but the Tang inherits much of the state it built.

Prologue: Jiangdu, 618

The palace at Jiangdu was quiet the way a place goes quiet before something terrible happens.

It was the spring of 618. Emperor Yangdi had come south two years earlier to the lush canal city now called Yangzhou. He had escaped the rebellions consuming the north, but he had not escaped the news. Every report was worse than the last. Roads were cut. Canals were blocked. Rival leaders ruled whole provinces and called themselves kings. The northern heartland, where his father had built a capital and an empire, had slipped beyond his reach.

The men guarding Emperor Yangdi knew it too. Most were northerners, far from their families, and they had watched the dynasty shrink around them. They whispered about deserting. Then they began whispering about something worse. Officers gathered around the general Yuwen Huaji, and a plan took shape in the barracks.

Traditional histories say Emperor Yangdi sensed the end coming. They describe him studying his reflection and remarking on what a fine head he had, and wondering aloud who would cut it off. Whether he truly said it or not, the story captures a ruler who could see the ending but could no longer change it.

On the night the conspirators moved, the gates did not hold, because the men assigned to hold them had joined the plot. Soldiers flooded the palace. Emperor Yangdi, who had once sent armies said to number in the hundreds of thousands across rivers and mountains, could not command a single guard to defend him. The rebels seized him and killed him. The traditional account says he was strangled.

Six hundred miles to the northwest, another ending had already begun. Li Yuan, a former Sui governor, had marched into Chang'an the previous winter. He placed Emperor Yangdi's young grandson on the throne as a puppet, claiming to defend the dynasty rather than replace it. When word arrived that Emperor Yangdi was dead, Li Yuan stopped pretending. He set the boy aside and proclaimed a new dynasty: the Tang.

The house of Sui vanished. Almost nothing else did. The capital, the law code, the government offices, the canals, the household registers, and the reunified empire all survived and passed into new hands.

That is the mystery at the heart of this story. The Sui ended more than three centuries of division. How did it collapse before its fortieth birthday? Why did farmers, soldiers, officials, governors, and finally Emperor Yangdi's own guards stop obeying it? And why did the dynasty that replaced it keep so much of what it had built?

The answer is a tragedy in three acts. Wendi built the machine. Emperor Yangdi drove it past the breaking point. The Tang inherited what survived.

Act I: Seizing the Realm

A Child Emperor and an Adult with an Army

The Sui did not appear from nowhere. Civil war had torn apart the regimes of north China since 523. In 577, Northern Zhou defeated its rival Northern Qi and reunited the north. That victory gave the Zhou court battle-tested armies and removed the main threat on its eastern flank. Only twelve years separate the Zhou conquest of Qi from the Sui conquest of the southern Chen in 589. Reunification was the result of decades of northern warfare, not a sudden Sui invention.

Yang Jian was one of the most powerful insiders at the Northern Zhou court. His daughter had married Emperor Xuan. In 579 Xuan abdicated in favor of his young son, Emperor Jing, Yang Jian’s step-grandson, and when Xuan died in 580, the boy was left on the throne. The child possessed the title; Yang Jian controlled the government and army. He made himself regent, defeated Zhou loyalists who resisted him, and took the throne in 581. He then had dozens of Zhou princes killed and later killed the boy emperor, making a restoration of the old ruling house far less likely.

The Sui therefore began with a coup inside the northern elite, not with a popular movement. Its founder faced an immediate contradiction. Yang Jian had used force and family position to seize power, but he needed to present himself as the rightful ruler of a moral and universal empire. That problem of legitimacy would shape his use of law, ritual, and Buddhism.1

Names to know

Wendi, meaning “Cultured Emperor,” is a posthumous title given to Yang Jian after his death in 604. During his rule, years were counted by the reign names Kaihuang (581–600) and Renshou (601–604). His son Yang Guang is remembered as Yangdi, another posthumous title—and one chosen by the dynasty’s enemies.

A Mixed Northwestern Ruling World

The people surrounding Yang Jian did not fit a simple division between “Chinese” and “foreign.” The Sui founders belonged to an intermarried northwestern aristocracy that had served northern regimes founded by the Xianbei, peoples from the grasslands whose leaders ruled much of north China before the Sui. Yang Jian claimed Han Chinese ancestors, but his family had married into Xianbei clans for generations. His wife, Empress Dugu, also came from a family with both Chinese and non-Chinese connections. Britannica aptly describes the culture of such families as “as mixed as their descent.”

This matters because the Tang founders came from the same political and military world. The transition from Sui to Tang would eventually look less like an entirely new ruling class replacing an old one than like one branch of the northwestern elite capturing the institutions built by another.

The River That Divided China

Yang Jian had taken a throne, but most of the south remained under the Chen dynasty. Between them stood the Yangzi River, a wide and dangerous barrier that southern courts had used for centuries. Northern armies were most powerful on land. They could not simply march into the lower Yangzi region. The river had to be treated as an enemy in its own right.

The Sui answered with ships, preparation, and scale. By the winter of 588–589, armies and fleets formed an invasion front stretching along the river from Sichuan to the sea. The Chen rulers could not concentrate their defense against so many possible crossings. Sui forces broke through and captured Jiankang, modern Nanjing, in 589. The city was razed, and Chen nobles and officials were moved north so that the southern political class could not easily rebuild a rival court. More than three and a half centuries after the fall of the Han, north and south again answered to one emperor.

Victory Was Not the End

Capturing a capital did not mean controlling a region. In late 590, rumors that southern families would be forcibly moved north helped ignite a large revolt. General Yang Su crushed it. The court then changed tempo. Wendi exempted the former Chen territories from taxes for ten years. His son Yang Guang—the future Yangdi—governed the south as viceroy from 591 to 600. Married to a princess from the southern Liang royal house, he ruled relatively mildly and drew southern elites into the new order. Britannica credits much of the south’s successful integration to his administration.

The irony is important. The man later remembered as the dynasty’s monstrous destroyer had first been one of its most effective agents of reconciliation. The south was taken through overwhelming force, but it was held through a calculated mixture of punishment, tax relief, marriage ties, and cooperation with local elites.

Act I interpretation

Reunification required two conquests: Yang Jian first seized the northern throne, then Sui armies crossed the Yangzi and seized the south. Neither victory could last without a new answer to the harder question—how should one court govern the reunited realm?

Act II: Building the Machine

The word machine is a metaphor. The Sui did not create a single device called “the state.” It linked together officials, law, capitals, waterways, registers, taxes, labor obligations, military service, and religious claims. Each part solved a real problem left by conquest. Together they gave the court an extraordinary ability to see, move, and command resources across the empire.

Problem One: Who Governs?

Before the Sui, the Nine Rank System allowed local elites to rank candidates for office. Powerful families therefore exercised enormous influence over who governed their districts. Wendi ended that system. The court appointed prefecture and county officials itself, barred officials from serving in their home regions, and in 583 removed an entire layer of local government, the commanderies. Counties now answered directly to prefectures, and prefectural officials answered to the imperial court.

Written testing became one part of this new relationship. From 587, each prefecture sent three candidates a year to the capital, where written examinations helped determine who would serve. Around 605–607, Yangdi added the “presented scholar,” or jinshi, category, though historians debate the exact date. This was a beginning, not the complete civil-service examination system familiar from later Chinese history. Family position, education, recommendations, personal connections, and inherited privilege continued to matter, and even under the Tang examination graduates remained only one stream of officials.

Problem Two: How Does the Court Govern Consistently?

At the center, the Sui organized three major ministries with boards beneath them and used a censorate to investigate official conduct. The Kaihuang Code of 583 simplified and softened the harsher laws of Northern Zhou. The code itself has not survived, but it became the model for the Tang Code, one of the most influential legal systems in East Asian history.

These reforms were not glamorous. They did not produce the drama of a cavalry charge or a river crossing. Yet they helped ensure that a tax case, an appointment, or a criminal judgment could be handled through recognizable institutions rather than improvised separately by every local magnate. The Sui was turning conquest into routine government.

Problem Three: What Does Each Household Owe?

Imagine a farming family appearing before a clerk. The clerk records the people in the household and the land associated with them. That entry connects the family to several systems at once. Under the equal-field system, the state assigned farmland in exchange for taxes and labor. Under the fubing system, farmers served as soldiers in rotations, partly in exchange for land. Grain and cloth supported the government. Labor obligations supplied workers for roads, canals, walls, and capitals.

The official register of 609, preserved in the geography monograph of the Book of Sui, lists 8,907,546 households and 46,019,956 people. These are registered figures, not a complete census. People who fled, hid, or were never recorded do not appear, and registration in the former Chen territories was probably incomplete. Tang officials later believed that only about 70 percent of the population appeared on the rolls.

Even an imperfect register transformed government. It allowed officials to estimate how much land, tax, labor, and military service could be demanded. Registration made the empire visible to the court. It also created the mechanism through which the court could press the same family for grain, cloth, a canal worker, and a soldier.

Problem Four: How Does Southern Grain Reach Northern Power?

The Sui capitals sat in the northwest. Wendi built Daxing near the old Han capital; under the Tang it would be called Chang’an. Yangdi added a second great capital at Luoyang beginning in 605. Yet China’s richest agricultural regions increasingly lay far to the southeast around the Yangzi. Moving grain by water was far cheaper than hauling it overland. A canal system linking the Yellow River and Yangzi systems could feed capitals, provision armies, and tie north and south together economically.

The canals were built in stages and often reused older waterways. Wendi opened the Guangtong Canal from the capital toward the Yellow River in 584 and reopened the Shanyang Canal between the Huai and Yangzi in 587. Most construction came under Yangdi. The Tongji Canal linked Luoyang to the Huai in 605. The Yongji Canal reached north toward Zhuo commandery near modern Beijing in 608. The Jiangnan Canal was rebuilt south of the Yangzi to Yuhang, modern Hangzhou, in 610. By 611, eastern China possessed a trunk water route from the Yangzi delta to the northern frontier.

The achievement had a human cost. The Book of Sui reports more than a million men and women drafted for the Tongji Canal and similarly enormous numbers for the Yongji Canal. The figures may be rounded or exaggerated, but the labor burden was unquestionably immense. Workers dug channels, reinforced banks, carried earth, hauled timber, and supplied food. The finished canal moved grain efficiently because the construction sites had first mobilized human bodies on a staggering scale.

Primary source: a late Tang verdict on the canal

“Under the Sui, the people could not bear the harm it caused; under the Tang, the people cannot count its benefits.”

— Pi Rixiu, late ninth century · translation adapted from Quan Tang wen, ch. 797

Pi Rixiu wrote more than two centuries later, when Tang grain fleets still depended on Sui routes. His judgment holds the tragedy in one sentence. The people who dug the canal endured the immediate suffering. Later generations inherited the benefit. Whether the project appears successful depends partly on whose lifetime one measures.

Problem Five: Why Should the Conquered Accept This Dynasty?

Wendi had founded his dynasty through a violent seizure of power. He needed a moral language capable of reaching beyond the northern military elite. He and Empress Dugu were committed Buddhists. Northern Zhou had banned Buddhism in 574, and in 581 Wendi began restoring it. He presented himself as a cakravartin, an ideal Buddhist king who uses political power to protect the faith. He also restored Confucian court rituals, linking Sui rule to older imperial traditions.

In 601, Wendi distributed Buddha relics to temples across the empire in imitation of the Indian king Ashoka. His edict promised that “all the people within the four seas” might cultivate good karma and move toward enlightenment. The universal language mattered. A ruler who had killed rivals and a child emperor now represented himself as guardian of the spiritual welfare of everyone under heaven. Buddhism did not win the war of reunification; armies did that. Religion helped transform victory into a claim of rightful rule.

Act II interpretation

The Sui machine solved the problems of reunification. It placed local officials under the court, regularized law, counted households, moved grain, supplied armies, and gave the ruler a moral claim to obedience. Every tool of integration, however, could also become a tool of extraction.

Act III: Breaking the Empire

Yangdi Inherits a State That Can Do Almost Anything

Yang Guang became emperor when his father died in 604. As Yangdi, he inherited far more than a throne. He inherited a reunited realm, registered households, centrally appointed officials, developing canals, organized armies, and an imperial ideology that encouraged comparison with the great Han emperors. He also inherited confidence. The state had crossed the Yangzi, absorbed the south, built a capital, and organized millions of households. Why should any frontier remain beyond its reach?

Yangdi accelerated nearly every form of mobilization. He built Luoyang, expanded the canal network, repaired parts of the Great Wall, staged imperial tours, and launched distant campaigns. Some projects produced enduring benefits. The canal remained essential under the Tang. The problem was not that every project was foolish. It was that every project drew laborers, soldiers, grain, cloth, carts, animals, and time from the same communities.

Gamble One: Victory in the South, Death on the Road Home

Both Sui emperors regarded the empire’s frontiers as unfinished because they measured their realm against the lands once controlled by the Han. Wendi recovered the Hanoi region in 602. In 605, Yangdi’s general Liu Fang pushed farther south into Champa in central Vietnam. The campaign produced one of the period’s most vivid stories: Sui troops reportedly pretended to retreat and lured charging war elephants into hidden pits.

It was a battlefield victory and a strategic warning. Tropical disease devastated soldiers from the north. Liu Fang himself became ill and died on the return journey. The Sui had defeated the elephants but not distance, climate, disease, or the cost of maintaining an army far from its supply base.

Gamble Two: Goguryeo and the Salsu Catastrophe

The northeast proved still more dangerous. Goguryeo was a powerful Korean kingdom whose territory extended into southern Manchuria. It refused Sui demands for submission. Wendi’s invasion of 598 failed because of supply problems, disease, and storms. Yangdi prepared a much larger effort. The northern canal, enormous grain stockpiles, and levies of soldiers and carriers converged near modern Beijing in 611, even as floods struck Hebei.

In 612, Sui armies attacked Goguryeo’s fortresses in Liaodong but could not take them. A major force pushed toward Pyongyang, ran short of supplies, and retreated. At the Salsu River—today’s Chongchon—forces led by the Goguryeo general Ŭlchi Mundŏk shattered the exhausted army. Broken units, abandoned equipment, and hungry survivors struggled back toward Sui territory.

Source caution: numbers written by the victors

The Book of Sui claims 1,133,800 combat troops for the 612 invasion, with twice as many supply carriers. The Comprehensive Mirror says 305,000 men advanced toward Pyongyang and only 2,700 returned. David Graff estimates a smaller—but still enormous—force from the reported unit structure and argues that Tang historians probably inflated the totals. Treat 305,000 and 2,700 as traditional figures. The exact numbers are doubtful; the scale and devastation are not.

A ruler capable of learning from disaster might have stopped. Yangdi tried again. In 613 he launched another campaign, only to abandon it when the aristocrat Yang Xuangan rebelled inside China. In 614 he invaded once more. Goguryeo asked for peace, but the Sui gained no lasting control. Three campaigns in three years had consumed lives and supplies without producing territory the dynasty could hold.

The Bill Comes Due at Home

Return to the household entered in the register. A family might owe grain for state storehouses, cloth as a regular tax, labor for a canal or wall, transport service for a convoy, and military service from an adult son. Any one obligation might be survivable. Repeated demands were different. If a worker spent months away digging, fewer hands planted or harvested. If a soldier died in Liaodong, the loss was military, economic, and personal. If floods destroyed a crop after taxes and service had already exhausted the family’s reserve, flight or rebellion could become the only remaining choices.

In 611, Wang Bo led one of the first major revolts connected to the Korean draft, from Changbai Mountain in Shandong. A song traditionally attributed to his movement turns the state’s threat of execution into an argument for resistance:

Primary source: a rebel song

“When he hears the government troops are coming, / he grabs his sword and charges ahead. / If I would die in Liaodong anyway, / what harm is there in losing my head here?”

— Song attributed to Wang Bo, 611 · translation adapted from Quan Sui shi, ch. 8

The calculation is brutal. Obedience and rebellion both threaten death. Rebellion at least offers comrades, agency, and a chance to remain near home. Once subjects believed obedience was no safer than resistance, fear stopped protecting the dynasty.

The Empire Fractures

Revolt spread fastest through Shandong and southern Hebei, regions battered by floods, military drafts, and the movement of supplies. Bandit groups grew. Deserters joined them. Repression produced new enemies faster than it removed old ones. The resulting violence further disrupted agriculture, transport, and tax collection, making the state less able to respond to the crises it had helped create.

The collapse was not only a peasant uprising. Yang Xuangan, the rebel of 613, was an aristocrat and the son of the powerful minister Yang Su. By 617, governors and generals were raising their own armies. Li Yuan, the Sui governor at Taiyuan, allied with Turkish forces and captured Chang’an near the end of the year. He installed Yangdi’s grandson as a puppet while presenting himself as a restorer of order rather than an immediate destroyer of the dynasty.

Yangdi had withdrawn to Jiangdu in 616. Two years later his own officers killed him. The tragedy had reached its final scene: the state could still issue commands, but it could no longer secure the cooperation needed to carry them out.

Act III interpretation

The Sui did not fall from one defeat or one natural disaster. Repeated mobilization reduced the ability of households to survive shocks; failed wars made service appear pointless; rebellion spread from farmers and deserters to aristocrats, governors, and guards. The machine broke the society that powered it.

Epilogue: The Dynasty Died. Its State Survived.

Li Yuan Captures the Blueprint

Li Yuan was not an outsider sweeping away everything the Sui represented. He came from the same northwestern military aristocracy, had served as a Sui governor, and was connected to the Sui ruling house through marriage networks. After Yangdi’s death, he removed the puppet emperor in Chang’an and founded the Tang. The empire was not immediately secure—the Tang fought rival regimes into the mid-620s—but Li Yuan had captured both a capital and a blueprint for imperial rule.

Builder, Tyrant, or Both?

The surviving histories of the Sui were compiled under the Tang, a dynasty with every reason to portray its predecessor as unfit to rule. They made Yangdi the classic bad last emperor: extravagant, arrogant, cruel, and blind to suffering. Britannica describes much of this portrait as a product of a hostile record. Victor Cunrui Xiong’s biography emphasizes Yangdi’s administrative reforms, his successful government of the south, and the lasting value of his canals.

That correction should not become an acquittal. Tang historians shaped Yangdi’s image, but they did not invent the Korean disasters, forced labor, rebellion, or political collapse. The fairest judgment holds two ideas together. Yangdi was a capable administrator and ambitious builder whose projects strengthened later empires. He was also a ruler whose refusal to recognize limits inflicted enormous suffering and destroyed his dynasty.

The late Tang writer Pi Rixiu understood the irony. The people of the Sui could not bear the canal’s harm; the people of the Tang could not count its benefits. The dynasty died. Its machinery survived in the hands of rulers who had watched it break.

Closing Synthesis

The Sui story can be remembered through five anchor dates. In 581, Yang Jian seized the Northern Zhou throne. In 589, Sui armies crossed the Yangzi and reunited China. In 604, Yangdi inherited the empire and accelerated its mobilization. In 612, the Salsu disaster revealed the gap between imperial ambition and logistical reality. In 618, Yangdi died at Jiangdu and Li Yuan proclaimed the Tang.

The dates matter because they mark turns in a causal story, not because memorizing a list explains the dynasty. Act I ended division through force and careful political integration. Act II constructed institutions that made the reunited realm governable. Act III pushed those institutions beyond sustainable limits. Registration connected households to the state; it also exposed them to repeated demands. Canals moved grain efficiently; forced labor built them. Central appointments weakened local independence; governors still defected when the court lost authority. Armies projected power; failed invasions transformed conscripts into rebels.

Modern scholarship has shifted the explanation from morality alone toward institutions, logistics, and political cooperation. Arthur F. Wright recast the Sui as the founder of a new imperial order. Xiong separated Yangdi’s genuine accomplishments from the Tang caricature. Graff demonstrated how distance, transport, weather, and inflated source numbers complicate the story of the Korean wars. Mark Edward Lewis places Sui and Tang within a connected age of cosmopolitan empire.

The resulting judgment is more tragic than the old story of a foolish tyrant. The Sui created many of the conditions for Tang greatness. Its rulers proved that a reunified state could count households, appoint officials, move grain, and mobilize armies on a continental scale. They failed to recognize that state capacity is not the same as an unlimited human capacity to obey. Wendi built the machine. Yangdi drove it past the breaking point. The Tang inherited what survived.

Notes

  1. Yang Jian took the Northern Zhou throne in 581 by force and family position, not by blood. His daughter had married Emperor Xuan. In 579 Xuan abdicated in favor of his young son, Emperor Jing, Yang Jian’s step-grandson, and when Xuan died in 580, the child was left on the throne. The child held the name of emperor; Yang Jian held the government and the army. He made himself regent, defeated Zhou loyalists, and in the spring of 581 had Jing yield the throne. He then killed dozens of Zhou princes and later the boy emperor himself, so that a restoration of the old house would be nearly impossible. The Sui therefore began as a coup inside the northern elite. Its founder faced an immediate contradiction. He had used force to seize power, but he needed to present himself as the rightful ruler of a moral and universal empire. Legitimacy was not decoration. It was the machinery that turned a seizure into a dynasty.

    The first move was ritual. The transfer was staged as an abdication: the Zhou emperor “voluntarily” yielded the throne, in the pattern of the ancient Yao-to-Shun succession. That gave the coup a Confucian costume. It was presented as a succession, not a seizure. Yang Jian also restored Confucian court rituals last used by the Han, linking Sui rule to an older imperial tradition rather than to the Xianbei military house he had just displaced. Dynastic cosmology did the same work in another language. He claimed the fire virtue in the Five Phases cycle, positioning Sui as the legitimate successor to Zhou’s wood virtue. The coup was thereby written into cosmic order instead of against it. Even the name of the dynasty was a claim of permanence. His fief had been Sui, written 隨, a character whose “walking” radical suggested instability. He dropped the radical and ruled as 隋.

    The second move was patronage. Yang Jian did not purge the northwestern elite that had served the Zhou. He came from it. The Sui founders belonged to an intermarried aristocracy that had served northern regimes founded by the Xianbei. Yang Jian claimed Han ancestors, but his family had married into Xianbei clans for generations, and Empress Dugu came from the same mixed world. He kept those families close — former officials, marriage partners, men with armies — and turned potential rivals into stakeholders. The later transition to the Tang would look less like a new ruling class replacing an old one than like another branch of that same elite capturing institutions the Sui had built. Absorption, not extermination, was the political half of legitimacy.

    The third move was religion, and it was the one aimed beyond the northern military elite. Northern Zhou’s Emperor Wu had banned Buddhism and Daoism in 574, destroyed images and scriptures, and returned clergy to lay life, largely to seize monastery wealth and manpower for his wars. The ban was relatively bloodless and short. Yang Jian lifted it when he took power and, with Empress Dugu, began restoring Buddhism in 581. He presented himself as a cakravartin, an ideal Buddhist king who uses political power to protect the faith. In 601, on his sixtieth birthday, he sealed Buddha relics in jars and had delegations of monks carry them to shrines across the empire, to be enshrined simultaneously. The act imitated Ashoka, the Indian king who had also unified a realm and then distributed relics. Wendi’s edict promised that “all the people within the four seas” might cultivate good karma and move toward enlightenment. A man who had killed rivals and a child emperor now represented himself as guardian of everyone’s spiritual welfare. Buddhism did not win the war of reunification. Armies did that. Religion helped turn victory into a claim of rightful rule, and it sat beside the Confucian claim rather than replacing it.

    The fourth move was competence. Wendi ended the Nine Rank System’s local rectifiers, who had graded candidates by pedigree and produced the old verdict that there were no poor families in the upper ranks and no great families in the lower. Appointment of prefectural and county officials passed to the central Board of Civil Office. Officials were barred from serving in their home regions, terms were short, and in 583 an entire layer of local government, the commanderies, was abolished. The Kaihuang Code of the same year simplified and softened Northern Zhou law and became the model for the Tang Code. Three ministries, with boards beneath them, and a censorate to investigate official conduct gave the center a routine way to appoint, judge, and check. These reforms did not produce the drama of a river crossing. They signaled that a tax case, an appointment, or a criminal judgment would be handled by recognizable institutions rather than by the founder’s whim or a local magnate’s family. That was legitimacy through the capacity to govern.

    None of these claims was sufficient by itself. Ritual without an army would not have held the south. Buddhism without registers and canals would not have fed the capitals. The code without the abdication ceremony would have looked like the administration of a usurper. Together they answered the contradiction of 581. Force had made Yang Jian emperor. Ritual, cosmology, patronage, a Buddhist mandate, and a state that governed by rules made it possible for others to treat him as one. The Tang founders, drawn from the same northwestern world and watching the machine break under Yangdi, copied the playbook almost exactly: capture the capital, install a Sui puppet, then discard him and proclaim a new house while keeping the law, the offices, the registers, and the capital. ↩

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Arthur F. Wright, The Sui Dynasty: The Unification of China, A.D. 581–617 (1978). The standard English narrative of the dynasty. Wright treats the Sui as a creative founding era rather than a failed interlude and is especially good on Wendi’s government and use of Buddhism.

Denis Twitchett, ed., The Cambridge History of China, vol. 3, Sui and T’ang China, 589–906, Part I (1979). Contains Wright’s chapter on the Sui and detailed chapters on the early Tang. The best single reference for dates, institutions, and the Sui–Tang transition.

Victor Cunrui Xiong, Emperor Yang of the Sui Dynasty: His Life, Times, and Legacy (2006). A full reassessment of Yangdi that separates his reforms and building projects from the Tang caricature. Essential for understanding why “builder” and “tyrant” need not be mutually exclusive.

David A. Graff, Medieval Chinese Warfare, 300–900 (2002). Chapters on the north–south wars and Goguryeo campaigns explain how the Sui crossed the Yangzi and why its Korean wars collapsed. Graff’s critique of the 612 troop figures is a model for handling inflated numbers.

Mark Edward Lewis, China’s Cosmopolitan Empire: The Tang Dynasty (2009). A thematic history of the Sui–Tang era. Useful for connecting the Sui canal, capital, and registers to the Tang world of the next four lectures.

Patricia Buckley Ebrey, The Cambridge Illustrated History of China, 2nd ed. (2010). A concise, well-illustrated survey by a leading social historian. Helpful for placing the Sui within the longer pattern of division and reunification.

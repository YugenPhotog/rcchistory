The Sui Reunited China. Why Did It Fall So Fast?

Conquest, integration, and overmobilization, 581–618

Essential question

How could a dynasty reunite China, build a stronger state, and still fall within a single generation?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain how the Sui reunified China in 589 through military force, careful planning, and a deliberate effort to win over the conquered south.
  2. Identify the Sui institutions the Tang inherited: the law code, the three central ministries, court control of local appointments, household registers, the capital, and the Grand Canal.
  3. Analyze how the same tools that held the empire together (registers, canals, and a central court) also made it possible to demand too much from ordinary households.
  4. Evaluate the Goguryeo wars and the revolts after 611 as evidence of overmobilization, not simply of one emperor's bad character.
  5. Distinguish what the surviving sources report from what historians now accept, especially on troop numbers, the examination system, and the reputation of Yangdi.

Introductory Framing

For more than three and a half centuries after the fall of the Han in 220, no single court ruled all of China. Then, in about a decade, a northern official named Yang Jian took the throne (581), crossed the Yangzi River, and destroyed the last southern dynasty, the Chen (589). His Sui dynasty rebuilt a capital, issued a new law code, took control of local appointments, counted millions of households, and began a canal network that tied the rich south to the northern capitals. Yet the Sui lasted only thirty-seven years. By 618 its second emperor had been murdered by his own officers, and a Sui governor named Li Yuan had founded the Tang.

The central problem of the lecture is therefore not simply why the Sui failed. It is why a dynasty could succeed and fail at the same time. Its conquest held. Many of its institutions lasted for centuries because the Tang kept them. What failed was the Sui's ability to keep asking more of the same people. The lecture organizes this story under three headings: conquest (ending rival courts), integration (linking officials, grain, households, and religion to one court), and overreach (repeated building projects and wars that pressed the same households again and again). The key new idea is overmobilization. To mobilize is to draft soldiers and workers and move food and equipment. Overmobilization means demanding more of those people and supplies than communities can spare.

Modern historians have pushed back against the traditional picture of the Sui as a short, cruel interruption. Arthur F. Wright's history of the dynasty and his chapter in The Cambridge History of China treated the Sui as the builder of a new imperial order. Victor Cunrui Xiong's biography of Yangdi argues that the second emperor's reforms helped lay the foundation for the Tang, even though his strategic mistakes were fatal. David A. Graff's military history shows how distance, supply, and weather shaped the Korean wars. Mark Edward Lewis treats the Sui and Tang as one "cosmopolitan empire." Together this work asks students to hold two facts at once: the Sui was a remarkable state-builder, and its state-building made its collapse possible.

I. Reunification Grew Out of a Century of Northern Warfare

The Northern Background

The Sui did not appear out of nowhere. From 523, civil war tore apart the regimes that ruled north China. In 577 the Northern Zhou defeated its rival, the Northern Qi, and reunited the north. That victory gave the Zhou court a large, battle-tested army and removed the main threat on its eastern flank. Only twelve years separate the Zhou conquest of the Qi (577) from the Sui conquest of the Chen (589). Reunification was the result of decades of northern change, not a sudden Sui invention.

Yang Jian was an insider at the Zhou court. His daughter was empress to the Zhou emperor Xuan, and when Xuan died in 580 the throne passed to a young boy, Emperor Jing, Yang Jian's step-grandson. Yang Jian made himself regent, defeated rivals and Zhou loyalists who rose against him in 580, and took the throne in 581. He then had dozens of Zhou princes killed, and later the boy emperor as well, so that the old ruling house could not return. The Sui began as a transfer of power inside the northern ruling elite, not as a popular movement.

Two naming conventions trip up students and can trip up lecturers too. "Wendi" ("Cultured Emperor") is a posthumous title, given after Yang Jian's death in 604. While he ruled, years were counted by his reign names, Kaihuang (581–600) and Renshou (601–604). His son is known as Yangdi, another posthumous title, and one chosen by his enemies.

A Mixed Northern Ruling World

The Sui founders came from an intermarried northwestern aristocracy that had served a series of northern regimes founded by the Xianbei, peoples from the northern grasslands whose leaders ruled much of north China before the Sui. Yang Jian claimed Han Chinese ancestors, but his family had married into Xianbei clans for generations, and his wife, Empress Dugu, came from a family with both Chinese and non-Chinese connections. Britannica's account of Wendi notes that the culture of such families "was as mixed as their descent." It is misleading, then, to picture the Sui as "Chinese" rulers replacing "foreign" ones. The Tang ruling family came from the same world.

Crossing the Yangzi and Holding the South

The Yangzi River was the south's great defensive barrier. Northern armies were strongest on land, and southern courts had long relied on the river to stop them. The Sui answered with ships. By late 588 Sui forces and fleets were positioned along the river from Sichuan to the sea, and in 589 they took the Chen capital, Jiankang (modern Nanjing). The Sui razed the city and moved Chen nobles and officials north, breaking up the southern political class that might have rebuilt a rival court.

Conquest was only half the job. In late 590, rumors that southerners would be moved north helped set off a large revolt, which the general Yang Su crushed. The Sui then added a softer approach. Wendi exempted the former Chen lands from taxes for ten years, and his son Yang Guang, the future Yangdi, governed the south as viceroy from 591 to 600. Married to a princess of the southern Liang royal house, Yang Guang ruled fairly mildly and drew southern elites into the empire. Britannica credits the south's successful integration largely to his administration. This matters for the rest of the lecture: the man later remembered as a tyrant was also the Sui's most successful manager of the south.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Reunification combined inherited northern military power, a river campaign built on ships, and a deliberate mix of punishment and reward in the south. Conquest ended division, but only integration could make unity last.

II. Integration: Appointments, Law, Registers, and Religion

Taking Appointments Away from Local Families

Before the Sui, the Nine Rank System let local elites rank candidates for office, so powerful families largely decided who held government posts. Wendi ended that system. The court now appointed prefecture and county officials itself, and in 583 it removed a whole layer of local government, the commanderies, so that counties answered directly to prefectures (a prefecture is a local district). Officials were barred from serving in their home regions. From 587, each prefecture sent three candidates a year to the capital, where written tests helped decide who would serve. Britannica calls this local-government reform even more important than the new central offices, because it made local officials "agents of the central government."

At the center, the Sui built a government of three central ministries with boards beneath them, plus a censorate that watched officials' conduct. The Kaihuang Code of 583 simplified and softened the harsher laws of the Northern Zhou. The code itself has not survived, but it was the model for the Tang Code, the most influential body of law in East Asian history.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Do not say the Sui "invented" the civil service examinations. The Sui began the shift toward written tests; Yangdi added the "presented scholar" (jinshi) examination around 605–607, though the exact date is debated. Even under the Tang, examinations supplied a small share of officials; Britannica estimates no more than about 10 percent even in the late Tang. The mature exam system took centuries to develop.

Counting Households

The Sui "saw" its people by writing them down. The official household register of 609, preserved in the monograph on geography in the Book of Sui (Suishu, ch. 29), records 8,907,546 households and 46,019,956 people. These are registered figures, not a full count. People who fled, hid, or were never recorded do not appear, and registration in the old Chen territories was probably incomplete. Tang officials later believed that only about 70 percent of the population was on the rolls.

The registers mattered because the state's other systems depended on them. Under the equal-field system, the state assigned farmland to households in return for taxes and labor. Under the fubing system, farmers served as soldiers in turns, in exchange for land. Grain and cloth taxes paid for the government. The Sui kept modified versions of these systems from earlier northern dynasties, and the Tang kept them again. A register is a tool of integration: it lets a court count people, land, taxes, and soldiers. The same tool also made it possible to draft those people again and again.

Religion and the Right to Rule

Wendi had seized the throne by force, so he needed ways to show that he deserved it. He and Empress Dugu were committed Buddhists. The Northern Zhou had banned Buddhism in 574, and in 581, the year he founded the Sui, Wendi began restoring it. He presented himself as a cakravartin, an ideal Buddhist king who uses political power to protect the faith. In 601 he sent Buddha relics to temples across the empire, copying the Indian king Ashoka. His ministers also revived Confucian state rituals to link the dynasty with the Han. Religion supported Sui rule and reached every part of the reunited realm, but the order of events matters: Buddhism helped legitimate the dynasty; the armies won the reunification.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Sui turned conquest into a working empire by tying local government to the court, simplifying law, counting households, and giving the new ruler a religious claim to power. These were real achievements, and the Tang kept most of them. They also gave the court unusual reach into ordinary households.

III. The Grand Canal as Achievement and Burden

Why the Capitals Needed the South

The Sui capitals sat in the northwest. Wendi built a new capital, Daxing, near the old Han capital in the 580s; under the Tang it would be called Chang'an. Yangdi added a second great capital at Luoyang from 605. But China's richest farmland increasingly lay far to the southeast, around the Yangzi. Moving heavy loads of grain by water was far cheaper than hauling them overland, so a canal system that linked the Yellow River and Yangzi River systems would feed the capitals and armies and make a unified empire easier to govern.

The canal was built in stages, and it largely linked and reused older rivers and canals. In 584 Wendi opened the Guangtong Canal from the capital to the Yellow River, following an old Han-era route; in 587 he reopened the Shanyang Canal between the Huai and the Yangzi. Most of the system, however, was dug under Yangdi between 605 and 610. The Tongji Canal linked Luoyang to the Huai River (605). The Yongji Canal ran north toward Zhuo commandery near modern Beijing (608). The Jiangnan Canal was rebuilt south of the Yangzi to Yuhang, modern Hangzhou (610). By 611, Britannica notes, the eastern plain had a trunk route from the Yangzi delta to the northern frontier.

Forced Labor and Mixed Memory

The canals were built with drafted labor. The Book of Sui (ch. 3) reports that more than a million men and women were drafted for the Tongji Canal in 605, and again for the Yongji Canal in 608. Those totals come from histories written after the dynasty fell and may be rounded upward, but no one doubts that the burden was enormous or that it fell on the same registered households that paid taxes and supplied soldiers. The northern canal was built with war in mind: it carried troops and grain toward the Korean frontier.

Primary Evidence: A Late Tang Verdict on the Canal

Primary source

“The Sui dredged the Qi and the Bian and cut through the Taihang: the people of the Sui could not bear the harm, and the people of the Tang cannot count the benefits. … Was it not that Heaven lent us the cruel Sui to bring about our great benefit?”

— Pi Rixiu, “Inscription on the Bian River” (Bianhe ming), late ninth century · translation adapted; Chinese text in Quan Tang wen, ch. 797

Pi Rixiu wrote more than two centuries later, when Tang grain fleets still used the Sui routes. His verdict holds both sides of the lecture's problem in one sentence: the canal was a crushing burden for the people who dug it and a lasting gift to the dynasty that followed. Notice, too, his word "cruel." Even an admirer of the canal took for granted the Tang judgment that the Sui was harsh. His inscription ends by warning his own rulers not to launch similar projects, so the passage is praise and caution at once.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Grand Canal is the clearest example of the Sui paradox. It was a tool of integration that fed capitals and armies for centuries, and it was a tool of overreach that drafted hundreds of thousands, perhaps millions, of workers in a few years. Whether it counts as success or failure depends on whose lifetime you measure.

IV. Goguryeo and the Arithmetic of Overmobilization

An Unfinished Empire

Both Sui emperors treated the empire's frontiers as unfinished. They measured their realm against the lands once held by the Han, which had included parts of modern Vietnam and Korea. In the south, Wendi recovered the Hanoi region in 602, and in 605 Yangdi's general Liu Fang pushed into Champa in central Vietnam. The Sui troops beat Champa's war elephants by luring them into hidden pits, but disease ravaged the army and Liu Fang died on the way home. Winning a battle was not the same as winning a war the state could afford.

The northeast was worse. Goguryeo, a strong Korean kingdom whose lands reached into southern Manchuria, refused to accept Sui overlordship. Wendi's attack in 598 failed because of supply problems and storms. Yangdi prepared a far larger effort: the northern canal, stockpiles of grain, and a vast levy of soldiers and carriers gathered near modern Beijing in 611, when terrible floods in Hebei delayed the campaign.

The Campaigns of 612–614

In 612 the Sui armies besieged the fortresses of Liaodong but could not take them. A force sent on toward the Goguryeo capital at Pyongyang ran short of food, turned back, and was destroyed by the Goguryeo general Ŭlchi Mundŏk at the Salsu River (today's Chongchon). In 613 Yangdi launched a new campaign but had to call it off when the aristocrat Yang Xuangan, son of the powerful minister Yang Su, rebelled inside China. In 614 Goguryeo sued for peace, but the Sui gained no lasting control. Three costly campaigns in three years had produced nothing that could be held.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Present the famous numbers as traditional figures, not facts. The Book of Sui (ch. 4) says the 612 army numbered 1,133,800 combat troops, "called two million," with twice as many supply carriers; the Comprehensive Mirror (ch. 181) says 305,000 men crossed the Liao and only 2,700 returned. David Graff estimates about 600,000 combat troops from the army's reported unit structure (roughly 670,000 with the fleet), notes that the official total would mean an implausibly large share of the registered population, and suggests the figures may have been inflated by Tang historians. The losses were clearly enormous; the exact totals are not reliable.

Primary Evidence: A Rebel Song

Primary source

“Before Changbai Mountain stands the Man Who Knows the World, / dressed in red silk and a padded vest. … / When he hears the government troops are coming, / he grabs his sword and charges ahead. / If I would die in Liaodong anyway, / what harm is there in losing my head here?”

— “Don’t Go to Liaodong to Die for Nothing,” song attributed to the rebel Wang Bo, 611 · translation adapted; Chinese text in Quan Sui shi, ch. 8

In 611 Wang Bo led one of the first revolts against the Korean draft, from Changbai Mountain in Shandong (not the mountain of the same name on today's Korean border). The song is traditionally credited to him, but authorship cannot be proven. Its argument is what matters. The first lines turn the rebel into a hero; the last two make a cold calculation. If the draft is a death sentence, rebellion costs nothing extra. The song shows overmobilization from below: when obedience looked as deadly as revolt, the state's threats lost their force.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Korean wars did not fail only on the battlefield. They failed in the villages that supplied the soldiers, carriers, grain, and carts. A distant campaign became a domestic crisis because the same registered households bore every demand at once.

V. Collapse, the Hostile Record, and the Sui Inheritance

From Strain to Revolt

Revolts spread fastest in Shandong and southern Hebei, the regions hit hardest by floods, the draft, and the transport of supplies. Bandit groups grew, deserters joined them, and harsh repression drove more people into rebellion. The collapse was not only a peasant uprising. The 613 rebel Yang Xuangan was an aristocrat, and by 617 Sui governors and generals were acting for themselves. In 615 the eastern Turks besieged Yangdi at Yanmen on the northern frontier for about a month. In 616 he withdrew to his southern capital at Jiangdu (Yangzhou).

In 617 Li Yuan, the Sui governor at Taiyuan, raised an army with Turkish help and took Chang'an at the end of the year, placing Yangdi's grandson on the throne as a puppet. In the spring of 618 Yangdi was murdered at Jiangdu by officers led by Yuwen Huaji. That summer Li Yuan set aside the puppet and declared the Tang dynasty. Even then the Tang was only one contender among several; the empire was not fully pacified until the mid-620s.

Builder or Tyrant?

The histories of the Sui were written under the Tang, a dynasty with every reason to make its predecessor look unfit to rule. They made Yangdi the classic "bad last emperor": extravagant, arrogant, and cruel. Britannica calls this figure "mythical" and "to a large extent the product of the hostile record." Xiong's biography goes further, stressing Yangdi's administrative reforms, his success in the south, and his canal network. Neither view denies the suffering. The fair conclusion is that Yangdi's projects and wars were real and devastating, and that the moral portrait of him in the sources was shaped by the people who replaced him.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Sui fell because it lost the cooperation of the farmers, soldiers, and elites it depended on, not only because of one emperor's personality. What it built survived, and the Tang, a family from the same northwestern elite, inherited both the institutions and the lesson.

Closing Synthesis

The Sui story moves in three steps. Conquest ended political division when a northern state adapted its power to the Yangzi and took Jiankang in 589. Integration made that unity work: the court took control of appointments, simplified the law, counted households for land, taxes, and soldiers, dug canals to move southern grain, and wrapped the new ruler in Buddhist and Confucian authority. Then came overmobilization. Yangdi's capital, canals, wall-building, and wars against Goguryeo drafted more people and goods than communities could spare, and when floods and repression followed, revolts spread and elites defected.

The central historiographical shift is from moral judgment to structural explanation. Traditional history, written under the Tang, blamed a wicked last emperor. Wright recast the Sui as a founder of institutions; Xiong rehabilitated much of Yangdi's record without excusing his failures; Graff showed how logistics and distance defeated the Korean campaigns and why the famous troop numbers deserve suspicion; and Lewis places the Sui at the start of a single Sui–Tang era. This scholarship does not make the Sui less harsh. It explains why a strong state could be an unstable one.

For the lecture, the most important distinction is between the state's capacity and its limits. The tools that made the Sui strong were the same tools that let it demand too much. The question worth leaving with students is whether any ruler could have used such tools to finish the conquest of the frontier without exhausting the households that paid for it, and what the Tang would have to do differently to avoid the same fate.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Arthur F. Wright, The Sui Dynasty: The Unification of China, A.D. 581–617 (1978). The standard English narrative of the dynasty. Wright treats the Sui as a creative founding era rather than a failed interlude, and he is especially good on Wendi's government and use of Buddhism.

Denis Twitchett, ed., The Cambridge History of China, vol. 3, Sui and T'ang China, 589–906, Part I (1979). Contains Wright's chapter on the Sui and detailed chapters on the early Tang. The best single reference for dates, institutions, and the Sui–Tang transition.

Victor Cunrui Xiong, Emperor Yang of the Sui Dynasty: His Life, Times, and Legacy (2006). A full reassessment of Yangdi that separates his reforms and building projects from the Tang caricature. Essential for the "builder or tyrant?" question.

David A. Graff, Medieval Chinese Warfare, 300–900 (2002). Chapters on the north–south wars and on the Goguryeo campaigns explain how the Sui crossed the Yangzi and why its Korean wars collapsed. Graff's critique of the 612 troop figures is the model for handling inflated numbers.

Mark Edward Lewis, China's Cosmopolitan Empire: The Tang Dynasty (2009). A thematic history of the Sui–Tang era. Useful for connecting the Sui canal, capital, and registers to the Tang world of the next four lectures.

Patricia Buckley Ebrey, The Cambridge Illustrated History of China, 2nd ed. (2010). A concise, well-illustrated survey by a leading social historian. Helpful for placing the Sui within the longer pattern of division and reunification.

Audit disposition. Chapter 5 had no existing background essays for HIST 270, so this audit covers a newly drafted foundation rather than a retroactive check of older work. The five-lecture roadmap carried by the Chapter 5 decks passes: it follows the Sui–Tang arc from reunification to the late Tang settlement and turns chronology into five manageable problems. All five essays pass and are locked for lecture preparation. The audit found several date and number problems in the decks and in the textbook material they cite; those are recorded in Section 8 for later revision and were not changed here.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. The textbook is the course baseline, not its ceiling, and essays must be checked against scholarly and primary sources rather than against the textbook alone. Dates, names, numbers, and quotations were verified against reference works written by specialists, standard monographs, and the Chinese texts of the dynastic histories and literary collections. Quotations appear only where the wording could be sourced, either from a named published translation or as a translation adapted from an identified Chinese text. Popular myths that the chapter invites are named and corrected in the essays rather than silently avoided.

The five Chapter 5 lecture decks were read in full to establish each lecture’s guiding question, content, and key terms. The essays were then drafted to prepare the instructor for those lectures, not to replace them. No deck was changed.

Audience: first-year students at a community college. The essays therefore keep plain language, define necessary terms, and avoid specialist vocabulary, while holding the same analytical standard as the rest of the background-essay library.

2. Textbook baseline

The decks cite the course textbook’s Chapter 5 (pp. 77–91) throughout, and that chapter supplies the chronological spine: Sui reunification in 581–589 and the canals; Yangdi’s Goguryeo wars and the Sui collapse; the Tang founding, Taizong, and early Tang institutions; Chang’an, foreign contacts, and the great families; Buddhism, Empress Wu, and Xuanzong’s court; An Lushan’s rebellion, provincial autonomy, the two-tax system and salt monopoly, Han Yu, Dunhuang, and the late Tang collapse into the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms.

Lecture therefore should not retell the dynasty in sequence. Its value lies in five analytical problems: why a successful reunification collapsed so quickly; how a new dynasty turned inherited institutions into lasting power; who could enter the open, cosmopolitan world of Chang’an and its elite; what religion and culture could and could not do for rulers; and what the Tang paid to survive 755. Those problems supply causation, contingency, evidence, and institutional change that a survey chapter necessarily compresses.

Several textbook statements, as quoted in the decks, need caution. The date of the Mount Tai sacrifices (the rites took place in the first month of 666, not 665), the date of the Great Cloud commentary (presented in 690), the place of Suzong’s enthronement (Lingwu, in the northwest), the size and fate of the Dunhuang collection, and the share of revenue from salt are all addressed in the essays and listed in Section 8. Popular narratives the chapter can reinforce, especially the Yang Guifei blame story and census drops read as death tolls, are corrected in the essays.

Textbook baseline: course textbook, Chapter 5, as cited in the lecture decks. The textbook PDF itself was not available for this audit, so the baseline is reconstructed from the decks’ citations and quoted material.

3. Inventory and authority determination

The Chapter 5 folder contains five lecture decks and no separate planning document. The decks’ guiding questions, lecture boundaries, and key-term lists together serve as the roadmap equivalent. Lecture 1 exists in a corrected version whose dates and numbers set the standard for the rest of the chapter: the Sui founded in 581 and conquered the Chen in 589; the 612 disaster at the Salsu River is distinguished from the 613 campaign halted by Yang Xuangan’s revolt; the canals reached the Beijing area in 608 and Hangzhou in 610, mostly dug under Yangdi between 605 and 610 by reusing older waterways; the register of 609 lists about 8.9 million households and 46 million people; Wendi is a posthumous title; Yangdi was murdered at Jiangdu in 618; and Li Yuan took Chang’an in 617. The essays follow those corrections.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

Sui reunification, the canals, and overmobilization

Lecture 1 is strongest when framed as a problem of overmobilization rather than a moral tale about a wicked second emperor. Arthur Wright’s The Sui Dynasty and Victor Cunrui Xiong’s Emperor Yang of the Sui Dynasty show that the hostile image of Yangdi was shaped by early Tang historians, while the burdens of canal work and repeated Goguryeo campaigns were real. David Graff’s estimates of the 612 army, roughly 600,000 or more rather than the traditional 1,133,800, give students a model of how to read inflated troop numbers. The Sui did not invent the examinations from nothing; it recruited some officials by examination, but birth and recommendation still dominated.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “The Sui dynasty”.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “Foreign affairs under Yangdi”.

Revisionist biography: Victor Cunrui Xiong, Emperor Yang of the Sui Dynasty.

Military analysis: David A. Graff, Medieval Chinese Warfare, 300–900.

Primary source: Book of Sui (Sui shu), ch. 29, geography treatise with the 609 register.

Primary source: Comprehensive Mirror (Zizhi tongjian), ch. 181, Salsu River losses.

Early Tang institutions and frontier strategy

Lecture 2 correctly treats the early Tang as a dynasty that inherited Sui institutions and made them last. Recent scholarship emphasizes how Taizong shaped the record of his own 626 coup, and how the “Heavenly Khan” title reflected a genuinely steppe-facing empire. Jonathan Skaff’s work on Sui–Tang relations with Turko-Mongol neighbors makes the frontier a shared political world rather than a wall. The law code, the equal-field system, and the fubing militia should be taught as ideals with uneven practice.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “Administration of the state”.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “The era of good government”.

Frontier interpretation: Jonathan Karam Skaff, Sui-Tang China and Its Turko-Mongol Neighbors.

Primary source: Old Tang History (Jiu Tang shu), ch. 71, biography of Wei Zheng.

Chang’an, Eurasian connections, and the great families

Lecture 3 should keep openness and hierarchy together. Mark Edward Lewis’s synthesis supplies the planned city, wards, markets, and foreign communities. Nicolas Tackett’s study of tomb inscriptions shows that a capital-based elite of great families kept its hold on office until the late ninth century, which qualifies any story of examinations opening government to talent. Taizong’s genealogy of 632–638 is the best single document for showing the throne trying, and partly failing, to rank the old families.

Established synthesis: Mark Edward Lewis, China’s Cosmopolitan Empire: The Tang Dynasty.

Elite history: Nicolas Tackett, The Destruction of the Medieval Chinese Aristocracy.

Primary source: Old Tang History (Jiu Tang shu), ch. 65, biography of Gao Shilian and the genealogy.

Religion, Empress Wu, and Xuanzong

Lecture 4 benefits most from recent work on Wu Zhao. N. Harry Rothschild’s studies, building on Antonino Forte’s analysis of the Great Cloud commentary, replace the usurper caricature with a ruler who assembled legitimacy from Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian traditions. Stephen Teiser’s Ghost Festival explains how Buddhism joined Chinese family duty. For the suppression of 845, the Old Tang History preserves the official figures, which show the fiscal motive clearly and include at least one number that cannot be taken literally.

Recent interpretation: N. Harry Rothschild, Emperor Wu Zhao and Her Pantheon.

Religious history: Stephen F. Teiser, The Ghost Festival in Medieval China.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “Rise of the empress Wuhou”.

Primary source: Old Tang History (Jiu Tang shu), ch. 18A, annals of Wuzong.

An Lushan’s rebellion and the late Tang settlement

Lecture 5 is strongest when it treats the rebellion as a structural turning point rather than a story of blame. Pulleyblank’s classic study and Graff’s military history explain how professional frontier armies concentrated power; Twitchett’s financial history explains the two-tax system and the salt monopoly. The census drop between about 755 (roughly 52.9 million people) and 764 (about 16.9 million) measures a collapse of registration, not a death toll. Du Fu’s poems and Han Yu’s memorial of 819 give the lecture two voices from inside the changed world; the Dunhuang Library Cave provides a view from outside the court.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “Provincial separatism”.

Reference synthesis: Britannica, “An Lushan”.

Poetry in translation: Stephen Owen, trans., The Poetry of Du Fu (open access).

Primary source: Han Yu, “Memorial on the Bones of the Buddha,” trans. Geoff Humble.

Archive: International Dunhuang Programme, “What was stored in Cave 17?”.

5. Five-lecture roadmap audit

Verdict: PASS

The roadmap complements rather than duplicates the textbook. Its arc is coherent and cumulative: a reunification that overreached; a successor dynasty that made inherited institutions last; a capital and elite that were open and hierarchical at once; religion and culture as tools and limits of authority; and a rebellion that forced the state to trade direct rule for survival. Each question suits first-year students because it turns chronology into a concrete problem of people, institutions, and choices.

Each lecture has a workable narrative engine. Lecture 1 can move from the canals to the army lost at the Salsu River and the murder at Jiangdu. Lecture 2 can open with the Xuanwu Gate coup and follow Taizong’s effort to shape how it would be remembered. Lecture 3 can walk students through the gates and wards of Chang’an and then show Taizong’s genealogy placing his own family first. Lecture 4 can begin in 690 with Wu’s new dynasty and its Buddhist prophecy. Lecture 5 can begin with the march south in 755 and Du Fu trapped in the occupied capital. Causation, context, comparison, evidence, and consequence recur naturally without a quota. No roadmap adjustment is warranted.

6. Individual background essay audits

Essay 1 — The Sui Reunited China. Why Did It Fall So Fast?

Verdict: PASS — NEWLY DRAFTED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: follows every Lecture 1 correction, separates 612 from 613, dates the canal work and its reuse of older waterways, and cites the 609 register from the Book of Sui. Historiography: Wright, Xiong, Graff, and Lewis give both the hostile-record critique and the structural account of overmobilization. Primary sources: Pi Rixiu’s “Inscription on the Bian Canal” and the 611 song against dying in Liaodong show later judgment and contemporary protest. Balance and context: Yangdi’s achievements and the costs of his projects are held together; the examination myth and inflated troop numbers are corrected. Narrative engine: a canal that served the Tang for centuries, and an army that never came home. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Consequence, Evidence, and Context. Substantive change: none.

Primary source: Pi Rixiu, “Bian he ming”.

Essay 2 — How Did a New Dynasty Make Borrowed Power Last?

Verdict: PASS — NEWLY DRAFTED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: distinguishes the 624 law code from the 653 code with commentary, dates Taizong’s Goguryeo invasion to 645, and places the conquests of 668 and 676 under Gaozong. Historiography: Twitchett, Wechsler, and Skaff support an account of borrowed institutions and a steppe-facing empire. Primary sources: Wei Zheng’s “three mirrors” passage shows how the court presented good government and remonstrance. Balance and context: equal-field and fubing ideals are separated from practice; the coup’s memory is treated as a problem of evidence. Narrative engine: a son kills his brothers and then works to control the record. Natural Five Moves: Context, Evidence, Cause, and Consequence. Substantive change: none.

Primary source: Old Tang History, ch. 71.

Essay 3 — Chang’an Was Open to the World. Who Could Get In?

Verdict: PASS — NEWLY DRAFTED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: ward plan, curfew, post stations, foreign communities, and the Nestorian stele of 781 are responsibly stated; population figures are marked as estimates. Historiography: Lewis, Schafer, and Tackett support a picture of openness joined to hierarchy. Primary sources: Taizong’s complaint about the Cui and the 632–638 genealogy show the throne ranking families. Balance and context: “cosmopolitan” is not equated with tolerance or equality; examinations are placed in proportion. Narrative engine: a visitor entering the grid city, and an emperor rewriting the list of great clans. Natural Five Moves: Comparison, Context, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Primary source: Old Tang History, ch. 65.

Essay 4 — What Could Religion and Culture Do for a Ruler?

Verdict: PASS — NEWLY DRAFTED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: dates the Mount Tai rites to 666, the Great Cloud commentary to 689–690 with presentation in 690, and Wu’s abdication to 705 at about eighty; the 845 figures are quoted from the Old Tang History with a caution on the land figure. Historiography: Weinstein, Teiser, Forte, Rothschild, and Owen support a legitimacy-centered account. Primary sources: the 845 record exposes the fiscal logic of suppression. Balance and context: Wu’s documented purges are kept alongside documented governance; the infant-daughter story is flagged as hostile tradition. Narrative engine: a woman proclaims a new dynasty with a Buddhist prophecy in hand. Natural Five Moves: Context, Evidence, Comparison, and Consequence. Substantive change: none.

Primary source: Old Tang History, ch. 18A.

Essay 5 — The Tang Survived An Lushan. What Did Survival Cost?

Verdict: PASS — NEWLY DRAFTED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: places Suzong’s enthronement at Lingwu, treats the salt monopoly as “a major part” of central revenue by about 780, describes the Dunhuang collection as tens of thousands of items dispersed to several countries, and treats Arab figures for Guangzhou as inflated. Historiography: Pulleyblank, Graff, Twitchett, Tackett, Hartman, and Rong support a structural account. Primary sources: Du Fu’s “Spring View” and Han Yu’s memorial give an inside view of war and of the Confucian response. Balance and context: the Yang Guifei blame story and the census-drop death toll are corrected. Narrative engine: a veteran army marches south and a poet watches spring come to a broken capital. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Consequence, Evidence, and Context. Substantive change: none.

Primary source: Du Fu, “Chun wang”.

7. Primary-source and narrative shortlist

Lecture 1. Pair the 611 song against dying in Liaodong with the later Tang poet Pi Rixiu’s verdict on the Bian Canal. Students can compare a contemporary protest with a hindsight judgment that credits the canal while condemning its builder.

Source: Song against dying in Liaodong (Wu xiang Liaodong lang si ge).

Source: Pi Rixiu, Inscription on the Bian Canal (Bian he ming).

Lecture 2. Use Wei Zheng’s “three mirrors” passage to show how the court presented Taizong as a ruler who accepted criticism, then ask what such a record was meant to accomplish after the 626 coup.

Source: Old Tang History, ch. 71.

Lecture 3. Let Taizong’s objection to the Cui family’s top ranking carry the lecture. It reveals the prestige of the old families, the throne’s ambitions, and the limits of imperial power in one exchange.

Source: Old Tang History, ch. 65.

Lecture 4. The 845 record turns religious policy into numbers students can read critically, including one figure that cannot be literally true.

Source: Old Tang History, ch. 18A.

Lecture 5. The strongest chapter-wide narrative opportunity is Du Fu in occupied Chang’an in the spring of 757. Han Yu’s memorial of 819 then shows an educated official redefining Chinese identity against Buddhism in the changed world after the rebellion.

Source: Du Fu, Spring View (Chun wang).

Source: Han Yu, Memorial on the Bones of the Buddha, trans. Geoff Humble.

8. Downstream issues to remember — do not implement in this audit

Lecture 2 deck. Taizong’s invasion of Goguryeo began in 645, not 644 (the decision and preparations date to 644). The title Taizong accepted in 630 is usually rendered “Heavenly Khan” (Tian Kehan), not “Great Khan.” Several slides lack presenter notes.

Lecture 3 deck. The ward count appears as 108; some reconstructions count about 110, so present the number as approximate. The population figure for Chang’an is an estimate and should be labeled as one.

Lecture 4 deck. The Mount Tai rites took place in the first month of 666; the court traveled in 665. The Great Cloud commentary should be dated 689–690 and described as presented in 690. A presenter note says Wu retained power “into her seventies,” while the slide says “into her eighties”; she abdicated in 705 at about eighty, so the slide is right and the note should change. The statement that Du Fu abandoned hopes for office after the rebellion needs nuance: he held brief minor posts from 757 and left service in 759.

Lecture 5 deck. Suzong took the throne at Lingwu in the northwest, not in western Shaanxi. The Dunhuang figures (about 13,500 scrolls, “most” sold) understate the collection, which numbers in the tens of thousands of items, and oversimplify its dispersal to London, Paris, Beijing, St. Petersburg, and Japan. The claim that salt provided more than half of central revenue by 779 is stronger than the reference literature, which describes a major part by about 780. Figures of 120,000 or more foreigners killed at Guangzhou in 879 come from Arab accounts and should be marked as inflated. Any wording that makes Yang Guifei the cause of the rebellion should be reframed around frontier armies and court factions.

Exam materials. Avoid questions implying that the Sui invented the examinations, that the 612 army numbered over a million, that Wu Zetian’s alleged crimes are established fact, that Yang Guifei caused the rebellion, or that the 764 census drop measures deaths. Assess causation, institutional change, and the reading of biased or inflated sources.

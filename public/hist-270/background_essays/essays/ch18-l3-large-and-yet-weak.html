Large and Yet Weak: The Second Opium War and Self-Strengthening

Background for the lecture "Large and Yet Weak": the Arrow War, the burning of the Yuanmingyuan, Feng Guifen's question, and the self-strengthening projects · 1856–1893

Central interpretation

The Second Opium War (1856–1860) finished what the first had begun. Britain and France forced the Qing to accept resident envoys in Beijing, more treaty ports, travel and preaching in the interior, and a legal opium trade. They made the point by burning the emperor's garden palace, the Yuanmingyuan. The shock produced a new court, under Prince Gong and two empresses dowager, and a new question, put most sharply by Feng Guifen: why were small Western nations strong and huge China weak? "Self-strengthening" was the answer of an important minority of officials: learn Western technology to defend a Confucian order. It built real arsenals, ships, schools and a foreign office. It did not rebuild the state.

The story in two acts, matching Review Questions 7 and 8, plus the Pierian Spring

  1. The Arrow War and the Burning (Q7): Russia on the Amur, the Arrow, Guangzhou, the Tianjin treaties, the Dagu repulse, the march on Beijing, the looting and burning, and the Conventions of Peking.
  2. Large and Yet Weak (Q8): the regency of 1861, Feng Guifen's essays, the Zongli Yamen, the Jiangnan Arsenal, the students in America, and Li Hongzhang's projects.
  3. Pierian Spring, Foreigners in China: the European palaces of the Yuanmingyuan, the treaty ports and concessions, the Imperial Maritime Customs, the missionaries, and the Tianjin massacre of 1870.

Act I: The Arrow War and the Burning (Review Question 7)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, "The Second Opium War," pp. 321–322. Reading-guide sentence (p. 321): "Russia, seeing China's weakness, penetrated the Amur River valley, violating the borders agreed to in 1689."

Russia on the Amur

Ebrey begins the war with Russia, not Britain, and the deck follows her (slide D). The Treaty of Nerchinsk (1689) had kept Russia out of the Amur basin. In the 1850s the governor-general of Eastern Siberia, Nikolai Muravyov, moved settlers and soldiers down the river. The Treaty of Aigun (1858) gave Russia the north bank of the Amur, and the Treaty of Peking (14 November 1860), negotiated by Nikolai Ignatiev while Beijing was under allied occupation, gave it the coast east of the Ussuri down to the new port of Vladivostok. Russia gained more territory from the crisis than Britain and France together, without fighting.

A pretext at Guangzhou

Britain wanted the 1842 treaties revised: more ports, access to the Yangzi, and an envoy in Beijing. In October 1856 Qing officers boarded the Arrow, a Chinese-owned lorcha whose British Hong Kong registration had in fact expired, and arrested its crew as suspected pirates (slide E). The British consul Harry Parkes and the governor of Hong Kong, Sir John Bowring, treated it as an insult to the flag. The French joined, citing the execution of the missionary Auguste Chapdelaine in Guangxi in early 1856. In London the House of Commons censured the government; Palmerston called an election and won it. The Indian Rebellion of 1857 delayed the expedition. At the end of 1857 the allies took Guangzhou and captured its governor-general, Ye Mingchen, who died in captivity in Calcutta in 1859 (slide F).1

Tianjin, 1858, and the Dagu repulse, 1859

In May 1858 allied gunboats took the Dagu forts at the mouth of the Beihe, and Qing envoys signed the Treaties of Tianjin with Britain, France, Russia and the United States (slide G). They provided for resident ministers in Beijing, ten new ports (including Yangzi river ports as far as Hankou), travel in the interior with passports, protection for missionaries and Chinese Christians, and indemnities of four million taels to Britain and two million to France. One British article banned the character yi ("barbarian") from official Chinese documents about Britons. A tariff agreement negotiated at Shanghai in November 1858 also did something the treaties' text did not: it legalized the import of opium, at a duty of thirty taels a chest. Opium, the cause of the first war, became a lawful import as a result of the second.2

The court never meant to keep the Beijing clause. When the British envoy Frederick Bruce arrived to exchange ratifications in June 1859 and insisted on going up the Beihe, the rebuilt Dagu forts under Sengge Rinchen opened fire. Four British gunboats were lost and several hundred men killed or wounded (slide H). It was the Qing's one clear victory over a Western force in these wars, and it guaranteed a bigger expedition.

The march on Beijing, 1860

In 1860 Lord Elgin returned as British plenipotentiary, with Baron Gros for France. Their armies, about 11,000 British and Indian troops under Lieutenant-General Sir James Hope Grant and about 6,700 French under General Cousin-Montauban, landed at Beitang in August, took the Dagu forts from the land side, and advanced on Tianjin and Beijing (slide I). In September the Qing seized Parkes, Elgin's secretary Henry Loch, and a group of British and French officers, journalists and Indian cavalrymen who had come to negotiate under a flag of truce. After the allied victory at Baliqiao on 21 September, the Xianfeng emperor fled to his summer retreat at Rehe (Chengde), leaving his brother Prince Gong to negotiate.

The French reached the Yuanmingyuan on 6 October, and the British soon after; both armies looted it for days. Then the captives' fate became known: about half of the 39 prisoners had died from mistreatment. Elgin wanted a punishment that would fall on the emperor rather than the people of Beijing. Ebrey reports that the Russian envoy talked the British out of burning the palace in the city, the Forbidden City (p. 322). On 18 and 19 October British troops set fire to the Yuanmingyuan and its neighboring imperial gardens (slides B and J). Elgin was the envoy who gave the order; the army's commander was Hope Grant.3

Ebrey calls the burned site "the entire 10-square-mile complex" (p. 322). The Yuanmingyuan proper, its three linked gardens, covered about 3.5 square kilometers, roughly 1.3 square miles. The larger figure fits only if one includes the neighboring imperial gardens in the western hills that were also burned. Its buildings, libraries and collections had been assembled over 150 years.4

The Conventions of Peking and their meaning

Prince Gong signed the Conventions of Peking with Britain (24 October) and France (25 October) (slide K). They ratified the Tianjin treaties, raised the indemnity to sixteen million taels (Ebrey's "16 million ounces of silver"), opened Tianjin itself, ceded the Kowloon peninsula to Britain, and allowed Chinese laborers to emigrate on foreign ships, legalizing the "coolie" trade. The French convention promised the return of confiscated Catholic property; its Chinese text, but not the authoritative French text, added a right for missionaries to rent and buy land in the provinces.

Ebrey's conclusion is that "after 1860 they increasingly saw propping up the faltering Qing Dynasty as in their interest" (p. 322; slide L). The treaties were only worth as much as the government that had signed them. That is why British and French forces helped defend Shanghai against the Taipings in 1862 (Lecture 2). Foreign management of customs had begun at Shanghai in 1854; after 1860 it spread to every treaty port (Pierian Spring, below). In sum, the war took more of China's sovereignty: its ability to set tariffs, exclude envoys and missionaries, keep foreign warships off its rivers and judge foreigners on its soil (slide M).

Answer synthesis · Q7

Britain and France wanted the 1842 treaties revised and used the Arrow incident and a missionary's execution as pretexts; Russia exploited the crisis on the Amur. The allies took Guangzhou and the Dagu forts, imposed the Treaties of Tianjin (1858), and after the Qing resisted in 1859 marched on Beijing and burned the Yuanmingyuan (1860). The outcomes were envoys in Beijing, new ports including the Yangzi, travel and preaching in the interior, legal opium, Kowloon for Britain, new indemnities, and the Amur and Ussuri lands for Russia. Each clause narrowed what the Qing state could decide for itself.

Act II: Large and Yet Weak (Review Question 8)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, "Self-Strengthening," pp. 322–323. Reading-guide sentence (p. 322): "He called for hiring a few 'barbarians' to help set up shipyards and arsenals in each major port."

A new court, 1861

The Xianfeng emperor died at Rehe in August 1861, leaving a five-year-old son, the Tongzhi emperor (slide O). Ebrey says that "The child's uncle, Prince Gong, and his mother, Empress Dowager Cixi, served as regents" (p. 322). The real arrangement was more complicated. Xianfeng had named eight regents, led by the Manchu grandee Sushun. In November 1861, as the court returned to Beijing, Cixi, the senior empress dowager Ci'an (Xianfeng's empress) and Prince Gong overthrew them in what is called the Xinyou coup. Sushun was beheaded and two princes were ordered to take their own lives. The two empresses dowager then "listened to government from behind the curtain" as joint regents until 1873, and Ci'an remained Cixi's partner until her death in 1881. Prince Gong became prince-counselor, the leading minister, not formally a regent.5 The change of rulers mattered, as Ebrey notes: it let a new group of ministers try new policies.

Feng Guifen's question

Feng Guifen (1809–1874), a Suzhou scholar who had placed second in the palace examination of 1840, fled to Shanghai when the Taipings took Suzhou in 1860 (slide P). There he watched foreign troops defend the city, and in 1861 he wrote the essays later published as Protests from the Jiaobin Studio. China, he noted, was a hundred times larger than France and two hundred times larger than Britain: "Why are Western nations small and yet strong? Why are we large and yet weak?" (p. 322; slide Q).

Primary source · Feng Guifen, 1861

"Why are Western nations small and yet strong? Why are we large and yet weak?"

Quoted in Ebrey, p. 322. A fuller translation is in Teng and Fairbank, China's Response to the West (1954), and in Wm. Theodore de Bary and Richard Lufrano, eds., Sources of Chinese Tradition, vol. 2, 2nd ed. (New York: Columbia University Press, 2000).

His answers were practical (slides R–S). Hire a few "barbarians" to set up shipyards and arsenals in each major port. Reward the Chinese managers with examination degrees if their ships and guns matched the foreigners'. Translate Western books on mathematics and science. Ebrey also notes his proposal that officials nominate their superiors by a vote that would actually be counted, a striking idea of accountability within the bureaucracy rather than democracy. The formula later associated with self-strengthening, "Chinese learning as the essence, Western learning for practical use" (zhongti xiyong), was popularized by Zhang Zhidong in 1898, but it captures Feng's position well (slide T).

Institutions: the Zongli Yamen and the schools

In January 1861 the court created the Zongli Yamen, an office for foreign affairs under Prince Gong (slide U). It was supposed to be temporary, and it stayed a committee of officials who held other posts. In 1862 it opened the Tongwen Guan, a school for interpreters in Beijing, which later added mathematics, astronomy and international law. Henry Wheaton's Elements of International Law was translated by the missionary W. A. P. Martin and published in 1864. Ebrey notes that by 1880 China had embassies in London, Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Washington, Tokyo and St. Petersburg (p. 323).

Arsenals, shipyards and students

Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang founded the Jiangnan Arsenal at Shanghai in 1865 (slide V). It produced its first steamship in 1868 and housed a translation bureau where John Fryer and Chinese collaborators such as Xu Shou and Hua Hengfang translated more than a hundred scientific and technical works. In 1866 Zuo Zongtang founded the Fuzhou Navy Yard, run by Shen Baozhen with French advisers led by Prosper Giquel, with a naval school attached. Fuzhou's ships and graduates matter for Lecture 4, because the French destroyed the Fujian fleet in front of the yard in 1884.6

In 1872 the first of 120 boys of the Chinese Educational Mission, organized by the Yale graduate Yung Wing, sailed for the United States (slide W). They lived with families in New England and studied in American schools. In 1881, alarmed that the boys were becoming too American, and angered by American refusal to admit them to the military academies, the court recalled them. Many later served as engineers, naval officers and diplomats.7

Li Hongzhang's projects

Ebrey's table of Li's projects, 1862–1893 (p. 323; slide X), runs from gun factories at Shanghai through the China Merchants' Steam Navigation Company (1872), the Kaiping coal mines, telegraphs, a cotton mill, a naval academy at Tianjin, and the Beiyang (Northern) Fleet, formally established in 1888. Li governed from Tianjin as governor-general of Zhili from 1870 to 1895, and again in 1900–1901. Many of the enterprises were organized as guandu shangban, "official supervision and merchant management." Merchants supplied capital and managers; officials supplied protection, monopolies and political risk. Albert Feuerwerker's study shows how that mix bred both growth and corruption.8

Li and others came to see that guns and ships required schools, mines, railways and revenue, and that these required changes in how officials were trained and how the state raised money (slide Y). That recognition is the bridge to Lecture 4's question of why the reforms fell short. Benjamin Elman warns against judging self-strengthening only by the defeat of 1894–1895: by the measure of science, translation and industry, the achievements were substantial.9

Answer synthesis · Q8

Feng Guifen and the self-strengtheners wanted to make China strong enough to resist the West while keeping its Confucian order. Feng proposed hiring foreign experts to build shipyards and arsenals, rewarding successful managers with degrees, translating Western science, and reforming how officials were chosen. Prince Gong, Zeng Guofan, Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang built a foreign office, language schools, arsenals and shipyards, sent students abroad, and founded mines, telegraphs, shipping lines and a modern navy.

Pierian Spring: Foreigners in China

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, "Foreigners in China," pp. 324–326.

The European palaces

The ruins students recognize from photographs of the Yuanmingyuan are the Xiyanglou, the "Western Mansions," built in a European baroque style from the 1740s to the 1760s by the Jesuit painter Giuseppe Castiglione, with fountains engineered by Michel Benoist (slide CC). The most famous fountain had twelve bronze animal heads of the Chinese zodiac; each spouted water for its own two-hour period of the day, and all twelve together at noon. Several heads were looted in 1860, and their return became a political issue in the 2000s. Geremie Barmé's essay on the ruins traces how they became a site of national memory.10

Treaty ports and concessions

In the larger treaty ports, foreign powers held concessions or settlements with their own municipal councils, police and courts (slide DD). Shanghai's International Settlement, formed in 1863 from the British and American settlements, governed itself through a council elected by foreign ratepayers; Chinese residents, the great majority, had no vote. Ebrey writes that "By 1900 there were one hundred treaty ports" (p. 325). The figure is too high for 1900. The Maritime Customs listed about thirty treaty ports open at the end of 1899; the number passed ninety only by the late 1910s, counting ports China opened on its own initiative.11

The Imperial Maritime Customs

During the Small Swords' occupation of Shanghai's Chinese city in 1853–1855, the British, American and French consuls arranged for foreign inspectors to collect customs duties for the Qing. After 1860 the system was extended to every treaty port. Under Robert Hart, inspector-general from 1863 to 1911, the Customs was a Qing agency staffed at the top by foreigners. It was honest, efficient and a major source of Qing revenue, and its revenue was later pledged as security for foreign loans and indemnities (slide EE).12

Missionaries and the Tianjin massacre

After 1860 missionaries could live and buy property in the interior, and the treaties protected Chinese converts (slide FF). Ebrey counts 886 Catholic and about 3,000 Protestant missionaries by 1900, more than half of them women (p. 326). Missionaries ran schools, hospitals and orphanages, and some intervened in lawsuits on behalf of converts, which local gentry deeply resented. Paul Cohen's study of gentry anti-Christian writing remains the starting point.13

In June 1870 rumors spread in Tianjin that French Sisters of Charity were kidnapping children for their orphanage and using their eyes and hearts for medicine. On 21 June the French consul, Henri Fontanier, confronted the magistrate, fired a pistol and was killed by the crowd. The mob then killed priests, nuns and other foreigners and dozens of Chinese Christians, and burned the cathedral and other churches (slide GG). Zeng Guofan, sent to settle the case, executed Chinese rioters and paid an indemnity, and was condemned for it by many fellow officials. The episode set the pattern that would explode in 1900: rumor, local fear of converts' privileges, foreign gunboat pressure, and a court caught between them.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Elgin was the envoy, not the commander. He was the plenipotentiary who ordered the burning; Hope Grant commanded the army. Slides B and J now call him the envoy.
  • The regency of 1861. Ebrey omits Ci'an, the eight regents and the coup. Cixi and Ci'an were co-regents; Prince Gong was prince-counselor. Slide O (and Lecture 4's slides C and E) now say so, with the coup in slide O's instructor note.
  • Legal opium. The 1858 tariff rules legalized the opium trade. Ebrey does not mention it; slides G and K now do, since it is the natural link back to Lecture 1.
  • Customs before 1860. Foreign customs inspection began in Shanghai in 1854 and spread to every treaty port after 1860, as slide L now says.
  • "Ten square miles." Ebrey's figure for the Yuanmingyuan is about seven times the size of the garden itself.
  • "One hundred treaty ports by 1900." About thirty in 1899; over ninety only by about 1917. Slide DD now says about thirty, with an instructor note on Ebrey's figure.
  • The zodiac fountain. Each of the twelve heads marked a two-hour period of the day, as slide CC now says.
  • Li Hongzhang in Zhili. 1870–1895 and 1900–1901, as the deck says; Ebrey's table implies continuous tenure.
  • The Fuzhou Navy Yard (1866) is not in Ebrey's text. Slide V now introduces it, because Lecture 4's Fuzhou story needs it.

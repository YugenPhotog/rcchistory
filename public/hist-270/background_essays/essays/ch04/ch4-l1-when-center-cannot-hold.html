Central interpretation

The lecture’s larger question: what happens when a 400-year empire collapses, and why could no one put it back together? Its answer, kept from Steven Austin’s FA26 framing: the late Han did not simply “fall.” It devolved into militarized regional states competing over food, manpower, and legitimacy. Even reunification under the Western Jin proved fragile, because the institutions built during war (hereditary soldiers, state farms, ranked recruitment, fortified households, and princely armies) were hard to reverse.

Part 1: How the Han Fell Apart (Review Question 1)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 4, “The Three Kingdoms (220–265) and the Western Jin Dynasty (265–316),” pp. 62–64, with “Palace Eunuchs” in Chapter 3, pp. 44–45. The Reading Guide anchors Q1 on p. 62 with the sentence beginning “Wei made the…”.

A transformation in state power (slides C, I). The lecture treats the fall of Han as a transformation in state power, not a vacuum. The court’s decision in 184 to let generals and local officials raise their own armies was understandable in the emergency, but it moved military organization onto personal loyalty. The comparison with Qin is the analytical hinge: Qin fell quickly to rebel coalitions, while the Han collapse produced durable regional military institutions with supplies, dependents, territory, and ambitions. The old in-class multiple-choice item on this point is now question 3 of self-check R.

The court crisis (slides J–M and Pierian Spring AU–AX). The crisis of 189 makes sense only as the end of a long struggle over access to the emperor among eunuchs, consort families, scholar-officials, and generals. Present eunuch power as structural (location inside the palace, control of information) rather than as a morality tale of corrupt men. The Partisan Prohibitions of 166 and 169 matter because they estranged the educated elite whose prestige increasingly came from elite society rather than appointment, which sets up the withdrawal of Part 4. In the style pass the four deep-dive slides (old slides 10 to 13) moved to the Pierian Spring because Chapter 3, Lecture 3 now teaches the same material; slide J links to both. The move is reversible.

Cao Cao and Wei institutions (slides N–Q). Cao Cao’s importance is institutional: garrison farming made the state a landlord rather than a tax collector dependent on magnates (p. 63); hereditary military households and Xiongnu cavalry supplied the army; the Nine Rank System (220) tried to reconnect recruitment to local society. The lecture first presents the Nine Rank System as a Wei solution, then returns to its aristocratic consequences in Part 3.

Part 2: The Three Kingdoms Stalemate (Review Question 1)

Periodization (slides T–U). Distinguish the breakdown phase (184–220), when the Han existed formally but central power was disappearing, from the Three Kingdoms proper (220–280). Reunification came by one bloc absorbing its rivals, not by reviving Han institutions.

Why the stalemate lasted (slides V–Y). Geography and institutions worked together: the Yangzi and the Sichuan basin made conquest costly, while hereditary military households let rival regimes sustain armies for decades. Wei had more than twice the population of Shu or Wu (p. 63) and prevailed once its advantages accumulated. The three military transformations (hereditary soldiers, cavalry, walled strongholds) point forward to Lecture 2’s stirrup and armored cavalry (p. 65).

Part 3: A False Dawn

The Western Jin (slides AB–AD). The 280 census of 16 million (p. 64) measures administrative reach, not deaths. The founder’s decision to give armies and land to Sima princes, meant to prevent another coup, produced the War of the Eight Princes. Ebrey also mentions strife among empresses’ families (the Jia family) at Luoyang (p. 64), which the deck notes but does not put on a slide face.

The Nine Rank System and great families (slide AE). This is Chapter 4’s question about hereditary status (p. 61): weaker central governments could not bypass entrenched lineages, so office, marriage, genealogy, education, and refinement became linked as marks of family rank. Ability did not become irrelevant, but pedigree became hard to escape.

Part 4: Civilization Through Style (Review Questions 6 and 11)

Withdrawal (slides AH–AI). The Seven Sages turned distance from politics into a mark of cultural freedom and superiority; it was an elite performance not available to everyone. Xuanxue reworked the Laozi, Zhuangzi, and Book of Changes (p. 64) and is often credited with creating vocabulary later used to understand Buddhist ideas (Lecture 3).

The three arts (slides AJ–AK). Calligraphy, poetry, and painting became arenas in which aristocrats displayed pedigree, cultivation, and emotional self-command when office was dangerous. Chapter 4’s central point applies: political fragmentation did not mean cultural decline (p. 61). The Reading Guide anchors Q6 on p. 69 (“During the Period…”).

Yan Zhitui (slide AL). His biography (pp. 68–69) crosses the north-south divide repeatedly: Liang, captivity under Hou Jing, enslavement by Western Wei in 554, escape, Northern Qi, displacement by Northern Zhou in 577, and Sui after 581. What remained portable was literary education and the arts; he combined Confucian service with Buddhist faith. Ebrey’s own questions for analysis (how many moves, how many involuntary; why the northern courts employed him) are good prompts for discussion of Q11. The Reading Guide anchors Q11 in the Biography on pp. 68–69 (“In 554, however…”).

Corrections and Cautions

  • Image fixes, not content changes. Three of the four hotlinked images in the live deck no longer load (Cao Cao portrait, the 220 CE map, and the Lantingji Xu). They were replaced with local WebP files of equivalent public images: a Qing Romance portrait of Cao Cao, a 262 CE Three Kingdoms map (CC BY-SA 4.0), and the Feng Chengsu Tang copy. The old Yellow Turban slide reused the Cao Cao portrait as a “late Han illustration”; it now has its own AI placeholder.
  • Dates that differ from Ebrey (left unchanged on slides). Jin founded 266 (Ebrey heads the section 265–316; Sima Yan took the throne in the Chinese year 265, early 266 in the Western calendar). Three Kingdoms 220–280 (Ebrey’s heading: 220–265). Wang Xizhi 303–361 in the popup (Ebrey p. 70: 307–365; both ranges appear in scholarship).
  • Spellings. The slides use Cao Pi and Shu-Han; Ebrey writes Cao Pei and Shu (pp. 62–63). The notes on slide T point this out.
  • Yellow Turbans. The popup on slide H says the rising was “suppressed by about 205”; Ebrey says the original uprising was suppressed within a year, with similar groups rising later (p. 62). The notes follow Ebrey.
  • Seven Sages. The callout “If the state is insane, live truthfully” is presented as the spirit of the Sages, not a quotation; no source is given for it. The old brick-relief captions gave two dates (3rd century and 3rd–4th century); the Nanjing brick murals are usually dated to the Eastern Jin or Southern Dynasties (Ebrey p. 62 says tombs near the Eastern Jin capital), so the new caption avoids a century.
  • Population figures. 59.6 million is the Han census of 2 CE and 16.16 million the Jin census of 280; the drop measures registration, not deaths (popup on slide F).
  • 189. Ebrey’s compressed sentence (p. 62) has “the warlord who gained control of the capital” slaughter the eunuchs; in the fuller narrative He Jin’s allies (Yuan Shao’s troops) massacred the eunuchs and Dong Zhuo then seized the emperor, as the slides say.

About the Images

Chapter 4’s period style is Northern Wei / Western Wei Dunhuang cave-mural painting (Mogao Caves 254, 257, and 285): flat figures with dark oxidized contour lines on a red-ochre or cream ground, malachite green, lapis blue, white, and black, narrative registers, and small stylized mountains. Ebrey’s Material Culture feature on Cave 285 (p. 71) anchors the choice. The 31 AI images are labelled on each slide as AI-generated interpretations, not historical records. Four images are real: the Qing Cao Cao portrait, the Seven Sages brick rubbing, the Feng Chengsu Orchid Pavilion Preface, and the 262 CE map. Concept slides use the shared chalkboard cartoons (professor, Confucius, Sima Qian).

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao, 155–220 AD (2010). The fullest English account of Cao Cao’s rise, state farms, and the end of the Han.

Mark Edward Lewis, China between Empires: The Northern and Southern Dynasties (2009). The best short synthesis of the Period of Division, including military households, the Nine Rank System, and aristocratic culture.

Albert E. Dien and Keith N. Knapp, eds., The Cambridge History of China, vol. 2: The Six Dynasties, 220–589 (2019). Political narrative and institutional chapters for the Three Kingdoms and the Jin.

Ssu-yü Teng, trans., Family Instructions for the Yen Clan (1968). Yan Zhitui’s advice to his sons, the source of Ebrey’s biography.

Liu Yiqing, Shih-shuo Hsin-yü: A New Account of Tales of the World, trans. Richard B. Mather (1976; 2nd ed. 2002). The anecdotes behind the Seven Sages and the culture of witty conversation.

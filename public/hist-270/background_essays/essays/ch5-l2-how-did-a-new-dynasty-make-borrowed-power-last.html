How Did a New Dynasty Make Borrowed Power Last?

Succession, institutions, and frontier strategy in the early Tang, 618–676

Essential question

How did a new dynasty turn inherited institutions into durable power?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain why the Tang founders could take over Sui government quickly, given their place in the same northwestern military aristocracy.
  2. Analyze the violent succession of 626 and the ways Taizong's government and historians later shaped how it was remembered.
  3. Describe how law, standard texts, household registration, land allocation, rotating military service, and examinations linked households to the state.
  4. Compare the Tang's frontier tools (fortification, diplomacy, marriage alliance, divide-and-rule, and recruitment of non-Chinese soldiers) and judge their results.
  5. Distinguish influence from control, using the Turkish, Central Asian, and Korean cases.

Introductory Framing

When Li Yuan declared the Tang dynasty in 618, he was not an outsider storming the gates. He was a Sui governor, a member of the same northwestern aristocracy that had produced the Sui, and a relative of the Sui royal house by marriage. Most of the men who staffed his government had served the Sui. The dynasty changed; much of the governing world did not. Yet the Tang lasted nearly three hundred years where the Sui had lasted thirty-seven. Something about the way the early Tang used its inheritance made the difference.

The central problem of the lecture is therefore not how the Tang invented a new state. It did not. It is how a new ruling house took over machinery it had not built (a law code, household registers, land allocation, rotating farmer-soldiers, a capital, and a canal) and made that machinery work for generations. The lecture's answer has three parts: choices at court, institutions across society, and flexible policy at the frontiers. The useful umbrella term is state capacity: a government's ability to raise resources, staff offices, and pursue security.

Historians have approached the early Tang from several directions. Woodbridge Bingham argued as early as 1941 that the official histories exaggerated Li Shimin's role in the founding at his father's expense. Howard Wechsler's study of Wei Zheng explained how Taizong's partnership with outspoken ministers became a model of good government. Denis Twitchett showed how Tang official history was written, and rewritten, under imperial pressure. Jonathan Karam Skaff and David Graff placed Tang expansion inside a shared frontier world of Turkic and Chinese warriors, where alliances mattered as much as armies. The lecture should keep all of these in view: real institutional strength, real political violence, and a carefully managed memory of both.

I. A New Dynasty Built from the Sui's Own Ruling Class

From Sui Governor to Tang Emperor

Li Yuan governed Taiyuan, in modern Shanxi, as Sui authority broke down. Early in 617 he defeated the eastern Turks near his base; in the summer of 617 he raised an army, with Turkish help, and marched on the capital, which fell at the end of the year. After Yangdi's murder in 618, Li Yuan set aside his puppet Sui emperor and declared the Tang. He is known by his temple name, Gaozu (r. 618–626). His son Li Shimin became the second emperor, Taizong (r. 626–649).

Declaring a dynasty did not end the civil war. The Tang had to defeat rival contenders one by one: Wang Shichong at Luoyang, Dou Jiande in Hebei, and others in the northwest, the middle Yangzi, and the southeast. Li Shimin's victory over Dou Jiande in 621 forced Wang Shichong to surrender and gave the Tang most of the eastern plain. Resistance in Hebei continued until 623, and the last southern rebel was suppressed in 624. The Tang won by force, but it also treated most surrendered rebels leniently and often confirmed them in office, a pattern that helped it gather support.

Continuity at the Top

Britannica's history of the period stresses how little changed at court. Most of the highest officials were former Sui officials, many of them Li Yuan's colleagues from Taiyuan, or descendants of officials of earlier northern regimes. A majority of the chief ministers were related by marriage to the Tang or Sui imperial family. Gaozu kept the Sui central government of ministries and boards, kept court control over local appointments, and by the 630s local administration closely resembled the Sui pattern. He inherited a bankrupt state, so his government was small and cheap.

This continuity is the first half of the answer. The Tang founders already knew the institutions and the elite networks of the state. They did not need to invent a bureaucracy while fighting a civil war; they needed to win the war and keep the bureaucracy running.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Tang could build quickly because it built on the Sui. A new dynasty from inside the old ruling class could take over the state's machinery and personnel almost intact, which let it spend its early energy on winning the civil war rather than on inventing a government.

II. The Coup of 626 and the Making of Taizong's Image

A Succession Decided by Force

Li Shimin had commanded armies from a young age and was a gifted strategist. His older brother Li Jiancheng was the heir apparent, and by 625–626 the rivalry between them had split the court into factions. In 626, at the Xuanwu Gate of the palace, Li Shimin ambushed and killed Jiancheng and another brother, Li Yuanji. Their ten sons were executed. Within weeks Gaozu named Li Shimin heir, and soon afterward he abdicated. Li Shimin took the throne as Taizong.

The violence should not be softened. The reign later remembered as the "good government of the Zhenguan era" (Zhenguan was Taizong's reign name, 627–649) began with fratricide and the killing of children. The dynasty's reputation for effective government coexisted with coercive politics at its center.

Who Wrote the Story?

Much of what we know about the founding comes from records compiled by Taizong's own officials. In 643, according to the Tang historical record, Taizong pressed his historians to let him read the court diary and the draft "Veritable Records" of his reign, something emperors were not supposed to do. He objected that the account of 626 was too vague and had it revised to present the killing as a righteous act that saved the state. Modern historians, following Bingham and Twitchett, conclude that the official histories enlarge Li Shimin's role in the founding and shrink his father's.

Visual evidence carries the same message. Taizong's tomb at Zhaoling was decorated with stone reliefs of six warhorses he had ridden in the founding wars, made between 636 and 649. One of them, Saluzi ("Autumn Dew"), shows the general Qiu Xinggong pulling an arrow from the horse's chest after a battle near Luoyang in 621; it is now in the Penn Museum in Philadelphia. The reliefs are commemoration, not eyewitness evidence. They show how Taizong wanted to be remembered: as an emperor who had won his throne on horseback.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Avoid presenting Taizong's reign as a smooth, inevitable golden age. The coup, the executions, and Taizong's pressure on his historians are part of the story. Use the tomb reliefs as political memory: they can show how the ruler wished to be seen, but they cannot confirm any particular battle scene.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The early Tang's stability began with a succession crisis settled by force, and the record of that crisis was shaped by the man who won it. Studying the founding means studying both what happened and how the winner arranged for it to be remembered.

III. Governing Through Advice: Taizong and His Ministers

A Court That Listened

What made Taizong's reign distinctive, in Britannica's words, was not new institutions but "the atmosphere of his administration" and the close interplay between the emperor and an unusually able group of advisers. The most famous was Wei Zheng, who had once served Taizong's murdered brother. Taizong not only spared him but made him a trusted critic. Wei Zheng is remembered for a long series of remonstrances, formal criticisms of the emperor's plans and conduct. Taizong also balanced regional groups within the aristocracy so that no single region dominated his court.

Why would a powerful ruler want to hear criticism? The answer is practical as well as moral. A large empire could not be governed from one man's instincts. Advice helped a ruler learn about problems, compare options, and avoid costly mistakes. The Sui had shown what happened when an emperor ignored warnings about overwork and war. Taizong's court knew that lesson well, because many of its members had lived through it.

Primary Evidence: Three Mirrors

Primary source

“With bronze as a mirror, one can straighten one's cap and robes. With the past as a mirror, one can understand the rise and fall of states. With people as a mirror, one can see one's own gains and faults. I have always kept these three mirrors to guard against my own errors. Now Wei Zheng has died, and I have lost a mirror.”

— Taizong on the death of Wei Zheng, 643 · translation adapted; Chinese text in the Old Tang History (Jiu Tang shu), ch. 71

The passage presents advice as a tool of self-correction: a minister is a mirror in which the emperor sees his faults. It also shows what the historians wanted readers to remember about Taizong. The same sources that preserve this lament were compiled under imperial supervision, and the relationship had its strains; Taizong's anger at Wei Zheng is also recorded. The mirror image is an ideal as much as a description.

That ideal should not be read in modern terms. Tang government was not consultative in a democratic sense. Advisers came from a narrow elite, the emperor could ignore them, and he did ignore most of his ministers when he chose to invade Goguryeo in 645. What the Zhenguan court offered was a model of how a strong ruler could make better decisions by protecting honest criticism, a model later rulers and officials cited for centuries.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Taizong's government gained strength from information and expertise, not only from force. The ideal of the ruler who listens became one of the Tang's most lasting political legacies, even though it was partly a product of the historians who served him.

IV. Law, Texts, Land, Soldiers, and Recruitment

A Code That Outlasted the Dynasty

Gaozu issued a new codification of law in 624, and it was revised regularly, roughly every twenty years in the early Tang. The version issued with an official commentary in 653, under Taizong's son Gaozong, is the earliest Chinese legal code that survives complete. That wording matters: it is the earliest to survive, not the first ever written. The Tang Code became a model for later Chinese dynasties and was adapted in Japan in the early eighth century and, later, in Korea and Vietnam. Law made imperial rule more repeatable than any ruler's personal decisions, because officials across a huge empire could apply the same rules and penalties.

Standard Texts and an Official Past

The court also standardized the texts officials were expected to know. Under Taizong, scholars prepared an official edition of the Confucian classics with a standard commentary, the Correct Meaning of the Five Classics, finally issued in 653. Taizong's court also compiled official histories of the dynasties of the previous three centuries, including the Sui. These projects created authoritative versions of the classics and a shared account of the recent past. They were part of governing, not a literary hobby: schools and examinations could now point students toward the same body of knowledge, and the official histories explained why earlier dynasties had lost the right to rule.

Land, Taxes, and Rotating Soldiers

The Tang kept modified versions of three linked systems it inherited through the Sui from earlier northern regimes. Seeing them together shows why household registration mattered so much.

Two cautions keep this table honest. First, these systems were adapted, not invented, by the Tang. Second, the rules on paper were never enforced to the letter everywhere. Britannica judges that equal-field allocation was probably never enforced at all in the south. As a legal framework for registering land and limiting its sale, however, it lasted until the An Lushan rebellion, the subject of Lecture 5. The tax system also had a built-in unfairness: it taxed everyone at the same rate regardless of wealth, so it weighed most heavily on the poor.

Schools and Examinations

Tang government schools at the capital prepared the sons of officials and nobles; other candidates came from local schools. Recruitment through examinations grew over time. In the mature Tang system, the mingjing ("illuminating the classics") examination tested knowledge of the classics, while the more prestigious jinshi ("presented scholar") examination tested formal poetry and essays on policy. Examinations created a route into office that did not depend only on birth.

The route was narrow. The jinshi examination passed only a few dozen men in a typical year, and most officials still entered through family privilege, clerical service, or the guards. Recommendation and personal connections also mattered. The examinations' main early effect was to create a highly educated group within the elite and a body of officials who owed their careers to the dynasty. Lecture 3 takes up the question of who actually benefited.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The early Tang's institutions worked as a system. Registration made land, taxes, and military service possible; law and standard texts made administration more uniform; examinations began to create officials who depended on the dynasty. Light, regular demands, rather than the Sui's repeated emergency levies, let the system last.

V. The Frontier: Influence, Alliance, and the Limits of Conquest

A Repertoire, Not a Single Policy

Tang rulers used many tools toward the Turkic powers of the steppe: fortifications, trade and tribute missions, marriage diplomacy, dividing rival groups, and recruiting non-Chinese soldiers into Tang armies. No single strategy explains Tang success. Skaff's work stresses that the Tang court and its steppe neighbors shared many political customs, such as oaths, marriage alliances, and gift exchange, so frontier relations were a negotiation between partners who understood each other, not simply a clash between "Chinese" and "nomads."

630: Victory over the Eastern Turks

In 630, with the eastern Turks divided by leadership quarrels and revolts among their subject peoples, Tang forces invaded, defeated them, and captured their khan. Tang control extended into northern Shaanxi and southern Mongolia. Taizong was recognized by steppe leaders as Tian Kehan, usually translated "Heavenly Khan" (the course textbook gives "Great Khan of the Turks"). The title shows a Tang emperor claiming authority in steppe terms as well as Chinese ones. Large numbers of Turks were settled along the frontier, including the Ordos region, thousands of Turkic families lived in Chang'an, and many Turks served in Tang armies.

Campaigns in Central Asia

In the 640s and 650s, Tang and Turkic forces campaigned together in Central Asia. By the late 640s a Tang military administration reached beyond the limits of modern Xinjiang, and under Gaozong the western Turkic lands were placed under a nominal Tang protectorate (659–661). The textbook compares this reach to the Han; Britannica says Tang western dominions extended farther than in the Han's great days. Two qualifications matter. The campaigns depended on Turkic allies, not Tang armies alone, and "overlordship" usually meant tribute, garrisons at key points, and recognized local rulers, not uniform direct rule.

Goguryeo: The Limits of Conquest

Korea was Taizong's one clear foreign failure. After a coup in Goguryeo in 642 and attacks on Silla, a Tang ally, Taizong decided to invade against the advice of most of his ministers. He prepared the campaign in 644; the Tang armies invaded in 645, won early victories, but failed to take key fortresses and withdrew with heavy losses. Another inconclusive campaign followed in 647. Under Gaozong, a Tang army allied with Silla finally took Pyongyang in 668. But by 676 rebellions had forced the Tang to withdraw to southern Manchuria, and Silla, not the Tang, controlled most of the peninsula. The ally became the main winner.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Date Taizong's invasion of Goguryeo to 645 (preparations began in 644). The 668 conquest and the 676 withdrawal happened under Gaozong, not Taizong. Use the sequence to show that winning a campaign did not guarantee durable rule.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Early Tang frontier power rested on alliances and negotiated relationships as much as on armies. It produced wide influence in the steppe and Central Asia, but influence was not the same as control, and in Korea the Tang's own ally inherited the victory.

Closing Synthesis

Early Tang strength came from continuity combined with political choices. The founders came from the Sui's own ruling class and kept its officials, its central and local government, its law, and its systems of registration, land, taxes, and rotating soldiers. Taizong seized power through a violent coup, then governed through capable advisers, standardized law and texts, expanded schools and examinations, and kept demands on households relatively light. At the frontiers, the Tang combined force with diplomacy and alliance, winning great influence in the north and west while learning in Korea that victories do not always produce lasting control.

The central historiographical shift is from celebration to careful reading of sources. Traditional history presented the Zhenguan era as a golden age created by a wise emperor. Bingham and Twitchett showed how the record of the founding was shaped by Taizong himself; Wechsler explained the Wei Zheng ideal as both a real practice and a political model; Skaff and Graff moved the frontier story from Chinese conquest toward a shared world of alliances. None of this denies the early Tang's achievements. It explains them as institutions and choices rather than as the virtue of one man.

For the lecture, the most important distinction is between inheriting a state and making it last. The Sui built much of the machinery; the Tang's contribution was to run it without breaking the households that powered it. The question to leave open is how long that balance could hold, as Tang rulers faced a growing empire, a changing elite, and frontier armies that would eventually need to be professional and permanent.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Denis Twitchett, ed., The Cambridge History of China, vol. 3, Sui and T'ang China, 589–906, Part I (1979). The fullest English account of the founding, Taizong's reign, and early Tang institutions. The standard reference for dates and administrative detail.

Howard J. Wechsler, Mirror to the Son of Heaven: Wei Cheng at the Court of T'ang T'ai-tsung (1974). A study of Wei Zheng and the practice of remonstrance. Essential for explaining why Taizong's court became a lasting model of good government, and what that model left out.

Denis Twitchett, The Writing of Official History under the T'ang (1992). Explains how court diaries, Veritable Records, and dynastic histories were compiled, and how emperors, Taizong included, could shape them. The key source for teaching the 626 coup critically.

Woodbridge Bingham, The Founding of the T'ang Dynasty: The Fall of Sui and Rise of T'ang (1941). An early and still-cited argument that official histories inflated Li Shimin's role and minimized Li Yuan's. Useful for showing students that doubts about the record are not new.

Jonathan Karam Skaff, Sui-Tang China and Its Turko-Mongol Neighbors: Culture, Power, and Connections, 580–800 (2012). Treats Tang frontier relations as diplomacy within a shared Eurasian political culture. Best for the Heavenly Khan title and the logic of alliance.

David A. Graff, Medieval Chinese Warfare, 300–900 (2002). Chapters on Li Shimin's campaigns and the early Tang expeditionary armies explain how the Tang won its civil war and why the Korean campaigns were so hard.

Mark Edward Lewis, China's Cosmopolitan Empire: The Tang Dynasty (2009). A thematic synthesis covering law, land, military service, and foreign relations. Useful for linking this lecture's institutions to the social history of the rest of the chapter.

Audit disposition. Chapter 14 had no HIST 270 background essays, so this reconnaissance covers four new essays and a retroactive audit of the four published FA26 decks (From Beggar Monk to Autocrat, Two Frontiers, One Life Through the Ming World and The Garden and the Flood). The four-lecture roadmap passes, and the decks are clear, well paced and faithful to Ebrey's words. The main problems were textbook errors repeated in visible text: the order of events in 1368, the "fifty-thousand-man" army at Tumu, Guizhou "in the 1380s," Esen as "khan," the Altan settlement of "1570," the "1,500-mile" wall, the Dutch in the 1500s and the Dalai Lama's meeting of "1577." There were also several overclaims and two omissions, the sea ban and the Manchu war after 1618. The rule for this revision was that the decks contradict Ebrey only where a reliable source (the Cambridge History of China, the Dictionary of Ming Biography, a scholarly monograph or a peer-reviewed article) confirms the correction. The confirmed fixes have been applied to working copies of the four decks: the visible slides now teach the confirmed history, and each instructor note records the textbook's version and the confirming source, which is also listed on each deck's Sources slide. Items that could not be confirmed, or where the check supported Ebrey (the Hu Weiyong toll, Hai Rui's 1565 and the rebels at Kaifeng), were left as the textbook has them. Section 8 gives every item with its verdict, source and action.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. Ebrey's Chapter 14, "The Ming Empire in China (1368–1644)," pp. 227–246, is the course baseline, not its ceiling. Dates, names, numbers and quotations in the decks were checked against the textbook (from an OCR of the course PDF, page by page), against standard monographs and the Cambridge History of China, volumes 7 and 8, and, where the decks quote primary sources, against published translations. Where Ebrey's statements conflict with current scholarship, the essays follow the scholarship and flag the textbook. Where a deck followed the textbook into error and a reliable source confirmed the correction, the essays give the confirmed history and this audit lists the slide, the source and the fix applied.

The rule for fixes is the course rule: visible slides teach the correct history; the textbook's discrepancy is explained only in an instructor note; outside works are cited in a brief notes line and on the Sources slide; no visible "outside the textbook" tags on any slide; slide letters are permanent and no slide is added or relettered.

Audience: the instructor's lecture preparation and advanced students. The essays keep plain language and define terms, but cite real scholarship in footnotes and end with annotated reading lists.

2. Textbook baseline

Ebrey's chapter supplies the spine: the founding and Taizu's government (pp. 227–229); Chengzu and the move to Beijing (pp. 229–230); the weaknesses of the imperial institution (p. 230); relations with other countries, Zheng He, the Mongols and the Great Wall, and the coast (pp. 230–235); the examinations and the Biography of Tan Yunxian (pp. 234–237); Wang Yangming and his followers (pp. 237–240); local society (pp. 240–241); urban culture, the Material Culture feature on Suzhou gardens and the Documents feature on The Peony Pavilion (pp. 241–244); the fall of the Ming (pp. 244–245); and the summary (p. 246). The reading guide sets eleven review questions, all covered by the decks.

Missing pages. Pages 231–232 (the opening of the Zheng He section and part of the discussion of eunuchs) are missing from the course PDF, as the arc plan records. The Lecture 2 deck already sources its Zheng He slides to Ma Huan and outside scholarship and says so on its Sources slide. A complete scan should be obtained before the next revision.

The chapter is clear and well proportioned. Statements needing correction or caution:

  • "In 1368 his armies took the Yuan capital ... declared himself emperor" (p. 228): the proclamation (January) preceded the capture of Dadu (September).
  • Hu Weiyong case, "as many as fifteen thousand" (p. 229): correct as Taizu's own figure; Langlois estimates more than 40,000 dead in all the purges of 1380–1394 (CHC 7, p. 140).
  • Taizu "most likely" mentally ill (p. 229): the author's judgment.
  • Guizhou created "in the 1380s" (p. 229): 1413.
  • Chengzu's reign "1403–1425" (p. 229): 1402–1424.
  • Tumu, "fifty-thousand-man army" (p. 230): a huge army, said by some to number half a million (CHC 7, p. 322).
  • Both founders "led armies into Mongolia" (p. 233): only Chengzu campaigned in person.
  • Esen "the khan of the Western Mongols" in 1449 (p. 233): taishi; khan from 1453.
  • Trade with Altan Khan "in 1570" (p. 233): 1571.
  • Third Dalai Lama's meeting with Altan "in 1577" (p. 233): 1578.
  • The Ming wall "about fifteen hundred miles" (p. 233): about 8,850 km of Ming walls and barriers in the 2009 survey.
  • Dutch traders in the 1500s (p. 234): they arrived about 1600 (CHC 8, p. 349).
  • No navy; coastal people moved inland (p. 234): true of the early Ming, not of the wokou wars of the 1550s–1560s.
  • Rebels broke the Yellow River dikes in 1642 (p. 245): supported by CHC 7, p. 635; the claim that the defenders cut them was not confirmed, so the deck follows Ebrey.
  • The fall of the Ming (pp. 244–245): the Manchu war after 1618 and its tax surcharges are not mentioned.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

The scholarship on the Ming has moved in four directions since the textbook's framework was set, and each affects the decks.

The founder and the despotism thesis. The older picture of an autocracy that deformed the whole dynasty, associated with Charles Hucker and F. W. Mote, remains useful but is qualified. Dreyer and Langlois give the founder's politics in detail; Farmer reads his legislation as a coherent program for a "fixed and stable" social order; Hok-lam Chan dismantled many of the stories of literary persecution; Sarah Schneewind traced how later ages invented their own Taizu. John Dardess's concise history (2012) argues for a "resilient empire," which is a useful counterweight to Lecture 1's "throne for giants."

The frontiers. Edward Dreyer's Zheng He (2007) replaced the inflated popular literature with a sober account of the voyages. Arthur Waldron's The Great Wall of China (1990) turned the wall from a timeless symbol into a sixteenth-century policy outcome, and Henry Serruys's volumes remain the base for Mongol trade. The 2009 Chinese national survey gives the current figures for the wall's length. Johan Elverskog has reframed Altan Khan's turn to Tibetan Buddhism from the Mongol side.

Silver and the global economy. Richard von Glahn's Fountain of Fortune (1996), Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez's work on 1571, and Timothy Brook's The Troubled Empire (2010) and Vermeer's Hat (2008) place the Ming at the center of the first global economy. Two debates matter for the decks: the relative weight of Japanese and American silver in the sixteenth century, and Atwell's thesis of a silver shock after 1639, which von Glahn and others have challenged.

Society, culture and collapse. Brook's Confusions of Pleasure, Clunas on material culture, Elman on the examinations, Faure and Dardess on lineages, Handlin Smith on charity, Furth on medicine and Ko on women readers support Lectures 3 and 4. On the fall, Kenneth Swope's Military Collapse of China's Ming Dynasty (2014) restores the Manchu war to the story, Geoffrey Parker's Global Crisis (2013) and Brook supply the climate history, and Roger Des Forges and the 2020 Kaifeng excavations by Michael Storozum and colleagues document the flood of 1642.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

The roadmap passes. Each lecture carries a single story arc: a founder and his throne (Lec1); two frontiers and the silver that crossed them (Lec2); one man's life through the examinations, philosophy and the villages (Lec3); the cities and the collapse (Lec4). The division of review questions is balanced, the Pierian Spring sections hold the optional material (Zhu's boyhood, the princes and the southwest, the Dalai Lama, Tan Yunxian, the Society for Sharing Goodness, Zhang Xianzhong) and the cross-deck recap slides (Lec4 N and Y) knit the chapter together.

Three structural weaknesses are worth fixing. First, Lecture 2's title promises that the Ming "closed the sea," but no slide explains the sea ban itself. Second, Lecture 4's account of the collapse lacks the Manchu war, although the next chapter begins with it and Lecture 4's own "Coming next" slide (AA) points to the Manchus. Third, the American crops appear in Lectures 2 and 3 as a cause of Ming population growth; their main demographic effect came under the Qing. None of these requires a new slide.

6. Individual background essay audits

Lecture 1, From Beggar Monk to Autocrat. Three acts on Review Questions 1–3 and a Pierian Spring on the princes and the southwest. Treats the founder's background and policies as linked but not determined; gives the correct sequence of 1368, the purges with the Hu and Lan Yu figures separated, the literary-inquisition caution, Yongle's contested maternity, the Yongle Encyclopedia, Beijing and the canal, Tumu, Hai Rui and Wanli. Status: draft for review.

Lecture 2, Two Frontiers. Three acts on Review Questions 4–6 and a Pierian Spring on the Dalai Lama and the wall. Explains the goals and the end of the voyages, the evolution of Mongol policy to the 1571 settlement, the wall as Waldron describes it, the sea ban, the wokou, the 1567 opening, Japanese and American silver, and the new crops. Notes the missing textbook pages. Status: draft for review.

Lecture 3, One Life Through the Ming World. Three acts on Review Questions 7–9. The examinations and their odds, the 1397 case and quotas, Tan Yunxian, Wang Yangming's life and doctrines, the Taizhou radicals, population and commerce, the lijia and the Single Whip, lineages, compacts and charity. Status: draft for review.

Lecture 4, The Garden and the Flood. Two acts on Review Questions 10–11. Urban culture from gardens to The Peony Pavilion; the collapse with the Manchu war restored, Wei Zhongxian, the climate, the rebels, the silver debate and the disputed Kaifeng flood. Status: draft for review.

All four essays follow the series format (central interpretation, story outline, acts keyed to review questions with textbook anchors, Pierian Spring, Corrections and Cautions, footnotes and annotated reading) and use the permanent slide letters.

8. Deck issues, verification and fixes applied

Each row names the permanent slide letter, the issue, the verdict with the source that settled it, and the action taken. "Confirmed" means a reliable source supports the correction; "Ebrey supported" means the check upheld the textbook; "Not confirmed" means no reliable source was found, so the textbook stands. The confirmed fixes have been applied to working copies of the four decks, pending Steven's approval before they are published. Every fix keeps the slide's letter and position, and no slide was added. Visible slides teach the confirmed history; the textbook's version is explained only in an instructor note with a brief "Source beyond Ebrey" line; and the confirming works are listed on each deck's Sources slide under "Further reading beyond Ebrey." All visible "outside the textbook" tags, old and new, have been removed from the four decks, and their sources kept in the notes and on the Sources slides.

Audit conclusion

The Chapter 14 decks are well built and faithful to Ebrey. Lecture 3 is the most accurate; Lectures 1 and 2 carried most of the textbook errors, because Ebrey's political and frontier narrative is where the chapter is weakest. Every item that would contradict the textbook was checked against a reliable source before any change. Corrections confirmed in that check have been applied to the visible slides: Lec1 G, CC and JJ; Lec2 J, L, M, O, R, U and Z. So have the overclaim softenings (Lec1 N, O, R and FF; Lec2 K, N, T and V; Lec3 X; Lec4 P and U) and the two omissions (the sea ban in Lec2 and the Manchu war in Lec4). In three cases the check supported Ebrey, so the slides keep her version: the Hu Weiyong toll (Lec1 M), Hai Rui's memorial of 1565 (Lec1 EE) and the rebels cutting the Kaifeng dikes (Lec4 W). Items not confirmed this pass were also left unchanged; they include the pass rates on Lec3 F, which need a check against the printed table. The four essays now teach the same history as the revised slides. The deck changes are in working copies and will not be published until Steven approves them.

The Han Founding and the Confucian State

Background for the lecture “The Han Founding and the Confucian State”: what Han kept from Qin and what it changed, how it managed kingdoms, court and the Xiongnu frontier, and how Emperor Wu paid for expansion and brought Confucian scholars into government · 206–87 BCE

Central interpretation

The lecture’s argument, in Steven Austin’s FA26 wording, is that Han did not reject Qin. Han rulers kept the centralized bureaucratic state while reducing some burdens, experimenting with frontier policy, expanding state economic power, and increasingly recruiting officials through Confucian learning. In shorthand: Confucian rhetoric plus Legalist bureaucratic methods. Qin built the imperial machine; Han made it durable, but not invulnerable.

The story in three parts

  1. Founding a More Durable Empire (Q3, slides E–T): the Qin collapse, Xiang Yu and Liu Bang, the civil war, Emperor Gao’s settlement, early recovery, selective inheritance, kingdoms and commanderies, local government; then the vulnerabilities to dynasty (decentralization, the frontier, the court) and the Rebellion of the Seven Kings; self-check S and the Q3 checkpoint T.
  2. Managing the Xiongnu Frontier (Q4, slides U–AB): who the Xiongnu were, the strategic problem, heqin and its two readings, Emperor Wu’s offensives; self-check AA and the Q4 checkpoint AB.
  3. Paying for Expansion and the Confucian State (Q5–Q6, slides AC–AN): Emperor Wu, war revenue and the monopolies of 119 BCE, Confucian learning in government, the scholar-official paradox, the synthesis, and why Han lasted; self-check AK, the Q5 (AL) and Q6 (AM) checkpoints, and the bridge to Lecture 3.

The Pierian Spring (AO–AR) carries Empress Lü’s regency, her clan’s near usurpation, and the recurring problem of regency, framed by Q3.

Part 1: Founding a More Durable Empire (Review Question 3)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 3, “The Han Dynasty” (pp. 42–43). The Reading Guide anchors Q3 on p. 42 with the sentence beginning “Emperor Gao did…” (he kept Qin’s centralized government but removed its most unpopular features).

The bridge from Qin (slide F). This is a brief bridge, not a second Qin lecture. Connect Lecture 1’s argument (Qin built a powerful but demanding imperial machine) to the 209–207 BCE rebellions and the collapse of Qin authority. Avoid implying that the entire population rebelled for one single reason; Ebrey notes that many rebels wanted the old states restored (p. 42).

Xiang Yu and Liu Bang (slides G–H). Avoid personality caricature. The contrast is institutional and political, not a moral fable: Liu Bang combined military force with coalition-building, rewards, appointments and alliances more effectively than Xiang Yu. Ebrey says almost nothing about Xiang Yu (he appears only in a document excerpt, p. 46); his career comes from Sima Qian’s Shiji 7. Ebrey’s own point is about social origins: the First Emperor came from the old Zhou aristocracy, Liu Bang from a modest family of commoners, which shows how thoroughly Qin had destroyed the old order (p. 42).

The settlement (slides I–K). Gaozu’s settlement answered exhaustion after war. The Han did not simply restore a peaceful version of Qin; it had to negotiate with the forces that had helped win the civil war. Reduced burdens plus retained capacity is the first instance of selective inheritance. Ebrey’s supporting details: capital at Chang’an, some laws eliminated, taxes cut, several decades of recovery (p. 42); officials graded by rank and salary, appointed for merit, subject to dismissal, demotion and transfer as under Qin; poll tax of 120 cash, a month of labor service, land tax of one-thirtieth (p. 43).

Kingdoms and commanderies (slide L). Use “semi-autonomous kingdoms,” not unqualified “feudal.” Steven’s mechanism is coalition politics: the allies who helped Liu Bang win expected rewards. Ebrey frames the same grants as a response to “the desire to restore the old order” and adds that Gao soon eliminated the non-relative fief holders, after which the fiefs of imperial relatives were gradually reduced (p. 42). The two framings are compatible; the deck keeps Steven’s and the notes cite Ebrey’s.

Local government (slide M). Students have heard that the Han was a “bureaucratic empire,” but need a concrete picture. Do not turn this into a detailed administrative chart. The concept is a chain: emperor, central government, commanderies, counties, local communities. Connect it to the chapter title, “The Founding of the Bureaucratic Empire.”

Vulnerabilities (slides N–R). The Xiongnu were a multiethnic steppe confederation, not a single tribe. “Barbarian Threat” is Steven’s requested student-facing heading; the notes explain that Han sources used hierarchical labels for peoples beyond the court’s cultural and political world. On eunuchs: some may have entered service as captives from southern populations, and elite officials often regarded them with contempt (pp. 44–45). Do not say court officials called eunuchs “southern barbarians”; keep the student-facing focus on proximity, kinship and access. The Rebellion of the Seven Kings (154 BCE) is not in Ebrey; it comes from Sima Qian (Shiji 106 on Liu Pi, King of Wu; 57 on Zhou Yafu). The outcome did not erase kingdoms immediately, but it reduced their capacity to act as independent political centers.

Part 2: Managing the Xiongnu Frontier (Review Question 4)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, “The Xiongnu and the Great Wall” and “The Han Dynasty” (pp. 41–43). The Reading Guide anchors Q4 on p. 43 with “After Emperor Gao’s debacle…”.

Who the Xiongnu were (slides V–W). Not an early version of the Mongols; a multiethnic confederation under a ruler called the chanyu, pastoral, highly mobile, with its own hierarchy, diplomacy, alliances and economic relationships, at times dealing with Han almost as an equal. The better question is not “Why couldn’t the Han simply defeat the Xiongnu?” but how an agricultural empire defends a fixed frontier against a mobile steppe confederation that does not have to fight by the same rules. Frame steppe mobility and imperial security needs as interacting constraints; the Han repeatedly combined policies rather than moving through a simple linear progression. The map is geographic orientation, not a separate content lecture.

Heqin (slides X–Y). Heqin was diplomacy, economic exchange and political symbolism, not simply “paying barbarians not to attack.” Each court could narrate the relationship differently (Ebrey: the Xiongnu considered the payments tribute, the Han preferred to see friendship, p. 43), and violence did not disappear. Ebrey adds the court critics’ two objections: the policy strengthened the enemy, and the raids continued anyway (p. 43).

Emperor Wu’s offensives (slide Z). Keep this concise. Wei Qing, Huo Qubing, Zhang Qian, the Central Asian campaigns and Silk Road detail belong in other lectures (Lecture 4 for the Silk Road). Ebrey judges the campaigns of limited value and notes that Han consolidated vacated land with Qin methods: forts, officials, settlers (p. 43).

Part 3: Paying for Expansion and the Confucian State (Review Questions 5–6)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey pp. 43–44. Q5 is anchored on p. 43 (“In 119 B.C.E.…”), Q6 on p. 44 (“Emperor Wu soon…”).

Emperor Wu and revenue (slides AD–AE). Emperor Wu was the first Han emperor to privilege Confucian scholars within government (p. 44). Do not imply that all rival traditions disappeared, that government became purely Confucian, or that Wu personally followed only Confucian ideas; Ebrey stresses his fascination with omens, spirits and immortals (p. 43). On the economy, avoid the absolute claim that the state changed from a tax collector into an economic actor. Earlier regulation existed; Wu expanded the scope and directness of intervention to meet imperial needs.

Confucian learning (slides AG–AI). Preserve Dong Zhongshu’s recommendations and the 124 BCE Imperial University. Recommendation and education increased Confucian influence; they did not create complete state orthodoxy or eliminate intellectual diversity. The scholar-official paradox is the central institutional point: officials could legitimate and administer imperial rule while also claiming a moral duty to criticize it. Treat the synthesis slide as the intellectual climax: “Confucian state” should not be mistaken for a government that abandoned coercive and bureaucratic techniques (Ebrey, p. 44). The closing synthesis (AJ) is institutional durability through adaptation, with the system still vulnerable to succession crises and court politics.

The Pierian Spring: Empress Lü and the Problem of Regency

The Empress Lü subsection moved from the main lecture to the Pierian Spring in the 2026 style pass (it answers no Reading Guide question directly and has its own companion mini-lecture). Define empress dowager briefly. Stress that Lü’s power came through motherhood, regency, control of appointments and access to the court; do not dwell on traditional stories about her cruelty. “Near usurpation” is the right term for the Lü clan’s royal titles and the Liu restoration of 180 BCE; keep the focus on clan power, succession and titles rather than motives we cannot prove. Use Wu Zetian and Cixi only as quick later examples; this is a structural problem of succession and delegated power, not a claim that all later dynasties used exactly the same institutions. Ebrey’s Wang Mang section (p. 44) shows the same pattern at the end of the Western Han.

Corrections and Cautions

  • Ebrey says that when Emperor Gao died “his heir was a child” and the empress dowager ruled until her death fifteen years later (p. 43). The deck follows Sima Qian: Emperor Hui reigned 195–188 BCE (he was a teenager at accession) and Lü then ruled through infant emperors until 180 BCE. The two accounts agree on the fifteen years.
  • The court-vulnerabilities slide treats eunuchs alongside early Han consort families; in Ebrey eunuch power belongs mainly to the Eastern Han, second century CE (pp. 44–45). The notes say so.
  • The 200 BCE encirclement of Emperor Gao by Maodun (Ebrey pp. 42–43), which the Q4 anchor sentence refers to as “Emperor Gao’s debacle,” and Maodun’s rise (p. 42) are not on any slide face; the notes and the Q4 checkpoint send students to the pages. Flagged for Steven.
  • Ebrey’s fuller list of Emperor Wu’s revenue measures (confiscating nobles’ land, selling offices and titles, property taxes on boats, carts and shops, p. 43) is in the notes, not on the slide face.
  • The date 124 BCE for the national university comes from Ban Gu’s Hanshu; Ebrey gives no date (p. 44).
  • Page references are to the course PDF of Ebrey’s Chapter 3 (pp. 36–55).

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Sima Qian, Records of the Grand Historian: Han Dynasty I–II, trans. Burton Watson (rev. ed. 1993). The basic narrative of Xiang Yu, Gaozu, Empress Lü, the Seven Kings and the Xiongnu.

Michael Loewe, The Government of the Qin and Han Empires, 221 BCE–220 CE (2006). A clear institutional survey of commanderies, kingdoms, ministries and recruitment.

Nicola Di Cosmo, Ancient China and Its Enemies: The Rise of Nomadic Power in East Asian History (2002). The standard study of the Xiongnu and of heqin as diplomacy rather than appeasement.

The Silk Road and China’s Wider World

Background for the lecture “The Silk Road and China’s Wider World”: why Emperor Wu looked west, what Zhang Qian found, how Han armies and foreign merchants opened the Silk Road, and how the trade changed China’s economy and its view of the world · about 140 BCE to 200 CE

Central interpretation

The lecture’s larger question: how did a search for allies against the Xiongnu connect China to the far side of Eurasia? Its answer, kept from Steven Austin’s FA26 framing: war pushed Han China west, and what it found there was a trade network already in motion. The Han did not invent Eurasian trade. Zhang Qian’s reports and the Ferghana campaigns changed China’s access to that trade, its security, and its scale, and they changed what the Han court knew about the world.

The story in three parts

  1. Looking West for Allies (Q11, slides D–I): the Xiongnu problem, the envoy concept, Zhang Qian’s first mission, his second mission and reports; self-check I.
  2. Armies, Caravans, and Silk (Q11, slides J–R): the Silk Road concept, the Ferghana campaigns of 104 and 102 BCE, the course map, the merchants, the goods that came east, silk making, and the Niya arm cover; self-check R.
  3. A Wider World (Q11, slides S–W): other civilizations, Ban Chao in the Western Regions, and what the trade changed in China’s economy; self-check W.

The Q11 checkpoint (X), the Chapter 3 synthesis (Y), and the bridge to Chapter 4 (Z) end the main lecture. The Pierian Spring (AA) carries three short stories, early Eurasian contacts (AB), the Bactrian camel (AC), and Buddhism on the road (AD), and a self-check (AE) that sends students back to Q11.

Part 1: Looking West for Allies (Review Question 11)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 3, “Central Asia and the Silk Road” (p. 52), with Emperor Wu’s campaigns against the Xiongnu on p. 43. The Reading Guide anchors Q11 on p. 52 with the sentence beginning “Through the trade…”.

The problem (slide E). The story begins with a security problem, not a commercial master plan. Emperor Wu’s armies of one hundred thousand to three hundred thousand troops were of limited value against the Xiongnu, who were a moving target: if they did not want to fight, they decamped, and Han troops could not carry enough food to stay long (p. 43). The immediate Han objective was strategic leverage, which is why the court looked for the Yuezhi, driven west by the Xiongnu several decades earlier (p. 52).

The mission and the report (slides F–H). The envoy concept slide (F) sets up the two halves of an envoy’s job: the official objective and the information he brings home. Zhang Qian’s direct objective failed; his report widened the Han court’s geographical and political horizon. Keep discovery distinct from creation: the report revealed a connected world that predated Han involvement, in which Chinese silk already moved west through merchants (p. 52). The deck dates the missions as historians do, from Sima Qian’s Records: the first from 138 BCE to about 126 BCE, including about ten years as a Xiongnu captive, and the second, to the Wusun, from about 119 to 115 BCE. Ebrey gives only “In 115 B.C.E., Zhang was sent again” (p. 52), which is the year he returned. The second mission belongs as a follow-up, not a second full narrative, which is how slide H treats it.

Part 2: Armies, Caravans, and Silk (Review Question 11)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, p. 52, and Material Culture: “Silk from the Silk Road” (p. 53), with the tomb scene of silk reeling and weaving on p. 48.

Ferghana and overlordship (slides L–M). Ebrey dates the campaigns to 104 and 102 BCE; the deck adds, from the Records, that Li Guangli’s first campaign failed and the second forced Ferghana to submit and surrender its horses. Ebrey and says recognition of Chinese overlordship gave China control over the trade routes; the oasis city-states did not resist because Chinese garrisons made their trade more convenient (p. 52). The old deck’s caution still applies in spirit: the Han projected power through overlordship and garrisons without governing every society across Central Asia, and security was uneven and negotiated with local powers. The deck now follows Ebrey’s wording (“control over the trade routes”) on the slide face and lets the overlordship link to Lecture 3 carry the nuance. The course map (M) places the Western Regions under a dashed overlordship zone rather than inside Han borders.

Merchants and goods (slides N–O). Avoid presenting “the Silk Road merchant” as one type. Ebrey names Sogdian, Parthian, and Indian merchants and two products, silk thread and cloth woven in Chinese or Syrian workshops (p. 52). Keep the list of goods selective; the point is two-way exchange: gold, horses, glass beads and cups east, and the four foods that came to be grown in China (p. 52), which is the Reading Guide’s anchor sentence.

Silk as evidence (slides P–Q). Use silk as material and political technology: sophisticated to produce, easy to transport relative to its value, and useful as prestige wealth. The Niya arm cover (Q) is the textile Ebrey illustrates (p. 53); it demonstrates movement through the network and ties silk to frontier war and to the Five Planets cosmology of Lecture 3.

Part 3: A Wider World (Review Question 11)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 52 and 55 (the opening of the Silk Road section and the chapter summary), Biography: “The Ban Family” (p. 48), and Emperor Wu’s war finance (p. 43).

A wider world became knowable (slide T). Avoid claiming a sudden shattering of assumptions. Han officials could retain a hierarchy with China at the center while incorporating practical knowledge of distant states. Ebrey supplies both halves: the Han learned theirs was not the only civilization with cities and writing (p. 52), and the gift system still confirmed that the Han court was the center of the civilized world (p. 55).

Ban Chao (slide U). New to the deck. Ebrey’s Ban Family biography says Han overlordship in the Western Regions had been lost since Wang Mang’s time; Ban Chao, sent in 73 CE, restored it through patient diplomacy and a show of force (p. 48). Ebrey dates his appointment as protector general to 92; the Hou Hanshu and standard references give 91 CE, which the deck uses, with Ebrey’s year noted in the slide notes. The slide shows that holding the routes took generations, and it links to the Ban family slide in Lecture 3.

Connection had a cost (slide V). Make the causal order explicit: strategic motive, then political and military expansion, then commercial and intellectual consequences, then rising costs. The deck uses Ebrey’s evidence for cost: Emperor Wu’s monopolies and taxes (p. 43) and the gift system, perhaps 10 percent of revenue, which also reduced the cost of defense (p. 55).

Checkpoint and synthesis (slides X–Y). Test causation and distinction, not recall of a catalog. The strongest Q11 answer identifies both the preexisting network and the Han role in changing access, security, and scale. The synthesis integrates all four lectures through Ebrey’s summary question, how different China was after four centuries of Qin and Han rule (p. 55).

The Pierian Spring: Early Contacts, the Camel, and Buddhism

The Pierian Spring is required independent study. Slide AB uses Ebrey’s Connections essay, “Buddhism in India and Its Spread Along the Silk Road” (p. 56): East Asia was never completely isolated, since wheat and the chariot came from west Asia in Shang times and animal art spread across the steppe in late Zhou times, with a real Xiongnu bronze plaque in place of Ebrey’s belt plaque (p. 43). Slide AC gives the Bactrian camel its own slide (p. 52), as the old deck’s note suggested. Slide AD is the one bridge to Buddhism; keep it to one bridge and leave monasteries, translation, and art to Chapter 4, Lecture 3. The old “In 184” bridge slide was dropped; Lecture 3 already prepares the crisis of 184, and the Chapter 4 decks open with it.

Corrections and Cautions

House rule (Steven Austin, 2026-11-18). Accurate dates may appear on slides even when Ebrey omits them; historical accuracy trumps Ebrey. Common or conventional knowledge is not attributed to Ebrey on slides. Ebrey’s key terms stay where students need to match the textbook.

Where the deck departs from Ebrey for accuracy. (1) Zhang Qian’s dates. Ebrey gives no date for the first mission and dates the second to 115 BCE. The deck gives 138 to about 126 BCE for the first and about 119 to 115 BCE for the second, following the Records and standard references (Britannica; Loewe). (2) Where he went. Ebrey says he reached “Bactria, Parthia, and Ferghana (in the region of modern Afghanistan)” (p. 52). He reached Ferghana, Sogdiana, and the Yuezhi in Bactria; Parthia and India he knew from reports, and his deputies reached them on the second mission. Ferghana lies north of Afghanistan, in the Ferghana Valley. The slide says he reached Ferghana and Bactria and heard of Parthia; the notes give Ebrey’s grouping. (3) Relay trade. Ebrey says Sogdian, Parthian, and Indian merchants carried silk “all the way to Rome” (p. 52). Recent scholarship (Hansen) stresses that goods moved in stages; the slide says silk changed hands in stages and eventually reached Rome, and the notes quote Ebrey. (4) Ban Chao. Protector general in 91 CE, not 92. (5) The Niya arm cover is usually dated to the late Han or Jin period, about the 2nd to 3rd century CE; Ebrey’s box does not date it, and the caption now does. (6) Silk thickness. Ebrey’s “0.25 millimeters thick” (p. 53) is too large for a single silk filament, which is closer to a hundredth of a millimeter; the deck says only that the filament is finer than a human hair. (7) The term “Silk Road” was coined by the German geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen in 1877; the concept slide no longer attributes the name to Ebrey, and the notes give its origin.

Corrections to the old FA26 deck. The old deck said Zhang Qian reached only Bactria and Ferghana and called Ferghana temporary influence rather than overlordship; the new deck follows the overlordship framing and the fuller list above. Its “heavenly horses” point returns in accurate form: the 102 BCE campaign won Ferghana’s prized horses. Its “glassware” is now Ebrey’s “glass beads and cups” (p. 52).

Notes

Every fact and date in the deck is drawn from Ebrey, Chapter 3. The course map uses Natural Earth public-domain geographic data. Dates and facts that Ebrey omits or gets wrong (Zhang Qian’s missions, Li Guangli’s campaigns, Ban Chao’s appointment in 91 CE, the Niya date, the Richthofen coinage) follow Sima Qian’s Records, the Hou Hanshu, and standard references such as Britannica and Loewe’s Biographical Dictionary of the Qin, Former Han, and Xin Periods (2000).

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Valerie Hansen, The Silk Road: A New History (2012). Builds the story from excavated documents at oasis sites, including Niya, and argues for local, relay-style trade.

Ying-shih Yü, Trade and Expansion in Han China: A Study in the Structure of Sino-Barbarian Economic Relations (1967). The classic study of Han tribute, trade, and frontier policy.

Michael Loewe, A Biographical Dictionary of the Qin, Former Han, and Xin Periods (221 BC–AD 24) (2000). Reliable dates for Zhang Qian, Li Guangli, and the Han court.

Nicola Di Cosmo, Ancient China and Its Enemies: The Rise of Nomadic Power in East Asian History (2002). The Xiongnu and the Han frontier, including Zhang Qian’s missions.

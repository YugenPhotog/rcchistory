The Shang King Ruled Through His Ancestors

Background for the lecture “The Shang Sacred State”: war kings, oracle bones, the king as high priest, and what the royal tombs reveal · ca. 1500–1045 BCE

Central interpretation

The Shang king was a war leader whose deepest claim to rule was religious. As high priest he was the person best qualified to reach the royal ancestors and the high god Di, and divination on bone turned that claim into a running record of royal decisions. The royal tombs at Anyang show the same system in material form: enormous wealth, women and men of the royal house as commanders, and ritual killing on a scale that linked war, sacrifice and the ancestors. Behind both stood coerced labor.

The story in three acts, matching Review Questions 2 and 3

  1. Setting and the war kings (slides B–H): dragon bones, the two river systems, the Neolithic background, Xia versus Shang, Anyang, and kings who fought, hunted and took captives.
  2. The sacred king (Q2, slides I–O): Di, nature spirits and ancestors; how pyromantic divination worked; Wu Ding’s questions; offerings; the king as high priest; why religion meant power.
  3. The tombs (Q3, slides P–X): the Xibeigang cemetery, Lady Hao’s intact tomb, her wealth and military command, followers and victims, the growth of sacrifice, commoners and coerced labor.

Act I: Setting and the War Kings

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 1, “The Geography of the Chinese Subcontinent” and the opening of “The Shang Dynasty” (pp. 8–11 in the course PDF).

The deck opens with the dragon-bone puzzle (slide B) because it gives students a reason to care before it asks them to learn anything. The geography slide (D) is deliberately brief: dry north with loess, millet and wheat; a dangerous Yellow River contained by dikes; a wetter Yangzi valley suited to rice; and the ring of barriers the textbook emphasizes on p. 10. The textbook’s chronology frames the Neolithic background (slide E): agriculture by about 10,000 BCE, distinct regional cultures by 4000, walled settlements by about 2500, and bronze-using civilization soon after 2000 BCE.

Slide F handles the Xia carefully. The textbook treats the Xia as a tradition that archaeology has not confirmed, while the Shang are documented by their own inscriptions. Many Chinese archaeologists identify Erlitou with the Xia; Western scholars tend to keep the question open because Erlitou has produced no writing. The deck uses an Erlitou jue and gu as a neutral image and does not take sides.

Slides G and H establish the Shang as a militarized polity before turning to religion: five capitals in tradition, Anyang as the last (ca. 1200–1045 BCE), excavations 1928–1937, palaces on rammed-earth platforms, armies of 3,000–5,000, hunts that doubled as military exercises, shifting alliances, captives as a resource, and the chariot from about 1200 BCE. Putting war first matters for the argument: students should see that religious legitimacy did not replace force but worked alongside it.

Act II: The Sacred King (Review Question 2)

Textbook anchor: p. 10, the paragraph beginning “The oracle bone…” and the discussion of Di, nature gods and ancestors that follows.

The textbook’s key sentence is careful: the king “acted as the high priest, the person best qualified to offer sacrifices to the royal ancestors and the high god, Di.” Di could command rain, thunder and wind; nature spirits (sun, moon, rivers, mountains) also received offerings; and royal ancestors could intervene with Di, send curses and dreams, and help in battle. Slides I–M follow that order: the spirit world, the mechanics of divination (prepared scapula or plastron, a charge stated aloud, heat applied to hollows, cracks read, the record carved), Wu Ding’s range of questions from rain and war to his toothache, and offerings of millet, wine, cattle, sheep and human victims.

Slide N turns the narrative into the causal mechanism students need for Q2. Divination did more than consult the spirits. It produced a record in which the king asked, the spirits answered, and outcomes were attributed to his ritual competence. Failures could be blamed on the wrong offering or the wrong ancestor rather than on the king. David Keightley’s work is the foundation for this reading: he argued that divination was a technique of royal decision-making and of legitimation, and that the Shang religious system and its political system were the same institution seen from two sides. K. C. Chang pushed further, arguing that the Shang king was a kind of chief shaman whose access to the spirit world, mediated by bronzes, animals and ritual, was the core of political authority. Many scholars now find Chang’s shamanism model overstated, but his insistence on the link between ritual art and power remains influential.

Source caution · oracle bones show the king’s view only

The oracle bones are royal records. They were produced by and for the court, mostly during the reigns of Wu Ding and later kings, and they show what kings worried about, not what farmers believed. Their inscriptions also survive unevenly: Wu Ding’s reign is heavily represented, later reigns more formulaic. Be careful about turning “the king was the person best qualified to sacrifice” into “only the king could approach the spirits.” Noble lineages also sacrificed to their own ancestors and owned ritual bronzes.

Answer synthesis · Q2

Ancestor worship made the royal dead powerful intermediaries with Di; divination was the technique by which the king consulted them on rain, harvest, war and family; and the king’s role as high priest made him the person best qualified to perform the offerings. Together they established his authority (descent from the most powerful ancestors) and maintained it (each recorded divination and each good outcome confirmed his ritual competence, while failures could be blamed on the offering).

Act III: The Tombs (Review Question 3)

Textbook anchor: pp. 11–12, the paragraph beginning “From inscribed bones…” on Lady Hao, the discussion of followers and victims, and the rammed-earth and labor passage beginning “Whatever the status…”.

Slides P–Q set up the evidence: the Xibeigang royal cemetery north of the Huan River, cross-shaped shaft tombs with ramps, nearly all looted, and the single unrobbed royal tomb, that of Lady Hao (Fu Hao), discovered in 1976. The textbook notes its relatively modest size (about 13 by 18 feet, about 25 feet deep) and its location outside the main royal cemetery. Slide R gives the wealth (about 1.6 metric tons of bronze, around sixty vessels inscribed with her name, 755 jades, 63 stone and 564 bone objects, and tribute objects from the south and the Northern Zone). Slide S gives her military role: 130 weapons in the tomb, the large yue axes, and inscriptions recording that she led campaigns, once with 13,000 troops against the Qiang.

Slides T–U handle sacrifice. The textbook distinguishes followers, who kept ornaments and might have coffins, from victims, often captives, decapitated or cut at the waist. It traces escalation from early graves with no more than three to a late royal tomb with 90 followers, 74 human sacrifices, 12 horses and 11 dogs; ceremonies of 300–400 captives; and the 1976 discovery of about 1,200 victims in 191 pits. Slides V–W widen the frame: nobles on rammed-earth platforms, commoners in semi-subterranean houses, specialized urban workshops, farmers with stone tools, and the textbook’s conclusion that coercion backed by violence was essential to the state. Roderick Campbell’s recent work makes the same link explicit: Shang kingship rested on violence and kinship together, with sacrifice as a public display of the king’s power over life and death.

Source caution · the followers’ “choice”

The textbook says followers “could decide” to accompany their lord. That is an inference from burial treatment, not a documented decision. Many scholars doubt that retainers had any meaningful choice. The deck reports the textbook’s distinction because the burial evidence for two categories is strong, but it flags the voluntariness question in the notes.

Answer synthesis · Q3

Hierarchy: royal tombs packed with bronze and jade contrast with commoners’ pit houses and farmers’ stone tools, and even the dead were ranked (followers versus victims). Military leadership: Lady Hao’s 130 weapons, yue axes and the 13,000-troop campaign show that royal rank and command went together, even for a royal woman. Wealth: 1.6 tons of bronze and tribute from distant regions show the reach of royal power. Ritual sacrifice: human and animal offerings in tombs and pits, fed by war captives, tie war directly to ancestor worship.

How the Two Questions Connect

Slide Y draws the lecture together as three mutually reinforcing supports: ancestors (religious authority), war (captives and tribute) and labor (walls and tombs). Students who can explain how one successful campaign strengthened all three can answer both questions well. The old deck framed the king’s role as a “religious monopoly.” The revised deck softens that to match the textbook’s “best qualified,” while keeping the core argument that religious legitimacy was the foundation of Shang kingship.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • “Monopoly” and “alone.” The previous deck said the king alone could reach Di and that Di was never petitioned directly. The textbook says the king was “best qualified,” and that Wu Ding asked Di or the ancestors. The revised deck follows the textbook. The scholarly picture is that Di rarely received direct offerings and was usually approached through ancestors, but the oracle bones do record charges about Di’s intentions.
  • What Di controlled. The previous deck said rain, wind and harvest; the textbook says rain, thunder and wind. Harvest was a concern Di affected through weather. The deck uses the textbook’s list.
  • The largest vessel. The textbook gives 875 kg for the largest Shang bronze. The Houmuwu ding is usually cited at about 830 kg (832.84 kg) in modern measurements. The deck now shows “about 830 kilograms” with the textbook’s 875 kg beside it, on the face of slide Z (which since 2026-10-24 shows a photograph of the vessel itself) and in the notes for slide L. The earlier “800-kilogram” wording on slide Z has been corrected.
  • The first farming. The textbook dates the first farming in north China to about 10,000 BCE (p. 10). Current archaeobotanical work (Lu et al., PNAS 2009) places the beginnings of millet cultivation at about 8000 BCE. Slide E shows 8000 BCE with the textbook’s 10,000 beside it, and its source line marks the newer date as outside the textbook.
  • Chariot dates. The textbook says chariots came into use about 1200 BCE (p. 10), but a later figure caption on a chariot burial says about 1100 BCE (p. 18). The deck uses about 1200 BCE.
  • Discovery of the oracle bones. The textbook says the bones were connected to the Shang “after 1898.” The popular story of Wang Yirong recognizing writing on medicinal dragon bones in 1899 is probably partly legend. The deck presents it as a story and keeps the textbook’s cautious date.
  • Shang silk in Egypt. The textbook mentions silk in an Egyptian tomb (the OCR text reads “comb”). The claim, based on silk fibers reported from a Deir el-Medina burial of about 1000 BCE, is debated. The deck mentions it once, in the notes for slide AB, with the textbook page and a note that specialists dispute the identification; Lecture 2 states it as disputed on slide AI.
  • Details from outside the textbook. Lady Hao conducting sacrifices, the 16 human victims and 6 dogs in her tomb, the YH127 archive pit of 1936 (about 17,000 pieces) and the 1908 tracing of the bones to Anyang come from excavation reports and oracle-bone scholarship. The notes flag each one.
  • Images that are not Shang originals. The rammed-earth wall photo on slide W is an Eastern Zhou wall at Xinzheng (same technique). The yue axes on slides S and Y are museum copies. Slide T shows a reconstruction of Lady Hao’s tomb. The Lady Hao statue on slide Q is modern. Captions say so.
  • The hydraulic hypothesis. The previous deck’s notes suggested that dike building pushed early China toward large-scale organization. That echoes Karl Wittfogel’s “hydraulic society” thesis, which most historians now reject as a general explanation. The revised deck mentions dikes only as a feature of the landscape.
  • AI images. Seven images are AI-generated and labeled: the title collage, the Act I, II and IV dividers (slides AG, AI and AL; AG and AL approved by Steven on 2026-10-24), the king reading an oracle bone (slide M), Lady Hao leading her army (slide S) and the dragon-bone shop (slide AD). The Act I scene shows a stone-looking village wall; Shang walls were pounded earth, and the notes say so. Do not cite them as evidence.
  • How corrections are handled. Where the textbook’s figure is out of date, the slide shows the corrected figure with the textbook’s number beside it, and the student notes explain only briefly. The fuller explanation lives in this section, for instructor preparation.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

David N. Keightley, Sources of Shang History: The Oracle-Bone Inscriptions of Bronze Age China (1978). The foundational English guide to how the bones were made, read and dated.

David N. Keightley, The Ancestral Landscape: Time, Space, and Community in Late Shang China (2000). What the inscriptions reveal about the Shang worldview, ancestors and Di.

K. C. Chang, Art, Myth, and Ritual: The Path to Political Authority in Ancient China (1983). The classic argument that control of ritual and communication with the spirits was the path to power.

Li Feng, Early China: A Social and Cultural History (2013). The clearest recent survey, strong on Erlitou, Anyang and the Shang–Zhou transition.

Roderick B. Campbell, Violence, Kinship and the Early Chinese State: The Shang and Their World (2018). Sacrifice, warfare and lineage as the building blocks of Shang power.

Robert L. Thorp, China in the Early Bronze Age: Shang Civilization (2006). An archaeologist’s synthesis of Anyang, Zhengzhou and the royal tombs.

Michael Loewe and Edward L. Shaughnessy, eds., The Cambridge History of Ancient China (1999). The reference work; see Keightley’s chapter on the Shang and Bagley’s on Shang archaeology.

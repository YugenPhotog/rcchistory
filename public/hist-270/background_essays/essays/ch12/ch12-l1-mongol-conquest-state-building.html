The Mongols Conquered China in Two Stages

Background for the lecture "Mongol Conquest and State-Building": steppe conquest, learning to tax, Khubilai's choice, and the river war · 1206–1279

Central interpretation

The Mongols conquered China in two very different stages. In the north (1211–1234) they fought as steppe raiders, taking plunder, killing on a vast scale and only slowly learning to tax what they had won. In the south (1268–1279) they won as an empire, with Chinese advisers, a Chinese-crewed navy, Muslim siege engineers and an army that was mostly Chinese. Khubilai's Yuan dynasty was the product of that learning: a Chinese-style state ruled by a khan who never stopped being a Mongol.

The story in four acts, matching Review Questions 1–4

  1. The Conquest of the North (Q1): Chinggis unites the steppe, crushes Xia and Jin, and the north pays a terrible price.
  2. Learning to Tax (Q2): Yelü Chucai argues that farmers are worth more alive, and merchant tax-farmers compete for the job.
  3. Khubilai's Choice (Q3): Chinese advisers, a civil war and a new capital at Dadu produce the Yuan dynasty.
  4. The River War (Q4): Xiangyang, trebuchets, Bayan's army and the last stand at Yashan.

Act I: The Conquest of the North (Review Question 1)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 12, the section on Chinggis and the conquest of Xia and Jin (around p. 198 in the course PDF).

Temüjin's rise is told in the Secret History of the Mongols, the only account written by Mongols themselves. It is frank, even about the young Temüjin killing his half-brother, but it is still a family story, written to honor Chinggis's line. In 1206 a great assembly (quriltai) proclaimed him Chinggis Khan. He reorganized his followers into decimal units of tens, hundreds and thousands that cut across old tribal loyalties, and built a guard drawn from the sons of commanders. Those two reforms turned a confederation into an army that answered to one man.

The Tangut Xia submitted first (1209–1210), giving Chinggis a tributary on his flank. In 1211 he turned on the Jurchen Jin. The textbook's numbers are worth remembering because they make the point clearly: Chinggis led roughly 50,000 bowmen, with a similar force under his sons, against a Jin army said to number 150,000 cavalry and 300,000 infantry. The Mongols won by speed, discipline and the Jin's own internal weakness, and at first they did not try to hold territory. Their pattern was to plunder and withdraw. Zhongdu (Beijing) fell in 1215. Jin moved its court south to Kaifeng, which held out until 1233; the dynasty ended in 1234. Xia, which had rebelled, was destroyed in 1227, the year Chinggis died.

The human cost

North China's registered population fell drastically between the late Jin and the early Mongol censuses. Students should not read this as a simple body count. The drop combines killing, famine, epidemic disease, flight south, enslavement and people simply vanishing from tax registers. Historians argue about how much each factor contributed; what is not in doubt is that the conquest of the north was among the most destructive episodes in Chinese history.

Answer synthesis · Q1

Chinggis built a new kind of steppe army through decimal units and personal loyalty, used it to make Xia a vassal and then to break Jin in the field, and at first treated North China as a place to plunder rather than govern. The result was military success at an enormous human cost.

Act II: Learning to Tax (Review Question 2)

Textbook anchor: around p. 199 (Ögödei, Yelü Chucai, the pasture proposal).

Under Ögödei (r. 1229–1241) some Mongol advisers proposed clearing the North China plain of its farmers and turning it into pasture. The Khitan official Yelü Chucai answered with numbers: regular taxes on land, commerce, wine, vinegar, salt and mining could bring in 500,000 ounces of silver, 80,000 bolts of silk and more than 20,000 tons (400,000 shi) of grain each year. Ögödei let him try, and tax bureaus were set up across the north. The textbook also preserves smaller rescues from the same years: when Kaifeng fell in 1233, the poet Yuan Haowen wrote asking that 54 men of letters be spared.

The reform did not last in its pure form. Central Asian merchants, often organized in partnerships called ortogh, offered to pay the court a fixed sum in advance for the right to collect taxes. This tax farming pleased a court that wanted silver quickly, but it encouraged collectors to squeeze as much as they could. By the 1240s three systems overlapped: Chinese-style bureaus, merchant tax farming and the shares of land and people granted to Mongol princes and commanders as appanages.

Source caution

Almost everything we know about Yelü Chucai comes from his epitaph and from the Yuan shi biography based on it, both written by admirers. They present him as a lone Confucian voice saving civilization. Historians such as Igor de Rachewiltz and Thomas Allsen accept that he mattered but read the pasture debate as partly a set piece. Treat the dialogue as a later, polished account of a real policy argument.

Answer synthesis · Q2

Yelü Chucai persuaded Ögödei that settled farmers taxed regularly were worth more than empty pasture, and bureaus were created to collect from them. Merchant tax farmers then competed for the same revenue, so Mongol rule in the north mixed Chinese bureaucracy, Central Asian commerce and steppe appanages.

Act III: Khubilai's Choice (Review Question 3)

Textbook anchor: around p. 201 (Khubilai, his advisers, Dadu, the Yuan name).

Khubilai, grandson of Chinggis, was put in charge of North China under his brother Möngke. He gathered advisers of many backgrounds. The monk-official Liu Bingzhong held "Three Teachings" views mixing Buddhism, Daoism and Confucianism; Yao Shu and others urged restraint. The textbook tells how, during the Dali campaign in Yunnan (1253), advisers quoted Mencius that only a ruler who "takes no pleasure in killing" can unify the realm. Khubilai never learned to read Chinese, but he learned how Chinese political language worked.

When Möngke died in 1259, Khubilai and his brother Ariq Böke each claimed the title of Great Khan. The textbook calls the result a four-year civil war (1260–1264). Khubilai won with the resources of North China, which tied him to it. He began building a new capital, Dadu (modern Beijing), around 1267, kept Shangdu as a summer capital in the grasslands, and in 1271 proclaimed the dynastic name Yuan, "origin," taken from the Book of Changes. His government added a new level of administration, the province, which survives in China today.

Historians still debate how "Chinese" Khubilai became. Morris Rossabi's biography stresses his use of Chinese forms; others emphasize that he remained a Mongol khan who kept Mongols and Central Asians above Chinese and treated Chinese institutions as tools. The deck's slide V frames this as "emperor and khan" deliberately: both answers are partly right.

Answer synthesis · Q3

Khubilai combined Chinese advisers and institutions (a Chinese capital, a dynastic name, provinces) with Mongol rule. He won the civil war because he held North China, and governing it in Chinese style made him a more effective conqueror of the south, without making him any less a khan.

Act IV: The River War (Review Question 4)

Textbook anchor: around p. 201 (Xiangyang, Bayan, the fall of Hangzhou, Yashan, Wen Tianxiang).

The Southern Song was protected by rivers, so the Mongols had to fight on water. The siege of the twin cities Xiangyang and Fancheng on the Han River lasted five years (1268–1273), with thousands of boats and tens of thousands of troops on each side. The Mongols built a navy largely with Chinese shipwrights and sailors, guided by the Song defector Liu Zheng. In 1271 Khubilai asked his relative Abaqa, ruler of the Ilkhanate in Iran, for siege engineers. Ismail and Ala al-Din built counterweight trebuchets; Fancheng fell early in 1273 and Lü Wenhuan surrendered Xiangyang in March.

In 1274 Bayan led about 200,000 men, mainly Chinese, down the Yangzi. In 1275 he defeated Jia Sidao's force of about 130,000 men and 2,500 ships; the massacre at Changzhou was used as a scare tactic to frighten other cities into surrender. Hangzhou surrendered in 1276. Loyalists held out until the naval battle at Yashan in 1279, where Lu Xiufu leapt into the sea with the boy emperor. Wen Tianxiang refused all offers to serve and was executed in 1283.

Tonio Andrade and Peter Lorge both use Xiangyang to argue that the Song–Yuan wars were a turning point in gunpowder and naval warfare. Students are often surprised that the decisive Mongol victory in China came from boats, bombs and imported engineers rather than from cavalry.

Answer synthesis · Q4

Technology (Muslim counterweight trebuchets, gunpowder bombs), multiethnic forces (Chinese sailors and soldiers, Iranian engineers, Song defectors) and naval power (the Han River blockade, the Yangzi fleet, Yashan) worked together. None alone would have been enough; together they let a steppe empire conquer a river-and-coast state.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Khubilai's reign. The course textbook prints "1360–1394" in one place. That is a typo; Khubilai reigned 1260–1294.
  • Dadu's date. The textbook associates Dadu with 1264 (when the capital was designated); construction of the new city began in 1267. The deck uses "around 1267" for the building.
  • Trebuchet stones. The textbook says "up to a hundred pounds"; the Yuan shi gives 150 jin, about 90 kg. The deck says "a hundred pounds or more."
  • Marco Polo and the trebuchets. Polo's claim that his family built the Xiangyang engines is false; the siege ended before they reached China.
  • Hao Jing, Khubilai's envoy to the Song, was detained for 16 years.
  • Population figures for North China reflect lost registration as well as deaths; do not present them as a death toll.
  • AI images in the deck are labeled as interpretations. Do not cite them as evidence.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Morris Rossabi, Khubilai Khan: His Life and Times (1988). The standard biography, readable and balanced on the "how Chinese was he" debate.

David Morgan, The Mongols, 2nd ed. (2007). The best short introduction to the whole empire.

Thomas T. Allsen, Mongol Imperialism (1987) and Culture and Conquest in Mongol Eurasia (2001). How the Mongols taxed, counted and moved people and knowledge across Eurasia.

Ruth W. Dunnell, Chinggis Khan: World Conqueror (2010). A concise life that is careful about sources, and the leading scholar of the Tangut Xia.

Igor de Rachewiltz, trans., The Secret History of the Mongols (2004). The authoritative translation and commentary; also his essays on Yelü Chucai.

Frances Wood, Did Marco Polo Go to China? (1995). The skeptical case. Read with Hans Ulrich Vogel, Marco Polo Was in China (2013), for the response.

Tonio Andrade, The Gunpowder Age (2016), and Peter Lorge, The Asian Military Revolution (2008). Xiangyang and the Song–Yuan wars in the history of gunpowder warfare.

Herbert Franke and Denis Twitchett, eds., The Cambridge History of China, vol. 6 (1994). The reference work for Jin, Xia and Yuan institutions and dates.

Columbia University, Asia for Educators, "The Mongols in World History." Free primary sources and maps, afe.easia.columbia.edu/mongols.

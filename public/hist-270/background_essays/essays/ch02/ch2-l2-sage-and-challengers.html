The Sage and His Challengers: Confucius, Mozi, Mencius and Xunzi

Central interpretation

Confucius answered the disorder of the Eastern Zhou with a claim that sounded impractical to every ruler who heard it: a state is put right by the moral example of those who govern, not by force or punishment, and the qualities that fit a man to govern come from study and self-cultivation, not from birth. Every later thinker in this lecture defined himself against that claim. Mozi tested Confucian family love, ritual and music by a single standard, whether they benefited the people, and found them wasteful and partial. Mencius and Xunzi both defended Confucius, but they disagreed about where goodness comes from: Mencius found its sprouts in human nature and made government a matter of nurturing them, while Xunzi held that people are born selfish and made ritual, teachers and strict institutions the means of reshaping them. The argument over human nature was in the end an argument about what government is for.

The story in three segments, matching Review Questions 5–7

  1. Confucius: Rule by Example (Q5, slides E–N): the Analects, the ideal early Zhou, filial piety and the five relationships, the concept of ren and li, rule by virtue, the redefined junzi, study and the spirits.
  2. Mozi: Does It Help? (Q6, slides O–V): the craftsman and universal love, the concept of utility, mourning and music, the Mohist siege defenders, the Confucian reply.
  3. Is Human Nature Good? (Q7, slides W–AF): the concept of human nature, Mencius and King Hui, the child at the well, Gaozi and water, Xunzi’s selfish nature, the boat and the water, ritual and music.

The cold open (slide B) follows Confucius on the road looking for a ruler who would listen; the closing slide (AG) returns to him as the teacher no ruler would hire and points to the Daoists and Legalists of Lecture 3. The Pierian Spring (AH–AT) carries all of Herbert Fingarette’s argument (Q5) and finishes two textbook stories, Mencius on Heaven (Q7) and Confucius on Guan Zhong (Q5).

Act I: Rule by Example (Review Question 5)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 2, “The Hundred Schools of Thought” and “Confucius and the Analects” (pp. 26–27), with the box on Confucian virtues (p. 26) and the Guan Zhong biography (p. 22). The Reading Guide anchors Q5 on pp. 26–27.

The cold open (slide B) uses the textbook’s plain account of a failed career: Confucius served in Lu without gaining much influence, left, and wandered through neighboring states with students looking for a ruler who would take his advice (p. 26). The route map follows the traditional itinerary of 497–484 BCE from the Shiji, which the notes mark as tradition. Opening on failure sets up the closing irony: the man no ruler would employ became the most influential teacher in Chinese history. The textbook gives Confucius’s dates as “early historians dated” them, 551–479 BCE, and the deck keeps that hedge.

Slide F explains the Analects as sayings recorded by disciples and later followers, compiled over generations, so students treat “Confucius said” as “the Confucian tradition remembered him saying.” The Dunhuang manuscript of 890 CE on slide C shows how long the book was copied by hand. Slide G presents Confucius’s golden age, the early Zhou and the Duke of Zhou, where everyone played his part; it recaps the three founders from Chapter 1, Lecture 3. Slide H gives filial piety and the five relationships (ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife, elder and younger brother, friend and friend), using the textbook’s box on virtues. The Li Gonglin Classic of Filial Piety scroll (about 1085 CE) is much later than Confucius, and the caption says so.

Slide I is the concept slide that the rest of the act depends on. Ren is glossed as humanity, benevolence or perfect goodness, and li as ritual and propriety, the proper form in which care is expressed. The deck’s teaching formula, “ren is the care; li is the form it takes,” is an interpretive simplification for first-year students, and the notes say so. Slide J teaches Analects 12.2 on ren and 2.3, the contrast between leading by regulations and punishments, which makes people evasive and shameless, and leading by virtue and ritual, which gives them a sense of shame and makes them good (p. 27). This is the heart of Q5’s “political philosophy,” and the slide links to the Pierian Spring, where Fingarette argues that this kind of rule works the way ritual does.

Slide K teaches the redefinition of the junzi. The word originally meant “son of a lord,” a status by birth; Confucius used it for a man of moral cultivation, contrasted with the “petty man” who thinks of profit (pp. 26–27). Students should connect this with Lecture 1’s shi: a ruler choosing officials could now ask whether a man was a junzi in the moral sense. The Wu family shrine scholars (2nd century CE) illustrate how the Han remembered the type. Slide L covers learning and the spirits: Confucius denied being born wise and said he loved the ancients and tried to learn from them (7.19), found a day and night of thinking useless compared to study (15.30), sacrificed as though the spirits were present, and talked mostly of society and politics rather than gods or ghosts (p. 27). The Zheng bronze gui recaps Chapter 1’s ancestor offerings.

Source caution · how much of the Analects is Confucius?

The deck’s description of the Analects as compiled by later followers is the scholarly consensus. Some scholars, notably E. Bruce Brooks and A. Taeko Brooks (The Original Analects, 1998), argue that the book accreted over two and a half centuries and that only a small core (often located in Book 4) goes back to Confucius’s own circle; others, including Edward Slingerland, treat the text as reasonably coherent while accepting layering. The deck says “the Analects says” or “Confucius, as the Analects remembers him,” and avoids strong biographical claims.

Act II: Does It Help? (Review Question 6)

Textbook anchor: the Mozi section on pp. 27–28, with the bell set photograph and caption (p. 28). The Reading Guide anchors Q6 on p. 27.

Slide P introduces Mozi (ca. 480–390 BCE) as a well-read man from among the master craftsmen rather than the aristocracy (p. 27); the modern portrait is labeled as imagined. The deck presents “concern for everyone” (jian ai, often translated “universal love”) as the opening move: giving other people’s families and states the concern one gives one’s own, with Mozi’s two arguments (whom you would trust with your family, and the sage kings’ example) and his strong support for the merit principle (p. 27). Confucians heard it as an attack on the graded love that starts at home. Slide Q is the concept slide for utility: judge any idea or custom by whether it benefits people. The Mozi’s own standards (enriching the poor, increasing the population, bringing order) are beyond the textbook and stay in the notes; “utility” is the Reading Guide’s word and is defined on the slide, with a note that the term “utilitarian” is a modern Western comparison.

Slide R applies the test to the two Confucian practices Mozi attacked most sharply: elaborate funerals with three years of mourning, which wasted wealth and kept people from work, and ceremonial music, which cost labor and metal and fed no one. The bells of Marquis Yi of Zeng (433 BCE) show exactly the kind of investment Mozi had in mind; the caption corrects the textbook’s wording (see Corrections). Slide S covers the Mohist opposition to aggressive war and their organized teams of siege defenders, who went to help the attacked; it recaps Lecture 1’s siege slide. The Mozi includes technical chapters on defensive engineering, beyond the textbook, which explain why the Xinzheng rammed-earth wall makes a good image. Slide T shows how the argument came out (p. 28): the Mohist school declined and lost its identity; Confucians absorbed the merit principle and the criticism of extravagance; Mencius borrowed Mozi’s arguments against aggression; but Confucians rejected equal treatment of everyone as unnatural and refused to put ritual and music to a utility test. Mencius’s polemic that Mohist love “acknowledges no father” (Mencius 3B.9) is a good extension, beyond the textbook.

Act III: Is Human Nature Good? (Review Question 7)

Textbook anchor: the Mencius section (pp. 28–29) and the Xunzi section (pp. 29–30), with the inlaid flask caption (p. 30). The Reading Guide anchors Q7 on pp. 29–30.

Slide X defines “human nature” (xing) as what people are like before teaching shapes them, so students see that Mencius and Xunzi share the question while disagreeing on the answer. Slide Y stages Mencius 1A.1, the meeting with King Hui of Liang (Wei), where the king asks what will profit his state and Mencius rejects the word “profit” in favor of humaneness and right; the textbook uses the passage to show Mencius’s view of benevolent government, light taxes and opposition to war for gain (pp. 28–29). The Ming woodblock series on Mencius’s life supplies the image. Slide Z gives the child-at-the-well argument (2A.6): anyone would feel alarm and compassion, not for any advantage, which proves the presence of the “sprouts” of goodness. Slide AA gives the debate with Gaozi (6A.2), in which Gaozi compares nature to water that flows east or west as channeled, and Mencius answers that water always flows downward, as human nature tends toward good.

Slide AB turns to Xunzi: people are born with desires and envy, and goodness is the product of conscious effort, as a warped board must be steamed and pressed straight or metal ground on a whetstone (p. 29). Slide AC presents the political conclusion: Xunzi’s image of the ruler as a boat and the people as water that can carry or capsize it, and his insistence that Heaven does not respond to prayer, so rain after a rain dance is simply rain (p. 29). The slide recaps the Mandate of Heaven from Chapter 1, Lecture 3, because Xunzi keeps the idea that rulers can lose their people while stripping Heaven of agency. Slide AD explains why Xunzi nonetheless kept ritual and music: they shape desires and give feelings proper expression, and their value lies in what they do for people, not in any effect on spirits (p. 30). It links to the Pierian Spring, where Fingarette’s question of whether ritual is sincere returns.

Source caution · “good” and “bad” are rough translations

Mencius’s claim is that human nature is shan (good) in the sense of having innate tendencies toward goodness that must be nurtured, not that people are born virtuous; Xunzi’s chapter title Xing e is often rendered “Human Nature Is Bad,” but e here means ugly, crude or unformed as much as evil. Both men agreed that cultivation is necessary. The deck uses the textbook’s “good” and “bad” on the slide faces and explains the nuance in the notes, so students do not caricature either position.

The Pierian Spring: Fingarette, Mencius on Heaven, Guan Zhong

The required independent-study section opens with the course’s standard divider wording and contains twelve story slides, each ending with a review-question box that links back to its checkpoint. Following the arc-plan decision of 2026-10-29, all of the Fingarette material from the archived “sacred as secular” page and the old Lecture 2 Pierian Spring is here, rewritten as a ten-slide sequence for Q5:

  1. AI, the puzzle: is Confucianism a religion? Fingarette (Confucius: The Secular as Sacred, 1972) argued that the question assumes a Western template of creed, deity and afterlife.
  2. AJ, li as performance: ritual turns ordinary acts into shared, meaningful performances.
  3. AK, the handshake: Fingarette’s own example of meaning living in a recognized form, illustrated with the Promontory Summit handshake of 1869.
  4. AL, everyday li: family and social life as the arena of cultivation.
  5. AM, ritual as “magic”: Fingarette’s provocative term for the way a correct ritual act changes social reality without force.
  6. AN, sincerity: the charge that ritual is empty form and the answer that form can train sincerity (compare Xunzi on slide AD).
  7. AO, religious in a broader sense: holiness located in human community.
  8. AP, comparison table: a deliberately rough contrast with a common Abrahamic pattern, labeled as a simplification.
  9. AQ, limits: critics, notably Benjamin Schwartz, who argued that Fingarette underplays Confucius’s inner moral life, Heaven (tian) and the spirits.
  10. AR, modern rituals: graduations, oaths and apologies as secular-sacred acts students already know.

Two further slides finish textbook stories: AS, Mencius on Heaven speaking through the people and a true king arising every five hundred years (p. 29), for Q7; and AT, Confucius defending Guan Zhong against a disciple (Analects 14.18, quoted in the Guan Zhong biography, p. 22), for Q5, with a recap link to Lecture 1’s Guan Zhong slide. The online study notes stress that the Fingarette sequence is one modern philosopher’s interpretation, not the textbook’s, and every slide carries an “outside the textbook” tag.

Scholarly context · Fingarette and his critics

Fingarette, a philosopher trained in the analytic tradition and influenced by J. L. Austin’s idea of performative utterances, read the Analects without the later Neo-Confucian vocabulary of mind and inner nature. His book was enormously influential in the West and helped make Confucius a subject for philosophers. Benjamin Schwartz (The World of Thought in Ancient China, 1985) replied that Confucius clearly cared about inner disposition and that Fingarette’s “no inner psyche” reading was a twentieth-century behaviorism projected backward. Later scholars (Tu Weiming, Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, Philip J. Ivanhoe) have developed or qualified his reading. The deck presents Fingarette as a productive lens and gives the main objection a slide of its own.

How the Three Questions Connect

The lecture’s single thread is Confucius’s claim that order comes from moral example, and each segment tests it. Q5 states the claim and its foundations. Q6 attacks it from outside: Mozi accepts the goal of order but measures Confucian means by benefit and finds them wasteful. Q7 defends it from inside, but the defenders split over whether the example awakens goodness already present (Mencius) or imposes goodness on raw material (Xunzi). Students who can place each thinker on two axes, where goodness comes from and what the state should therefore do, have the lecture’s argument. The closing slide sets up Lecture 3, where the Daoists reject the whole project and the Legalists, two of them Xunzi’s students in tradition, keep only his pessimism.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Xunzi’s dates (textbook p. 29: ca. 310–ca. 215 BCE). Most reference works place Xunzi’s death around 238–235 BCE, shortly after he lost his post at Lanling in Chu; a death as late as 215 would put him under the unified Qin empire. Slide AB follows the textbook’s dates; the notes give the usual range.
  • The Xunzi passage cited as “(13)” (p. 29). The quotation beginning with human nature being bad and goodness being the product of conscious activity comes from chapter 23, “Human Nature Is Bad” (Xing e), not chapter 13. Slide AB cites chapter 23 in the notes and explains the discrepancy.
  • The bell set caption (p. 28). The caption calls it a “64-piece bell set” of the “Marquis of Yi.” The set from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng (Suizhou, Hubei, 433 BCE) is usually counted at 65 bells (64 chime bells plus a large bo bell given by the king of Chu), and “Marquis Yi of Zeng” is the correct name. Slide R’s face uses the caption’s point, not its numbers; the notes correct both.
  • Arc plan page references. The Chapter 2 arc plan put Mencius’s child at the well, Gaozi, Heaven and Ran Qiu on p. 28; in the course PDF they are on p. 29. The deck cites p. 29.
  • The “Golden Rule.” The saying “Do not do unto others what you would not have them do unto you” appears in Analects 12.2 (the textbook’s citation) and again in 15.24. It is often called a negative Golden Rule; slide J quotes the textbook’s translation and avoids the Western label on its face.
  • Mozi’s origins. The textbook places Mozi among the master craftsmen (p. 27), and slide P follows it. Some scholars note that this is inferred from his teachings and his followers’ engineering skills, and that “Mo” may even be a nickname connected with tattooed convicts or ink-line carpentry; the point is debated.
  • Mencius and “benefit.” Mencius’s rejection of “profit” in 1A.1 does not mean he ignored material welfare; he insisted on light taxes and secure livelihoods (p. 29). The notes guard against reading him as indifferent to economics.
  • Old deck classroom material. The Summer 2026 deck and the archived Fingarette page had in-class prompts, discussion questions and suggested answers; the revised deck turns them into Study Buddy notes and self-checks with the exact Reading Guide wording on checkpoints and no suggested answers. The syntheses above are for instructor preparation only.
  • Images. No image is AI-generated. The portraits of Confucius, Mencius, Zigong, the Duke of Zhou and Mozi are imagined, from much later periods, and the captions say so; Ma Yuan’s water paintings (ca. 1200 CE) are used as visual metaphors for the Gaozi and Xunzi arguments.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Herbert Fingarette, Confucius: The Secular as Sacred (1972). The short, provocative book behind the Pierian Spring.

Benjamin I. Schwartz, The World of Thought in Ancient China (1985). The fullest survey of the classical thinkers, with the best-known critique of Fingarette.

A. C. Graham, Disputers of the Tao: Philosophical Argument in Ancient China (1989). Indispensable on Mozi, the Mohists and the human nature debate.

Edward Slingerland, trans., Confucius: Analects, with Selections from Traditional Commentaries (2003). A readable translation with commentary on each passage.

E. Bruce Brooks and A. Taeko Brooks, The Original Analects (1998). The strongest case for reading the Analects as layered over centuries.

Ian Johnston, trans., The Mozi: A Complete Translation (2010). Includes the defensive engineering chapters.

Bryan W. Van Norden, trans., Mengzi: With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (2008). The standard teaching translation of Mencius.

Eric L. Hutton, trans., Xunzi: The Complete Text (2014). The complete modern translation; chapter 23 is “Human Nature Is Bad.”

Philip J. Ivanhoe and Bryan W. Van Norden, eds., Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy (2nd ed., 2005). Convenient selections from all four thinkers.

The Way and the Law: Daoists and Legalists

Central interpretation

The Daoists and the Legalists were the two most radical answers to Confucius, and they ran in opposite directions. Both rejected the Confucian premise that cultivated men leading by moral example could restore order. The authors of the Laozi and Zhuangzi concluded that purposeful striving was itself the disease: rulers should do less, people should want less, and individuals should find freedom by aligning themselves with the Dao, the natural order, rather than with the ambitions of states. The Legalists concluded that virtue was too rare and too naive to govern anyone: rulers should rely on clear laws, automatic rewards and punishments, secrecy and competition among officials, and Qin under Lord Shang showed that such a system could mobilize a whole population for farming and war. One school withdrew from the state; the other made the state total.

Act I: The Laozi, Do Less (Review Question 8)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 2, “Daoism and the Laozi and Zhuangzi” (pp. 30–31). The Reading Guide anchors Q8 on p. 30 with the sentence beginning “The authors of…”: striving to make things better generally makes them worse.

The textbook’s framing is deliberately oppositional: Confucians believed in moral and political effort; those “who later came to be labeled Daoists” disagreed, defended private life, wanted rulers to leave the people alone, and turned attention from human affairs to the whole natural order (p. 30). Slide F teaches that framing and the reminder from Lecture 2 that “Daoism” is a Han-era bibliographic label (Sima Tan and Sima Qian; the Han shu bibliography). Students should not imagine a Daoist school with members. Slide G introduces wu wei as a term: the textbook never uses the transliteration, only the translation “no action,” so the slide tags the term as standard scholarly usage.

Slide H handles authorship. The textbook says Master Lao “may not be a historical figure” and dates both books to the third century BCE (p. 30). The legend in Shiji 63, that Laozi was an archivist of Zhou who left through the pass and wrote the book for the keeper Yin Xi, is presented as legend; Sima Qian himself records rival identifications (Lao Laizi, the Grand Historian Dan) and admits uncertainty. Slide I uses chapter 8 on water; the notes add the well-known chapter 78 image of water overcoming the hard and strong, flagged as outside the textbook’s quotations. Slide J is the School of Life video on wu wei (about five minutes); its Tang poets and painters are a later reception history, which the notes point out. Slide K presents chapter 3 as a primary source and asks students to judge whether the sage ruler is benevolent or manipulative, a long-running interpretive debate (compare the “primitivist” readings of chapter 80 and the Legalist reception of the Laozi in Han Feizi chs. 20–21). Slide L pairs the textbook’s list (less knowledge, no tools, no writing, no envy, no travel or war) with chapter 80’s village within earshot of its neighbor.

Act II: Zhuangzi, Stay Free (Review Question 8)

Textbook anchor: pp. 30–31, from “Zhuangzi (369–286 B.C.E.)” to the paragraph on why rulers, artists and the feminine found merit in Daoism.

The cold open uses the tortoise parable (p. 30; Zhuangzi ch. 17, “Autumn Floods”), in which the king of Chu sends two officials to Zhuangzi as he fishes in the Pu River; the textbook simplifies to “an envoy.” Slide O teaches “the usefulness of the useless” through the shrine-oak story (Zhuangzi ch. 4, “In the World of Men”; compare Huizi’s gourd and tree in ch. 1), and slide P defines relativity with the frog in the well (ch. 17) and the giant Peng bird (ch. 1). Slide Q is the Seek Learning video on the butterfly dream (ch. 2, about five minutes), which ends with Descartes and Kant; instructors may want to note that the dream is usually read in Daoist terms as the “transformation of things” (wu hua) rather than as Cartesian skepticism, a point several translators (Graham, Ziporyn) make.

Slide R is the wife’s death (ch. 18, “Supreme Happiness”), quoting the textbook’s translation; the original has Huizi find Zhuangzi squatting and drumming on a pot. Slide S is the wheelwright Pian (ch. 13, “The Way of Heaven”), whose duke is Duke Huan of Qi, a nice callback to Lecture 1. Slides T and U teach the textbook’s closing paragraph: many people felt the pull of both traditions; Daoism accepted the feminine (yin), validated spontaneity for artists, and offered rulers the image of power without action (p. 31). The common formula “Confucian in office, Daoist in retirement” is flagged as a modern simplification.

Source caution · inner and outer chapters

The received Zhuangzi (33 chapters, edited by Guo Xiang, d. 312 CE) is usually divided into the “inner chapters” (1–7), often attributed to Zhuangzi himself, and “outer” and “miscellaneous” chapters by later hands (A. C. Graham distinguished primitivist, Yangist and syncretist layers). Several stories in the deck, the tortoise (ch. 17), the wife (ch. 18) and the wheelwright (ch. 13), come from the outer chapters. The textbook treats them as Zhuangzi’s; the deck follows the textbook and says “the book Zhuangzi” where it can.

Act III: The Legalists, Trust No One (Review Question 9)

Textbook anchor: “Legalism” (pp. 31–32). The Reading Guide anchors Q9 on p. 31 with the sentence beginning “These theorists, labeled…”.

Slide Y gives the textbook’s causal frame: fear of extinction made rulers receptive to experts in accumulating power. Slide Z defines Legalism. Slide AA tells the pole story from Shiji 68 (the three-zhang pole at the south gate of the market, ten then fifty gold), flagged as outside the textbook and as exemplary rather than documentary. Slides AB and AC teach the textbook’s list of Lord Shang’s reforms: ranks by enemy heads, counties in place of fiefs, recruitment of migrants, land sales, freedom from serf-like obligations and heavy state burdens (p. 31). Instructors can add the twenty-rank system (ershi jue), the household mutual-responsibility groups of five and ten, and the doubled tax on households with two adult sons living together, none of which are in the textbook. Recap links go to Lecture 1’s merit and serf-to-taxpayer concepts.

Slides AD–AG teach Han Feizi: studied with Xunzi, alarmed at Han’s weakness, trust is control (ch. 17, “Guarding Against the Interior”), wives and concubines, secrecy and manipulated competition, virtue as naive, parents favoring sons, clear laws and automatic rewards, and laws that make people work and fight for hegemony (pp. 31–32). The comparison slide (AF) sets Han Feizi against Analects 2.3 from Lecture 2; the textbook does not make this pairing explicitly, but Han Feizi’s “Five Vermin” chapter (ch. 49) attacks Confucian appeals to the former kings in just these terms. The “two handles” (er bing, ch. 7) of reward and punishment and the triad of fa (law), shu (technique) and shi (positional power) are useful instructor vocabulary; the deck avoids them to keep the concept load light.

Slide AH is the Qin official’s tomb. The textbook does not name it; it is Shuihudi tomb 11 (Yunmeng, Hubei), excavated in 1975, the tomb of a local official named Xi (d. ca. 217 BCE) buried with about 1,155 inscribed bamboo strips, including the Qin lü shiba zhong (eighteen statutes), answers to legal questions and model case documents. The statutes on granaries, accounting, penal labor and the inspection of officials, with fines often assessed in suits of armor or shields, match the textbook’s description closely (p. 32). Slide AI closes with the textbook’s points that Legalism allowed no debate and that only Qin followed it systematically, with Qin’s “extraordinary but brief success” left to Chapter 3 (p. 32).

Scholarly context · was there a “Legalist school”?

As with Daoism, fajia is a Han classification. Recent scholarship (for example Yuri Pines, Paul R. Goldin) stresses that the thinkers grouped as Legalists differed among themselves, that Shang Yang’s concerns were agrarian and military mobilization while Han Feizi’s were the ruler’s control of ministers, and that “Legalism” is better read as a set of overlapping administrative and political ideas than as a school. Some scholars also doubt the tradition that Han Feizi studied with Xunzi. The deck keeps the textbook’s labels and claims and puts these qualifications here.

The Pierian Spring: Death, Laozi, Guodian, Two Legalist Deaths

The required independent-study section opens with the course’s standard divider wording and holds five story slides, each ending with a review-question box and a back-link. AN finishes Zhuangzi on death with the captive girl (Lady Li of Ai, Zhuangzi ch. 2; textbook p. 31) for Q8. AO tells the meeting of Confucius and Laozi from Shiji 63 and the many Zhuangzi versions, for Q8. AP introduces Guodian tomb 1 (Jingmen, Hubei; looted and then excavated in 1993; dated ca. 300 BCE), whose three bundles of bamboo strips hold about two-fifths of the received Laozi in a different arrangement; a lacquer cup inscribed “teacher of the Eastern Palace” suggests the occupant tutored a Chu crown prince. AQ tells Lord Shang’s fall in 338 BCE from Shiji 68: refused at an inn under his own registration law, he fled, was killed in battle at Mianchi, and his corpse was torn apart by chariots. AR tells Han Feizi’s death in Qin in 233 BCE from Shiji 63, with Li Si’s role. Both Legalist stories serve Q9.

One textbook paragraph that the arc plan had put in this deck’s Pierian Spring, the list of other schools (logicians, hedonists, utopians, hermits, agriculturalists, natural philosophers; p. 32), opens the textbook’s “Yin and Yang” section and has been left for Lecture 4, where it introduces Q10.

How the Two Questions Connect

The lecture has a single hinge: both schools reject Confucian trust in cultivated men, but one answers with less government and the other with more. Q8 asks students to explain a rejection and an alternative; Q9 asks them to explain a system and its justification. Students who can place Confucius, the Daoists and the Legalists on one axis (what makes order: virtue, nature or law) and one question (what should the individual do: serve, withdraw or obey) have the argument of Lectures 2 and 3 together. The closing slide puts Zhuangzi and the Qin army on opposite banks of the same river to make that visible, and the deck links Han Feizi back to Xunzi, his teacher in tradition, and Lord Shang back to Lecture 1’s merit and taxpayer concepts.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • Dating the Laozi and Zhuangzi (p. 30: both “date to the third century B.C.E.”). The Guodian strips (tomb ca. 300 BCE) show Laozi material circulating by the late fourth century, though probably not yet as the 81-chapter text, and the inner chapters of the Zhuangzi are usually placed in the late fourth century, within Zhuangzi’s own lifetime. The deck keeps the textbook’s date on the slides and explains the evidence in the notes for slides H and AP.
  • Zhuangzi’s dates (p. 30: 369–286 BCE). These are traditional reconstructions and are usually given as “ca.” in reference works. The deck uses the textbook’s figures.
  • The tortoise story (p. 30). The textbook has the king of Chu send “an envoy”; in Zhuangzi ch. 17 there are two officials. Trivial, but the slide image shows two.
  • “Chancellor” Lord Shang (p. 31). Shang Yang’s highest Qin title was da liangzao (a high military-administrative rank), not chancellor (xiang) in the later sense; “chancellor” is a common English convenience. The deck uses the textbook’s word.
  • Han Feizi and Xunzi (p. 31). The teacher-student link comes from Shiji 63 and is accepted by most textbooks, but some scholars question it. Note also that the textbook’s Xunzi dates (p. 29, “ca. 310–ca. 215”) would make Xunzi outlive his student by nearly twenty years; the usual death date is about 238–235 BCE (see the Lecture 2 essay).
  • The Qin official’s tomb (p. 32). The textbook’s “third-century” tomb is Shuihudi 11, closed about 217 BCE, after unification; its statutes mix pre- and post-221 material. Students should not read it as purely Warring States evidence.
  • Lord Shang’s death. Popular retellings say he was “torn apart by chariots alive.” Shiji 68 reports that he was killed at Mianchi and that King Huiwen then had his body torn apart by chariots as a public warning. Slide AQ follows the Shiji.
  • Old deck problems fixed. The Summer 2026 deck had “Q8 Answer” and “Q9 Answer” slides, pause prompts that replaced the Reading Guide wording, and AI “portraits” of Laozi and Zhuangzi in a brush-ink style with pseudo-calligraphy. The revised deck has verbatim green checkpoints with no suggested answers, and no portraits.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

A. C. Graham, Disputers of the Tao: Philosophical Argument in Ancient China (1989). Still the best single account of the Daoists and Legalists as arguments, not schools.

Benjamin I. Schwartz, The World of Thought in Ancient China (1985). Long chapters on the Laozi, Zhuangzi and Legalism.

Brook Ziporyn, trans., Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings, with Selections from Traditional Commentaries (2009). Readable translation with commentary; good on the butterfly dream.

Philip J. Ivanhoe, trans., The Daodejing of Laozi (2002). A clear teaching translation.

Sarah Allan and Crispin Williams, eds., The Guodian Laozi (2000). Proceedings of the conference that first assessed the Guodian strips.

Yuri Pines, trans., The Book of Lord Shang: Apologetics of State Power in Early China (2017). The standard translation and introduction to Shang Yang.

Burton Watson, trans., Han Feizi: Basic Writings (2003). Includes “The Two Handles” and “The Five Vermin.”

A. F. P. Hulsewé, Remnants of Ch’in Law (1985). Annotated translation of the Shuihudi legal texts.

Paul R. Goldin, “Persistent Misconceptions about Chinese ‘Legalism,’” Journal of Chinese Philosophy 38 (2011). Short, sharp critique of the label.

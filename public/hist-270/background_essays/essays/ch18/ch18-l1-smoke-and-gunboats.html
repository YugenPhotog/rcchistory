Smoke and Gunboats: Opium, Silver, and the First Unequal Treaty

Background for the lecture "Smoke and Gunboats": the opium trade, the silver drain, Lin Zexu's campaign, the war of 1839–1842, and the Nanjing settlement · 1800–1850

Central interpretation

The First Opium War was not a clash between a modern West and a backward China so much as the collision of two working systems, each confident it was right. British India needed opium revenue and British merchants wanted free access to China. The Qing state was already under strain from population growth and a shrinking supply of silver, and it treated opium as a moral and fiscal emergency. Lin Zexu's campaign of 1839 was a reasonable policy by Qing standards. British steam and naval power turned it into a defeat. The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) and the supplementary treaties of 1843–1844 were the first of the "unequal treaties." They opened the ports, but they did not yet open Beijing.

The story in two acts, matching Review Questions 2 and 3, plus the Pierian Spring (Review Question 1)

  1. Smoke and Silver (Q2): the Canton system, Indian opium, the smuggling boom, the silver drain, the court debate, and Lin Zexu at Guangzhou.

Act I: Smoke and Silver (Review Question 2)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, Chapter 18, "The Opium War," pp. 316–317. Reading-guide sentence (p. 317): "Unless trade in the drug was suppressed, he argued, the Qing would have no soldiers to fight the enemy and no funds to support an army."

The Canton system and the tea problem

From 1757 the Qing confined European trade to Guangzhou (Canton). Foreign merchants lived in a strip of rented "factories" outside the city walls for the trading season. They dealt only with a guild of licensed Chinese firms, the Cohong, and paid duties through the superintendent of customs, known to foreigners as the Hoppo. The system was not a refusal to trade. It was a way of keeping a profitable trade under the control of a few accountable people, far from the capital (slide C).1

The British problem was tea. By the late eighteenth century Britain drank millions of pounds of Chinese tea a year, and the government taxed it heavily. China wanted little that Britain made, so the East India Company paid in silver, much of it Spanish-American dollars. Opium solved the imbalance. The Company held a monopoly on opium grown in Bengal and auctioned it in Calcutta. It did not carry the drug to China itself, which would have been illegal. Licensed private "country traders" did that work, selling to Chinese smugglers off the coast and paying their silver into the Company's treasury at Guangzhou in exchange for bills on London (slide E). The Qing banned the opium trade in edicts of 1729 (against selling and opium dens) and 1796 (against imports), and repeated the bans afterward. Ebrey gives 1800 as the date of the ban (p. 316), which is one of these restatements.2

From a luxury to a mass habit

Opium had long been a medicine in China. What changed was the practice of smoking it, first mixed with tobacco and then on its own in a pipe, which made it a social pleasure for officials, merchants, soldiers and eventually laborers (slide D). Supply then exploded. After 1819 the Company faced competition from Malwa opium grown in the princely states of western India, and it answered by producing more. In 1834 Parliament ended the Company's monopoly on the China trade, and new firms such as Jardine, Matheson & Co. joined the market. The result was a price war along the Guangdong coast. Ebrey's figures make the point: imports rose "from forty-five hundred chests smuggled into China in 1810 to forty thousand chests in 1838, enough to supply 2 million addicts" (p. 317; slide F).

The silver drain, and what else drained the silver

By the 1830s silver was flowing out of China, not in. Ebrey estimates the outflow at about 2 million ounces a year in the 1820s and about 9 million in the 1830s. Because land taxes were assessed in silver but most farmers earned copper cash, the change was felt in the countryside: "A tax obligation of 1 ounce of silver took about 1,500 cash to pay in 1800, but 2,700 cash in 1830" (p. 317; slide G). Treat the second figure as an illustration. Exchange rates varied by region and year, and most series show the sharpest rise in the 1830s and 1840s.

Historians now agree that the silver crisis was real and that Qing officials blamed it on opium. They no longer agree that opium alone caused it. Lin Man-houng argues that the wars of independence in Spanish America cut world silver output in the 1810s and 1820s, and that this global shortage, together with opium, explains why China's silver stock shrank.3 Alejandra Irigoin adds a monetary twist: Chinese merchants trusted the old Spanish Carolus dollar and discounted the new republican coins, so the collapse of the Spanish coinage system disrupted the supply of the one coin China would accept at face value.4 The deck's slide G now says the outflow happened mainly because of opium, and its instructor note adds the world silver shortage. For lecture, it is worth saying aloud that the Qing believed opium was draining their silver, and that this belief drove policy whether or not it was the whole truth.

The court debate and Lin Zexu

In 1836 the official Xu Naiji proposed legalizing opium and taxing it, paying for it only in goods so that silver would stay home. The Daoguang emperor circulated the proposal, and it was beaten back. In 1838 Huang Juezi proposed the opposite, the death penalty for users, and the emperor asked his governors for their views (slide H). Lin Zexu, then governor-general of Hunan and Hubei, answered that the state should strike at dealers and importers. His argument is the reading-guide sentence: without suppression there would be no soldiers and no money. Lin was a jinshi of 1811 with a reputation for honesty and effective famine relief. At the end of 1838 the emperor made him imperial commissioner with full powers (slide I).

Lin arrived at Guangzhou on 10 March 1839. Ebrey summarizes his first months: he arrested some seventeen hundred Chinese dealers and seized seventy thousand pipes (p. 317). He then demanded that the foreign merchants surrender their stocks. When they stalled, he stopped all trade and confined them in the factories (slide J). Charles Elliot, the British superintendent of trade, ended the standoff by ordering British merchants to hand their opium to him, as Crown property, and then to Lin. More than 20,000 chests, about 2.6 million pounds, were surrendered. Elliot's promise that London would compensate the merchants turned a private commercial loss into a debt of the British state, and gave the opium interest its case for war.5

From 3 to 25 June 1839, at Humen, five hundred laborers mixed the opium with salt and lime in trenches and flushed it out to sea (slide B). Lin wrote a prayer to the spirit of the sea apologizing for the pollution. He also drafted a letter to Queen Victoria asking how Britain could sell abroad a drug it forbade at home. It was never formally delivered, though an English translation was printed in the missionary-run Chinese Repository.

Primary source · Lin Zexu to Queen Victoria, 1839

"Suppose there were people from another country who carried opium for sale to England and seduced your people into buying and smoking it; certainly your honorable ruler would deeply hate it and be bitterly aroused."

Translation in Ssu-yü Teng and John K. Fairbank, China's Response to the West: A Documentary Survey, 1839–1923 (Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press, 1954). Use it to show that Lin argued in moral terms that he expected any ruler to accept.

Answer synthesis · Q2

Opium became a crisis because a mass habit, fed by a price war among British and Indian suppliers, seemed to be draining China's silver at the same time as rising silver prices raised every farmer's taxes. The court debated legalization and chose suppression. Lin Zexu struck at the supply: he arrested Chinese dealers, blockaded the foreign merchants until they surrendered their opium, and destroyed it in public at Humen.

Act II: Gunboats and a Treaty (Review Question 3)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 317–318. Reading-guide sentence (p. 317): "Concluded at gunpoint, the 1842 Treaty of Nanjing provided benefits for Britain but not for China, making it 'unequal.'"

Why Britain went to war

Ebrey puts the British case in two sentences: Lin's act was "an affront to British dignity," and the British saw China as "out of step with the modern world" of free trade and treaties (p. 317; slide L). Both points matter. The merchants wanted compensation and open ports. The government of Lord Palmerston framed the war as a defense of British subjects and of diplomatic equality. Critics at home said plainly that it was a war for opium. On 8 April 1840 the young William Gladstone told the House of Commons that he did not know "a war more unjust in its origin, a war more calculated in its progress to cover this country with permanent disgrace." A motion censuring the government failed by only nine votes.6

The fleet, the north, and two rejected deals

The expedition reached China in June 1840. It blockaded Guangzhou, took Dinghai on Zhoushan island, and sailed north to the mouth of the Beihe, the river that led toward Beijing (slide M). The alarmed emperor sent the Manchu grandee Qishan to negotiate and dismissed Lin. In January 1841 Qishan and Elliot reached the Convention of Chuanbi (Chuenpi): Hong Kong would be ceded, six million dollars paid, and officials would deal with each other as equals. Both governments rejected it. Daoguang had Qishan arrested; Palmerston recalled Elliot, who had already raised the flag on Hong Kong (26 January 1841), and sent Sir Henry Pottinger with orders to get more.

The Nemesis and the gap in firepower

Ebrey's most striking detail is a single day in 1841 on which one steam warship destroyed "nine war junks, five forts, two military stations, and a shore battery" (p. 318). The ship was the East India Company's iron steamer Nemesis, and the day was 7 January 1841, when the British stormed the Chuanbi forts and the Nemesis destroyed war junks in Anson's Bay (slide N). It was shallow-draft, armed with pivot guns and rockets, and could tow sailing warships up rivers against the wind and tide.7

Two of Ebrey's phrases need care. "The Qing had no navy" (p. 318) means no navy that could face a British fleet. The Qing did maintain provincial war-junk fleets for coastal defense and against piracy. Ebrey also says the British were armed with "rifles." Most British infantry in 1840–1842 carried smoothbore percussion or flintlock muskets. Their advantage lay in drill, volley fire, artillery and naval support, not in rifling. The deck (slide O) correctly says "musket volleys."

Up the Yangzi to Nanjing

Pottinger's campaign of 1841–1842 took Xiamen, Dinghai again, Zhenhai and Ningbo. A Qing counteroffensive at Ningbo in March 1842 failed badly. In May the British took Zhapu, a banner garrison town near Hangzhou Bay; in June they took Wusong and Shanghai; and in July they stormed Zhenjiang, where the Grand Canal crosses the Yangzi (slides P–Q). By cutting the canal, they threatened the grain supply of Beijing. At Zhapu and Zhenjiang the Manchu garrisons fought hard, and many bannermen killed their families and themselves rather than surrender. This is the moment of Ebrey's biography of Guancheng (p. 319), and it also undercuts the stereotype that the Qing simply collapsed. The dynasty's soldiers fought; their weapons, training and command could not match the British.

The treaty and the "unequal" clauses

The Treaty of Nanjing was signed aboard HMS Cornwallis on 29 August 1842 by the Qing negotiators Qiying and Yilibu and by Pottinger (slide R). It opened five ports (Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai) to British residence, trade and consuls; ceded Hong Kong island "in perpetuity"; abolished the Cohong; and required an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars: six million for the confiscated opium, three million for debts owed by Chinese merchants, and twelve million for Britain's war costs. It promised a "fair and regular" tariff, but the rate was fixed only in the Supplementary Treaty of the Bogue (Humen) of 8 October 1843 at about 5 percent (slide S). Opium was not mentioned at all.

The clauses that made the settlement "unequal" came in two layers. Extraterritoriality was spelled out in the British general regulations of 1843 and the American Treaty of Wangxia (1844): foreigners accused of crimes in China would be tried by their own consuls (slide T). The most-favored-nation clause in the Bogue treaty gave Britain any privilege later granted to another power, and the Americans and French obtained the same (slide U). Ebrey groups all of this into one "set of treaties," and in doing so she also says the treaties "mandated ambassadors in Beijing" (p. 317). They did not. Resident envoys in Beijing were the central demand of the Second Opium War and were conceded only in 1858–1860. The deck avoids the error.8

Why call the treaty unequal (slide V)? Partly because it was imposed by force. More precisely, because the obligations ran one way. China gave up control of its tariffs and of jurisdiction over foreigners, and gained nothing in return, not even the right of its own subjects to equal treatment abroad. Chinese historians later made 1842 the starting point of a "century of humiliation." Students should know that the phrase is a twentieth-century nationalist framing, but the inequality it names was real.

After the war

The court divided between those who accepted the treaties as a way to manage the foreigners and those who wanted them reversed (slide W). James Polachek argues that the war party grew out of a literati network around the "Spring Purification" circle, which used the opium issue to attack Manchu grandees at court; the inner politics of Beijing shaped the war as much as events on the coast.9 Lin himself was exiled to Ili in Xinjiang in 1841, recalled in 1845, and served again as a provincial official. He died in November 1850 on his way to fight the rebels who would become the Taiping. The Daoguang emperor died that same year.

Mao Haijian's study of the war from the Qing archives reaches a severe verdict on the court: it misjudged British power at every stage, accepted false victory reports, and learned little from defeat. His book is a useful corrective to accounts that make Lin a flawless hero.10

Answer synthesis · Q3

The war grew out of the opium crisis, Lin's seizure of British opium, and Britain's demand for open trade and diplomatic equality. British steam warships, artillery and disciplined infantry won it. The Treaty of Nanjing (1842) and the treaties of 1843–1844 opened five ports, ceded Hong Kong, imposed a 21-million-dollar indemnity, fixed a low tariff, and added extraterritoriality and most-favored-nation treatment. They were "unequal" because they were imposed at gunpoint and every obligation ran from China to the foreign powers.

Pierian Spring: Too Many People, Too Little Silver (Review Question 1)

Textbook anchor: Ebrey, pp. 314–316. Reading-guide sentence (p. 315): "As farms grew smaller and surplus labor depressed wages, the average standard of living suffered."

The eighteenth-century Qing peace let China's population roughly double, to about 300 million by 1800, and it kept growing to about 430 million by 1850 (slides AA–BB). New World crops such as maize and sweet potatoes let farmers settle hill country, but the best land was taken. Ebrey links the squeeze to social symptoms: more conflict over water and tenancy, more female infanticide, a shortage of marriageable women, and a growing "floating population" of unmarried men who worked as boatmen, porters or charcoal burners (slide CC). Those men will reappear in Lecture 2 as recruits for sects and rebel armies.11

The state did not grow with the population. The number of counties, about thirteen hundred, stayed nearly fixed, so a magistrate who once governed tens of thousands now governed hundreds of thousands, with clerks and runners who lived on fees (slide DD). The Daoguang emperor (r. 1821–1850) answered with frugality, famously wearing patched clothes and cutting court expenses (slide EE). Frugality could not pay for water control. In 1824 floods and silting closed the Grand Canal at its Yellow River crossing; in 1826 the court shipped grain to Tianjin by sea as an experiment, then went back to the canal. Jane Kate Leonard shows that Daoguang managed the crisis carefully but could not fix the underlying decay; the Yellow River's change of course in 1855 finally broke the canal system (slide FF).12

The banners, the hereditary Manchu, Mongol and Chinese-martial military caste, were a second fiscal burden. The court could not let them go without betraying the conquest elite, and it could not afford to keep them well (slide GG). The Opium War tested them, and at Zhapu they died fighting.

Biography · Guancheng, a bannerman of Zhapu

Ebrey's biography (p. 319) draws on Pamela Kyle Crossley's reconstruction of a Manchu family in the Zhapu garrison. Guancheng, away on duty in 1842, came home to find much of his community dead, and compiled a record so the dead would be remembered and their families compensated (slide II). Crossley uses such families to show how banner identity hardened in the nineteenth century, even as the banners' military role faded.13

Answer synthesis · Q1

Population growth shrank farms, lowered wages and living standards, and produced a floating population of poor, unmarried men. The state's tax base and its number of officials did not grow, so revenue fell short of the cost of water control, relief and the banners. The rising price of silver then raised everyone's real taxes. Daoguang's frugality could slow the decline but not reverse it.

Corrections and Cautions

Details that are easy to get wrong

  • "Ambassadors in Beijing." Ebrey (p. 317) lists resident ambassadors among the 1842–1844 terms. They came only with the treaties of 1858–1860. Extraterritoriality and most-favored-nation treatment came in 1843–1844, not in the Nanjing text itself.
  • "The Qing had no navy." True only of a blue-water navy. Provincial war-junk fleets existed and fought.
  • "Rifles." British infantry mostly carried smoothbore muskets; the deck's slide O says "musket volleys."
  • Hong Kong. British merchants and ships sheltered in Hong Kong harbor after leaving Macao in 1839 (Ebrey says they "settled" there); the British occupied the island in January 1841, and it was ceded in 1842.
  • Dollars, not ounces. The 1842 indemnity was 21 million silver dollars, as Ebrey's main text (p. 317) and slide S say. Ebrey's reparations table (p. 325) lists it as "21 million ounces of silver"; Lecture 4's slide Q now gives dollars, with the tael equivalent, to match this deck. A Spanish dollar held about 0.72 tael of silver, so the sum was roughly 15 million taels.
  • The opium ban. Ebrey dates the ban to 1800; the first edicts were 1729 and 1796. Slide D now gives all three dates, with an instructor note.
  • The silver drain. Present opium as the Qing explanation and the main cause, and mention the global silver shortage (Lin Man-houng) when time allows; slide G's notes now do both.
  • Guancheng's career. He was not a high examination graduate: he passed the provincial examination and served as a magistrate. Slide II now says so; an earlier version said he "rose by passing the examinations."

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Julia Lovell, The Opium War: Drugs, Dreams and the Making of China (2011). The best single narrative, strong on both sides and on how the war was remembered in modern China.

Mao Haijian, The Qing Empire and the Opium War: The Collapse of the Heavenly Dynasty (2016). A leading Chinese historian's archival account of how the Qing court misread the war; an essential counterweight to heroic accounts of Lin.

Hsin-pao Chang, Commissioner Lin and the Opium War (1964). Old but still the most detailed study of Lin's campaign in Guangzhou.

Man-houng Lin, China Upside Down: Currency, Society, and Ideologies, 1808–1856 (2006). The case that the silver crisis was global, and how it shaped Qing economic thought.

James M. Polachek, The Inner Opium War (1992). Court factions and literati politics behind war and peace policy.

Pamela Kyle Crossley, Orphan Warriors (1990). The source of Ebrey's Guancheng biography and a moving history of a banner family.

Peter C. Perdue, "The First Opium War: The Anglo-Chinese War of 1839–1842," MIT Visualizing Cultures (2011). An online unit with British and Chinese images of the war, useful for slides and student work.

Audit disposition. Chapter 18 had no HIST 270 background essays, so this reconnaissance covers four new essays and a retroactive audit of the four published FA26 decks (Smoke and Gunboats, Jesus's Younger Brother, Large and Yet Weak and The Empress and the Boxers). The four-lecture roadmap passes. Lecture 1 is the strongest deck, with one cross-deck inconsistency and three small weaknesses. Lecture 2 has a cluster of quotations in the speaker notes that paraphrase Ebrey while presenting the words as hers. Lectures 3 and 4 repeat several textbook errors, the most serious being the Yalu losses, the Fuzhou death toll, Yuan Shikai's role in 1898 and the scope of the 1901 examination suspension. All findings are recorded in Section 8. Steven approved the fixes on 25 October 2026, and they have now been applied to all four published decks without relettering any slide; Section 8 records what was changed.

1. Governing standards and scope

Research precedes presentation. Ebrey's Chapter 18, "China in Decline (1800–1900)," pp. 314–332, is the course baseline, not its ceiling. Dates, names, numbers and quotations in the decks were checked against the textbook (from an OCR of the course PDF), against standard monographs and the Cambridge History of China, volumes 10 and 11, and, where the decks quote primary sources, against published translations. Every quotation that a deck attributes to Ebrey was compared with the printed text. Where Ebrey's statements conflict with current scholarship, the essays follow the scholarship and flag the textbook. Where a deck follows the textbook into error, the essays give the corrected history and this audit lists the slide.

Audience: the instructor's lecture preparation and advanced students. The essays keep plain language and define terms, but cite real scholarship in footnotes and end with annotated reading lists.

2. Textbook baseline

Ebrey's chapter supplies the spine: population and fiscal strain (pp. 314–316); opium, Lin Zexu and the Opium War (pp. 316–318); the Taiping and the regional armies (pp. 318–320); the Nian and Muslim risings (p. 321); the Second Opium War (pp. 321–322); self-strengthening and its failures (pp. 322–324); Cixi, the tributaries, France and Japan (pp. 324–327); the reformers of 1895–1898 (pp. 327–328); the Boxers (pp. 328–330); and a comparative conclusion and summary (pp. 330–332). The reading guide sets eleven review questions, from population pressure (RQ1) to the end of the tribute system (RQ11).

The chapter is clear and well organized, but it compresses a century and several of its details are dated. Statements needing correction or caution:

  • The 1842–1844 treaties "mandated ambassadors in Beijing" (p. 317): that came in 1858–1860.
  • "The Qing had no navy" and British "rifles" (p. 318): overstatements.
  • Opium as the sole cause of the silver drain (p. 317): contested.
  • Women "could take the civil service examinations" (p. 318): poorly attested.
  • Yang Xiuqing's death tied to the concubine beating (p. 319): conflates 1853 and 1856.
  • "None of the Taipings survived"; Taiping armies "relocating to Taiwan and Vietnam" as the Black Flags (p. 321): too strong and inaccurate.
  • "By 1867 all of Gansu was in Muslim hands" (p. 321): overstated.
  • The regency of 1861 as Prince Gong and Cixi (p. 322): omits Ci'an and the coup.
  • The "10-square-mile" Yuanmingyuan (p. 322) and "one hundred treaty ports" by 1900 (p. 325): both too large.
  • The reparations table (p. 325): wrong units for 1842 and 1901, wrong years for Taiwan and Liaodong.
  • Fuzhou, "fifteen minutes" and "three thousand" killed (p. 324); the Yalu, no Japanese ship "seriously damaged" (p. 326).
  • Guo Songtao's diary suppressed "on his return" (p. 324).
  • "Some twelve hundred signed" Kang's 1895 petition; a "five-hour audience"; reforms "redesigning the Qing as a constitutional monarchy"; a coup "with the help of Yuan Shikai's army" (p. 327).
  • The examinations cancelled for five years, nationally (p. 330); Yuan Shikai as a governor-general (p. 330).
  • "80 provinces" in the Mai Menghua document (p. 329): probably an error for eighteen.
  • The chapter summary (p. 332) says Cixi "was quickly removed from power" in 1898 and that the court withdrew before the siege; both reverse the sequence.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

Opium, silver and the first war

Julia Lovell's The Opium War (2011) is the best narrative and is strong on memory; Mao Haijian's The Qing Empire and the Opium War (English 2016) is the essential view from the Qing archives; Hsin-pao Chang (1964) and Peter Ward Fay (1975) remain useful for detail. The silver question has moved: Man-houng Lin (2006) and Alejandra Irigoin (2009) place the drain in a global monetary crisis. James Polachek (1992) explains the court politics of war and peace. For RQ1, Jane Kate Leonard (1996) on the Grand Canal crisis and Susan Mann Jones and Philip Kuhn (CHC 10, 1978) on dynastic decline; Pamela Crossley (1990) for Guancheng.

Reference synthesis: Peter C. Perdue, "The First Opium War," MIT Visualizing Cultures. Primary source: Lin Zexu's letter to Queen Victoria (Teng and Fairbank, 1954).

The Taiping and the other risings

Spence's God's Chinese Son (1996) for Hong; Platt's Autumn in the Heavenly Kingdom (2012) for the last years and foreign intervention; Kuhn's Rebellion and Its Enemies (1970) and CHC 10 chapter for structure; Meyer-Fong's What Remains (2013) for the human cost; Richard J. Smith (1978) on the Ever-Victorious Army. For RQ6: Perry (1980) on the Nian; Atwill (2005), Lipman (1997) and Hodong Kim (2004) on the Muslim-led risings; Bradley Camp Davis (2017) on the Black Flags.

Primary sources: the Taiping texts translated in Franz Michael and Chung-li Chang, The Taiping Rebellion: History and Documents (1966–1971); Theodore Hamberg's The Visions of Hung-Siu-tshuen (1854), based on Hong Rengan's account.

The Arrow War and self-strengthening

J. Y. Wong's Deadly Dreams (1998) for the war; James Hevia's English Lessons (2003) and Young-tsu Wong's A Paradise Lost (2001) for the Yuanmingyuan; Mary Wright (1957) and Kwang-Ching Liu (CHC 10) for the Restoration; Kuo and Liu (CHC 10), Feuerwerker (1958), Kennedy (1978), Pong (1994) and Elman (2004, 2005) for self-strengthening; Rhoads (2011) for the students in America; Bickers (2011), van de Ven (2014) and Cohen (1963) for the foreign presence.

Primary sources: Feng Guifen's essays and Woren's memorial in Teng and Fairbank (1954); Elgin's letters and journals (ed. Walrond, 1872).

Cixi, war and the crisis of 1898–1901

Eastman (1967) for court politics in the French crisis; Paine (2003) and Evans and Peattie (1997) for the war with Japan; Kwong (1984) for the Hundred Days; Huang Zhangjian (1970) and Mao Haijian (2005) for the archival revision of Kang's accounts of 1895 and 1898; Esherick (1987) and Cohen (1997) for the Boxers, with Harrison (2013) and Xiang (2003). Rawski (1998) for imperial institutions; Rhoads (2000) for the bannermen; Hao Chang (1971) for Liang Qichao.

Primary sources: Kang Youwei's China Mail interview (7 October 1898); Mai Menghua's essay (in Ebrey, pp. 328–329); the Boxer Protocol (1901), in the Customs treaty collection; accounts of the siege by participants.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

The arc plan assigns RQ2–3 to Lecture 1 with RQ1 in its Pierian Spring; RQ4–5 to Lecture 2 with RQ6 in its Pierian Spring; RQ7–8 to Lecture 3; and RQ10, RQ11 and RQ9 to Lecture 4, with the Boxers as a closing story without an RQ. The roadmap passes. It keeps the chapter's chronology while giving each lecture a single narrative problem, and the fourth deck gives Cixi, 1884, 1895, 1898 and 1900 the room the textbook's compressed final pages do not. Two seams need care: the regency of 1861 is told in Lecture 3 and recapped in Lecture 4, so a correction to one must be made in both; and the reparations figures appear in Lecture 1 (slide S) and Lecture 4 (slide Q) and currently disagree.

6. Individual background essay audits

Essay 1 — Smoke and Gunboats

Passes. Follows the deck's correct history (dollars for 1842, muskets not rifles, the 1843–1844 treaties as the source of extraterritoriality and MFN) and adds the silver-supply debate, the court politics of Polachek and Mao Haijian, and the Grand Canal crisis.

Essay 2 — Jesus's Younger Brother

Passes. Quotes Ebrey's actual words, which the deck's notes now use as well; corrects the Yang trigger, the "none survived" claim, the Black Flags' origin and the Gansu overstatement; presents women's examinations as a claim.

Essay 3 — Large and Yet Weak

Passes. Corrects Elgin's role, the 1861 regency, the size of the Yuanmingyuan and the treaty-port count; adds legal opium (1858) and the Fuzhou Navy Yard.

Essay 4 — The Empress and the Boxers

Passes. Gives the corrected Yalu and Fuzhou figures, the archival revision of the 1895 petition and the 1898 coup, the Dagu forts of 1900 and the local scope of the examination suspension; frames Cixi through the mainstream "operator" reading with a note on the Backhouse forgery and the revisionist biographies.

Audit conclusion

The Chapter 18 decks tell the century well and already fixed several of Ebrey's errors before this audit (the Lecture 1 treaty clauses, Li Hongzhang's tenure, Tongzhi's age in the notes). The audit found about ten misquotations in Lecture 2's notes, two content errors and a cluster of textbook figures in Lecture 3, and nine substantive corrections in Lecture 4. All of these fixes were applied on 25 October 2026. The visible slides now teach the corrected history, the textbook's discrepancies are explained in instructor notes, and every outside work is listed on its deck's Sources slide. One audit item was deliberately left as it was (Lecture 3 slide J's Yuanmingyuan area, which already had an instructor note). The four background essays match the fixed decks.

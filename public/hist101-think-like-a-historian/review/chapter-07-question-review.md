## ch07-explain-001 — Explain

**Thinking move:** cause
**Lecture(s):** ch07-lec01
**Stem:** Which interpretation best explains how the Constitution became a functioning national government after 1789?

**Choices and response-specific feedback**

- **A:** The Constitution itself supplied enough detail that later officials mainly had to follow written instructions.
  - The Constitution mattered, but the lecture emphasizes how many operating routines were not specified and had to be created in practice.
- **B:** Washington's personal popularity was the main reason the new government could function.
  - Washington's reputation helped, but popularity alone does not explain Cabinet practice, treaty consultation, public credit, or enforcement.
- **C — strongest interpretation:** Written powers became workable through precedents, administrative routines, and new fiscal capacity.
  - This interpretation explains both the missing operating rules and the government's growing ability to act.
- **D:** Hamilton's financial program alone transformed the Constitution into a working government.
  - Hamilton's program was central to capacity, but it does not explain the institutional precedents created by Washington and the First Congress.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The strongest explanation joins constitutional text to practice: precedents made institutions workable, while fiscal capacity made federal power effective.
**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Ask which interpretation explains both the institutional problem and the government's growing capacity to act.

**Walkthrough**

1. Identify the outcome: a written Constitution became an operating government.
2. Test each interpretation against more than one part of the lecture.
3. Washington's popularity explains legitimacy, and Hamilton explains fiscal capacity, but each covers only part of the evidence.
4. The precedent-and-capacity interpretation explains the Senate experiment, Cabinet development, finance, taxation, and enforcement together.
5. The weaker explanations are partial, not useless. They simply explain less of the evidence.

**Transfer strategy:** For competing explanations, prefer the interpretation that accounts for the most evidence without pretending the smaller explanations are completely false.

**Partial explanation to preserve:** Washington's reputation helps explain legitimacy, while Hamilton's program explains fiscal capacity. Each is useful but too narrow by itself.

**Qualification:** Precedent and capacity made the government workable, but public legitimacy also shaped whether people accepted its new routines and powers.

**Source-aligned claim:** The Constitution became real through precedent and capacity, not text alone.

## ch07-explain-002 — Explain

**Thinking move:** evidence
**Lecture(s):** ch07-lec01
**Stem:** Which interpretation gives the Whiskey Rebellion the greatest explanatory importance?

**Choices and response-specific feedback**

- **A:** It was mainly a dispute over whether western farmers liked paying taxes.
  - Tax burden mattered, but this interpretation is too narrow to explain why the federal government mobilized overwhelming force.
- **B — strongest interpretation:** It tested whether a constitutional republic could enforce a law while still claiming to protect liberty.
  - This captures the lecture's central tension between state capacity and republican legitimacy.
- **C:** It proved that the new federal government intended to punish all political dissent by force.
  - The mobilization was coercive, but the absence of a major battle and the later pardons complicate this interpretation.
- **D:** It showed that the Constitution had failed because resistance continued after ratification.
  - The episode showed conflict over legitimacy, not constitutional collapse.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The rebellion mattered because it tested whether enforcement under an elected constitutional government would be seen as legitimate rather than tyrannical.
**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Look for the interpretation that explains both federal enforcement and the continuing argument about liberty.

**Walkthrough**

1. Start with the event: resistance to the whiskey excise became armed enough to trigger federal mobilization.
2. Ask what larger problem the event revealed, not merely what the protesters disliked.
3. Federalists saw proof that laws could be enforced; Republicans could see dangerous coercive capacity.
4. The best interpretation explains why the same event could support both conclusions.
5. The narrow tax interpretation remains part of the story, but it does not explain the constitutional stakes.

**Transfer strategy:** When judging significance, ask what larger historical problem an event exposed or tested.

**Partial explanation to preserve:** The tax burden and western farmers' local economy help explain why resistance began, even though they do not explain the episode's full constitutional importance.

**Qualification:** The rebellion demonstrated federal capacity, but the lack of a major battle and the pardons qualify a simple story of unchecked coercion.

**Source-aligned claim:** The Whiskey Rebellion tested whether federal power could be both effective and legitimate.

## ch07-explain-004 — Explain

**Thinking move:** context
**Lecture(s):** ch07-lec02
**Stem:** Why did organized political opposition appear dangerous to many Americans in the 1790s?

**Choices and response-specific feedback**

- **A:** Because Americans had already accepted modern political parties and feared they were becoming too competitive.
  - The problem was almost the reverse: organized parties were not yet accepted as a normal part of republican government.
- **B:** Because the Constitution explicitly prohibited political parties and treated organized opposition as illegal.
  - The Constitution did not ban parties. The fear came from republican political culture and crisis, not a constitutional prohibition.
- **C:** Because every opposition newspaper openly supported a foreign invasion of the United States.
  - Partisan newspapers could be fierce, but this overstates the evidence and collapses criticism into treason.
- **D — strongest interpretation:** Because opposition looked like faction, and foreign crises made domestic critics easier to imagine as disloyal.
  - This combines the older fear of faction with the new pressure created by France, Genêt, XYZ, and the Quasi-War.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. Opposition became frightening because older fears of faction collided with foreign crisis, making partisan critics look potentially disloyal.
**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Look for an explanation that combines political culture with the pressure of international crisis.

**Walkthrough**

1. Begin with the political context: many Americans associated organized faction with selfish attacks on the common good.
2. Add the foreign-policy context: the French Revolution, Genêt, XYZ, and naval conflict made loyalty feel urgent.
3. Ask which explanation can account for both the fear of parties and the escalation of repression.
4. The combined interpretation has greater explanatory power than either factional fear or foreign crisis alone.
5. That does not prove repression was justified; it explains why it became politically plausible.

**Transfer strategy:** For context, combine the political assumptions people already held with the crisis that made those assumptions more powerful.

**Partial explanation to preserve:** Foreign danger was real, so security concerns were not invented. That still does not make every domestic critic disloyal.

**Qualification:** Foreign crisis intensified an older fear of faction; it made repression plausible without proving that repression was justified.

**Source-aligned claim:** Foreign crisis intensified an existing fear that organized faction could endanger the republic.

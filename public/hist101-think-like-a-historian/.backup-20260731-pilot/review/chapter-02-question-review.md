## Explain Questions

### ch02-explain-001 | Cause | Foundational

**Stem:** Which explanation best accounts for England's move toward permanent colonization?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Religious refugees created a single, unified demand for colonies.
  - Feedback: Religion mattered, but English colonization involved many actors who did not share one religious purpose.
- **B.** The Crown followed a complete imperial plan that had existed since the 1550s.
  - Feedback: England did not begin with a complete blueprint. Colonization developed through experiments, failures, and changing capacity.
- **C.** Religion, rivalry, profit, and migration opportunities converged as colonial capacity increased. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This is the strongest explanation because it combines several motives with the growing ability to sustain settlements.
- **D.** The defeat of the Spanish Armada immediately gave England uncontested control of the Atlantic.
  - Feedback: The Armada mattered, but it did not destroy Spanish power or make permanent settlement automatic.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. Permanent colonization grew from converging motives and increasing capacity, not one master cause.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Look for an explanation that combines motives with the ability to keep a settlement alive.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the outcome: England moved from limited Atlantic ventures toward durable colonies.
2. Reject answers that reduce a long process to one group, one event, or one national plan.
3. Separate motives from capacity. Wanting a colony did not mean England could sustain one.
4. Choose the answer that explains why several motives became effective at the same time.

**Transfer strategy:** For a multi-cause question, choose the explanation that shows how causes interacted instead of selecting a favorite cause.

**Hint:** Ask which choice explains both why England wanted colonies and why permanent settlement became possible.

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 1 background essay and Reveal deck; Chapter 2 textbook, section 2-4

**Taught claim:** English colonization resulted from the convergence of religion, rivalry, profit, and opportunity, combined with greater colonial capacity.

### ch02-explain-002 | Consequence | Developing

**Stem:** Why did tobacco transform Virginia rather than simply make the colony profitable?

**Choices:**
- **A.** It reduced the colony's need for workers and allowed settlements to remain compact.
  - Feedback: Tobacco increased labor demand and encouraged settlements to spread outward rather than remain compact.
- **B.** It linked profit to continuing demands for land and labor, expansion, inequality, and conflict. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This is the strongest answer because it traces the wider chain of consequences created by the crop.
- **C.** It ended dependence on Atlantic markets by creating a self-sufficient local economy.
  - Feedback: Tobacco tied Virginia more closely to Atlantic markets. It did not create economic self-sufficiency.
- **D.** It made relations with Powhatan communities more stable by reducing competition for territory.
  - Feedback: Tobacco expansion increased pressure on Native land and made relations more dangerous.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. Tobacco created a causal chain that reorganized land use, labor, settlement, and power.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Follow the consequences beyond the first increase in profit.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Start with the immediate result: tobacco became a profitable export.
2. Ask what profitable production required: fresh land and many workers.
3. Trace what those needs produced: expansion, bound labor, inequality, and conflict over territory.
4. Select the answer that explains the whole chain, not only the first step.

**Transfer strategy:** For a consequence question, trace the chain beyond the immediate effect and identify what changed in society and power.

**Hint:** Use the lecture chain: Tobacco to Profit to Land to Labor to Expansion.

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 2 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook, section 2-5

**Taught claim:** Tobacco reorganized Virginia around land-intensive export production, bound labor, expansion, and conflict over Native land.

### ch02-explain-003 | Comparison | Developing

**Stem:** Which comparison best explains why Virginia and New England became different societies?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Virginia preserved English society, while New England rejected all English institutions.
  - Feedback: Both regions adapted English institutions. Neither simply preserved or rejected England as a whole.
- **B.** Virginia was shaped only by profit, while New England was shaped only by religion.
  - Feedback: Motives mattered, but this comparison ignores environment, migration, labor, and economic connections.
- **C.** Virginia had no families, while New England had no commercial ambitions.
  - Feedback: Both statements are too absolute. The regions differed in proportions and patterns, not through complete absence.
- **D.** Different goals, environments, migration streams, and labor systems interacted in each region. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This is the strongest comparison because it uses the same four variables for both regions and explains their interaction.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The regions diverged because the same four variables combined differently in each place.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Use the same basis of comparison for both regions and avoid single-cause contrasts.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the two cases: Virginia and New England.
2. Use the same categories for both cases rather than changing the basis of comparison.
3. Avoid absolute claims such as 'only,' 'all,' or 'none.'
4. Choose the answer that explains how goals, environment, migration, and labor reinforced one another.

**Transfer strategy:** For comparison, apply the same categories to both cases and explain how the differences produced different outcomes.

**Hint:** Recall the lecture formula: Goals + Environment + Migration + Labor = Colonial Society.

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 2 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook, sections 2-5 and 2-6

**Taught claim:** Colonial societies developed through the interaction of goals, environment, migration, and labor.

### ch02-explain-004 | Context | Foundational

**Stem:** Which statement best describes religious freedom and religious toleration in Massachusetts Bay?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Puritans wanted freedom to build a godly community but limited competing religious practices. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This is the strongest answer because it distinguishes freedom for a community from toleration of disagreement within it.
- **B.** Puritans rejected any connection between religious belief and civil government.
  - Feedback: Puritan institutions linked religious purpose to family, town, education, and government.
- **C.** Massachusetts Bay welcomed religious pluralism but restricted Puritan worship.
  - Feedback: This reverses the historical pattern. Puritans could practice their faith, while dissenters faced serious limits.
- **D.** Religious freedom and religious toleration meant the same thing to seventeenth-century Puritans.
  - Feedback: The lecture treats these as different ideas. Confusing them hides the logic of the Puritan commonwealth.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. Massachusetts protected a Puritan community's religious project without embracing modern pluralism.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Hold freedom for a religious community apart from toleration of competing beliefs.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Define religious freedom in the Puritan setting: freedom to establish and practice a godly community.
2. Define toleration: allowing people with competing beliefs to practice within the same political community.
3. Compare the definitions with Massachusetts policy toward dissent.
4. Choose the answer that preserves both the Puritans' real freedom and the limits they placed on others.

**Transfer strategy:** For context, use the meanings historical actors gave to ideas rather than automatically importing modern definitions.

**Hint:** Which choice separates 'our community may practice' from 'people who disagree may also practice'?

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 3 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook, section 2-6

**Taught claim:** Puritan New England expanded religious freedom for Puritans while restricting broader religious toleration.

### ch02-explain-005 | Evidence | Developing

**Stem:** Which evidence best supports the claim that colonial government was representative but not modern democracy?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Colonies had elected bodies, and every adult resident could vote and hold office.
  - Feedback: The first part is true, but the second is not. Political participation remained limited by status, sex, religion, race, and property.
- **B.** Colonial assemblies existed only in name and never influenced taxation or local law.
  - Feedback: Assemblies exercised real power and helped create expectations of local participation.
- **C.** Assemblies and town meetings gave some colonists a voice while excluding much of the population. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This directly supports both parts of the claim: meaningful representation and restricted political equality.
- **D.** The Crown allowed colonies to become independent republics during the seventeenth century.
  - Feedback: Colonies remained within a monarchical and imperial order. Local participation did not equal independence.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The evidence shows real participation inside a political community with firm boundaries.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. The best evidence must support both representation and exclusion at the same time.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Break the claim into two parts: representative government and limited democracy.
2. Reject evidence that supports only one part or makes an absolute claim.
3. Look for evidence of elected institutions together with restricted membership.
4. Choose the answer that directly supports the entire claim.

**Transfer strategy:** For evidence, select the choice that directly supports every important part of the claim, not merely the topic in general.

**Hint:** The strongest evidence must show both participation and exclusion.

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 4 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook discussion of colonial governments

**Taught claim:** Assemblies and town governments built habits of local participation without establishing broad political equality.

### ch02-use-001 | Cause | Developing

**Stem:** An English promoter wants a permanent colony rather than a seasonal raiding base. Which plan best applies the lesson of Roanoke?

**Choices:**
- **A.** Provide reliable resupply, varied skills, families, and workable Native relations. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This plan addresses the connected capacities that a permanent settlement required.
- **B.** Send a small military force and assume nearby communities will provide food indefinitely.
  - Feedback: Roanoke showed that military purpose and expectations of Native support could not substitute for sustainable planning.
- **C.** Rely on a royal charter because legal possession guarantees local survival.
  - Feedback: A charter could authorize a venture, but it could not supply food, labor, diplomacy, or reliable shipping.
- **D.** Wait for one decisive victory over Spain before attempting any settlement.
  - Feedback: English colonial ventures began before the Armada, and military victory did not automatically create settlement capacity.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The lesson of Roanoke is that permanent settlement required a connected system of support.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Apply the distinction between claiming territory and sustaining a community.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the model being applied: Roanoke failed through interdependent weaknesses.
2. List what a settlement needs that a raid does not: food, people, skills, resupply, financing, and diplomacy.
3. Reject plans that treat legal authority or military purpose as sufficient.
4. Choose the plan that strengthens several capacities at once.

**Transfer strategy:** When applying a historical model, identify the mechanism that produced the earlier outcome and look for the choice that changes that mechanism.

**Hint:** A permanent colony had to solve several problems at the same time, not merely receive permission to exist.

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 1 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook, section 2-4

**Taught claim:** Roanoke exposed the logistical, diplomatic, environmental, financial, and demographic requirements of permanent settlement.

### ch02-use-002 | Consequence | Developing

**Stem:** A colony discovers a labor-intensive export crop that quickly exhausts nearby soil. Based on Virginia's pattern, what is most likely to happen next?

**Choices:**
- **A.** The colony will reduce production so that land and labor become less important.
  - Feedback: A profitable export usually gives producers incentives to expand production rather than voluntarily reduce it.
- **B.** The colony will shift immediately to family farms and end its dependence on Atlantic trade.
  - Feedback: The scenario points toward deeper export dependence and labor demand, not an immediate shift away from them.
- **C.** The colony will become politically equal because profitable crops distribute wealth evenly.
  - Feedback: Plantation exports often concentrated land, labor control, and political influence rather than distributing them evenly.
- **D.** Demand for fresh land and workers will grow, pushing settlement outward and increasing conflict. **[Correct]**
  - Feedback: This applies the Virginia model: export profit creates continuing land and labor pressures with political consequences.

**Concise correct feedback:** Exactly. The crop's ecology and labor demands would likely reproduce Virginia's expansionary chain.

**Concise incorrect feedback:** Not quite. Predict what profitable production requires, then follow those requirements outward.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the relevant model: profitable staple crop plus soil exhaustion plus labor intensity.
2. Infer the direct needs: more acreage and more workers.
3. Infer the broader effects: outward settlement, labor coercion, inequality, and conflict over land.
4. Choose the answer that follows the model without adding an unsupported result.

**Transfer strategy:** For prediction, carry forward the causal mechanism that was taught rather than guessing from surface similarities.

**Hint:** What happens when profit depends on both fresh soil and a large workforce?

**Source alignment:** Chapter 2 Lecture 2 background essay, Reveal deck, and Historian's Notebook; Chapter 2 textbook, section 2-5

**Taught claim:** The tobacco model links export profit to land, labor, expansion, and conflict.

# HIST 101 — Think Like a Historian
## Developmental Reasoning and Scaffold Progression

**Status:** Canonical production standard  
**Course:** HIST 101 — U.S. History to 1877  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Purpose:** Guide the design of all future *Think Like a Historian* guided-practice worksheets and question banks.

---

## 1. Core Principle

The visible student-facing structure remains constant throughout HIST 101:

**Explain → Use → Connect**

Students should not have to learn a new practice format every exam unit. The labels remain familiar while the sophistication of the historical reasoning underneath them increases across the course.

The developmental goal is to move students from recognizing good historical explanations to weighing interpretations, testing them against evidence, considering perspective and scale, and eventually building larger historical arguments across time.

---

## 2. Permanent Student-Facing Structure

Every guided-practice session contains exactly three questions:

### Explain
Students identify or evaluate the strongest historical explanation.

### Use
Students apply a historical idea, model, or interpretation to a new but bounded situation.

### Connect
Students connect evidence, arguments, or explanatory models across lectures or historical contexts.

The student-facing labels **Explain, Use, and Connect do not change from unit to unit**.

---

## 3. Developmental Progression Across the Course

### Exam 1 — How Do Historians Explain What Happened?

**Primary goal:** Introduce historical explanation.

#### Explain
Identify the strongest historical explanation.

#### Use
Apply a taught historical idea or model to a new but bounded situation.

#### Connect
Connect evidence or arguments across lectures.

#### Primary reasoning emphasis
- Cause
- Context
- Comparison
- Consequence
- Evidence

Students are learning that history is not simply remembering what happened. Historians explain why events happened, how context shaped choices, what changed, and which evidence best supports an explanation.

---

### Exam 2 — How Do Historians Decide Which Explanation Is Stronger?

**Primary goal:** Introduce competing interpretations and explanatory power.

This is the major developmental step after Exam 1. Students should begin to understand that more than one interpretation can be plausible, but that interpretations are not therefore equally strong.

#### Explain → Weigh Competing Explanations
Students compare two or more plausible explanations and decide which has greater explanatory power.

Questions should increasingly ask:
- Which explanation accounts for more of the evidence?
- Which explanation requires fewer exceptions?
- Which interpretation better explains both the immediate event and its broader context?
- What does the weaker explanation still explain well?

Avoid constructing one sophisticated answer surrounded by obviously weak distractors. Competing interpretations should be genuinely plausible.

#### Use → Test an Interpretation
Students receive a new piece of evidence, situation, quotation, or bounded hypothetical and determine what it does to an interpretation.

Possible tasks:
- Supports the interpretation
- Complicates the interpretation
- Weakens the interpretation
- Requires the interpretation to be qualified
- Supports one explanation more strongly than another

Students should learn that historians test explanations against evidence rather than merely selecting opinions they prefer.

#### Connect → Build a Larger Interpretation
Students connect evidence or arguments from multiple lectures and determine what the connection helps explain.

The task should go beyond identifying a shared theme. Students should ask what the combination of evidence reveals that either example alone cannot explain as well.

#### Primary reasoning emphasis
- Competing interpretations
- Explanatory power
- Evidence that supports or complicates an argument
- Qualification
- Contingency
- Synthesis
- Partial explanations versus broader explanations

---

### Exam 3 — How Can Perspective, Scale, or Evidence Change an Interpretation?

**Primary goal:** Show that historical interpretation depends partly on the questions asked, the evidence examined, and the scale or perspective used.

Maintain **Explain → Use → Connect**, but increasingly ask students to consider:

- Different historical perspectives
- Local versus national scale
- Short-term versus long-term explanation
- Political, economic, social, cultural, or environmental evidence
- How adding a new source changes an interpretation
- How an interpretation may look different from another historical actor's position

Students should begin recognizing that changing the evidence or analytical lens can change the historical interpretation without making history arbitrary.

---

### Final Exam — How Do Historians Build and Defend Larger Interpretations Across Time?

**Primary goal:** Synthesis across chapters and periods.

Maintain **Explain → Use → Connect**, but increase the scale of reasoning.

Students should:
- Compare explanations across different historical periods
- Identify recurring patterns while recognizing changed contexts
- Build causal chains across time
- Evaluate larger claims using evidence from multiple chapters
- Distinguish a broad interpretation from an overgeneralization
- Defend a historical interpretation while acknowledging qualifications and counterevidence

The final stage asks students to move from recognizing historical reasoning to constructing and defending larger historical explanations.

---

## 4. Exam 2 Scaffolding Standard

Because Exam 2 introduces a genuine cognitive jump, early guided practice should provide stronger scaffolding.

### Early Exam 2 Practice

After the normal three-question session, include:

## How a Historian Would Work Through These

For each question, model the reasoning process:

1. **What is each interpretation actually claiming?**
2. **What evidence does each explanation account for?**
3. **What evidence complicates or weakens each explanation?**
4. **Which interpretation has greater explanatory power?**
5. **Could the weaker interpretation still explain part of the story?**
6. **What qualification would make the argument stronger?**

The walkthrough should explain the reasoning process, not merely reveal the correct answer.

### Middle Exam 2 Practice

Begin fading the scaffold.

Use shorter prompts such as:
- What evidence matters most here?
- Which interpretation explains more?
- What evidence creates a problem for your choice?
- Is one explanation partial rather than wrong?
- What qualification would improve the argument?

### Late Exam 2 Practice

Students should attempt the reasoning independently.

A full walkthrough may remain available through **Walk Me Through It**, but it should no longer appear automatically after every question.

The objective is gradual independence before the exam.

---

## 5. Feedback Standard

Feedback should be **response-specific**.

For a strong answer:
- Explain why the reasoning works.
- Identify the evidence or explanatory relationship the student recognized.

For a plausible but weaker answer:
- Acknowledge what the interpretation explains.
- Identify the evidence it fails to explain as well.
- Prompt the student to compare explanatory reach.

For an incorrect or poorly supported answer:
- Do not merely state that it is wrong.
- Identify the reasoning problem.
- Direct attention to the relevant evidence or comparison.
- Invite another attempt when appropriate.

The goal is to coach historical reasoning rather than reward guessing.

---

## 6. Historiographical Principle for First-Year Students

Do not create a separate student-facing question category called **Historiography**.

Historiography should emerge naturally through Explain, Use, and Connect.

Students encounter historiography when they:
- compare plausible explanations,
- determine which interpretation has greater explanatory power,
- test arguments against evidence,
- identify complications,
- qualify claims,
- consider perspective or scale,
- and synthesize evidence into broader interpretations.

This keeps historiography integrated into historical practice rather than presenting it as a specialized side topic.

## 7. Optional Historian Coach / Interpretation Lab

A future custom chatbot may be added as an **optional secondary scaffold**.

Possible student-facing names:
- **Historian Coach**
- **Interpretation Lab**

The guided-practice system must remain fully functional without the chatbot.

### Chatbot purpose

The chatbot should coach reasoning rather than provide answers immediately.

It should ask questions such as:
- What evidence made you choose that interpretation?
- What does the competing interpretation explain well?
- Is there evidence your explanation leaves out?
- Does your explanation work for both parts of the problem?
- What would you need to qualify?
- Is the other interpretation wrong, or is it a partial explanation?

### Chatbot response sequence

When possible:

1. Ask the student to state or identify their reasoning.
2. Ask what evidence supports it.
3. Introduce overlooked evidence or a competing explanation.
4. Ask the student to revise or qualify the claim.
5. Only then provide a fuller explanation of the historical reasoning.

### Permanent chatbot guardrail

**Never make the chatbot the only source of scaffolding.**

A student who does not use the chatbot must still receive enough feedback and walkthrough support from the guided-practice activity itself to learn the skill.

## 8. Source and Assessment Guardrails

Think Like a Historian practice should be built only after the relevant instructional materials are substantially final.

### Source priority

Use, in order:

1. Locked professor background essays
2. Final formal lecture decks
3. Historian's Notebook
4. Study Buddy materials, if used
5. Assigned textbook
6. Formal in-class activities

### Optional enrichment exclusion

Material found only in:
- **The Rabbit Hole**
- **Want to Go Deeper?**
- other explicitly optional enrichment sections

must not be required for Think Like a Historian questions unless it has first been promoted into the formal instructional stream.

### Secure exam separation

Think Like a Historian may rehearse:
- concepts,
- reasoning moves,
- models,
- misconceptions,
- interpretive habits,
- and related evidence.

It should **not reproduce secure exam questions or answers verbatim**.

Practice should teach the reasoning needed for the exam without revealing the exam itself.

---

## 9. Question-Bank Production Standard

For each chapter:

- Target **30–45 total questions**
- Quality takes priority over quantity
- Maintain broad balance among Explain, Use, and Connect
- Every question should have a clear historical-reasoning purpose
- Distractors should represent plausible misunderstandings or weaker interpretations
- Avoid trivia disguised as reasoning
- Avoid questions whose answer depends on wording memorized from a slide
- Avoid testing optional enrichment
- Avoid requiring outside research

Each student practice session should continue to present exactly:

**1 Explain → 1 Use → 1 Connect**

---

## 10. Production Checklist

Before finalizing a Think Like a Historian bank, confirm:

- [ ] Explain, Use, and Connect remain the visible student-facing labels.
- [ ] Reasoning difficulty matches the current exam-unit developmental stage.
- [ ] Questions are grounded in the formal instructional stream.
- [ ] No Rabbit Hole / Want to Go Deeper material is required.
- [ ] Explain questions test historical explanation rather than recall.
- [ ] Use questions require bounded application or interpretation testing.
- [ ] Connect questions require meaningful synthesis rather than simple topic matching.
- [ ] Competing interpretations are genuinely plausible when used.
- [ ] Feedback explains reasoning rather than merely identifying correctness.
- [ ] Early Exam 2 sessions include full reasoning walkthroughs.
- [ ] Scaffolding fades across the unit.
- [ ] Any chatbot remains optional and coaching-oriented.
- [ ] Secure exam items are not reproduced.
- [ ] First-year readability is maintained.
- [ ] Accessibility requirements are met.

## 11. Developmental Arc in One View

**Exam 1:**  
*How do historians explain what happened?*

**Exam 2:**  
*How do historians decide which explanation is stronger?*

**Exam 3:**  
*How can perspective, scale, or evidence change an interpretation?*

**Final:**  
*How do historians build and defend larger interpretations across time?*

The structure remains familiar. The historical thinking becomes progressively more sophisticated.

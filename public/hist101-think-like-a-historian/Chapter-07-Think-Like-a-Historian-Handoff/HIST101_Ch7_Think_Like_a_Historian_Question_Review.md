## ch07-explain-001 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Cause  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec01  
**Difficulty:** developing

Which interpretation best explains how the Constitution became a functioning national government after 1789?

- **A.** The Constitution itself supplied enough detail that later officials mainly had to follow written instructions.
  - Feedback: The Constitution mattered, but the lecture emphasizes how many operating routines were not specified and had to be created in practice.
- **B.** Washington's personal popularity was the main reason the new government could function.
  - Feedback: Washington's reputation helped, but popularity alone does not explain Cabinet practice, treaty consultation, public credit, or enforcement.
- **C.** Written powers became workable through precedents, administrative routines, and new fiscal capacity. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This interpretation explains both the missing operating rules and the government's growing ability to act.
- **D.** Hamilton's financial program alone transformed the Constitution into a working government.
  - Feedback: Hamilton's program was central to capacity, but it does not explain the institutional precedents created by Washington and the First Congress.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the outcome: a written Constitution became an operating government.
2. Test each interpretation against more than one part of the lecture.
3. Washington's popularity explains legitimacy, and Hamilton explains fiscal capacity, but each covers only part of the evidence.
4. The precedent-and-capacity interpretation explains the Senate experiment, Cabinet development, finance, taxation, and enforcement together.
5. The weaker explanations are partial, not useless. They simply explain less of the evidence.

**Transfer strategy:** For competing explanations, prefer the interpretation that accounts for the most evidence without pretending the smaller explanations are completely false.

**Source claim:** The Constitution became real through precedent and capacity, not text alone.

## ch07-explain-002 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Evidence  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec01  
**Difficulty:** developing

Which interpretation gives the Whiskey Rebellion the greatest explanatory importance?

- **A.** It was mainly a dispute over whether western farmers liked paying taxes.
  - Feedback: Tax burden mattered, but this interpretation is too narrow to explain why the federal government mobilized overwhelming force.
- **B.** It tested whether a constitutional republic could enforce a law while still claiming to protect liberty. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This captures the lecture's central tension between state capacity and republican legitimacy.
- **C.** It proved that the new federal government intended to punish all political dissent by force.
  - Feedback: The mobilization was coercive, but the absence of a major battle and the later pardons complicate this interpretation.
- **D.** It showed that the Constitution had failed because resistance continued after ratification.
  - Feedback: The episode showed conflict over legitimacy, not constitutional collapse.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Start with the event: resistance to the whiskey excise became armed enough to trigger federal mobilization.
2. Ask what larger problem the event revealed, not merely what the protesters disliked.
3. Federalists saw proof that laws could be enforced; Republicans could see dangerous coercive capacity.
4. The best interpretation explains why the same event could support both conclusions.
5. The narrow tax interpretation remains part of the story, but it does not explain the constitutional stakes.

**Transfer strategy:** When judging significance, ask what larger historical problem an event exposed or tested.

**Source claim:** The Whiskey Rebellion tested whether federal power could be both effective and legitimate.

## ch07-explain-004 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Context  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec02  
**Difficulty:** developing

Why did organized political opposition appear dangerous to many Americans in the 1790s?

- **A.** Because Americans had already accepted modern political parties and feared they were becoming too competitive.
  - Feedback: The problem was almost the reverse: organized parties were not yet accepted as a normal part of republican government.
- **B.** Because opposition looked like faction, and foreign crises made domestic critics easier to imagine as disloyal. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This combines the older fear of faction with the new pressure created by France, Genêt, XYZ, and the Quasi-War.
- **C.** Because the Constitution explicitly prohibited political parties.
  - Feedback: The Constitution did not ban parties. The fear came from republican political culture and crisis, not a constitutional prohibition.
- **D.** Because every opposition newspaper openly supported a foreign invasion of the United States.
  - Feedback: Partisan newspapers could be fierce, but this overstates the evidence and collapses criticism into treason.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Begin with the political context: many Americans associated organized faction with selfish attacks on the common good.
2. Add the foreign-policy context: the French Revolution, Genêt, XYZ, and naval conflict made loyalty feel urgent.
3. Ask which explanation can account for both the fear of parties and the escalation of repression.
4. The combined interpretation has greater explanatory power than either factional fear or foreign crisis alone.
5. That does not prove repression was justified; it explains why it became politically plausible.

**Transfer strategy:** For context, combine the political assumptions people already held with the crisis that made those assumptions more powerful.

**Source claim:** Foreign crisis intensified an existing fear that organized faction could endanger the republic.

## ch07-explain-005 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Consequence  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec02  
**Difficulty:** developing

Which interpretation best explains the historical importance of Matthew Lyon winning reelection from jail?

- **A.** It proved that the Sedition Act had ended organized Republican politics.
  - Feedback: His reelection shows the opposite: voters could continue supporting an opposition politician even after federal prosecution.
- **B.** It showed a collision between federal repression and voters' claim to choose their own representative. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This captures why Lyon makes the Sedition crisis concrete rather than abstract.
- **C.** It demonstrated that every voter opposed the Sedition Act.
  - Feedback: His victory is evidence of political resistance, but it does not show unanimous public opinion.
- **D.** It mattered mainly because jail automatically made candidates more popular.
  - Feedback: The significance lies in the conflict over legitimate opposition, not a general rule about jailed candidates.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify the two facts that matter together: federal conviction and electoral victory.
2. Ask what each fact represents: government repression on one side, voter choice on the other.
3. The strongest interpretation explains the contradiction between those two forms of authority.
4. That contradiction helps explain why the Sedition Act became a test of legitimate opposition.
5. A claim about universal public opinion goes beyond the evidence.

**Transfer strategy:** When a single person's story matters, ask what larger institutions or principles collide inside that story.

**Source claim:** Matthew Lyon makes the conflict between repression and electoral legitimacy visible.

## ch07-explain-006 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Consequence

**Difficulty:** advanced

Which explanation best accounts for why the election and transfer of power in 1800–1801 became a constitutional precedent?

- **A.** The election ended political parties and restored national consensus.
  - Feedback: Partisan conflict continued. The precedent was not the disappearance of parties.
- **B.** The victorious party rewrote the Constitution before taking office.
  - Feedback: No constitutional rewrite created the central precedent discussed in lecture.

**Transfer strategy:** For political transitions, distinguish 'conflict disappeared' from 'institutions survived conflict.'

**Source claim:** The 1801 transfer helped normalize the idea that an opposition party could become the government without ending the republic.

## ch07-explain-007 — Explain
**Thinking move:** Cause  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec03  
**Difficulty:** developing

Which interpretation best explains why Jefferson accepted the Louisiana Purchase despite his concern about limited federal power?

- **A.** Jefferson abandoned all constitutional principles as soon as he became president.
  - Feedback: The lecture treats his dilemma as genuine. He worried about constitutional authority even while accepting the opportunity.
- **B.** The strategic value and fleeting nature of the opportunity outweighed his desire for a narrow reading of federal power. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This captures the tension between principle and governing contingency.
- **C.** The Constitution clearly and specifically authorized the president to purchase foreign territory.
  - Feedback: The lecture emphasizes the opposite: the constitutional authority was not neatly stated.
- **D.** Jefferson cared only about increasing presidential power and not about western security.
  - Feedback: This misses the New Orleans crisis and the strategic logic of the purchase.

**Walkthrough:**
1. Identify Jefferson's principle: federal power should remain limited to delegated authority.
2. Identify the strategic problem: New Orleans and the Mississippi affected western security and national unity.

4. The strongest interpretation explains why principle and circumstance pulled in different directions.
5. Calling this simple hypocrisy names the contradiction but does not explain the decision.

**Transfer strategy:** When an actor seems inconsistent, test whether changing circumstances created a genuine tradeoff between principles.

**Source claim:** Jefferson's Purchase decision joined limited-government principle to strategic contingency.

## ch07-use-001 — Use
**Thinking move:** Evidence  
**Lectures:** ch07-lec01  
**Difficulty:** developing

A historian argues, 'The early presidency worked exactly as the Constitution's text had already designed it.' New evidence shows Washington tried in-person Senate consultation, found it unworkable, and shifted toward advice from department heads. What does that evidence do to the historian's claim?

- **A.** It strongly supports the claim because experimentation proves the written design was complete.
  - Feedback: Experimentation points to gaps in the operating routine, not a fully specified design.
- **B.** It complicates the claim by showing that practice and precedent helped determine how the presidency actually worked. **[STRONGEST]**
  - Feedback: This is the best use of the evidence: it does not deny the Constitution mattered, but it shows text alone was insufficient.
- **C.** It proves the Constitution had no influence on presidential government at all.
  - Feedback: That goes too far. Washington was acting within constitutional offices and powers even while working out procedures.
- **D.** It is irrelevant because Senate consultation has nothing to do with executive practice.
  - Feedback: The episode directly concerns how the executive and Senate would use the Constitution's advice-and-consent language.

**Walkthrough:**
1. State the original claim precisely: the text had already designed how the presidency would operate.
2. Identify the new evidence: Washington experimented and changed his procedure after the first approach failed.
3. Ask whether that evidence fits a fully specified operating design.
4. It does not. But the Constitution still supplied the offices and powers, so the strongest conclusion is 'complicate,' not 'destroy.'
5. A better interpretation would say constitutional text and precedent worked together.

**Transfer strategy:** When testing a claim, avoid all-or-nothing choices. Evidence often requires qualification rather than total rejection.

**Source claim:** Presidential practice helped fill constitutional gaps.

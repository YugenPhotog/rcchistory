Primary Source Evaluation: Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics

Instructions

Read the following:

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II only — pages 160 to 165

Exactly where to start and stop

BEGIN Page 160, at the heading BOOK II, section 1.
First words: “Virtue, then, being of two kinds, intellectual and moral…”
END Page 165, at the end of section 9.
Last words: “…wherefore goodness is both rare and laudable and noble.”

Book I, which comes before your starting point, is not assigned. The essay by Julia Driver that begins on page 166, right after your stopping point, is not assigned either — it is a modern scholar explaining Aristotle, and using it would break the source rule below. Stop at the end of section 9 and close the book.

This reading introduces virtue ethics — an approach to morality that asks not “What should I do?” but “What kind of person should I become?” Aristotle argues that moral virtue is a matter of character, developed through habit and practice, and that virtuous action lies in finding the “mean” between extremes.

Your task is to analyze Aristotle's argument closely: identify his central claims about virtue and character, explain how his reasoning works, and connect his ideas to broader ethical questions in the course.

Requirements

Your Primary Source Evaluation must address the following elements in a clear, organized response:

  1. Explain the Author's Claim: What is Aristotle's central argument about how people become virtuous? What role does habit play in moral development?
  2. Analyze its Meaning: How does the doctrine of the mean work? Choose one virtue Aristotle discusses and explain how it represents a mean between two extremes. Why does Aristotle insist the mean is “relative to us”?
  3. Connect to Course Themes: How does Aristotle's virtue ethics differ from the other ethical theories we've studied (utilitarianism, Kantian ethics, social contract theory)? What does virtue ethics emphasize that the other approaches overlook or minimize?

The evaluation should be written in original prose and must demonstrate close reading, clear explanation, and thoughtful analysis of the primary text. Use at least one direct quotation from the reading to support your analysis. Target length: 600 to 900 words.

A note on requirement 2. The word “mean” does not mean average. Aristotle says so directly, using an athlete named Milo as his example. If your explanation of the mean would still be true if you replaced it with “everything in moderation,” you have not yet got his argument — and he explicitly denies that some actions have a mean at all. Read section 6 slowly.

Using The Phronimos for Revision

After completing your original rough draft using only the textbook, primary source, and YOUR OWN reasoning ability, you may use The Phronimos to help you think more deeply about your analysis and improve your work through revision.

What is The Phronimos?

The name comes from your reading. Aristotle says the mean is fixed “by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it.” The Greek word for that person is phronimos. The bot is named after a standard, not a sage.

It is an AI tutor designed to ask questions—not provide answers. It is not Aristotle and will not tell you what Aristotle “really meant.” It will help you find gaps in your reasoning, test your reading against the actual text, and push you to apply the doctrine of the mean to particular cases rather than restate it in general terms.

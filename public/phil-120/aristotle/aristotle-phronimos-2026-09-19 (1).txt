STUDENT:
Aristotle's central claim in Book II is that moral virtue is not something we are born with and not something taught the way geometry is taught. It comes about through habit. He notes that the word for moral virtue, ethike, is a slight variation on ethos, meaning habit, and argues that nature gives us only the capacity to receive the virtues, which are then made perfect by habit. His example is the stone, which cannot be trained to move upward no matter how many times it is thrown. Virtue is different: we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts.

The doctrine of the mean describes what those acts look like. Aristotle distinguishes the intermediate in the object, which is the same for everyone, from the intermediate relative to us, which is not one and not the same for all. Courage is the mean concerning fear and confidence, with cowardice as the deficiency and rashness as the excess. The mean is not an average, and Aristotle shows this with the trainer who will not order six pounds of food simply because it sits between ten and two. What is right for Milo is not right for a beginner. The standard is not arithmetic and not personal preference; it is what the man of practical wisdom would determine.

This is what separates virtue ethics from the theories we have studied. Utilitarianism asks about consequences and Kant asks about duty, but both are asking what makes an action right. Aristotle asks what makes a person good, and section 4 shows why the difference matters: an act done by chance or at someone else's suggestion does not count as virtuous, because the agent must choose it for its own sake and act from settled character.

STUDENT:
The difficulty is that if doing just acts is what makes you just, then anyone already doing just acts would have to be just already, the way someone doing grammatical things is a grammarian. Aristotle's answer is that the analogy with the arts breaks down: you can do something grammatical by chance or at another person's suggestion, and with virtue the agent's condition is part of what we are assessing, not just the product. So the acts that build the state and the acts that flow from it look alike from outside and differ in the agent. I think that resolves the logical circle, though it leaves open how the very first acts get chosen before any of the conditions are met.

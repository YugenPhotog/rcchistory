The Problem of Human Evil

God, Evil, and the Human Will after the Holocaust

Content note: this lecture describes the hanging of a child at Auschwitz and the burning of a town’s Jews in a barn. The images are paintings, not photographs. Look away if you need to; you can still listen.

Background image: Expressionist painting of rows of prisoners seen from behind on a parade ground at dusk, three dark posts against a red sky.

Script

Because human nature is fundamentally the same and universal regardless of time or place we are able to understand each other – whether our neighbors or classmates today or the ancient Confucians, Hindus, or Buddhists. Because we have the same nature, when we read about the ancient’s desire for power, wealth or love, we can understand that as we desire the same things. When we read about the ancients desire for the good life, or to take care of their families and children we can understand that too.

The wrench in the works is human evil. How can we understand evil? If human nature is fundamentally the same and universal regardless of time and place (which I propose that it is) then how do we account for the outright evil that allows for these atrocities to take place. In other words, what must necessarily transform the human heart to turn a common person, a neighbor, a doctor into a cold blooded killer and rapist? Or, is the natural man fundamentally evil and it is only law and civilization that prevents (Thomas Hobbes) these kinds of atrocities from taking place on a daily basis.

Many people want to understand evil by pathologizing it – they assume evil doers are mentally ill. And while this may be true for some, for most evil is the only explanation. And this kind of evil is unfathomable. It is so far distant from normal human nature, that surely we cannot share the same nature. whether good or evil. Our ability to think (inner dialogue), to choose, and our subsequent actions determine our nature. Monsters or Saints are made not born.

And where is God? How can an good, omnipotent god allow these atrocities to take place. And if we choose continued faith, how can we accept or explain the suffering of millions of children during these atrocities and even every day? Genocide has forced theologians to re-evaluate the existence, goodness, and omnipotence of God. Jewish theology has a long history of explaining suffering; but the holocaust is of an order of magnitude so much greater that it fundamentally altered Jewish theology and practice even through today.

Script

Seventy years before Auschwitz, a novelist puts that protest in its sharpest form.

Fyodor Dostoevsky. The Brothers Karamazov. 1880.

Ivan Karamazov is a brilliant skeptic. He tells his younger brother Alyosha, a novice monk, story after story of children tortured by adults. He grants that God may exist. He grants that at the end of time there may be a universal harmony, in which everything is explained.

He refuses it anyway. No harmony is worth the tears of one tortured child.

“It isn’t God I don’t accept, Alyosha; I am only most respectfully returning Him the ticket.”

After Auschwitz, Ivan’s protest stops being a classroom exercise. The question is no longer whether there is evil in the world. It is whether any account of God can stand next to a child on a gallows.

What “returning the ticket” means

Ivan is rejecting a classic theodicy: the idea that present suffering—especially the suffering of innocent children—can be justified by pointing ahead to a final “higher harmony,” eternal peace, or heavenly reward in which everything is made right.

The “ticket” is his metaphor for admission into that final divine harmony—the kingdom of God, the reconciled order at the end of time.

By returning the ticket, he refuses to take part in any moral order whose foundation includes the unavenged tears of even one innocent child. He declines the arrangement even if that means forgoing the heavenly payoff himself.

Important nuance from the text: “It isn’t God I don’t accept, Alyosha; I am only most respectfully returning Him the ticket.” He is not simply denying that God exists. Alyosha quietly names this stance rebellion.

“Attributed to Epicurus”

The classic form of this dilemma is credited to the Greek philosopher Epicurus (341–270 BCE), but no writing of his containing it survives.

It comes to us through the Christian writer Lactantius, around 313 CE, who attributes it to Epicurus in order to answer it (On the Anger of God 13.20–21). The attribution is disputed; some scholars think it derives from later skeptical sources.

Script

Behind Wiesel, a man asked where God was. Ivan asked whether any harmony could be worth one child’s tears. The question is very old, and it has a name.

Theodicy. Two Greek words. Theos, god. Dikē, justice. A theodicy is an attempt to justify God’s goodness in the face of evil. The philosopher Leibniz coined the word, in 1710.

The problem is often drawn as a triangle. Three claims. God is all-powerful. God is all-good. Evil is real.

Any two sit together comfortably. Hold all three, and the triangle breaks.

If God is good and cannot stop evil, God is not all-powerful. If God can stop it and will not, is God good?

The classic form of this dilemma is credited to the Greek philosopher Epicurus. But no writing of his that contains it survives. It comes to us through a Christian writer, Lactantius, around the year 313, who repeats it in order to answer it. So we say: attributed to Epicurus.

After Auschwitz, believers had to face that triangle again.

Parallel with Dostoevsky

Parallel with Dostoevsky: In Dostoevsky’s “Grand Inquisitor,” the bishop interrogates a silent Jesus, accusing him of burdening humanity with freedom and justifying the Church’s power in his place. Both scenes stage a human trial of the divine that ends in the accused’s silence, forcing the audience to weigh the charges themselves.

Script

Rubenstein leaves that meeting convinced. He can no longer believe in the God of covenant and history: the God who chooses a people, and acts in its history to reward and punish.

If that God exists, then Auschwitz was God’s will. Rubenstein will not say that.

So he puts the choice starkly. Either God willed Auschwitz, as Grüber said. Or the old picture of a God who rewards and punishes in history is false.

A kind, brave man, who had risked his life for Jews, had followed the old theology to its logical end. And the end was monstrous. For Rubenstein, that is the proof that the theology has to go.

In After Auschwitz, in 1966, he writes that we live in the time of the death of God. We live, he says, in a cold, silent universe, indifferent to human purposes.

He does not abandon Judaism. Its rituals and its community, he argues, are more necessary than ever.

Many Jews are scandalized. Rubenstein later softens some of his claims. But after his book, no one can talk about God after the Holocaust as though nothing had happened.

The Book of Job, the Akedah, and Lamentations

The Book of Job begins like a folk tale. Job is a wealthy man in the land of Uz, "blameless and upright, one who feared God and turned away from evil" (Job 1:1, NRSV). In the heavenly court, "the satan," the accuser, suggests that Job is good only because God has made him prosperous. God permits a test. In one day Job loses his livestock, his servants, and his ten children. Then he is struck with sores from head to foot.

Three friends come to comfort him, and for most of the book they argue. Their position is simple: God is just, so suffering must be punishment, so Job must have sinned. Job insists he has not, and demands that God answer him. Finally God speaks "out of the whirlwind" (38:1): "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding" (38:4). God never explains the wager. God shows Job the size and strangeness of creation, and Job falls silent.

Yet at the end God turns to the friends: "My wrath is kindled against you and against your two friends; for you have not spoken of me what is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7). The protester was right; the defenders of easy justice were wrong. The book refuses the idea that suffering is always deserved. That is why Wiesel and other post-Holocaust writers return to it.

Two other biblical resources mattered to Jews long before 1945. The Akedah, the binding of Isaac (Genesis 22), became in medieval Jewish memory a model for martyrdom, especially after the Rhineland massacres of the First Crusade in 1096. The Book of Lamentations mourns the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon in 587 BCE and is read every year on the fast of Tisha B'Av. Both gave Jews a language for catastrophe; neither offered an explanation.

Berkovits, Jonas, and Greenberg

Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), an Orthodox rabbi, argued in Faith after the Holocaust (1973) that God's power is shown in self-restraint. For human beings to be free and responsible, God must at times hide his face, hester panim, a biblical phrase (Deuteronomy 31:17–18). The price of human freedom is that human beings can commit Auschwitz. Berkovits did not think this made the suffering acceptable; he thought faith had to live with the question.

Hans Jonas (1903–1993), a philosopher whose mother was murdered at Auschwitz, gave a lecture in 1984 called "The Concept of God after Auschwitz." He proposed a God who, in creating a free world, gave up power, so that God suffers with the world but cannot intervene. Jonas chose to keep God's goodness and give up God's omnipotence: one corner of the triangle removed.

Irving Greenberg (born 1933), an American Orthodox rabbi, proposed, in an address given in New York in the early 1970s and published in 1977, a "working principle" for theology after the Holocaust: "No statement, theological or otherwise, should be made that would not be credible in the presence of the burning children." He had in mind the Hungarian Jewish children thrown alive into fire pits at Auschwitz-Birkenau in 1944. He also spoke of "moment faiths," moments when faith is possible and moments when it is not.

The Qur'an addresses suffering as trial. God "created death and life to test you [people] and reveal which of you does best" (67:2), and "We shall certainly test you with fear and hunger, and loss of property, lives, and crops" (2:155), with good news for those who, when disaster strikes, say "We belong to God and to Him we shall return" (2:156). The Qur'an also tells the story of human evil's origin. Karma, rebirth, and the roots of harm

In Hindu traditions, karma means action and its consequences. Every action leaves an imprint that ripens, in this life or a later one, within the cycle of rebirth, samsara. Suffering is therefore not the arbitrary act of a creator but the fruit of past action. This moves the problem away from God's justice: there is no all-powerful judge who could have prevented it, only a moral order that works like a law of nature. In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna teaches Arjuna to act according to his duty without attachment to the fruits of action, a teaching about how to act in a world shaped by karma.

Buddhism, which has no creator God, has no theodicy in the Western sense. The Buddha taught that life is marked by dukkha, suffering or unsatisfactoriness, which arises from craving. The roots of harmful action are three: greed, hatred, and delusion. Evil is not a cosmic power but a condition of the untrained mind, and it can be uprooted through the path of ethical conduct, meditation, and wisdom.

Karma raises its own hard question. Does it mean the victims of genocide deserved their fate? Many teachers in both traditions reject that use of karma. They point out that karma is meant to guide one's own conduct, not to judge other people's suffering, and that compassion is the right response to suffering. Students should notice how morally intolerable the other reading would be, and why.

Ivan Karamazov’s rebellion

The lecture borrowed Ivan Karamazov's image of returning the ticket. Here is the whole scene, which many readers consider the most powerful statement of the problem of evil ever written.

The Brothers Karamazov (1880) was Fyodor Dostoevsky's last novel. It tells of a coarse, greedy father, Fyodor Karamazov, and his three sons: Dmitri, passionate and reckless; Ivan, an educated intellectual; and Alyosha, the youngest, a novice in the local monastery under a beloved elder, Father Zosima. In Book 5, "Pro and Contra," Ivan and Alyosha meet in a tavern. Ivan, who is about to leave town, wants for once to talk to his brother about the things that matter.

Ivan begins by saying he accepts God. What he cannot accept is God's world. Then he tells stories, collected, he says, from newspapers and histories. Turkish soldiers in Bulgaria who tossed babies in the air and caught them on bayonets in front of their mothers. A well-educated couple who beat their five-year-old daughter and locked her on freezing nights in an outhouse, where she wept and prayed to "dear little God" to protect her. And a retired general on his estate, with kennels of hundreds of hounds, whose favorite hound was hurt in the paw by a stone thrown in play by a serf boy of eight. The general had the boy locked up overnight, then in the morning, before the household and the boy's mother, had him stripped and made to run, and set the hounds on him. They tore him to pieces before his mother's eyes.

Zosima’s answer, and Hick on Ivan

Dostoevsky knew Ivan's argument was strong; he wrote to his editor that the next book was meant as its answer. That answer, in Book 6, "The Russian Monk," is not an argument but a life: the teachings of the dying Father Zosima. Zosima tells of his older brother Markel, who died young and who, near the end, told their mother that "each of us is guilty before everyone, for everything, and I more than all." Zosima made those words the heart of his teaching. Earlier in the novel, in his last words to the monks (Book 4, chapter 1), he says that a monk's goal is reached only when he realizes that he is "guilty before all people, for all and for everything, for all human sins, of the world and of each one." Instead of standing outside the world and judging God for it, Zosima calls people to take responsibility for its suffering through active love. Whether that answers Ivan is something readers have argued about ever since.

John Hick took Ivan seriously in Evil and the God of Love. He accepted that no theodicy could make a tortured child's suffering acceptable in itself, and he did not claim to justify any particular horror. His soul-making view could only argue that a world in which real persons are formed in freedom requires a real possibility of terrible evil, and that the final good God brings about, beyond this life, will be so great that it will be seen to have made the whole process worthwhile. Hick admitted that this answer could be judged only from the end, and that Ivan's protest remains the strongest objection to it.

Milgram and Zimbardo

In July 1961, three months after the Eichmann trial opened, the Yale psychologist Stanley Milgram began an experiment. Volunteers were told they were helping study learning and were asked to give electric shocks of increasing strength to a "learner," actually an actor, whenever he made a mistake. In the best-known version, about two-thirds of participants went all the way to the highest level on the board, 450 volts, despite the learner's protests, because a man in a lab coat told them to continue. Milgram published Obedience to Authority in 1974 and linked his results directly to Arendt.

Later research complicated the picture. Gina Perry's study of the Yale archives (2012) found that experimenters often departed from the script and that many participants suspected the shocks were not real. Obedience rates varied widely across Milgram's many versions. Still, partial replications, such as Jerry Burger's in 2009, found that many people will go further than they expect.

In August 1971, Philip Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment assigned college students to be "guards" or "prisoners" in a mock prison. It was stopped after six days because some guards became abusive. For decades it was taught as proof that situations make people cruel. But in 2018 Thibault Le Texier, using the Stanford archives, showed that the guards had been coached toward toughness and that the study was not the neutral experiment it claimed to be. Treat it as a cautionary story, not as evidence.

Hobbes and Rousseau

Lecture 1 explained how Thomas Hobbes used the unit's epigraph. His fuller view of human nature appears in Leviathan (1651), chapter 13. Without a common power to keep them in awe, he argued, human beings live in a condition of war of every man against every man, in which the life of man is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." People are driven by competition, fear, and the desire for glory. Only a strong sovereign can restrain them.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau answered in his Discourse on the Origin of Inequality (1755). Hobbes's "natural man," he said, was really social man, already corrupted. In the state of nature human beings were simple, solitary, and moved by self-preservation and by pity, a natural reluctance to see others suffer. Corruption came with society: property, comparison, vanity, and inequality.

The Holocaust is uncomfortable for both. It happened inside one of the most orderly, lawful, educated states in Europe, which seems to refute Hobbes's faith in the sovereign. And it was done with the tools of advanced civilization, which might seem to support Rousseau, except that the killers of Jedwabne used clubs and a barn.

In Eichmann before Jerusalem (German 2011; English 2014), the historian Bettina Stangneth studied the writings and taped conversations Eichmann produced among Nazi exiles in Argentina in the 1950s. They show a committed, proud antisemite, nothing like the colorless functionary Arendt saw in the courtroom. Many scholars now think Eichmann performed thoughtlessness at his trial to save his life.

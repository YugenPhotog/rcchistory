Ethical Foundations

The Ten Commandments

The first table of the Law calls on Christians to worship only God, not to worship images, and to keep the Sabbath day holy.

The second table tells Christians to honor parents and abstain from murder, adultery, theft, false witness, and coveting.

The Sermon on the Mount

Beatitudes contain implicit moral directions.

Jesus pointed to the necessity of controlling one’s actions, thoughts, and emotions.

To love the Lord your God with all your being, and to love your neighbor as yourself.

Understanding This Slide

This transition moves from Jesus’ teachings to broader Christian moral formation. Ethical traditions draw on Hebrew scripture, Jesus, apostolic writings, natural law, virtues, conscience, community, and changing historical problems.

There is no single Christian position on every public issue. The shared task is discerning how love of God and neighbor should shape character, institutions, and responsibility.

Ethical Foundations: Paul’s Letters

  • The Pauline Epistles draw on the Ten Commandments to shape the moral life of Christians
  • They stress the moral virtues of the Christian life: trust in God, hope in the future God will bring, peace in one’s heart and in the church, and love for all people
  • They ground the ethical life of the Christian in the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus

And now faith, hope, and love abide, these three; and the greatest of these is love.

1 Corinthians 13:13 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Pauline ethics does not treat moral conduct as a way to purchase salvation. Virtues are the fruit of life in Christ and in the Spirit, expressed through communities learning fidelity, hope, peace, mutual service, and love.

Paul also adapts inherited commandments to mixed Jewish-Gentile congregations. His letters address concrete conflicts, so students should distinguish enduring principles from first-century social settings.

Also useful: Paul’s list of the “fruit of the Spirit”: “love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control” (Galatians 5:22–23).

Ethics

Christian Ethics: From Neighbor to Society

Following Jesus involves both personal character and public responsibility

Character

Faith, hope, love, humility, forgiveness, and reconciliation.

Neighbor

The Good Samaritan expands moral concern beyond familiar boundaries.

Society

Churches address poverty, labor, war, racism, human rights, and care for creation.

Liberation

Black, feminist, Latin American, and other theologies begin from experiences of oppression.

Shared scriptures do not automatically produce one political program; Christians debate how love and justice apply.

Understanding This Slide

Connect personal ethics to institutional ethics. Catholic social encyclicals, ecumenical organizations, Protestant reform movements, Orthodox social teaching, and Christian relief agencies represent different ways churches apply moral traditions beyond individual conduct.

Liberation theology asks how Scripture sounds when read from the standpoint of people experiencing poverty or oppression. Black and feminist theologians similarly expose the way supposedly universal theology can overlook race or gender. Present these as influential Christian approaches, not as positions accepted uniformly by all churches.

The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:30–37), the story on the Section I divider: a man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho is beaten by robbers; a priest and a Levite pass by on the other side; a Samaritan “was moved with pity,” bandaged his wounds, and paid for his care. Jesus asks, “Which of these three… was a neighbor?” and ends, “Go and do likewise” (10:36–37).

Optional discussion (the original Pause & Reflect prompt, original slide 19, kept here): “Which is the most demanding expression of kingdom ethics: enemy-love, solidarity with outsiders, inner transformation, or public justice? Why?” Require students to connect their answer to at least one teaching or example from the section. The categories overlap: inner transformation becomes visible in relationships and institutions, while public justice depends on formed character and communities. (The reading-guide pause for this section is L, on the Sermon on the Mount.)

Christian Worship and Ritual

  • The first believers worshiped in the Jerusalem temple and synagogues
  • The Roman Catholic Church built itself around seven sacraments
  • Eastern Orthodox churches preserve shared liturgical traditions across regional branches
  • Protestant reforms, Catholic renewal, and Vatican II each reshaped “the work of the people” (liturgy)

Understanding This Slide

Christian worship begins within Jewish sacred space and practice: Temple, synagogue, scripture, prayer, psalms, festivals, and meals. Distinct Christian forms develop around baptism, the Lord’s Supper, Sunday gathering, and proclamation of Jesus.

Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant worship should not be portrayed as a simple progression. Each contains internal diversity and multiple reform movements, while the phrase work of the people captures the participatory meaning of liturgy.

Story first: Acts says the first believers spent “much time together in the temple” and broke bread “at home” (Acts 2:46). They had no church buildings at all.

Sources

Acts 2:46, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS).

From House Church to Basilica

  • Christian worship became increasingly public and elaborate after legal toleration and imperial patronage in the 300s
  • Formal church buildings were erected; church officials dressed for worship in clothing modeled after Roman government garb
  • A liturgical calendar was developed; church buildings were designed for the community
  • Large basilicas changed the scale and social character of Christian gatherings

Mini-lecture: From House Church to Basilica →

Understanding This Slide

Legal toleration and imperial patronage enabled public basilicas, processions, professional clergy, councils, and an increasingly elaborate calendar. These developments made worship more visible and connected to civic life.

Large buildings changed scale and social relationships but did not simply erase intimacy. House churches, monasteries, chapels, parish communities, and domestic devotions continued alongside monumental worship.

Charismatic and Pentecostal Worship

  • Charismatic movement: stresses the use of supernatural gifts of the Holy Spirit
  • Charismatic: groups inside Roman Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant churches
  • Pentecostal: Protestant churches where these practices are the norm

All of them were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages, as the Spirit gave them ability.

Acts 2:4 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Pentecostal churches place gifts such as tongues, prophecy, healing, testimony, and spontaneous prayer near the center of communal worship. Charismatic renewal brings similar practices into older denominations.

These movements are global and internally diverse. Their worship often stresses direct experience of the Holy Spirit, lay participation, music, embodied response, and expectation of divine action.

Story first: in April 1906, in a former church building on Azusa Street in Los Angeles, a Black holiness preacher named William J. Seymour led meetings where Black, white, and Latino worshipers prayed together and spoke in tongues. The revival ran for about three years and is often treated as a starting point of modern Pentecostalism, though similar revivals happened elsewhere (for example in Wales, India, and Topeka, Kansas). Today Pentecostal and charismatic Christians number in the hundreds of millions, many of them in Africa, Latin America, and Asia.

Acts 2 explains the name: the gift of the Spirit came at the Jewish festival of Pentecost (Lecture 1, slide U). Paul lists “various kinds of tongues” and their “interpretation” among the gifts of the Spirit (1 Cor. 12:10).

About the image (AI-generated): a present-day Pentecostal service in a simple church building, of the kind common in the Global South. An imagined scene, not a specific congregation.

Worship and Ritual

Baptism and Eucharist

The two practices recognized across nearly all Christian traditions

Baptism

Initiation into Christian life. Water signifies cleansing, death and rebirth, and incorporation into the Church. Infant or believer’s baptism; immersion or pouring.

Eucharist / Communion

Remembers Jesus’s Last Supper. Bread and wine proclaim participation in Christ. Understood as sacrifice, real presence, spiritual presence, or memorial.

Common ritual forms express Christian unity, while their interpretation reveals major denominational differences.

Understanding This Slide

Baptism is associated with forgiveness, reception of the Spirit, union with Christ's death and resurrection, and membership in the Church. Traditions disagree over infant baptism, the proper mode, and whether baptism itself conveys grace or testifies to faith already present.

For Catholics, transubstantiation names the change of bread and wine into Christ's body and blood while appearances remain. Orthodox churches strongly affirm mystery and real presence without making one scholastic explanation universally definitive. Protestant views range from Lutheran sacramental presence to Reformed spiritual presence and memorial interpretations.

The oldest description of the Eucharist (1 Cor. 11:24, NRSV): Paul, writing about 20 years after Jesus’ death, repeats what Jesus said over the bread: “This is my body that is for you. Do this in remembrance of me.”

Sources

1 Corinthians 11:24, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS).

The Seven Sacraments

Initiation

Baptism · Confirmation · Eucharist

Healing

Reconciliation · Anointing of the Sick

Service and Vocation

Marriage · Holy Orders

The sacraments are efficacious signs of grace, instituted by Christ and entrusted to the Church, by which divine life is dispensed to us.

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1131

Orthodox Christianity also celebrates these sacred mysteries; most Protestants recognize baptism and Communion as the rites directly instituted by Jesus.

Understanding This Slide

A sacrament is commonly defined as a visible sign through which divine grace is communicated. Catholic teaching groups the seven as sacraments of initiation, healing, and service of communion. Eastern Orthodox Christians often call them mysteries and do not always emphasize an exhaustive numerical list in the same way.

Many Protestant traditions reserve "sacrament" for baptism and the Lord's Supper. Others prefer the word "ordinance" to emphasize obedience and testimony rather than a rite that conveys grace. Avoid implying that churches with two sacraments have only two important rituals.

For the reading-guide question: “efficacious” means they actually do what they signify. The Catechism also teaches that the sacraments work ex opere operato, “by the very fact of the action’s being performed,” not by the holiness of the priest (CCC 1128), and that “for believers the sacraments of the New Covenant are necessary for salvation” (CCC 1129).

The original subtitle “Catholic organization of ritual life from initiation through vocation and healing” is replaced on the slide by the Catechism definition.

Sources

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1128, 1129, 1131, vatican.va (archive/ENG0015), checked 2026-10-19; quotations verbatim.

The Christian Liturgical Year

Worship organizes time around the story of Christ

Advent
Expectation→Christmas
Birth→Epiphany
Revealed→Lent & Holy Week
Passion→Easter
New life→Pentecost
Spirit

The calendar teaches doctrine through repeated practices, colors, readings, fasting, and feasting.

Understanding This Slide

The liturgical year is most formal in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and some Reformed traditions, but Christmas and Easter shape worship across much of Christianity. A lectionary coordinates readings from Scripture so congregations move through the biblical story over time.

Eastern and Western calendars do not always place feasts on the same civil dates because of different calendar systems and calculations. "Ordinary Time" does not mean unimportant time; the term refers to numbered weeks devoted to Jesus's teaching and the Church's ongoing life.

The seasons in full (original slide text): Advent: expectation and preparation. Christmas: incarnation and birth of Jesus. Epiphany: Christ revealed to the nations. Lent and Holy Week: repentance, Passion, and crucifixion. Easter: resurrection and new life. Pentecost and Ordinary Time: Spirit, Church, discipleship, and mission. Worship and Belief

Death, Resurrection, and Christian Hope

Christian hope centers on resurrection and renewed creation, not only an immortal soul

Resurrection

God raises the dead; embodied life is transformed rather than merely discarded.

Judgment and Afterlife

Traditions teach heaven and hell differently; Catholic teaching also includes purgatory.

Funeral Practice

Prayer, Scripture, remembrance, burial or cremation, and hope in Christ’s victory over death.

Christian traditions agree on resurrection in the creeds while differing over the sequence, imagery, and details of the afterlife.

Understanding This Slide

The Apostles' and Nicene Creeds affirm resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Popular Christianity often speaks mainly of souls going to heaven, but classical Christian teaching culminates in bodily resurrection and the renewal of creation.

Purgatory in Catholic teaching is purification of those destined for communion with God, not a second chance after final rejection. Orthodox teaching uses different categories, and Protestants generally reject purgatory. Funeral customs vary culturally, but they typically combine mourning with proclamation of resurrection hope.

The earliest summary (1 Cor. 15:3–4, NRSV): “that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the scriptures, and that he was buried, and that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the scriptures.” Christian hope for the dead is built on this claim.

1 Corinthians 15:3–4, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS).

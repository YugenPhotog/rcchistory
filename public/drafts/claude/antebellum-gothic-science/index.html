History · Religion · EthicsUnited States, 1820–1861

The Threshold
Antebellum Gothic Science

When science made the dead move and the living feel nothing, where did life end, and who got to decide?

A lecture and self-study exhibition in four rooms · Press → to begin

Welcome students into the room as if into an exhibition. The image on screen is a single line: the threshold between alive and dead, with a spark crossing it. That spark is the whole lecture in miniature. Between roughly 1820 and 1861, Americans encountered sciences and technologies that made that line harder to see: electricity that could make dead muscles move, mesmerism that seemed to suspend the mind, ether that let a living body sleep through surgery, and a telegraph that sent words without bodies. Gothic writers did not invent these anxieties; they wrote them down.

Set expectations: we will take these people seriously. They were not naive. Many were careful observers working with the best tools they had. Our job is to sort what they knew, what they guessed, what they believed, what was dressed up as science, and what was imagined in fiction, and then to ask whose bodies paid for the knowledge.

A mysteryGlasgow · 4 November 1818

A hanged man’s face begins to move.

In a packed anatomy theater, Andrew Ure and James Jeffray sent battery current into the body of Matthew Clydesdale, hanged for murder that day. Witnesses saw the chest heave and the face contort.

Established: the experiment happened
Speculation: could current revive the dead?

Begin with the mystery, not the explanation. Describe the scene: a crowded anatomy theater in Glasgow, a body brought straight from the gallows, a voltaic battery, and then movement. Let students sit with the strangeness for a moment. Ask: what would you have concluded if you had been in the room?

Then separate the layers. Established: electricity stimulates muscle; Galvani showed this decades earlier. Speculation: Ure wondered whether stimulating the phrenic nerve might restore breathing in apparent death by suffocation. Popular embellishment: later retellings made it a near-resurrection. Point out that the image is the 1831 frontispiece of Frankenstein, a literary work, and that both Glasgow and London are European. The American story is about reception and transformation.

The questionWhat this lecture argues

Science explained more of the natural world every decade. Why did the boundary between life and death, and between a person and a specimen, seem harder to draw?

Claim 1

New sciences really did blur old boundaries: a living body could be made insensible, a dead one made to move.

Claim 2

Gothic fiction was one way Americans thought through that uncertainty, not a retreat from science.

Claim 3

The power to draw the line was unequal. The bodies studied, displayed, and measured were mostly poor, enslaved, or Black.

Read the question aloud slowly. Then unpack the three claims. Emphasize that this is an argument students can test, not a set of facts to memorize. The first claim concerns knowledge, the second culture, the third power. Tell students that each of the four rooms leans toward one of these claims: Invisible Forces and Doubtful Death toward the first two, the Body on the Table and the Measure of a Person toward the third.

ObjectivesBy the end you should be able to
  1. 01Explain how electricity, mesmerism, anesthesia, and anatomy unsettled the line between life and death.
  2. 02Distinguish knowledge, speculation, belief, pseudoscience, and fiction, as people saw them then and as we see them now.
  3. 03Interpret Poe and Hawthorne as commentary on scientific ambition.
  4. 04Evaluate how scientific authority was used to defend slavery, and how it was contested.

Keep this brief. Point out that objective 02 is the skill that runs through the whole lecture: the evidence labels on each slide are there to practice it. Objective 04 asks for evaluation, not just description: students should be able to say why claims like Cartwright's or Morton's carried authority, and how Douglass attacked that authority.

MethodThe evidence labels used throughout

Five kinds of knowing

Established

Demonstrated and repeatable then, and it still holds.

Speculation

A serious hypothesis by investigators, not yet tested.

Popular belief

Widely held, spread by rumor, print, or performance.

Pseudoscience

Uses the look of science, but its claims fail its own tests.

Literary imagination

Fiction that borrows scientific language to explore ideas.

Example · phrenologyThen (1830s):Respected new scienceNow:Pseudoscience

This is the methodological heart of the lecture. Walk through each label with one quick example: established (ether's insensibility), speculation (Ure on the phrenic nerve), popular belief (that premature burial was common), pseudoscience (drapetomania), literary imagination (Poe's Valdemar). Then make the then/now point with phrenology. Note for students that the labels use a shape and a word, never color alone.

Use the timeline to orient, not to drill dates. Point out the hollow European markers at both ends: the story begins with European science (the 1784 Paris commission, 1818 Britain) and ends with European science arriving again (Darwin, 1859). That is not a coincidence; it is the heart of the story.

I
Room IElectricity · Mesmerism · Telegraph · Spirits

Invisible Forces

The age’s most powerful new forces could not be seen. That made them hard to tell apart.

If electricity is real and invisible, why not a magnetic fluid in the body, or a channel to the dead?

Frame Room I: electricity and magnetism were real, measurable, and invisible. That gave plausibility to other invisible forces people claimed: animal magnetism, nervous force, spirit communication. The point is not that people were gullible, but that the evidence available made the distinctions genuinely hard.

I.1A problem of evidence

Four invisible forces, one problem

Electricity

Measured, stored, and sent down wires.

Established

Magnetism

Linked to electricity by Ørsted (1820) and Faraday (1831).

Established

“Animal magnetism”

Mesmer’s fluid, said to pass between bodies and heal.

ThenContestedNowPseudo

Spirit communication

Messages from the dead, delivered by raps.

ThenBeliefNowBelief

Use the diagram to explain galvanism concretely: discs of zinc and copper separated by brine-soaked cloth produce a steady current; wires carry it to probes touching nerves. Then walk across the four cards from left to right and top to bottom. The two established forces and the two contested ones look alike to an observer: all invisible, all with felt effects. Mention the 1784 Franklin–Lavoisier commission: it did not deny that mesmerized people experienced something; it denied the fluid. That nuance matters because mesmerism's afterlife leads to hypnosis and, eventually, to modern ideas about suggestion and the unconscious.

I.2Boston · Pawtucket · Lowell · 1836–37

The mesmerist and the mill girl

In 1836 the French magnetizer Charles Poyen toured New England. His breakthrough came with Cynthia Gleason, a weaver at a Pawtucket textile mill, whose trances he displayed to audiences.

  • Spectators rang bells and fired a pistol near her head to test the trance.
  • Physicians asked her to diagnose patients while entranced.
  • Mill owners listened closely: could a mind be managed?
The tranceReal stateThe fluidPseudoscience

Who held the power in a mesmeric demonstration: the magnetizer, the entranced subject, or the paying audience?

Tell the Gleason story vividly: the bell, the ammonia, the pistol. Then pivot to Ogden's argument about the mills. In 1836 New England textile mills were becoming the leading edge of industrial capitalism, and mill owners needed to discipline workers to the bell and the loom. Mesmerism appealed partly because it promised control of one mind by another. Ask the discussion question and let students debate: the magnetizer seems powerful, but Gleason's performance made his career, and the audience's willingness to pay made the whole enterprise possible.

Close by sorting: the trance state was real (hypnosis), the fluid was not.

I.3Washington → Baltimore · 24 May 1844

Words without a body

“What hath God wrought”

Numbers 23:23 · chosen by Annie Ellsworth for the first message

Electricity now carried meaning across 40 miles in an instant. If thoughts could travel by wire, what else might?

Have a student read the tape aloud. Note the scriptural source and the providential reading many Americans gave the telegraph. Then make the key conceptual move: the telegraph separated message from body. Point ahead to the next slide, where the Fox sisters' raps are called a "spiritual telegraph." Sconce's Haunted Media is the source for the link between the telegraph and spiritualism.

I.4Hydesville, New York · 31 March 1848

The spiritual telegraph

Kate (11) and Maggie Fox (14) said the knocks in their farmhouse answered questions. Within two years, packed halls watched them “receive” messages from the dead.

  • Supporters included Quaker abolitionists Isaac and Amy Post.
  • Spiritualism promised evidence of immortality, not faith alone.
  • In 1888 Maggie confessed the raps were a trick. The movement continued anyway.
ThenBelief, tested publiclyNowBelief

Tell the Hydesville story briefly and point to the image: three ordinary-looking young women. Emphasize the reform context: Spiritualism grew in the same "Burned-over District" soil as abolition and women's rights, and many early Spiritualists were reformers. Explain why it appealed: it promised empirical proof of immortality, it gave women public authority as mediums, and it comforted the grieving, which becomes enormously important during the Civil War. End on the 1888 confession and the movement's survival.

Primary sourceDecember 1845 · American Review

A man mesmerized at the moment of death

“I say to you that I am dead!”

Edgar Allan Poe, “The Facts in the Case of M. Valdemar” (1845)

Written like a clinical case report, the tale was read by many as true. A Scottish reader wrote to ask. Poe replied that “hoax” was “precisely the word.”

Literary imaginationdressed asMedical report

Why might readers in 1845 have found this believable?

This is the first specimen-plate slide: archival material on a light ground. Read the quotation dramatically, then turn to evidence. Ask the discussion question before revealing the answers: the scientific title, the case-report style, publication in a respectable magazine, real reports of mesmeric surgery, and the fact that ether anesthesia was still a year away. Then make the larger point: the story is fiction, but readers' belief is historical evidence. Close with the question of where the person is located.

CheckRoom I · Self-assessment
In 1784 a French royal commission investigated Mesmer’s claims. What did it conclude?

Give students thirty seconds to choose. The key idea is the distinction between real effects and the commission's explanation for them. It explains why mesmerism kept its credibility: its defenders could say the trance was undeniable, even if the fluid was in doubt.

II
Room IIAnatomy · Grave robbing · Ether · Experiment

The Body on the Table

Modern medicine needed bodies, the dead and the living. Someone had to supply them.

Whose body ended up on the dissecting table, and who had the power to refuse?

Shift tone. Room II is about material bodies and power. The Gothic image of the grave robber has a real history behind it, and that history ran along lines of class and race. Content note for students: this room discusses dissection, grave robbing, and surgery on enslaved women without anesthesia. Descriptions stay factual and avoid graphic detail.

II.1Massachusetts · 1815 → 1831

The law decided whose body was “unclaimed”

  1. 1815
    Massachusetts makes disturbing a grave a crime. Demand doesn’t drop; graves are guarded.
  2. 1831
    Massachusetts passes the nation’s first anatomy act: the unclaimed dead of public institutions may go to medical schools.
  3. 1854
    New York follows. Elsewhere, grave robbing goes on.

Explain the Paris method: hands-on dissection made better doctors, and created a demand for bodies. Walk through the diagram from left to right. Stress that the line weights are illustrative, a visual summary of what Sappol and Edward Halperin's work on race and anatomical dissection show, not measured data. Then the legal story: 1815 criminalization; 1831 first anatomy act; 1854 New York. The key word is "unclaimed." Ask: who is likely to go unclaimed?

II.2Augusta, Georgia · 1852–1908

$700

Price paid in 1852, at a Charleston slave auction, for a 36-year-old Gullah man named Grandison Harris, owned jointly by the faculty of the Medical College of Georgia.

The “resurrection man” who could not refuse

  • Officially porter and janitor; in practice he robbed graves at night.
  • Most bodies came from Cedar Grove, Augusta’s Black cemetery.
  • After emancipation he stayed on as a paid employee until 1908.
  • In 1989, workers found thousands of human bones under the old college.

What choices did Harris have, before 1865 and after?

Let the number sit on screen before speaking. Then tell the story plainly. Emphasize the double violation: Harris was enslaved and ordered to rob the graves of Augusta's Black community. Explain that Georgia outlawed dissection until 1887, so the college relied on secrecy. Close with the 1989 discovery and the 1998 reburial. Use the discussion question carefully: invite students to think about constrained choice without presuming to know what Harris felt. Our sources are almost entirely white accounts.

II.3Boston · 16 Oct 1846

Alive, and feeling nothing

Dentist William T. G. Morton etherized a patient while surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumor. The patient felt no pain.

“The state should, I think, be called ‘Anaesthesia.’”

Oliver Wendell Holmes to Morton, 21 Nov 1846
Established

Look closely at the daguerreotype together: the men in frock coats, the patient, the light from the window. Point out that it is among the first photographs of surgery and that historians, including Rajesh Haridas and Vanessa Rowley, debate its staging and identification. Tell the October 1846 story, then the priority disputes (Long in 1842). Read Holmes's coinage. Then the conceptual point: ether produced a real version of what mesmerists claimed, a body alive but insensible. It moved the threshold. Transition: ether existed after 1846; the next slide shows surgery on enslaved women performed without it.

II.4Montgomery, Alabama · 1845–1849

AnarchaBetseyLucy

Three of at least seven enslaved women on whom Dr. J. Marion Sims operated repeatedly, without anesthesia, to develop a repair for vesicovaginal fistula, an injury of childbirth.

Content note: medical experimentation on enslaved women. No graphic detail.

Consent belonged to their enslavers

  • Sims needed permission only from the women’s owners.
  • Ether was available from 1846. He did not use it for these operations.
  • He became famous as the “father of gynecology.”
  • New York City removed his Central Park statue in 2018.

Pause and lower your voice. Give the women's names before Sims's. Explain fistula briefly and clinically. State the key facts: consent from owners only; no anesthesia although ether was available after 1846; repeated operations; fame. Present the historiographical debate fairly: Wall's contextual defense versus Owens and Gamble. Note that the debate is over interpretation and judgment, not facts. Connect back to Claim 3: the threshold of acceptable experimentation was drawn differently for enslaved bodies.

CheckRoom II · Self-assessment
Massachusetts’s 1831 Anatomy Act made which bodies legally available to medical schools?

After students answer, push further: why would legislators find "unclaimed" an attractive category? It solved the supply problem without threatening the voters and families who had political power.

III
Room IIIPremature burial · Cemeteries · Fiction

Doubtful Cases of Death

If trance and anesthesia could mimic death, how could anyone be sure the dead were dead?

Where did the fear come from, and what did fiction do with it?

Room III turns to culture: fear of premature burial, the new garden cemeteries that tried to make death gentle, and the fiction of Poe and Hawthorne. The phrase "doubtful cases of death" comes from Eisenbrandt's patent on slide III.2.

III.1Philadelphia · Dollar Newspaper · July 1844

“To be buried while alive is, beyond question, the most terrific of these extremes which has ever fallen to the lot of mere mortality.”

Edgar Allan Poe, “The Premature Burial” (1844)

Poe’s narrator says it has happened “very frequently,” and lists cases. Then he describes his own catalepsy: trances that mimic death.

“Very frequently”Popular belief

Physicians did debate the signs of death, and cases of mistaken death were reported. But evidence that it was common was thin.

Read the quotation slowly. Then separate fact from fear: the signs of death really were uncertain, and physicians wrote treatises about it, but evidence that live burial was frequent was largely anecdotal. Bondeson's Buried Alive is a readable, careful source. Make the key conceptual point: new science increased the fear by demonstrating that the living could look dead.

III.2U.S. Patent 3,335 · Baltimore · 15 Nov 1843

Engineering a second chance

How the design was meant to work, from the patent text:

  1. 1A ring slips onto the finger of the person inside.
  2. 2A rod links the ring to a spring catch on the lid.
  3. 3“The slightest motion of either the head or hand” springs the lid open.
  4. 4Designed for an above-ground vault, not burial in earth.
The fearBeliefThe mechanismSound engineering

This plate is a primary source to read like a machine. Walk students through the four annotations, then ask what kind of evidence a patent is. It shows belief, hope, and a market, but not frequency. Point out the quotation from the patent text itself. Mention that Poe's story appeared eight months after this patent, also in a mid-Atlantic city: the fear was in the air.

III.3Cambridge, Massachusetts · 1831

The other answer: make death beautiful

Mount Auburn, America’s first garden cemetery, recast the grave as a peaceful landscape for mourning, reflection, and Sunday walks.

  • Same year as the Anatomy Act, and in the same state.
  • Burial plots sold to families who could pay.
  • Sentiment and the Gothic grew side by side.

Let the painting work: soft light, strolling visitors, a chapel among trees. This is the anti-Gothic. Then deliver the punchline about 1831: Mount Auburn and the Anatomy Act, the same year, the same state. Ask students to put the two side by side. Who got which kind of death?

III.4Gothic fiction as cultural criticism

The scientist who loves too precisely

“The Birth-Mark” (1843)

Aylmer removes a tiny mark from his wife’s cheek. The cure works, and it kills her.

“Rappaccini’s Daughter” (1844)

A scientist raises his daughter among poison plants. She becomes poisonous.

The Blithedale Romance (1852)

A mesmerist exhibits a “Veiled Lady” on stage, a woman whose will is not her own.

Literary imagination

Present Hawthorne not as anti-science but as a critic of an attitude: knowledge as license to remake others. Summarize the three works briefly. Mention his 1841 letter urging Sophia not to be mesmerized. Then draw the connection students should make: in every story, a man of science acts on a woman's body or mind, and the woman pays the price. Ask students to link this to Sims and to Cynthia Gleason.

Over three nights at Tremont Temple, the Egyptologist George Gliddon unwrapped a body before paying crowds.

Pseudoscience

This is the hinge slide between rooms III and IV. Move left to right: Poe's satire (fiction), Gliddon's Boston unwrapping (spectacle), Gliddon's skulls for Morton and Types of Mankind (race science). Emphasize that a single person connects all three. The lesson is that the line between entertainment and science was porous, and that the porousness had consequences.

IV
Room IVPhrenology · Craniometry · Medicine · Resistance

The Measure of a Person

The same tools that measured the body were used to rank human beings, and to defend slavery.

When science claims to measure human worth, who checks the measurers?

Content note: this room discusses scientific racism and shows one 1839 skull illustration. The aim is to understand how racist claims gained authority, and how they were contested at the time, not to dwell on the claims themselves.

IV.1Boston 1832 → New York 1840s

A science of self-improvement

  • 1832: J. G. Spurzheim dies in Boston mid-tour. His funeral launches a phrenological society.
  • Orson and Lorenzo Fowler turn it into a business: head readings, charts, busts, journals.
  • Reformers liked it: if faculties could be exercised, character could improve.
  • It also ranked “races” by skull shape.
Localized brain functionsPartly right
Character from bumpsPseudoscience

Look at the chart: each compartment holds a tiny scene. Phrenology was visually seductive and democratic. Tell the Spurzheim story briefly, then the Fowlers as entrepreneurs. Stress the double edge: optimism about self-improvement and reform on one side, racial ranking on the other. Close with the then/now sort: localization was partly right; bumps were wrong.

IV.2Philadelphia · 1839–1851

Content note: human remains used in racist science.

The “American Golgotha”

  • Samuel Morton collected about 1,000 skulls, many taken from graves and battlefields.
  • He filled them with seed, then lead shot, to measure capacity, and ranked races.
  • Polygenists used his data to argue races were separate creations.

Historians disagree on whether Morton’s measurements were biased (Gould 1981; Lewis et al. 2011; Weisberg & Paul 2016). They agree his framework was.

Handle this plate respectfully: remind students the image depicts a real person's remains. Explain Morton's method concretely: seed, then shot, poured into a cylinder. Then present the historians' debate in three steps: Gould (bias), Lewis et al. (measurements accurate, Gould erred), Weisberg and Paul (Gould's core point about the seed data stands). Make sure students leave with the consensus: the framework of ranking human worth by skull volume was the bias. Mention Penn Museum's 2021 apology.

IV.3New Orleans · Medical and Surgical Journal · May 1851

drapeto·mania

Greek drapetēs, “runaway” + mania, “madness”

The disease is “unknown to our medical authorities, although its diagnostic symptom, the absconding from service, is well known to our planters and overseers.”

Samuel A. Cartwright, report to the Medical Association of Louisiana, 1851
ThenMocked by some doctorsNowPseudoscience

Put the word on screen and let students parse it. Then read the quotation and ask: who defines the symptom? Answer: the planters and overseers. The desire for freedom becomes a disease. Emphasize that this was mocked by some physicians at the time, which is why "then" and "now" agree. This is the clearest example of scientific authority bent to defend slavery.

IV.4Hudson, Ohio · 12 July 1854

Douglass answers the ethnologists

At Western Reserve College’s commencement, Frederick Douglass took on Morton, Nott, and Gliddon in their own terms.

“Tried by all the usual, and all the unusual tests … the negro is a MAN”

“The Claims of the Negro, Ethnologically Considered,” 1854
  • He showed how “science” served the slaveholders’ interests.
  • He defended the unity of the human family.

Give Douglass the full frame: his daguerreotype gaze answers the skull plate two slides back. Explain the setting: a college commencement, a remarkable platform in 1854. Summarize his strategies: expose the interest behind the science, critique the images, point to unequal conditions, affirm human unity. Read the quotation with weight. Connect to the method: Douglass practiced exactly the skill of sorting knowledge from interest.

IV.5Science and the soul

Religion played three roles at once

Ally

Natural theology

Many scientists, like Amherst geologist Edward Hitchcock, saw science as reading God’s design. Morse’s first message quoted Scripture.

Rival

Proof over faith

Spiritualists offered séance “evidence” of immortality. Polygenists like Josiah Nott mocked clergy who defended Genesis.

Referee

One human family

Many Christians rejected separate creation of races because Genesis taught common descent. Abolitionists cited Acts 17:26: “one blood.”

Religion could defend slavery and attack it. What does that tell us about using religion, or science, as a single cause in history?

Use this slide to complicate any simple science-versus-religion story. Walk the three roles with examples. The key irony: on the question of human unity, orthodox religious belief often served as a check on the scientific racism of polygenists, even among some defenders of slavery. End with the discussion prompt.

Which best describes Frederick Douglass’s strategy in “The Claims of the Negro” (1854)?

After the answer, ask students to name one specific move Douglass made: the Richmond newspaper, the illustrations, the denial of education, the unity of humanity.

Sort each claim by how historians classify it now

ConclusionReturn to the threshold

The line moved. Who drew it?

Walk the diagram from left to right, recalling each case in a sentence. Then move to the bottom line: who decided? Restate the three claims as conclusions. End with the last sentence of the Go deeper panel; it is the line students should remember.

Epilogue1859 → 1861

November 1859 · London

Darwin’s Origin of Species

Common descent of all living things. Asa Gray championed it in America. Agassiz opposed it.

April 1861 · Fort Sumter

A war that made death industrial

Hundreds of thousands of dead. Embalming, Spiritualist séances, and army medical museums followed.

The questions of the threshold did not end in 1861. They became national.

A brief epilogue. Darwin's 1859 theory of common descent undercut polygenism (Asa Gray championed Darwin in the U.S., while Louis Agassiz, a polygenist, opposed him). The Civil War then brought death at unprecedented scale. Drew Gilpin Faust's This Republic of Suffering is the place to send interested students: embalming, identification of the dead, and Spiritualism's wartime growth all extend this lecture's themes.

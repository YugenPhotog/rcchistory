03. Opening: Glasgow, 1818

A mysteryGlasgow · 4 November 1818

A hanged man’s face begins to move.

In a packed anatomy theater, Andrew Ure and James Jeffray sent battery current into the body of Matthew Clydesdale, hanged for murder that day. Witnesses saw the chest heave and the face contort.

Established: the experiment happened
Speculation: could current revive the dead?

Go deeper: Glasgow, 1818: what actually happened

On 4 November 1818, Matthew Clydesdale, a weaver convicted of murder, was hanged in Glasgow. As British law then allowed, his body went to the university anatomists. Andrew Ure, a chemist at the Andersonian Institution, joined anatomy professor James Jeffray in applying current from a large voltaic battery to exposed nerves. Ure reported that the chest rose and fell as if breathing, the fingers moved, and the face passed through grotesque expressions. Some spectators fled the room.

It is important to separate what happened from what people made of it. Established Electricity stimulates nerves and muscles. Luigi Galvani had shown this with frogs in the 1780s and 1790s. Speculation Ure did not claim he had raised the dead. He did speculate that stimulating the phrenic nerve, which controls the diaphragm, might restore breathing in people who had only seemed to die from suffocation or drowning. Popular retellings later inflated the story into a near-resurrection.

Why begin in Scotland? Because almost every science in this lecture started in Europe: galvanism in Italy, mesmerism in Vienna and Paris, phrenology in Vienna. Americans received these ideas through books, lecturers, and newspapers, then changed them by attaching them to American questions: revival religion, self-improvement, the factory, the frontier, and above all slavery.

The question to carry forward

If an electric current can make a corpse move, what exactly is life? Is it a force, a fluid, an organization of matter, or a soul? Antebellum Americans asked that question in laboratories, pulpits, lecture halls, and short stories.

04. The central question

The questionWhat this lecture argues

Science explained more of the natural world every decade. Why did the boundary between life and death, and between a person and a specimen, seem harder to draw?

Claim 1

New sciences really did blur old boundaries: a living body could be made insensible, a dead one made to move.

Claim 2

Gothic fiction was one way Americans thought through that uncertainty, not a retreat from science.

Claim 3

The power to draw the line was unequal. The bodies studied, displayed, and measured were mostly poor, enslaved, or Black.

Go deeper: The central argument, stated in full

Between about 1820 and 1861, Americans gained remarkable new powers over the body and over distance. Galvanic current made dead muscles move. Mesmerists claimed to put subjects into trances in which they felt no pain and saw distant places. After 1846, ether let surgeons operate on patients who felt nothing. After 1844, the telegraph sent words across distances in moments, without a messenger.

Each of these powers unsettled a boundary that had seemed natural: between life and death, mind and matter, presence and absence. That is the first claim of this lecture.

The second claim is that Gothic literature was one of the ways Americans worked through these unsettled boundaries. When Edgar Allan Poe wrote about a man mesmerized at the moment of death, or Nathaniel Hawthorne wrote about a scientist who kills his wife while trying to perfect her, they were not rejecting science. They were asking what it could do, and what it might cost.

The third claim is about power. Someone had to decide which bodies could be dissected, displayed, measured, or experimented on. In practice, those bodies came overwhelmingly from people with the least power to refuse: the poor who died in almshouses, the enslaved, free Black communities whose graveyards were robbed, and Indigenous people whose skulls filled collections. Science gave its practitioners an authority that could heal, and it could also be used to rank human beings and defend slavery.

As you work through the evidence, test these claims. Which parts does the evidence support? Where does it complicate them?

Fiction that borrows scientific language to explore ideas.

Example · phrenologyThen (1830s):Respected new scienceNow:Pseudoscience

Go deeper: Why every claim gets two labels: then and now

It is tempting to look back and sort nineteenth-century ideas into “right” and “wrong.” Historians try to avoid that, for two reasons.

First, it is anachronistic. A Boston physician in 1835 who took phrenology seriously was not foolish. Phrenology was taught by trained anatomists, discussed in medical journals, and endorsed by reformers. Its central claim, that different mental functions are located in different parts of the brain, turned out to be partly true. Its method of reading character from bumps on the skull did not.

Second, judging only by present knowledge hides the real historical question: how did people at the time decide what counted as knowledge? Who had authority, and why?

So this lecture often gives two labels. The then label describes how educated contemporaries generally classified a claim. The now label describes how historians and scientists classify it today. When the two differ, something interesting happened in between.

A note on “pseudoscience”

The word is useful but slippery. Here it means a practice that presents itself as science, using measurement, journals, and expert vocabulary, while its claims fail the tests that science itself applies. Some ideas were pseudoscience already in their own day. For example, Samuel Cartwright’s “drapetomania” was openly ridiculed by some physicians when it appeared.

08. Room I · Invisible Forces

Room IElectricity · Mesmerism · Telegraph · Spirits

Invisible Forces

The age’s most powerful new forces could not be seen. That made them hard to tell apart.

If electricity is real and invisible, why not a magnetic fluid in the body, or a channel to the dead?

Go deeper: Why invisible forces were hard to tell apart

By the 1830s, educated Americans knew that the universe was full of powerful forces no one could see. Alessandro Volta’s pile (1800) produced a steady electric current. In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted showed that a current deflects a compass needle; in 1831 Michael Faraday showed that a moving magnet produces current. Electricity and magnetism were real, invisible, and connected, and electricity was clearly involved in how nerves and muscles work.

In that setting, other claims about invisible forces did not sound absurd. In the 1770s the Viennese physician Franz Anton Mesmer had claimed that a subtle magnetic fluid flows through living bodies and that a trained “magnetizer” could redirect it to cure illness. In 1784 a French royal commission, which included Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier, tested Mesmer’s claims. It concluded that the effects were real but caused by imagination, not by any fluid.

That verdict did not end the matter. Mesmerists answered that the commission had tested the wrong things. Their trances, insensibility to pain, and apparent clairvoyance kept drawing crowds. Later investigators, especially the Scottish surgeon James Braid in the 1840s, reinterpreted the trance as a psychological state he called “hypnotism.”

The problem for an antebellum observer

How would you tell a real invisible force from an imagined one? You could not see either. You might feel effects from both. Experts disagreed. The tests that sort these claims today, such as controlled blind trials, existed only in rough form. That is why the line between science and pseudoscience was genuinely hard to draw, and why it mattered so much who was drawing it.

10. Mesmerism comes to New England, 1836

I.2Boston · Pawtucket · Lowell · 1836–37

The mesmerist and the mill girl

In 1836 the French magnetizer Charles Poyen toured New England. His breakthrough came with Cynthia Gleason, a weaver at a Pawtucket textile mill, whose trances he displayed to audiences.

  • Spectators rang bells and fired a pistol near her head to test the trance.
  • Physicians asked her to diagnose patients while entranced.
  • Mill owners listened closely: could a mind be managed?
The tranceReal stateThe fluidPseudoscience

Who held the power in a mesmeric demonstration: the magnetizer, the entranced subject, or the paying audience?

Go deeper: Poyen, Gleason, and the management of minds

Charles Poyen trained in medicine in Paris and lived for a time in Guadeloupe, where his family owned a sugar plantation. He arrived in New England in the mid-1830s and began lecturing on animal magnetism in and around Boston in early 1836. Audiences were small at first.

His fortunes changed in the fall of 1836 when he began working with Cynthia Gleason, a weaver at a mill in Pawtucket, Rhode Island. Poyen put her into trances before invited “gentlemen.” Skeptics tried to wake her: they rang a tavern bell next to her ear, held ammonia under her nose, and fired a pistol near her head. When she did not stir, the demonstrations became news. Physicians began consulting Gleason as an entranced diagnostician.

The literary historian Emily Ogden, in Credulity: A Cultural History of US Mesmerism (2018), points out who was in the room. Poyen pitched mesmerism partly to mill owners, men who needed a disciplined workforce for the new power looms. Mesmerism promised a way to direct another person’s mind and body. Ogden calls this one founding strand of the American occult tradition, with the control of workers at its heart. She also notes that Gleason sometimes diagnosed Poyen himself, which raises the question of who was really in control.

Mesmerism spread quickly. Phineas Parkhurst Quimby, a Maine clockmaker who saw Poyen lecture, became a mesmeric healer. His later ideas about mind and health fed the New Thought movement.

Sorting the evidence

Established Some people can enter trance-like states in which they respond to suggestion and report reduced pain. Pseudoscience These states were not produced by a magnetic fluid, and the claims of clairvoyant diagnosis did not hold up.

11. The telegraph, 1844

I.3Washington → Baltimore · 24 May 1844

Words without a body

“What hath God wrought”

Numbers 23:23 · chosen by Annie Ellsworth for the first message

Electricity now carried meaning across 40 miles in an instant. If thoughts could travel by wire, what else might?

Go deeper: The telegraph and the disembodied word

On 24 May 1844, Samuel F. B. Morse, sitting in the Supreme Court chamber in the U.S. Capitol, sent a message along an experimental line to his partner Alfred Vail in Baltimore. The words, chosen by Annie Ellsworth, daughter of the U.S. Commissioner of Patents, came from the Bible: “What hath God wrought” (Numbers 23:23).

The choice of a scriptural phrase matters. Many Americans interpreted the telegraph as evidence of providence, a sign that God was drawing humanity together through human invention. Science and religion could be partners. Established The telegraph worked, and by 1861 lines crossed the continent.

But the telegraph also did something unsettling. For all of human history, a message had traveled with a body: a messenger, a letter carried by hand, a voice in a room. Now words arrived without anyone present. The scholar Jeffrey Sconce has shown how quickly Americans connected this “disembodied” communication with the possibility of speaking with the dead. Four years later, spiritualists would describe their séances as a “spiritual telegraph.”

Think about it

New technology often changes not only what people can do, but what they think is possible. What present-day technology makes you wonder where the boundary between a person and a machine lies?

12. Hydesville, 1848

I.4Hydesville, New York · 31 March 1848

The spiritual telegraph

Kate (11) and Maggie Fox (14) said the knocks in their farmhouse answered questions. Within two years, packed halls watched them “receive” messages from the dead.

  • Supporters included Quaker abolitionists Isaac and Amy Post.
  • Spiritualism promised evidence of immortality, not faith alone.
  • In 1888 Maggie confessed the raps were a trick. The movement continued anyway.
ThenBelief, tested publiclyNowBelief

Go deeper: Hydesville and the birth of Spiritualism

In late March 1848, the Fox family of Hydesville, a hamlet in western New York, began hearing knocks at night. On 31 March, Kate Fox snapped her fingers and challenged the noise to answer; the knocks replied. Neighbors crowded in. A rumor spread that the house was haunted by a peddler murdered there years earlier.

The girls’ much older sister, Leah Fox Fish, soon managed their public career. In November 1849 they gave a public demonstration in Rochester. Committees of skeptics examined them and could not find the trick. By the early 1850s, “mediums” were holding séances across the North.

Why it spread

Spiritualism arose in a region already full of religious and reform movements, a few miles from Seneca Falls, where the first women’s rights convention met in 1848. Early supporters such as Isaac and Amy Post were Quaker abolitionists. Spiritualism appealed to people who wanted religion to be as provable as chemistry. It also gave women a public voice as mediums at a time when they were rarely allowed to preach.

The telegraph supplied its central metaphor. If electricity could carry messages across miles, why not across the boundary of death?

The confession

In 1888 Maggie Fox publicly confessed that the raps had been produced by cracking the joints of her toes, and demonstrated the method. She later recanted the confession. The movement survived both. That survival is itself evidence: Spiritualism met needs, especially grief, that a debunking could not touch.

Popular belief Note the irony: Spiritualism borrowed the methods of science, such as public tests, witnesses, and committees, without accepting science’s conclusions.

13. Primary source: Poe’s “M. Valdemar,” 1845

Primary sourceDecember 1845 · American Review

A man mesmerized at the moment of death

“I say to you that I am dead!”

Edgar Allan Poe, “The Facts in the Case of M. Valdemar” (1845)

Written like a clinical case report, the tale was read by many as true. A Scottish reader wrote to ask. Poe replied that “hoax” was “precisely the word.”

Literary imaginationdressed asMedical report

Why might readers in 1845 have found this believable?

Go deeper: Reading “The Facts in the Case of M. Valdemar”

Poe’s tale appeared in December 1845 in the American Review and was reprinted in his Broadway Journal. Its narrator, a mesmerist, explains that no one has yet tried mesmerizing a person “in articulo mortis,” at the very moment of death. His dying friend, Ernest Valdemar, agrees to the experiment. Valdemar is entranced just before death, and for seven months his body stays suspended, neither alive nor decaying. Asked if he is asleep, his tongue answers:

“Yes;—no;—I have been sleeping—and now—now—I am dead.”

When the narrator finally tries to wake him, Valdemar cries, “I say to you that I am dead!” and his body collapses into putrefaction.

Why readers believed it

The title promises “facts.” The prose imitates the case reports physicians published in medical journals: dates, named witnesses (abbreviated as Dr. D— and Dr. F—), pulse rates. It appeared in respectable magazines alongside real reports of mesmerism, including accounts of surgery performed on mesmerized patients. And mesmeric anesthesia seemed plausible in the year before ether. Several readers wrote to Poe to ask whether it was true. When a Scottish druggist, Archibald Ramsay, asked in 1846, Poe answered that “hoax” was “precisely the word suited to M. Valdemar’s case.”

What the tale is really about

Look past the horror to the question underneath. If a mind could be held in place after the body dies, where exactly is the person? In the body, the will, or the mesmerist’s control? Poe uses the language of science to push a real scientific uncertainty past its limit.

Literary imagination The story is fiction. But its plausibility to readers is historical evidence about what educated Americans thought mesmerism might do.

14. Check yourself · Room I

CheckRoom I · Self-assessment

Question: In 1784 a French royal commission investigated Mesmer’s claims. What did it conclude?

  1. That mesmerized patients felt nothing at all and were faking
  2. That the effects were real but came from imagination, not a magnetic fluid
  3. That animal magnetism was a newly proven natural force
  4. That mesmerism should be banned as contrary to religion
Show answer and explanations

✕ That mesmerized patients felt nothing at all and were faking — The commission did not deny that patients reacted. It attributed the reactions to imagination, not to any fluid.

✓ Best answer: That the effects were real but came from imagination, not a magnetic fluid — Commissioners including Franklin and Lavoisier found the effects real but caused by imagination and suggestion, not by a magnetic fluid. That distinction let mesmerism survive and later fed into hypnosis.

✕ That animal magnetism was a newly proven natural force — No official body confirmed the fluid. Its supposed existence is exactly what the commission rejected.

✕ That mesmerism should be banned as contrary to religion — The commission ruled on evidence, not theology. Religious objections came from other quarters.

The Body on the Table

16. The supply of bodies, 1815–1854

II.1Massachusetts · 1815 → 1831

The law decided whose body was “unclaimed”

  1. 1815
    Massachusetts makes disturbing a grave a crime. Demand doesn’t drop; graves are guarded.
  2. 1831
    Massachusetts passes the nation’s first anatomy act: the unclaimed dead of public institutions may go to medical schools.
  3. 1854
    New York follows. Elsewhere, grave robbing goes on.

Go deeper: Anatomy, grave robbing, and the “unclaimed”

In the early nineteenth century, American medical education adopted what was called the Paris method: students learned anatomy by dissecting human bodies themselves rather than watching a professor. This was good medicine. It also created a constant demand for corpses that the law did not meet. Traditionally, Anglo-American law allowed dissection only of executed criminals.

Medical students and paid “resurrection men” filled the gap by digging up fresh graves. Discovery could provoke riots. The best-known, New York’s Doctors’ Riot of 1788, came before our period, but the fear persisted throughout it. Families who could afford to fought back with guarded graves, heavy stones, iron coffins, and locked vaults. In 1815 Massachusetts made disturbing a grave a crime.

The 1831 Anatomy Act

In 1831 Massachusetts passed the first American law legalizing a supply of bodies: the unclaimed dead from almshouses, prisons, and similar public institutions could be given to medical schools. New York passed a similar law in 1854; other states followed more slowly. In Georgia, for example, dissection remained illegal until 1887.

Notice what “unclaimed” meant in practice. It meant the poor who died without family able to pay for burial. The law protected respectable families by directing dissection toward people with the least power to object. Historians such as Michael Sappol (A Traffic of Dead Bodies, 2002) argue that anatomy laws therefore attached a new stigma to poverty itself: to die poor was to risk the dissecting table.

Race and the resurrectionists

Where grave robbing continued, it fell hardest on African American burial grounds, which lacked legal protection and whose communities had little power to prosecute. The next slide examines one medical school where that pattern is documented in bone.

17. Grandison Harris, Augusta, 1852

II.2Augusta, Georgia · 1852–1908

$700

Price paid in 1852, at a Charleston slave auction, for a 36-year-old Gullah man named Grandison Harris, owned jointly by the faculty of the Medical College of Georgia.

The “resurrection man” who could not refuse

  • Officially porter and janitor; in practice he robbed graves at night.
  • Most bodies came from Cedar Grove, Augusta’s Black cemetery.
  • After emancipation he stayed on as a paid employee until 1908.
  • In 1989, workers found thousands of human bones under the old college.

What choices did Harris have, before 1865 and after?

Go deeper: Grandison Harris and the bones in the basement

In 1852 the faculty of the Medical College of Georgia in Augusta bought Grandison Harris, a 36-year-old Gullah man, at a slave auction in Charleston, South Carolina, for about $700. All seven faculty members owned him jointly. He worked as the college’s porter and janitor and, in time, as a teaching assistant to students in the dissecting room. His other job was secret: he dug up recently buried bodies at night, mostly from Cedar Grove, the cemetery used by Augusta’s Black community, and brought them to the college.

Dissection was illegal in Georgia until 1887, so the college depended on this hidden supply. After the Civil War, Harris was free. He reportedly spent time in Hamburg, South Carolina, during Reconstruction and then returned to the college as a paid employee, continuing the work until he retired in 1908.

What the bones say

In 1989, construction workers renovating the Old Medical College building found thousands of human bones and fragments in the basement soil, along with dissection tools. Forensic study indicated that most came from African Americans. In November 1998 the remains were reburied at Cedar Grove under a marker reading “Known But to God.”

Interpreting Harris

It is difficult to recover Harris’s own view of his work; nearly all the surviving stories about him were told by white doctors and students. Historians caution against turning him into either a villain or a folk hero. He was an enslaved man ordered to rob the graves of his own community, by men who legally owned him. The question of agency, meaning what he could choose, is real but has no simple answer.

18. Ether, Boston, 1846–47

II.3Boston · 16 Oct 1846

Alive, and feeling nothing

Dentist William T. G. Morton etherized a patient while surgeon John Collins Warren removed a tumor. The patient felt no pain.

“The state should, I think, be called ‘Anaesthesia.’”

Oliver Wendell Holmes to Morton, 21 Nov 1846
Established

Go deeper: Ether and a new state between waking and death

On 16 October 1846, in the surgical amphitheater of Massachusetts General Hospital in Boston, the dentist William T. G. Morton administered sulfuric ether to a patient, Gilbert Abbott. The senior surgeon, John Collins Warren, removed a tumor from Abbott’s neck while he lay insensible. News spread across the Atlantic within weeks. The room is now known as the Ether Dome.

Priority was bitterly disputed. Crawford Long, a Georgia physician, had used ether in surgery in 1842 but did not publish until 1849. Horace Wells had tried nitrous oxide in 1845. Charles T. Jackson, Morton’s former teacher, also claimed credit. These disputes are a reminder that “discovery” is often a contest over credit as much as a single moment.

A month later, the physician and writer Oliver Wendell Holmes suggested a name in a letter to Morton: “The state should, I think, be called ‘Anaesthesia.’” (Holmes’s original letter is lost; it survives in two slightly different published versions.)

Why ether belongs in a lecture on the Gothic

Ether produced something that looked uncannily like the states the mesmerists had promised: a living person who did not feel, did not respond, and woke with no memory. It was real, repeatable, and spectacular. It also raised questions. Some physicians, such as Philadelphia’s Charles Meigs, opposed anesthesia in childbirth, arguing that pain had a natural purpose. Others worried about the vulnerability of an insensible patient.

Established Ether anesthesia worked, then and now. The next slide asks a harder question: who received it, and who did not?

19. Anarcha, Betsey, Lucy: Montgomery, 1845–49

II.4Montgomery, Alabama · 1845–1849

AnarchaBetseyLucy

Three of at least seven enslaved women on whom Dr. J. Marion Sims operated repeatedly, without anesthesia, to develop a repair for vesicovaginal fistula, an injury of childbirth.

Content note: medical experimentation on enslaved women. No graphic detail.

Consent belonged to their enslavers

  • Sims needed permission only from the women’s owners.
  • Ether was available from 1846. He did not use it for these operations.
  • He became famous as the “father of gynecology.”
  • New York City removed his Central Park statue in 2018.

Go deeper: Sims, the women, and the historians’ debate

Between 1845 and 1849, in a small hospital behind his office in Montgomery, Alabama, Dr. J. Marion Sims operated repeatedly on at least seven enslaved women who suffered from vesicovaginal fistula, a tear between bladder and vagina caused by prolonged labor. The condition caused constant leaking and social isolation. Three of the women are known by name: Anarcha, Betsey, and Lucy. By Sims’s own later account, Anarcha underwent some thirty operations before he achieved a successful repair.

Sims arranged with the women’s enslavers to house and treat them; the law required only the owners’ consent. He did not use anesthesia on them, although ether was available after 1846. He later claimed the operations were not painful enough to justify it. Ideas that Black people felt less pain were widespread among white physicians. The women also served as his surgical assistants between operations.

Once the technique worked, Sims moved to New York, founded the Woman’s Hospital, operated on white patients, generally with anesthesia, and became internationally celebrated.

How historians disagree

Some medical writers, notably L. Lewis Wall (2006), argued that Sims should be judged by the standards of his time: anesthesia was new and controversial, fistula was devastating, and the women may have wanted a cure. Many historians reject that defense. Deirdre Cooper Owens, in Medical Bondage (2017), and Vanessa Northington Gamble emphasize that consent under slavery was impossible, that the women’s pain and skill are erased in the hero story, and that anesthesia was in fact available to him. The weight of current scholarship stresses the second view. All sides agree on the basic facts.

In April 2018, New York City removed Sims’s statue from Central Park. Monuments to “the Mothers of Gynecology,” Anarcha, Betsey, and Lucy, now stand in Montgomery.

✕ Only the bodies of executed criminals — That was the older Anglo-American rule the 1831 law expanded beyond, because executions could not meet demand.

✕ Bodies donated by people who chose dissection in their wills — Organized body donation is a twentieth-century practice. Antebellum Americans overwhelmingly opposed dissection of their own families.

✓ Best answer: The unclaimed dead of public institutions such as almshouses and prisons — The law released the unclaimed dead of public institutions such as almshouses and prisons, in practice the poor. Respectable families were protected; poverty itself became a risk factor for dissection.

✕ Any body left unburied for more than three days — No law allowed general access. Unclaimed status, not time since death, was the test.

21. Room III · Doubtful Cases of Death

Room IIIPremature burial · Cemeteries · Fiction

Doubtful Cases of Death

If trance and anesthesia could mimic death, how could anyone be sure the dead were dead?

Where did the fear come from, and what did fiction do with it?

22. Poe, “The Premature Burial,” 1844

III.1Philadelphia · Dollar Newspaper · July 1844

“To be buried while alive is, beyond question, the most terrific of these extremes which has ever fallen to the lot of mere mortality.”

Edgar Allan Poe, “The Premature Burial” (1844)

Poe’s narrator says it has happened “very frequently,” and lists cases. Then he describes his own catalepsy: trances that mimic death.

“Very frequently”Popular belief

Physicians did debate the signs of death, and cases of mistaken death were reported. But evidence that it was common was thin.

Go deeper: How real was the fear of premature burial?

Poe’s “The Premature Burial” appeared in Philadelphia’s Dollar Newspaper in July 1844. It begins as an essay, citing supposedly true cases from Baltimore, France, and elsewhere, before becoming the story of a narrator who suffers from catalepsy, a condition of rigid, death-like trance, and lives in dread of waking in a coffin.

What was real

Established Before the stethoscope came into wide use (it was invented in France in 1816), and long before electrocardiography, the signs of death were genuinely hard to read. Physicians across Europe and America published treatises on how to distinguish true from apparent death. Drowning, cholera, and trance-like illnesses could all produce cases where a person seemed dead but was not.

What was exaggerated

Popular belief Claims that premature burial happened “very frequently” rested mostly on anecdotes repeated from paper to paper. Historians of death, such as Jan Bondeson (Buried Alive, 2001), find that well-documented cases were rare.

Why it mattered anyway

Fear does not need to match frequency to shape behavior. The new sciences in this lecture, including mesmeric trance and, after 1846, ether, showed that a living person could look dead. That made the old fear more plausible, not less. It produced waiting periods before burial, “waiting mortuaries” in Germany, and in America a small wave of inventions, which the next slide examines.

23. A life-preserving coffin, 1843

III.2U.S. Patent 3,335 · Baltimore · 15 Nov 1843

Engineering a second chance

How the design was meant to work, from the patent text:

  1. 1A ring slips onto the finger of the person inside.
  2. 2A rod links the ring to a spring catch on the lid.
  3. 3“The slightest motion of either the head or hand” springs the lid open.
  4. 4Designed for an above-ground vault, not burial in earth.
The fearBeliefThe mechanismSound engineering

Go deeper: Christian Eisenbrandt’s “life-preserving coffin”

On 15 November 1843, the U.S. Patent Office granted Christian Henry Eisenbrandt of Baltimore Patent No. 3,335 for a “coffin to be used in cases of doubtful death,” which he called a “life-preserving coffin.” His specification explains that people have been buried alive, and that his arrangement allows anyone not truly dead to open the coffin “by the slightest motion of either the head or hand.”

The design relied on a ring placed on the occupant’s finger, connected by a rod to a spring catch. Any movement would release the lid. Eisenbrandt intended the coffin for a vault rather than a grave, so the occupant would not be trapped under soil. A broadside advertised the invention in Baltimore in 1844.

Reading the patent historically

A patent is excellent evidence of what a person believed and hoped to sell, and weaker evidence about how common a problem actually was. Eisenbrandt’s coffin is one of the earliest American “safety coffins.” Dozens more were patented later in the century, many with bells, flags, or breathing tubes.

Notice the cultural logic. The inventor answers a Gothic fear with the confident tools of mechanical invention: springs, levers, and the Patent Office. That combination of dread and ingenuity is a theme of this whole lecture.

24. Mount Auburn: a gentler death, 1831

III.3Cambridge, Massachusetts · 1831

The other answer: make death beautiful

Mount Auburn, America’s first garden cemetery, recast the grave as a peaceful landscape for mourning, reflection, and Sunday walks.

  • Same year as the Anatomy Act, and in the same state.
  • Burial plots sold to families who could pay.
  • Sentiment and the Gothic grew side by side.

Go deeper: Mount Auburn and the domestication of death

Mount Auburn Cemetery opened in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1831, founded with the Massachusetts Horticultural Society. It was the first of America’s “rural” or garden cemeteries: rolling, planted landscapes with winding paths, ponds, and sculpted monuments, far from crowded church graveyards. Similar cemeteries soon opened in Philadelphia (Laurel Hill), Brooklyn (Green-Wood), and elsewhere. Families visited them like parks.

The garden cemetery expressed a gentle, consoling vision of death associated with liberal Protestantism and sentimental culture: death as sleep, the grave as a garden, mourning as a refining emotion. Historians sometimes call this the “beautification” or “domestication” of death.

Two answers to the same uncertainty

Notice the date. In 1831, Massachusetts both opened Mount Auburn and passed its Anatomy Act. One institution promised that the dead of respectable families would rest in permanent, protected beauty. The other directed the unclaimed poor to the dissecting table. Sentimental consolation and Gothic dread were not opposites; they were two responses to the same new uncertainty about what happens to the body, and they were distributed by class.

25. Hawthorne’s scientists

III.4Gothic fiction as cultural criticism

The scientist who loves too precisely

“The Birth-Mark” (1843)

Aylmer removes a tiny mark from his wife’s cheek. The cure works, and it kills her.

“Rappaccini’s Daughter” (1844)

A scientist raises his daughter among poison plants. She becomes poisonous.

The Blithedale Romance (1852)

A mesmerist exhibits a “Veiled Lady” on stage, a woman whose will is not her own.

Literary imagination

Go deeper: Hawthorne: knowledge, perfection, and possession

Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864) was not hostile to science. He was suspicious of a particular attitude: the belief that knowledge entitles its possessor to remake other people.

“The Birth-Mark” (1843)

Aylmer, a brilliant natural philosopher, marries the beautiful Georgiana and becomes obsessed with a small, hand-shaped mark on her cheek, the one flaw in her perfection. Using his laboratory, he concocts a potion that removes it. As the mark fades, Georgiana dies. Hawthorne suggests that the mark was bound up with her humanity, and that the desire for perfection is a kind of violence.

“Rappaccini’s Daughter” (1844)

In Padua, the scientist Rappaccini raises his daughter Beatrice in a garden of poisonous plants until she herself becomes poisonous, untouchable to anyone else. A father’s experiment makes his child his specimen.

Mesmerism and consent

Hawthorne returned to mesmerism in The House of the Seven Gables (1851) and The Blithedale Romance (1852), where a mesmerist exhibits a “Veiled Lady” whose will is controlled by another. The theme was personal: in 1841, during their engagement, Hawthorne wrote to Sophia Peabody urging her not to undergo mesmeric treatment for her headaches. He worried about one person gaining power over another’s inner self.

Why this counts as cultural criticism

Notice the pattern. In each story, a man of science acts on a woman’s body or mind, and the woman pays. Hawthorne is asking the same question this lecture asks about Sims and the dissecting room: who consents, and who is made into an object of knowledge?

III.5From fiction to spectacle to “science”

Poe, “Some Words with a Mummy”

Skulls for race science

Pseudoscience

One man connects the lecture stage to the skull collection. The dead body as specimen leads directly to Room IV.

Go deeper: From mummy-mania to race science

Egyptian mummies fascinated antebellum Americans. They seemed to stand at the boundary of death itself: bodies preserved for millennia, neither fully decayed nor alive. The first public mummy unwrapping in the United States took place in New York in 1824.

In April 1845 Poe published “Some Words with a Mummy,” a satire in which a group of learned gentlemen apply a voltaic pile to an Egyptian mummy named Allamistakeo. He revives and calmly informs them that ancient Egypt had surpassed their vaunted modern achievements. The joke is on American confidence in progress.

Five years later, the British-born Egyptologist George R. Gliddon, a former U.S. consular official in Egypt, staged a real version for profit. In June 1850, over three evenings at Boston’s Tremont Temple, he lectured on mummification and unwrapped a mummy before ticket-buying audiences, to promote his traveling panorama of the Nile. He staged more unwrappings in Philadelphia in 1851.

The connection to Room IV

Gliddon was more than a showman. He collected Egyptian skulls and sent them to the Philadelphia physician Samuel George Morton, who used them in Crania Aegyptiaca (1844) to argue that racial differences were ancient and fixed. In 1854 Gliddon and the Alabama physician Josiah Nott published Types of Mankind, dedicated to Morton’s memory. It argued that human races were separately created species and became a major text of proslavery “science.”

The mummy shows how quickly the dead body could move from Gothic fiction to public spectacle to scientific specimen, and how that specimen could be put to political use.

28. Phrenology: reading the skull

IV.1Boston 1832 → New York 1840s

A science of self-improvement

  • 1832: J. G. Spurzheim dies in Boston mid-tour. His funeral launches a phrenological society.
  • Orson and Lorenzo Fowler turn it into a business: head readings, charts, busts, journals.
  • Reformers liked it: if faculties could be exercised, character could improve.
  • It also ranked “races” by skull shape.
Localized brain functionsPartly right
Character from bumpsPseudoscience

Go deeper: Phrenology: hopeful, popular, and wrong

Phrenology began with the Viennese physician Franz Joseph Gall around 1800. Gall argued that the brain is made up of separate “organs” for different mental faculties, that a larger organ means a stronger faculty, and that the skull’s shape reveals the size of the organs beneath. His collaborator Johann Gaspar Spurzheim named and popularized the system.

Spurzheim came to the United States in 1832 for a lecture tour and died of typhoid in Boston on 10 November, after only a few months. His funeral drew a huge crowd, and the Boston Phrenological Society was founded that day. The Scottish phrenologist George Combe toured the U.S. from 1838 to 1840.

The Fowlers

Phrenology’s American boom owed most to Orson Squire Fowler and his brother Lorenzo Niles Fowler, who opened a phrenological office in New York and built a publishing and lecturing business with their partner Samuel Wells. For a fee, they would read your head and give you a chart of your faculties. Their message was optimistic and democratic: you could strengthen weak faculties through exercise and self-discipline. Reformers, educators, and even asylum doctors found this appealing.

The dark side

Phrenologists also claimed to read whole peoples. Charts and lectures ranked races and nations by skull shape, and these rankings lent supposedly scientific support to arguments for Indian removal and slavery.

Sorting the evidence

Partly right The idea that different brain regions do different work was later confirmed in a very different form, for example by Paul Broca’s 1861 work on language. Pseudoscience The skull’s outer shape does not reveal the brain’s inner structure, and the faculties phrenologists named do not correspond to brain regions. By the 1840s most leading physiologists had rejected it, though it stayed popular for decades.

29. Morton’s skulls, 1839

IV.2Philadelphia · 1839–1851

Content note: human remains used in racist science.

The “American Golgotha”

  • Samuel Morton collected about 1,000 skulls, many taken from graves and battlefields.
  • He filled them with seed, then lead shot, to measure capacity, and ranked races.
  • Polygenists used his data to argue races were separate creations.

Historians disagree on whether Morton’s measurements were biased (Gould 1981; Lewis et al. 2011; Weisberg & Paul 2016). They agree his framework was.

Go deeper: Morton’s skulls and a debate about bias

Samuel George Morton (1799–1851), a respected Philadelphia physician and naturalist, assembled the largest collection of human skulls in the world, roughly a thousand by his death. Admirers called it the “American Golgotha.” Many skulls came from Indigenous graves, battlefields, and hospitals, collected for him by correspondents around the world, without any consent from the dead or their communities.

Morton measured cranial capacity by filling skulls first with white pepper seed and later with lead shot, then pouring the contents into a graduated cylinder. In Crania Americana (1839) and Crania Aegyptiaca (1844), he reported average capacities by race, with Europeans at the top. His work was received as the finest American science of its day. Polygenists, including Josiah Nott, George Gliddon, and later Louis Agassiz, used it to argue that the races were separately created species.

The debate

In 1978 and in The Mismeasure of Man (1981), Stephen Jay Gould reanalyzed Morton’s data and argued that unconscious bias had shaped the measurements. In 2011 a team led by Jason Lewis remeasured about half the surviving skulls and found Morton’s lead-shot measurements largely accurate; they argued that Gould himself had erred. In 2016 Michael Weisberg and Diane Paul replied that Lewis’s remeasurements did not touch Gould’s strongest point, about the earlier seed measurements, which do show a pattern consistent with bias. Other scholars, such as Jonathan Kaplan and colleagues (2015), argued that the data cannot support racial rankings either way.

What is not in dispute

Whatever the accuracy of the numbers, the framework was the problem: the assumption that skull volume measures intelligence and that human groups can be ranked by it. Modern biology rejects both premises. The episode is a useful lesson in how careful measurement can still serve a biased question.

In 2021 the Penn Museum, which holds the collection, apologized for its possession of human remains taken without consent and began work toward repatriation and reburial.

30. “Drapetomania,” 1851

IV.3New Orleans · Medical and Surgical Journal · May 1851

drapeto·mania

Greek drapetēs, “runaway” + mania, “madness”

The disease is “unknown to our medical authorities, although its diagnostic symptom, the absconding from service, is well known to our planters and overseers.”

Samuel A. Cartwright, report to the Medical Association of Louisiana, 1851
ThenMocked by some doctorsNowPseudoscience

Go deeper: Cartwright and the medicalization of resistance

In March 1851 the Medical Association of Louisiana heard a report by Dr. Samuel A. Cartwright of New Orleans on “the diseases and physical peculiarities of the negro race.” Published that May in the New Orleans Medical and Surgical Journal and reprinted in DeBow’s Review, it named two “diseases.”

“Drapetomania” was the supposed mental illness that caused enslaved people to run away. Cartwright wrote that it was “unknown to our medical authorities, although its diagnostic symptom, the absconding from service, is well known to our planters and overseers.” A second invented disease, “dysaesthesia aethiopica,” turned resistance to work into a medical symptom. His prescribed “treatments” included whipping and hard labor.

What the source reveals

Read the quotation closely. The “symptom” is defined by the enslavers, not by physicians, and the only diagnostic evidence is the desire for freedom. Cartwright turns a rational act of resistance into a pathology, so that the defense of slavery can borrow the authority of medicine.

Was it accepted?

Not universally, even then. Cartwright’s report was reprinted approvingly in the South, but some Northern physicians ridiculed it; a satirical response appeared in the Buffalo Medical Journal in 1855. That is why both the “then” and “now” labels here read pseudoscience. Some contemporaries already saw through it. Its importance lies in showing how readily medical language could be enlisted for slavery.

31. Douglass replies, 1854

IV.4Hudson, Ohio · 12 July 1854

Douglass answers the ethnologists

At Western Reserve College’s commencement, Frederick Douglass took on Morton, Nott, and Gliddon in their own terms.

“Tried by all the usual, and all the unusual tests … the negro is a MAN”

  • He showed how “science” served the slaveholders’ interests.
  • He defended the unity of the human family.

Go deeper: “The Claims of the Negro, Ethnologically Considered”

On 12 July 1854, Frederick Douglass addressed the literary societies of Western Reserve College in Hudson, Ohio, during commencement week. His subject was the new “ethnology,” the race science of Morton, Nott, and Gliddon, whose Types of Mankind had appeared that year.

Douglass refused to treat the question as neutral. He pointed out that a respectable Richmond newspaper based its whole defense of slavery on denying Black humanity, and that ethnological “science” conveniently supplied that denial. He criticized the way ethnological illustrations exaggerated African features to fit a preconceived idea of inferiority. He showed that enslaved people had been deliberately denied education, so comparing their achievements with those of free white people was unfair.

“Tried by all the usual, and all the unusual tests, whether mental, moral, physical, or psychological, the negro is a MAN.”

He argued for the unity of the human family, a position that also had deep roots in Christian teaching that all peoples descend from common ancestors.

Why this matters for the argument

Douglass shows that scientific authority could be contested in its own time and on its own ground. He did not reject science; he exposed how interest and prejudice had shaped particular scientific claims. That is the same skill the “five kinds of knowing” asks of you.

32. Religion: ally, rival, referee

IV.5Science and the soul

Religion played three roles at once

Ally

Natural theology

Many scientists, like Amherst geologist Edward Hitchcock, saw science as reading God’s design. Morse’s first message quoted Scripture.

Rival

Proof over faith

Spiritualists offered séance “evidence” of immortality. Polygenists like Josiah Nott mocked clergy who defended Genesis.

Referee

One human family

Many Christians rejected separate creation of races because Genesis taught common descent. Abolitionists cited Acts 17:26: “one blood.”

Religion could defend slavery and attack it. What does that tell us about using religion, or science, as a single cause in history?

Go deeper: Religion and scientific authority

It is a myth that nineteenth-century science and religion were simply at war. In antebellum America they related in at least three ways, often within the same person.

Ally

The dominant framework was natural theology, popularized by William Paley’s Natural Theology (1802): the order of nature reveals a wise Creator. Many American scientists were devout. Edward Hitchcock, geologist and president of Amherst College, worked to reconcile the new geology’s long ages with Genesis. Samuel Morse chose Scripture for the first official telegraph message. Mount Auburn’s founders framed the garden cemetery as a place of Christian consolation.

Rival

Some movements offered science as a substitute for faith. Spiritualists claimed that séances provided experimental proof of the soul’s survival, so belief would no longer be necessary. Polygenists such as Josiah Nott openly ridiculed clergy who insisted on a single creation, presenting themselves as brave scientists freeing knowledge from theology.

Referee

Religion also judged scientific claims. Many Christians, including some Southern ministers who defended slavery on biblical grounds, rejected polygenism because it contradicted the Genesis account of one human family. Abolitionists often quoted Acts 17:26, that God “hath made of one blood all nations of men.” In this case, religious commitment worked as a check on a pseudoscience.

The lesson

Neither “science” nor “religion” had a single politics. Both were resources people used for competing ends. Historians therefore avoid treating either as a single cause.

35. Conclusion: who drew the line?

ConclusionReturn to the threshold

The line moved. Who drew it?

Go deeper: What happened at the threshold

Return to the question we began with: why did more scientific knowledge make the boundary between life and death, and between person and specimen, harder to draw?

Because the new knowledge was real. Galvanism showed that motion did not prove life. Ether showed that insensibility did not mean death. Mesmerism, whatever its theory, showed that a person could act while another seemed to direct their will. The telegraph showed that communication no longer required a body. Each of these broke a link that had once seemed natural. Antebellum Americans were not naive to find this unsettling; they were paying attention.

Because the Gothic helped people think. Poe pushed mesmerism to the moment of death to ask where a person resides. Hawthorne asked what happens when knowledge becomes a license to remake others. Fiction let readers test possibilities that laboratories could not.

Because someone had to draw the line, and power decided who. Legislators decided which dead were “unclaimed.” Enslavers gave “consent” for Anarcha, Betsey, and Lucy. A medical faculty owned the man who robbed Black graves for them. Physicians like Morton and Cartwright claimed the authority to rank human beings and to call the desire for freedom a disease. Others, like Frederick Douglass, contested that authority on its own ground.

The deepest Gothic insight of the period may be this: the most frightening thing at the threshold was not the corpse that moved or the spirit that knocked, but the confident living person who decided which bodies did not count.

37. Essay question & model response

WriteAnalytical question · 300–400 words

Choose one Gothic text and one historical case from this lecture. How does each draw the line between a person and a specimen, and who holds the power to draw it?

  • Classify at least two claims with the five kinds of knowing.
  • Use one quotation from a primary source.

Go deeper: A model response (about 380 words)

Write your own answer before reading this.

Nathaniel Hawthorne’s “The Birth-Mark” (1843) and the case of the Medical College of Georgia’s purchase of Grandison Harris in 1852 both show how antebellum science turned people into specimens, but they locate the danger differently.

In “The Birth-Mark,” the scientist Aylmer treats his wife Georgiana’s small facial mark as a flaw to be corrected. The story is literary imagination, but it reflects a real cultural worry about scientific ambition. Aylmer draws the line between person and specimen himself: once he sees the mark as a problem, Georgiana becomes an experiment. Hawthorne places the danger in an individual’s pride and in the intimacy of marriage, where she consents out of love.

The Georgia case moves the danger from individual pride to institutions and law. Because dissection was illegal in Georgia, the faculty relied on Harris to rob graves, mostly at Cedar Grove, the city’s Black cemetery. That these bodies were taken is established: in 1989 thousands of bones were found beneath the college, most of them identified as African American. Here no single genius draws the line. Slavery and race decided whose graves were unprotected, and Harris, legally owned by the faculty, could not refuse.

A primary source sharpens the contrast. Samuel Cartwright’s 1851 claim that “drapetomania” was “well known to our planters and overseers” shows medical authority deferring to enslavers, who define the “symptom.” Cartwright’s disease is pseudoscience, but it reveals the same structure as the Georgia case: those with power over bodies decide which bodies count as subjects of knowledge.

Hawthorne’s story is valuable because it makes readers feel the wrongness of turning a person into a project. But it centers a white woman who loves her husband and consents. The historical cases show that the people most often made into specimens had no voice in the matter. Read together, they suggest that the Gothic named a real danger in antebellum science, while the archive shows that the danger was distributed by race and power.

What makes this response strong

It states a thesis that contrasts the two cases. It classifies claims precisely, uses a short quotation and analyzes it, and ends by evaluating the limits of the literary source rather than just summarizing.

# You Are Not a Finished Object
## What the Dhammapada leaves in your pocket
### Instructor notes · REL 210 Lecture 3 close · ~10 minutes

These notes are for you. The slides stay clean: citation, verse, translator. You supply the understanding and the application.

**Aim of the close.** Two jobs at once. (1) They leave knowing what five verses actually claim. (2) They leave with one check they can run on a real hour of their life. Understanding without application is a quiz. Application without understanding is a poster.

**How to speak.** Read the verse slowly. Then teach the concept in one tight block. Then land it on a life they already have. Do not restate the verse in friendlier English after they have seen it.

**Clock.** Title 40 seconds. Five verses ~90–100 seconds each. Last slide 80 seconds. If late: teach 5, 338, and 239; click through 222 and 165.

**Translator.** Acharya Buddharakkhita, *The Dhammapada: The Buddha’s Path of Wisdom* (BPS, 1985). *Buddharakkhita* = “protected by the Buddha.”

## Slide 1 — Title

**On screen:** You Are Not a Finished Object / What the Dhammapada leaves in your pocket

### Concept
The Dhammapada is 423 verses in Pali, grouped in 26 *vagga* (chapters, “groups”). It is wisdom literature designed for memory and use, not a novel and not a creed. *Dhamma* here means the teaching / the way things are. *Pada* means verse, or the foot of a path.

The title of this close is not a slogan invented for the slide. It is the claim of the *Attavagga* (Self chapter), which you will reach in Dhp 165: you are responsible for what you do, and you are not a fixed statue.

### SAY
Most of you have not read this book. I am going to read five verses. After each one, what it means — then how it meets a life like yours. I am not asking you to become Buddhist. I am asking you to understand the line well enough to try it once.

### Do not
Do not explain “finished object” here. Let Dhp 165 earn the title.

## Slide 2 — Dhp 5
**Chapter:** *Yamakavagga* (The Pairs), verse 5
**On screen:** “Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal.”

### Concept
This is not “be nice.” It is a claim about how hostility works.

- *Vera* = hatred, enmity, the heat that keeps score.
- *Avera* = non-hatred. Not a mood of sweetness. The refusal to feed the same fire.
- *Sammanti* = are stilled, settled, put down.
- *Dhammo sanantano* = an ancient law. The text is not offering campus etiquette. It is saying: this is how the pattern behaves, whether you like it or not.

The pairing in verses 3–4 (not on the slide) names the story the mind repeats: “He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me.” Those who harbor that story do not still hatred. Those who do not harbor it do. The harm may be real. The verse is about what you do with the sentence after the harm.

**Where it sits.** Chapter 1, not the later Anger chapter (*Kodhavagga*, 221–234). Chapter 1 teaches by pairs: two paths, two outcomes. Hatred answered by hatred is one path. *Avera* is the other.

**Likely misunderstanding.** “So I should let people walk on me.” No. *Avera* is about not adding a second fire. You can still set a boundary, fail the groupmate, leave the thread. The verse is about the mental continuation of the war, not about having no standards.

### SAY
*Vera* (hatred) does not close the account that *vera* opened. The book’s other word is *avera* (non-hatred). That is not niceness. It is: do not send the second shot.

The dishes in the sink. The groupmate who vanishes the night before the deck is due. The family thread that always finds the old wound. The reflex is *vera* answering *vera* — prove they were wrong, make them feel it, win the hour.

The Dhammapada’s move is earlier than “be the bigger person.” Watch the sentence form before you send it. If the sentence is only there to keep the fire going, that is *vera*. You already know how that week ends. You have lived it.

### Apply today
Before a reply leaves your hands, ask: is this ending something, or continuing it? If it is continuing it, wait. That wait is the practice. You do not have to feel kind. You have to not spend the heat for a minute.

### If they push
“What if they really wronged me?” Then the verse is harder, not false. Justice and *avera* are not the same job. You can seek a fair outcome without using hatred as the engine. The text’s claim is only this: hatred will not settle hatred. That is the law it calls eternal.

## Slide 3 — Dhp 222
**Chapter:** *Kodhavagga* (Anger), verse 222
**On screen:** “One who checks arisen anger as a charioteer checks a rolling chariot — that one I call a true charioteer. Others merely hold the reins.”

### Concept
Anger here is not a personality type. It is a motion already underway.

- *Kodha* = anger.
- *Uppatitaṁ* = arisen; already up. Not “someday I might get angry.” It is here.
- *Rathaṁ bhantaṁ* = a chariot that is already rolling / running out.
- *Sārathi* = the true charioteer — the one who can still steer.
- *Rasmiggāha* = the one who merely holds the reins. Occupying the driver’s seat is not driving.

This is what *sati* (mindfulness, “remembering to be aware”) looks like in the Dhammapada’s ethics: not a vibe, not incense, **timing**. The skill is catching a state while it can still be checked.

**Likely misunderstanding.** “A good person doesn’t get angry.” The verse assumes anger *has arisen*. The praise is for the person who can brake a moving cart, not for the person who pretends they have no cart.

### SAY
Anyone can sit in the cart. Holding the reins is not the same as driving.

You already know the feeling: the reply is forming, the face is hot, you can see the next three sentences. That is *kodha uppatitaṁ* (anger already arisen). The cart is rolling.

The book’s question is not “are you an angry person?” It is: can you brake once while it is moving? That single brake — the unsent text, the breath before the door slam, the decision not to post it — is what the verse calls a charioteer.

Everyone else is just holding the straps and calling it control.

### Apply today
The next time you feel the cart go, name it: “This is already moving.” Then do one physical delay: lock the phone, stand up, walk to the water fountain. You are not solving the relationship in that minute. You are refusing to spend the state before you have seen it.

### If they push
“Sometimes anger is righteous.” The verse does not forbid moral judgment. It forbids letting arisen anger drive the vehicle unexamined. You can still be right and be a passenger.

## Slide 4 — Dhp 338
**Chapter:** *Taṇhāvagga* (Craving / Thirst), verse 338
**On screen:** “Just as a tree, though cut down, sprouts again if its roots remain firm, so until latent craving is rooted out, suffering springs up again and again.”

### Concept
*Taṇhā* is thirst. Not wanting a degree. Not having a goal. Thirst is the thing that promises arrival and then rebuilds itself when arrival doesn’t stay.

- *Taṇhā* = thirst, craving.
- *Anusaya* = latent tendency; the root that remains after you cut what you can see.
- *Dukkha* = suffering, unsatisfactoriness, the friction of not-staying-satisfied.
- *Punappunaṁ* = again and again.

The image is agricultural and ruthless. Cut the trunk, leave the root, watch the stump leaf out. That is why “I deleted the app for the weekend” so often fails by Wednesday. You cut a shoot. The *anusaya* (latent root) was never touched.

In the wider teaching, *taṇhā* is the Second Noble Truth’s engine: craving as a condition for *dukkha*. You do not need the whole Four Noble Truths on this slide. You need the mechanism: thirst → temporary ease → thirst again.

**Likely misunderstanding.** “Buddhism says desire is bad; I should want nothing.” The Dhammapada is not against effort or love of learning. It is against the thirst that cannot complete. Preference is not the problem. Compulsion that rebuilds after every hit is the problem.

### SAY
*Taṇhā* (thirst) rebuilds itself. That is why willpower-for-a-weekend so often fails.

Look at the feed. It promises a hit of arrival — the joke, the body, the grade, the ex, the outrage that makes you feel sharp — and it never stays arrived. So the hand goes back. That is not a character flaw unique to your generation. It is the creeper the verse is talking about.

Deleting the app cuts the shoot. A different tab is the stump leafing out. The root is the itch that says “just one more and I will have arrived.”

The practice is smaller than a digital detox. Before the thumb moves, say the word: “This is *taṇhā* (thirst).” Not a scolding. A label. Once it has a name, it is harder for it to sound like “just how I am.”

### Apply today
When the hand goes to the phone, ask which of three thirsts it is:

1. Replaying what already happened.
2. Rehearsing what might happen.
3. Refusing the hour you are actually in.

Name which one. Then decide. Naming is already more than most people do with a phone in their hand.

### If they push
“I need my phone for school.” Fine. The verse is not anti-tool. It is anti-root-blindness. Use the tool on purpose. Notice when the tool is using the itch.

Do not open Dhp 348 or rebirth on this slide.

## Slide 5 — Dhp 165
**Chapter:** *Attavagga* (The Self), verse 165
**This is the title-verse.**

**On screen:** “By oneself is evil done; by oneself is one defiled. By oneself is evil left undone; by oneself is one made pure. No one can purify another.”

### Concept
Two claims that sound like they fight each other. Hold both.

**Claim 1 — responsibility.** *Attanā* (by oneself): the unwholesome act is yours; the repair is yours. *Nāñño aññaṁ visodhaye* — no other purifies another. No priest, no parent, no professor, no partner can do the inner work in your place. Teachers point. You walk. That is also Dhp 276, waiting two minutes later.

**Claim 2 — not a statue.** The same chapter-world includes *anattā* (not-self), said outright in Dhp 279: *sabbe dhammā anattā* (all things are not-self). You are not a fixed soul with a permanent brand. You are a process assembling a pattern — habits of attention, speech, story.

Western ears hear “no self” and think erasure: you don’t exist, nothing matters. That is not this verse. This verse is colder and kinder: there is no finished object in there to defend, and there is also no one else who can clean it for you.

*Pāpa* in older English is “evil.” In this ethics it is unwholesome action — the thing that stains the agent who does it. *Visujjhati* is “is purified”: not magical laundry, but the mind no longer carrying that pattern as identity.

**Likely misunderstanding.** “So it’s all my fault.” The verse is about agency, not about blaming the victim of other people’s harm. You did not cause every wound. You are the only one who can train the mind that woke up this morning.

### SAY
Two cheap stories after a bad exam, a dumped friendship, a week that got away.

Story one: “I am a broken person.” That freezes you into a statue of failure.
Story two: “Someone else should have saved me.” That outsources the only work that can actually move.

This verse blocks both. Damage done *attanā* (by oneself) is also the site of repair *attanā* (by oneself). That is not pep talk. It is a refusal to freeze.

People think Buddhism says “there is no self” and then stops. The *Attavagga* (Self chapter) is sterner. You are responsible — and you are not a statue. *Sabbe dhammā anattā* (all things are not-self). You are a process assembling a pattern. Patterns can be interrupted.

After a miss, not “I am stupid.” “This is a pattern that showed up. Patterns are not destiny.”

That is why this close is called *You Are Not a Finished Object*.

### Apply today
After the grade, the fight, the missed week, try the two-line script:

1. What pattern showed up? (name it: avoidance, spite, the scroll, the collapse after one miss)
2. What is one return to the same piece? (not a new personality — one next action)

You are not purifying your eternal soul. You are interrupting a habit while it is still a habit.

### If they push
“No one can purify another — so friendship is pointless?” No. Friends, teachers, and a *sangha* (community) can point, hold, and refuse to feed the worst story. They cannot do the seeing for you. That limit is respect, not loneliness.

Backup verses if you need them: Dhp 160, *Attā hi attano nātho* (oneself is one’s own protector); Dhp 159, self-control is hard — do what you tell others to do.

## Slide 6 — Dhp 239
**Chapter:** *Malavagga* (Impurity), verse 239
**On screen:** “By degrees, little by little, from time to time, a wise person should remove his own impurities, as a smith removes the dross from silver.”

### Concept
If 165 says the work is yours, 239 says what the work looks like in time.

- *Thokaṁ thokaṁ* = little by little.
- *Khaṇe khaṇe* = moment by moment / from time to time.
- *Medhāvī* = a wise person — here, the one who returns.
- *Mala* = impurity, dross. Not “you are dirty.” The slag that is not the metal.
- *Kammāra* = smith.

The image fights two campus religions at once: perfectionism (“if it isn’t clean, throw it out”) and despair (“I already ruined it”). A smith does not throw out the silver because last night’s pass was ugly. The metal is still the metal. The method is incremental heat and return.

This is the Dhammapada’s path-ethic in household language. Dhp 276 says the same thing from the other side: *Tumhehi kiccam ātappaṁ, akkhātāro Tathāgatā* — you yourselves must strive (*ātappa*, ardour, your own effort); the Buddhas only point the way. *Tathāgata* = the Buddha, “the one thus gone.” A lecture is pointing. Walking is yours.

Related handle, if they need the phone version: Dhp 21, *appamāda* (heedfulness, not-being-away) versus *pamāda* (heedlessness). Phone face-down for one reading block is *appamāda*. Opening the feed “just for a second” is *pamāda*.

**Likely misunderstanding.** “Little by little” means the change doesn’t matter. No. The verse is against dramatic purity tests, not against seriousness. The smith is working. He is just not doing it as a single explosion.

### SAY
*Thokaṁ thokaṁ, khaṇe khaṇe* (little by little, moment by moment).

That is the sentence for the week you decided was already dead because you missed two days. Perfectionism heard the miss and issued a verdict: finished object, failed semester, why bother.

The smith does not throw out the silver because last night’s work was ugly. He returns to the same piece. You are not a finished object. The metal is still on the bench.

If you have eight seconds more: “You yourselves must strive; the Buddhas only point the way.” I can point. I cannot walk it for you. That is not cold. That is the respect this book has for you.

### Apply today
After a miss, one return counts. Not a new planner. Not a new identity. The next page, the next problem set, the next honest hour.

That is *thokaṁ thokaṁ*. It will feel too small. That is how you know it is the method and not the fantasy.

### Question before you advance
Which of these five would have changed something you did this week?

Do not collect answers. Count to eight. Look at the back wall. Then go to the last slide.

## Slide 7 — Leave-behind
**On screen:** What it leaves in your pocket

### Concept
The Dhammapada was built to be carried, not finished. Five verses is not the book. It is a fair first-year portion if they actually use one line.

### SAY
Pick the verse that found you. Use it once before Friday. Not in a paper. As a check before you act.

- **5** — Don’t send the second shot.
- **222** — Brake the rolling cart.
- **338** — Name the thirst before the thumb.
- **165** — Pattern, not statue.
- **239** — Return to the same piece.

That is as much of the Dhammapada as a first-year room can honestly carry. And it is enough.

### Then stop
No joke. No “any questions.” Let the last line sit. Understanding happened on the verse slides. This slide is only the pocket.

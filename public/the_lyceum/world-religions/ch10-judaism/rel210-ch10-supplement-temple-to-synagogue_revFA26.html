From Temple to Synagogue
How Jewish worship moved from one altar to many houses of prayer
An optional companion to Lecture 3, slides P, U, and V
Background image: AI-generated painting imagining the Second Temple courtyard: a large altar of stone blocks with a long ramp up one side, where priests in white robes and turbans tend a smoking fire, while other priests stand on the stone pavement; behind rises a tall temple facade with columns and a gold-framed doorway hung with a purple curtain.

This optional mini-lecture expands Lecture 3, slides P (“Temple Sacrifice & Its Transformation”), U and V (“Worship in the Synagogue” and “Who Leads, Who Counts”). It tells one story: how a religion centered on one Temple and its altar learned to worship anywhere ten Jews gather.

Keep one thread visible: the synagogue did not simply replace the Temple on a single day. Prayer houses grew up beside the Temple for centuries; after 70 CE they took on much of its meaning.

Trail I
Two Temples & an Exile
Where did Israel worship, and what happened when the Temple fell?

This trail tells the story of the First Temple, its destruction in 586 BCE, the exile in Babylon, and the return to build a Second Temple. Students met the outline in Lecture 1.

Background (AI-generated, from Lecture 1, slide O): an imagining of Solomon’s Temple, not a reconstruction based on excavation.

One House, One Altar
King Solomon builds: a “house of the Lord” in Jerusalem (1 Kings 6:1)
One place: the Torah limits burnt offerings to “the place that the Lord will choose” (Deut 12:14)
Priests serve: they offer the sacrifices; the people bring them

Hear the plea of your servant and of your people Israel when they pray toward this place.

Solomon’s prayer at the Temple’s dedication, 1 Kings 8:30 (NRSV)

Tell it as a story: King Solomon, David’s son, builds the house of God that David was not allowed to build. When it is finished, Solomon stands before the altar and prays. He asks God to hear the people when they pray toward this house, even, he says, if they are one day carried captive to an enemy’s land and pray from there “toward their land… and the house that I have built for your name” (1 Kings 8:48).

Point out the surprise: in the Temple’s own founding story, prayer is already part of worship, and prayer can be said far away, facing Jerusalem. That is still how synagogues are oriented (Lecture 3, slide U).

586 BCE: The Temple Burns
Destruction: the Babylonians burn “the house of the Lord” (2 Kings 25:9)
Exile: many Judeans are taken to Babylon, far from the only altar
A small sanctuary: Ezekiel hears God say, “I have been a sanctuary to them for a little while” in exile (Ezek 11:16)
Later reading: the Talmud takes Ezekiel’s “little sanctuary” to mean the synagogues of Babylonia
Some scholars think synagogues began here, in exile. There is no direct evidence, so treat it as a theory.

Tell it as a story: in 586 BCE the army of King Nebuchadnezzar of Babylon takes Jerusalem and burns the Temple. Many of the leading families are marched off to Babylon. Psalm 137 remembers it: “By the rivers of Babylon—there we sat down and there we wept when we remembered Zion.” How do you worship God when the one place for sacrifice is gone and you live a thousand miles away?

The prophet Ezekiel, himself an exile, hears God promise to be “a sanctuary to them for a little while” among the nations. Centuries later the Talmud reads the verse (in its own translation, “a little sanctuary”) as referring to “the synagogues and study halls in Babylonia.” That is a later rabbinic reading, not evidence that synagogues existed in Ezekiel’s time.

Britannica: “Some scholars think” the destruction of 586 BCE gave rise to synagogues “after private homes were temporarily used for public worship and religious instruction.” Others trace them to the Second Temple period (Trail II). Keep both on the table.

Return and a Second Temple
Permission: the Persian king Cyrus lets the exiles “go up to Jerusalem” and rebuild (Ezra 1:3)
Rebuilt: the Second Temple is finished in the sixth year of King Darius (Ezra 6:15), usually dated 515 BCE
A new practice: Ezra reads the Torah aloud to the people, and teachers explain it

So they read from the book, from the law of God, with interpretation. They gave the sense, so that the people understood the reading.

Nehemiah 8:8 (NRSV)

Tell it as a story: about fifty years after the fall, Persia conquers Babylon, and King Cyrus allows the Judeans to go home and rebuild. When its foundations are laid, old people who remember Solomon’s Temple weep aloud while others shout for joy (Ezra 3:12). Later the scribe Ezra stands on a wooden platform in Jerusalem and reads the Torah aloud from early morning until midday, while teachers help the people understand.

Why it matters for this story: public Torah reading with explanation is exactly what later synagogues do every Sabbath (Lecture 3, slide U). The Temple is rebuilt, but a second kind of worship, reading and teaching, has begun to take shape beside it.

Dates: the sixth year of Darius I is usually dated 515 BCE; treat it as approximate.

Trail II
Prayer Houses beside the Temple
Where did the first synagogues come from?

This trail looks at worship in the Second Temple and at the first hard evidence for Jewish meeting houses: Greek inscriptions from Egypt, a building inscription from Jerusalem, and excavated halls at Gamla and Masada.

Scholars debate where synagogues came from. Present each piece of evidence as evidence, and the theories as theories.

Background (AI-generated, from Lecture 1): an imagining of the Second Temple.

A Day in the Second Temple
Daily offering: one lamb “in the morning, and the other lamb… at twilight” (Num 28:4)
Priests in shifts: descendants of Aaron, divided into 24 “watches,” each serving about a week at a time
Words with the offering: priests recite the Ten Commandments and the Shema
Song: Levites sing a psalm for each day of the week
Even at the altar, worship already had words: Torah, blessing, and song.

Picture the morning (from the Mishnah): before dawn, priests draw lots for the day’s tasks. The morning lamb is offered on the great altar. Then the priests gather in a hall, say a blessing, and recite the Ten Commandments and the three paragraphs of the Shema. The Levites, on a platform in the Temple court, sing the psalm for that day, for example Psalm 24, “The earth is the LORD’s,” on the first day of the week. In the late afternoon a second lamb is offered.

The Mishnah was written down around 200 CE, more than a century after the Temple fell, so it is a memory of the Temple, sometimes idealized, not an eyewitness report.

Connect: the Shema and the daily psalms are still in the Jewish prayer book today. Students saw the Shema in Lecture 2.

  • Mishnah Tamid 5:1 (William Davidson Edition, Sefaria), checked 2026-10-19: the priests “recited the Ten Commandments, Shema… VeHaya im Shamoa… and VaYomer.” Mishnah Tamid 7:4: “each daily psalm that the Levites would recite in the Temple. On the first day of the week… (Psalms, chapter 24).”
Standing In for the Whole People
The problem: the daily offering belongs to all Israel, but all Israel cannot stand in Jerusalem
The answer: each priestly watch had a matching group of ordinary Israelites, the ma’amad (“standing”)
At home: members who stayed behind gathered in their towns and read from Genesis

Some of them went up to Jerusalem, while the rest of them assembled in their towns and read the act of Creation.

Mishnah Taanit 4:2 (William Davidson Edition)

Tell it as a story: the Mishnah asks a sharp question. The daily lamb is offered on behalf of every Jew; how can a person’s offering be made when he is not standing beside it? Its answer: for every priestly watch there was a group of ordinary Israelites who “stood by” the offering for the community. Some went up to Jerusalem; the rest gathered in their own towns at the same time and read the creation story.

This is one theory of where synagogues came from. Britannica: “Other scholars trace the origin of synagogues” to this custom of community representatives praying together while their priests served in Jerusalem. Present it as a possible root, not a proven one; the Mishnah’s description was written down after the Temple fell.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Mishnah Taanit 4:2 (William Davidson Edition, Sefaria), checked 2026-10-19: “how can a person’s offering be sacrificed when he is not standing next to it?”; “For each and every priestly watch there was a corresponding non-priestly watch in Jerusalem of priests, Levites, and Israelites”; “some of them went up to Jerusalem, while the rest of them assembled in their towns and read the act of Creation.”
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “synagogue” (checked 2026-10-19): “Other scholars trace the origin of synagogues to the Jewish custom of having representatives of communities outside Jerusalem pray together during the two-week period when priestly representatives of their community attended ritual sacrifices in the Temple of Jerusalem.”
The First Hard Evidence: Egypt
Where: Jewish communities in Ptolemaic Egypt, far from Jerusalem
When: the reign of Ptolemy III, 246–221 BCE
What: Greek stone inscriptions dedicating a proseuchē, a “prayer hall”
Caution: a prayer hall is not yet the later synagogue; Jews met in many kinds of buildings

On behalf of king Ptolemaios… and queen Berenike… the Jews dedicated this prayer hall.

Dedication inscription from Schedia, Egypt, 246–221 BCE (trans. attalus.org)

Tell it as a story: in a river-port town near Alexandria, the local Jews put up a building and set a stone over it. In Greek, the language of their neighbors, they dedicate their “prayer hall” in honor of the king and queen. A nearly identical stone comes from Krokodilopolis in the Fayum. These are the oldest dated evidence for a Jewish public building outside the Temple.

Britannica: “The oldest dated evidence of a synagogue is from the 3rd century bce.” Hedge, as the Posen Library does: many scholars see the proseuchai as forerunners of synagogues, but “a direct correlation… should be resisted,” because the synagogue was not one uniform institution.

Notice that these Jews still had a Temple in Jerusalem. The prayer hall grew up beside it, not instead of it.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Schedia and Krokodilopolis inscriptions (OGIS 726; CIJ II 1440 / CPJ III 1532A), trans. attalus.org, checked 2026-10-19: “On behalf of king Ptolemaios the son of Ptolemaios and queen Berenike, his wife and sister, and their children, the Jews dedicated this prayer hall.” Dated to the reign of Ptolemy III Euergetes, 246–221 BCE.
  • Posen Library, “Early Evidence for the Synagogue” (checked 2026-10-19): the earliest archaeological evidence is “Greek dedicatory inscriptions from Egypt” naming proseuchai; “a direct correlation to or identification between the two institutions should be resisted.”
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “synagogue”: “The oldest dated evidence of a synagogue is from the 3rd century bce, but synagogues doubtless have an older history.”
A Synagogue in Jerusalem
Found: in 1913, in a cistern just south of the Temple Mount
Date: most scholars say before 70 CE, while the Temple still stood (a few argue later)
Builder: Theodotos, a priest and “head of the synagogue,” like his father and grandfather

…built the synagogue for the reading of the Law and the teaching of the commandments, as well as the guest-house… for the lodging of those who are in need of it from abroad.

The Theodotos inscription, Jerusalem, in Greek (trans. J. Price, CIIP)
Reading and teaching, not sacrifice, and no mention of prayer.

Tell it as a story: in 1913 the archaeologist Raymond Weill was digging in the oldest part of Jerusalem. In a cistern filled with the rubble of a demolished building he found a limestone block with ten lines of Greek. A man named Theodotos, a priest, says he built a synagogue for reading the Law and teaching the commandments, with a guest-house and water installations for visitors “from abroad,” likely pilgrims coming to the Temple.

What it shows: synagogues existed in Jerusalem itself, right beside the Temple, and their core purpose was Torah reading and teaching. Prayer is not mentioned; some scholars think prayer happened there anyway, others think the Temple met that need. Keep the question open.

Dating: most scholars date it to the Herodian or early Roman period (1st century BCE to 70 CE); a minority propose a later date. Say “most scholars.”

Halls of Benches: Gamla and Masada
Gamla
A town in the Golan. Its hall, built no earlier than the late 1st century BCE, is often called the earliest purpose-built synagogue found in the land. Rome destroyed the town in 67 CE.
Masada
During the revolt against Rome, Jewish rebels turned a room of Herod’s fortress into an assembly hall, adding rows of benches.
What they lack
No Torah shrine, no Jewish symbols, and no fixed facing toward Jerusalem. Those features become common only in later synagogues.
Before 70 CE a synagogue was mainly a place to gather, read, and learn; the altar was still in Jerusalem.

Picture it: a plain rectangular hall with stone benches stepped up around all four walls, so everyone faces the middle, where the Torah would be read. That is what archaeologists found at Gamla and at Masada.

Masada adds a detail students like: a back room with pits holding scroll fragments of Deuteronomy and Ezekiel. Archaeologist Jodi Magness suggests it served as a genizah, a place where worn-out sacred writings are buried rather than thrown away.

Hedge the dates: Gamla’s builders and date are debated (late 1st century BCE or later; one scholar argues about 50 CE). The identification of some early sites as synagogues has itself been challenged. Say “often identified as.”

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Jodi Magness, “Ancient Synagogues in Palestine: An Overview” (British Academy, doi 10.5871/bacad/9780197267653.003.0001), via search excerpt checked 2026-10-19: “At Masada and Herodium, synagogues were installed in pre-existing Herodian rooms by Jewish rebels at the time of the First Revolt”; the rebels added “columns and rows of benches along the walls”; pre-70 synagogues lack “an orientation towards Jerusalem, a Torah shrine, and Jewish symbols and iconography”; the Masada back room “seems to have served as a genizah” with “scroll fragments belonging to Deuteronomy and Ezekiel”; Gamla “was constructed not before the late first century BCE and was destroyed during the Roman siege in 67 CE.”
  • Wikipedia, “Gamla Synagogue” (checked 2026-10-19): dating debated; Steven Fine “believes that it was built after 40 BCE”; Uri Zvi Maoz argued for about 50 CE.
Pause & Connect
You are a Jew in Alexandria in 50 BCE. Where do you offer a sacrifice, and where do you hear the Torah read?

Give students a minute. A good answer: sacrifice only at the Temple in Jerusalem, perhaps on a pilgrimage; Torah reading and prayer in the local proseuchē or synagogue every Sabbath.

Trail III
After the Fall
How did Judaism survive without sacrifice?

In 70 CE Roman soldiers burned the Second Temple (Lecture 1, slide X). This trail tells how the rabbis rebuilt Jewish life around Torah, prayer, and good deeds.

Background (AI-generated, from Lecture 1, slide X): Jerusalem burning in 70 CE.

Yohanan ben Zakkai and Yavneh
The siege: Jerusalem is surrounded by Rome; inside, rebels will not let anyone leave
The escape: his students carry the sage Yohanan ben Zakkai out as if he were a corpse
The request: before the Roman general, he asks: “Give me Yavne and its Sages”
The new center: at Yavneh the sages keep the calendar, the law, and the festivals going
For the war itself: Roman Judea mini-lecture →

Tell it as a story (Talmud, Gittin 56a–b): during the siege, the zealots inside Jerusalem won’t let anyone surrender. Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai pretends to be sick, then dead, and his students Rabbi Eliezer and Rabbi Yehoshua carry him out of the city for “burial.” He goes to the Roman general Vespasian, greets him as king, and when Vespasian offers him a request, he asks: “Give me Yavne and its Sages and do not destroy it.” The Talmud even records a later criticism: perhaps he should have asked to save Jerusalem itself.

Hedge it: this is a legend written down centuries later, and the details (and the timing with Vespasian) are not historical evidence. What is secure is that after 70 CE Yavneh, a town near the coast, became the center of the rabbis’ work.

One example of their work: the Mishnah says that after the Temple was destroyed, Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai ruled that the shofar be blown on a Rosh Hashanah that falls on Shabbat at Yavneh, as it had been in the Temple. Temple practices were carried to a new place.

The Offering of Our Lips

Take words with you and return to the Lord… and we will offer the fruit of our lips.

Hosea 14:2 (NRSV); Jewish translation: “Instead of bulls we will pay [the offering of] our lips” (NJPS, 14:3)
Prayer as offering: the three daily prayers are timed to the old Temple offerings
A rabbinic debate: one rabbi says the patriarchs began the prayers; another says they “parallel the offerings”
Kindness as offering: “we have another means of atonement”: lovingkindness (Lecture 3, slide P)
Words, study, and good deeds took the altar’s place, without forgetting the altar.

Start with the verse: the prophet Hosea calls Israel to return to God and bring not animals but words. The Hebrew is striking: literally “we will pay bulls, our lips.” The NRSV smooths it to “the fruit of our lips”; the Jewish Publication Society translation (NJPS) reads “Instead of bulls we will pay [the offering of] our lips,” and notes that the Hebrew is uncertain.

The debate (Berakhot 26b): Rabbi Yosei son of Rabbi Ḥanina says the three daily prayers go back to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi says they were instituted “based on the daily offerings,” and parallel them “in terms of both time and characteristics”: morning, afternoon, and evening. The Talmud finds support for both. Either way, Jewish prayer remembers the Temple; the Amidah still asks for the Temple service to be restored.

Psalm 141:2 says the same thing in poetry: “Let my prayer be counted as incense before you, and the lifting up of my hands as an evening sacrifice.”

Trail IV
Synagogues through the Ages
What did synagogues and rabbis become?

This trail visits four buildings: a painted synagogue on the Roman frontier, a mosaic floor in Galilee, a Gothic synagogue in Prague, and the oldest synagogue building in the United States. Along the way, the rabbi’s job changes.

Background (AI-generated, from Lecture 3, slide U): a modern synagogue ark and reading table.

Dura-Europos: A Painted Synagogue
Where: a Roman frontier town on the Euphrates, in today’s Syria
When: an Aramaic inscription dates the last rebuilding to 244 CE
What: walls covered with Bible scenes: Moses, the Exodus, Ezra reading the Law
How it survived: buried in sand to strengthen the city wall before the Persians took the town about 256 CE

Tell it as a story: in 1932, archaeologists digging a ruined desert town on the Euphrates found a room with painted walls, rows of Bible stories in color, around an arched niche facing Jerusalem where the Torah scrolls were kept. At first some took it for a Greek temple. It was a synagogue. It survived because, as the Persian army approached about 256 CE, the defenders packed the buildings along the city wall with earth to strengthen it.

Why it matters: it overturned the old assumption that ancient Jews never made pictures of people. The paintings show Moses at the Red Sea, the Ark, Esther and Mordecai, and Ezra reading the Law. Ask: how do the paintings teach the same stories the Torah reading did?

The paintings are now in the National Museum of Damascus. Much of what remained at the site was damaged during the Syrian civil war.

About the image (real photograph, not AI): an archival black-and-white photo of the whole west wall, reproduced across two book pages; the niche in the center held the Torah.

Beit Alfa: A Mosaic Floor
Found: in 1928, by kibbutz members digging an irrigation channel in the Jezreel Valley
When: built in the 6th century CE, under the emperor Justin, probably Justin I (518–527)
Three panels: the binding of Isaac; a zodiac wheel around the sun god Helios; the Torah ark between two menorahs
Debated: why a zodiac in a synagogue? A calendar, a decoration, or a mystical symbol?

Tell it as a story: in 1928, members of Kibbutz Beit Alfa were digging an irrigation channel when they hit a mosaic floor. Excavated the next year, it turned out to be a synagogue floor in three panels. Nearest the door: Abraham about to sacrifice Isaac, with the hand of God and a ram caught in a thicket. In the middle: a zodiac wheel with Hebrew labels around Helios driving his chariot. Nearest the ark: a picture of a Torah ark flanked by two seven-branched menorahs, with a shofar, a lulav and etrog, and lions.

Why it matters: by now synagogues had become holy places in their own right, full of Temple memories (the menorahs) and facing Jerusalem. The zodiac is genuinely debated: some scholars see it as a Jewish calendar, others as a mystical or Hellenized Judaism, others as a common decorative design. Present all three.

About the image (real photograph, not AI): the zodiac panel and, below it, the binding of Isaac, with the dedication inscriptions in the foreground. The ark panel is beyond the zodiac, at the far end.

Prague and the Rabbi’s Job
The Old-New Synagogue
Built in Prague around 1270, it is the oldest synagogue in Europe still in use: a Gothic hall built by masons from the royal workshop.
A teacher, not a priest
Rabbis were scholars who taught and judged by Torah law. Any knowledgeable Jew could lead prayer once ten were present.
A paid office
For centuries the rabbi took no salary. Salaried communal rabbis appear in Spain from the 11th century and in German lands after about 1300.
Priests served an altar; rabbis serve a book, and the community that reads it.

Picture it: in Prague’s Jewish Town stands a steep-roofed Gothic building, its floor a few steps below the street. It was called the New or Great Shul when it was built in the late 13th century; when newer synagogues went up in the 16th century, people started calling it the Old-New (Altneuschul). It has been used for prayer for more than 700 years. Legends gathered around it, including the story that the remains of the Golem of Rabbi Loew lie in its attic.

The rabbi’s job: in the Talmud’s time and long after, a rabbi was a learned man who taught and judged, and earned a living some other way, since Torah was to be taught free. Communities in medieval Spain began paying rabbis by the 11th century; in Ashkenaz (German lands) a professional rabbinate formed only from the late 13th or 14th century, with a formal ordination title, Morenu ha-Rav, “our teacher the rabbi.”

Connect to Lecture 3, slide V: the rabbi “teaches and interprets”; prayer needs a minyan, not a clergy member.

Synagogues Today
Three names, three jobs: bet ha-tefillah, house of prayer; bet ha-knesset, house of assembly; bet ha-midrash, house of study
Touro Synagogue, 1763: in Newport, Rhode Island, the oldest synagogue building in the United States
“To bigotry no sanction”: George Washington’s 1790 letter to its congregation promised religious freedom
Today: a rabbi teaches, preaches, and counsels; the building also hosts schools and social programs

Tell it as a story: in 1658 Jews whose families had fled the Inquisition in Spain and Portugal reached Newport, Rhode Island, drawn by its religious freedom. In 1763 they dedicated Touro Synagogue. In 1790 President George Washington wrote to “the Hebrew congregation at Newport” that the new nation would give “to bigotry no sanction and to persecution no assistance” (as the National Park Service quotes it).

The three Hebrew names come straight from Britannica and sum up the whole story: prayer (Trail III), assembly (Trail II), and study (Ezra and Theodotos). Some Reform and Conservative congregations call their building a “temple”; Yiddish speakers say shul, “school.”

One altar became many houses of prayer. What did Judaism gain, and what did it lose?

Walk the flow as one story: one Temple and one altar; exile and the first idea of a “little sanctuary”; a rebuilt Temple with public Torah reading beside it; prayer halls in Egypt and synagogues even in Jerusalem; the fall in 70 CE; Yavneh and prayer in place of sacrifice; painted and mosaic synagogues; and the rabbi and synagogue of today.

Possible answers: gained portability (Judaism could be practiced anywhere ten Jews gathered), lay participation, and study as worship; lost the Temple, the priesthood’s central role, and the one place where all Israel met. The prayers still ask for the Temple’s return.

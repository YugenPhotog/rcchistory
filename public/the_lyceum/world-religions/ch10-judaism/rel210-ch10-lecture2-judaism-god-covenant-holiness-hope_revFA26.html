Teachings of Judaism · One God

One God: The Shema

Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord alone.

Deuteronomy 6:4 (NRSV)

Shema Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai Echad

  • Judaism teaches strict monotheism
  • Shema Yisrael, “Hear, O Israel”: a Jewish prayer that encapsulates the monotheistic essence of Judaism

Understanding This Slide

The Shema is the clearest entry into Jewish monotheism: “Hear, O Israel” is both confession and prayer. Its point is not merely that one deity exists, but that Israel owes undivided loyalty to YHWH.

Observant Jews recite the Shema morning and evening. Connect belief to practice: the doctrine of one God is rehearsed daily, taught to children, written in mezuzot, and embodied in covenant loyalty. Deuteronomy 6:4-9.

About the image (AI-generated): the mezuzah is fixed at a slant. That is the Ashkenazi custom, a compromise between authorities who said it should stand upright and those who said it should lie flat; many Sephardi Jews hang it upright. The Rema’s gloss on the Shulchan Arukh records it: “those who are scrupulous fulfill both [opinions] and place the mezuzah at an incline, at a diagonal.”

Why the Cryptic Name?

At the burning bush, Moses asks God’s name. The answer is a riddle:

God said to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM.”

Exodus 3:14 (NRSV)

Hebrew: Ehyeh asher ehyeh (transliteration)

  • What the name teaches: God simply is; no name can capture God; God will be present with the people
  • Unlike a local god whose name could be used to summon it

Understanding This Slide

Exodus 3 resists treating the divine name like the name of a manageable local deity. “I AM” or “I WILL BE” suggests self-existence, continuing presence, and freedom from human control.

Merged from the retired slide G (“What the Name Teaches”), whose five headings were from the original slide: Eternal self-existence (“I am” / “I will be”: God simply is, uncreated); Ineffability (no name can capture or control God); Active presence (God is the one who will be there with the people); Hebrew roots (YHWH and Ehyeh both echo hayah, “to be”); Contrast to pagan deities (not a manageable local god whose name can be used to summon it).

Understanding This Slide

These titles emphasize different aspects of divine relationship and majesty. El is a general Semitic term for God; El Shaddai is traditionally rendered God Almighty; El Elyon means God Most High; Adonai means Lord; HaShem means The Name.

Do not present the list as separate gods. The titles are different ways of addressing or describing the one God.

The meaning of Shaddai is debated. “God Almighty” follows the Latin Vulgate (Deus omnipotens); a scholarly proposal derives it from Akkadian shadû, “mountain.”

Definitions

Ethical Monotheism

Most significant contribution to world religion and history

Monotheism

Belief in one and only one god

Ethical Monotheism

Belief in one and only one god who is the source of morality and demands goodness, i.e., ethical conduct

Understanding This Slide

Define the distinction precisely. Monotheism is belief in one God. Ethical monotheism is belief in one God whose character is morally good and who therefore places moral obligations on human beings.

This is the lecture’s central concept. Ethical conduct is not an optional supplement to worship; it is a response to the nature and will of God.

Ethical Monotheism: One God, One Human Race

  • God is a law-giver who places moral obligations on people, not a capricious or arbitrary god
  • Significant implication: One God means One Human Race
  • No person or group is intrinsically more valuable than any other
  • The story behind it: after the flood, God makes a covenant with Noah’s family “and with every living creature” (Genesis 9:10)
  • So the rabbis teach one moral law for all humanity, the seven Noahide laws; Israel’s covenant adds further commandments
×
The Seven Noahide Laws

According to the Talmud (Sanhedrin 56a), all descendants of Noah, that is, all of humanity, are commanded to: (1) establish courts of justice; and not to (2) curse God, (3) worship idols, (4) commit forbidden sexual relations, (5) shed blood, (6) rob, or (7) eat a limb torn from a living animal.

Understanding This Slide

Follow the argument step by step: if one God created all humanity, then the human race forms one moral community. No people possesses a different creator or a separate moral universe.

Chosenness therefore cannot coherently mean that other people lack value. Jewish sources usually interpret chosenness as a distinctive covenantal vocation and burden of obligation.

Rabbinic tradition holds that all descendants of Noah are bound by seven basic laws; Maimonides calls gentiles who keep them “the pious among the gentiles” (Mishneh Torah, Kings 8:11, trans. Touger).

Tell the story first (about 1 minute): students met Noah in Lecture 1. After the flood, God promises Noah never again to destroy the earth by a flood and makes that covenant with Noah, his descendants, and every living creature, with the rainbow as its sign (Genesis 9:8–17). Because every human being descends from Noah in the story, the rabbis read this as one covenant, and one basic moral law, for all humanity. The popup lists the seven laws.

The first two bullets of the original slide (“God places moral obligations on followers”; “God is a law-giver God, not capricious or arbitrary”) are combined into one.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

What Must God Be Like?

What does God need to be like for this belief to be meaningful?

Contrast the Hebrew God to the polytheistic gods of the day.

Transcendent→Personal→Good→Holy

Understanding This Slide

Use comparison carefully. Many ancient religions associated gods with natural forces, cities, dynasties, and peoples. Israelite religion increasingly described YHWH as sovereign over nature and history rather than one force within them.

Avoid a simplistic progression from “primitive polytheism” to “advanced monotheism.” Polytheistic traditions also developed sophisticated ethics. The distinctive Jewish claim is that a single universal God grounds a unified moral order.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Transcendent

In the beginning when God created the heavens and the earth, …

Genesis 1:1 (NRSV)

  • God is above and separate from Nature (not a part of it)
  • Nature is finite – God is infinite
  • Implication: God is Eternal and Unchanging (Psalm 102:25–27; numbered 102:26–28 in Jewish Bibles)

Understanding This Slide

Transcendence means that God is not identical with nature. Creation depends upon God; God does not depend upon creation. This makes nature meaningful and good without making it divine.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

Nature Is Not God

  • Nature is amoral – God is the source of morality
  • Implication: Desacralize nature – foolishness to worship the creation instead of the Creator (Psalm 96:4–5)

For all the gods of the peoples are idols, but the Lord made the heavens.

Psalm 96:5 (NRSV)

×
Desacralize

To stop treating something as divine. In this view nature is created and good, but it is not a god to be worshiped or appeased; moral standards come from its Creator, not from natural power.

Understanding This Slide

The ethical implication is crucial: what happens in nature is not automatically what ought to happen morally. Human beings can resist cruelty, exploitation, and “survival of the fittest” because moral obligation comes from beyond natural power.

Tell the legend (about 1 minute): a midrash says that Abraham’s father, Terah, made and sold idols. Left in charge of the shop, Abraham smashed all the idols with a club and put the club in the hand of the largest one. When Terah demanded, “Who did this to them?” Abraham said the big idol had done it. Terah answered, “Are they sentient at all?” and Abraham replied, “Do your ears not hear what your mouth is saying?” (Genesis Rabbah 38:13). Brought before King Nimrod and told to bow to fire, Abraham argues step by step that water, clouds, and wind are each stronger than the last, so none of them deserves worship. That is this slide’s point: no part of nature is God.

Be clear with students that this is a later rabbinic legend (midrash), not a story in Genesis. The image (AI-generated) shows Abraham as a boy; the midrash does not give his age.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Good

God is Good

  • God rules by moral standards

Power alone cannot ground morality; goodness can.

Understanding This Slide

Ethical monotheism requires more than divine power. If God were arbitrary or morally capricious, divine commands could not establish a trustworthy moral order.

Jewish tradition repeatedly describes God as just, compassionate, faithful, and merciful. Human goodness is therefore measured by resemblance to divine character, not merely by obedience to power.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Holy

  • Definition: spiritually pure, consecrated, devoted to God, set apart
  • Holiness means becoming like God in how we live—created in God’s image, called to be gracious, compassionate, and just

You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy.

Leviticus 19:2 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Holiness means being set apart for God’s purposes, but it also carries ethical content. Leviticus 19 links the command to be holy with obligations concerning parents, workers, the poor, neighbors, strangers, honesty, and justice.

Imago Dei grounds human dignity: every human being bears God’s image. The concept supports moral agency and the obligation to treat other people as persons of sacred worth.

Maimonides summarizes the sages: “Just as God is called ‘Gracious,’ you too must be gracious. Just as God is called ‘Compassionate,’ you too must be compassionate. Just as God is called ‘Holy,’ you too must be holy.” (Mishneh Torah, De’ot 1:6, Sefaria Community Translation)

Theophany

An appearance of God to a human being. In Genesis 17:1 (NRSV) “the LORD appeared to Abram” and begins the covenant speech.

×
Cutting a covenant

Biblical Hebrew says one “cuts” (karat) a covenant. Jeremiah 34:18 refers to the same ceremony: covenant-breakers will be made “like the calf when they cut it in two and passed between its parts” (NRSV).

Understanding This Slide

Genesis 17 connects covenant with Abraham’s new name, descendants, land, divine protection, and circumcision as a sign. Genesis 12 adds the outward mission: all families of the earth are to be blessed through Abraham.

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): Abram is old and childless. One night God takes him outside and tells him to count the stars: so shall your descendants be. Abram believes, and God “reckoned it to him as righteousness” (Gen 15:6). God tells him to bring a heifer, a goat, a ram, a turtledove, and a pigeon. Abram cuts the animals in two and lays the halves opposite each other; the birds he does not cut (Gen 15:9–10). As the sun goes down, “a deep sleep fell upon Abram” (Gen 15:12). In the dark, a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch, signs of God’s presence, pass between the pieces. Years later God appears again, renames him Abraham, “ancestor of a multitude of nations,” and gives circumcision as the covenant’s sign (Gen 17).

Why walk between cut animals? Jeremiah 34:18 explains the ceremony: those who break the covenant God will “make like the calf when they cut it in two and passed between its parts.” Walking between the pieces says, in effect, “may I become like these animals if I break my word.” In Genesis 15, only God passes through.

About the image (AI-generated): the row of halves and the two whole birds follow Genesis 15:10. The text says the halves were laid “over against the other,” that is, in two facing rows, so the single row in the painting is a simplification.

Leviticus 19:2

You Shall Be Holy: Imitatio Dei

You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy.

Leviticus 19:2 (NRSV)

  • The rabbis read Leviticus 19:2 this way: God is the King, Israel is His retinue, and holiness means imitating God.
  • Imitatio Dei (imitation of God): human holiness reflects God’s holiness

What is the duty of the king’s retinue? To imitate the king.

Abba Shaul, Sifra, Kedoshim 1:1 (Hebrew: pamalya la-melekh… lihyot mehakah la-melekh)

Understanding This Slide

Imitatio Dei means imitating God.

Explaining imitatio Dei: a king’s retinue reflects the king it serves. Abba Shaul reads “You shall be holy, for I am holy” the same way: Israel is God’s retinue, so holiness means imitating God. Since no one can become God or match God’s power, the rabbis say what can be imitated is God’s ways, above all God’s acts of kindness.

The Talmud’s examples (Sotah 14a): Rabbi Ḥama son of Rabbi Ḥanina asks how a person can “walk after” God (Deuteronomy 13:5), since God is “a devouring fire” (Deuteronomy 4:24). His answer: “one should follow the attributes of the Holy One, Blessed be He.” “Just as He clothes the naked… so too, should you clothe the naked. Just as the Holy One, Blessed be He, visits the sick… so too, should you visit the sick. Just as the Holy One, Blessed be He, consoles mourners… so too, should you console mourners. Just as the Holy One, Blessed be He, buried the dead… so too, should you bury the dead.” Each example rests on a Torah story: God makes garments for Adam and Eve (Gen 3:21), appears to Abraham after his circumcision (Gen 18:1), blesses Isaac after Abraham’s death (Gen 25:11), and buries Moses (Deut 34:6).

Ask students: which of these four acts could they do this week?

Understanding This Slide

The Ten Commandments provide a compact moral architecture rather than the whole of Jewish law. The first obligations order Israel’s relationship with God; the later commands regulate family, life, sexuality, property, truth, and desire.

Jewish numbering differs slightly from some Christian traditions. The opening statement—God who brought Israel out of slavery—is foundational.

The name: Jews keep the English name “Ten Commandments” and also use the Hebrew Aseret ha-Dibrot, “the Ten Utterances” (Rashi on Exodus 24:12 writes aseret ha-dibrot). The Torah itself says aseret ha-devarim, “the ten words” (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13; 10:4); the NRSV translates it “the ten commandments.” The English term is not only a Christian one: the Jewish Publication Society’s Tanakh (NJPS, 1985) also says “the Ten Commandments” in these verses. The older JPS 1917 translation keeps the literal “the ten words.”

Statement, not command: the first utterance, “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt” (Exodus 20:2), commands nothing; it states who is speaking and what God has done. That is one reason “utterances” fits better than “commandments.” (Maimonides still counts this verse as a commandment: to believe in God, Sefer HaMitzvot, Positive Commandment 1.)

613: “Rabbi Simlai taught: There were 613 mitzvot stated to Moses in the Torah, consisting of 365 prohibitions… and 248 positive mitzvot” (Makkot 23b). The Ten are not the whole of Jewish law; Rashi says all 613 are contained within them.

Tell the scene first (about 1 minute): three months after leaving Egypt, Israel camps at the foot of Mount Sinai. On the third morning there is thunder, lightning, a thick cloud, and a trumpet blast so loud that the whole camp trembles; the mountain is “wrapped in smoke, because the LORD had descended upon it in fire” (Exodus 19:16–18, NRSV). Then God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17; repeated in Deuteronomy 5). Later Moses brings down two stone tablets.

The list on the slide is a short paraphrase in the traditional Jewish numbering, where the opening statement “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt” (Exodus 20:2) counts as the first commandment and the ban on other gods and idols is the second. Trimmed 2026-10-19: the Good Impulse card read “the inclination to do what is right and what God commands.”

Rabbinic teaching holds that God created human beings with two inclinations: the yetzer ha-tov (good impulse) and the yetzer ha-ra (evil impulse). Rav Nahman bar Rav Hisda reads the double yod in vayyitzer, “the LORD God formed” (Genesis 2:7), as a sign that God created both (Berakhot 61a).

Despite its name, the evil impulse is not simply bad. Genesis Rabbah reads “very good” in Genesis 1:31 as including it, because the drives behind building, marriage, family, and work come from it. The moral task is to rule it, not to destroy it.

The Talmud pictures Torah as the remedy: God says, “I created an evil inclination, which is the wound, and I created Torah as its antidote” (Kiddushin 30b). Humans are free and responsible: “you may rule over it” (Genesis 4:7, cited there).

Ask the class: if the evil impulse drives ambition and desire, what would a life with no evil impulse look like? Then connect back to Evil in God’s Name: religion does not remove the impulse; it asks people to govern it.

Sheol

Hebrew Bible: the grave or shadowy realm of the dead—not necessarily punishment.

Hades

Greek term often used to translate Sheol; broadly, the realm of the dead.

Gehenna

From the Valley of Hinnom; later imagery of divine judgment, punishment, or purification.

Understanding This Slide

Begin with a translation warning. English Bibles sometimes render several different words as “hell,” which makes separate concepts look identical. Gehenna develops from the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem and becomes an image of divine judgment in later Jewish and Christian literature. Tartarus belongs to Greek mythic vocabulary and appears once in the New Testament for imprisoned rebellious angels; it is not a normal Jewish destination for human souls.

About the photo (a real artifact, not AI): after Titus captured Jerusalem in 70 CE, the Temple treasures were paraded through Rome in his triumph. Leviticus 19:2 · Commentary

Nahmanides: Holiness beyond the Letter of the Law

  • Who: Nahmanides (Rabbi Moses ben Nahman, “Ramban,” c. 1194–1270), rabbi of Girona in Catalonia, who later settled in the Land of Israel
  • He goes beyond just refraining from forbidden acts
  • The Torah permits food, wine, and marital intimacy; holiness asks for moderation even in what is permitted, and for clean speech
  • Otherwise a person could keep every rule and still be “a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah”
  • “Be holy” is a general command that completes the detailed laws

Understanding This Slide

Nahmanides argues that technical legality is not enough: a person could obey every individual rule while overindulging in permitted food, wine, and sex, or speaking coarsely. His phrase for such a person is naval bi-reshut ha-Torah, “a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah.” The earlier wording (“selfish or exploitative”; “ethical and moral behavior that transcends legalistic adherence”) went beyond what Ramban says on this verse and has been corrected.

Ramban draws the parallel himself: after the detailed laws of honest dealing, Deuteronomy 6:18 adds the general command “Do what is right and good in the sight of the LORD” (NRSV), which he reads as going beyond the letter of the law.

Sources

Encyclopædia Britannica, “Nahmanides” (checked 2026-10-19): born c. 1194 in Gerona, Catalonia; rabbi at Gerona; forced to flee Spain after the 1263 disputation and settled at Acre, where he died in 1270.

Ramban on Leviticus 19:2, Sefaria Community Translation (“he would be a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah”); Chavel’s translation renders it “a sordid person within the permissible realm of the Torah.” Checked 2026-10-19.

Leviticus 19:2 · Commentary

Midrash: Why Leviticus 19 Was Said to Everyone

We are hereby taught that this section was stated in the presence of all. Why so? Because most of the major tenets of Torah are inherent in it.

Sifra, Kedoshim 1:1 (trans. Shraga Silverstein); compare Leviticus Rabbah 24:5

  • Rabbi Levi: the Ten Commandments are embedded in this chapter (Leviticus Rabbah 24:5)

The implication: the pursuit of holiness permeates and underlies all aspects of religious observance.

Understanding This Slide

The broader rabbinic point is that holiness demands formation of character, not merely avoidance of prohibited acts.

Correction: the earlier slide called “You shall be holy” “a comprehensive commandment from which many other commandments can be derived.” The midrash actually says the whole section (Leviticus 19) was proclaimed to the entire assembly because most of the Torah’s essential teachings depend on it, and Rabbi Levi matches each of the Ten Commandments to a verse in the chapter. The “implication” line is our interpretation, not a quotation.

Sources

Sifra, Kedoshim, Section 1:1, trans. Shraga Silverstein (Sefaria). Rashi on Lev 19:2 cites it: “this section was proclaimed in full assembly because most of the fundamental teachings of the Torah are dependent on it” (Rosenbaum & Silbermann).

Leviticus (Vayikra) Rabbah 24:5, The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah (2022): “Rabbi Ḥiyya taught: This portion was stated in an assembly because most of the essential principles of the Torah are dependent upon it. Rabbi Levi said: Because the Ten Commandments are included in it.”

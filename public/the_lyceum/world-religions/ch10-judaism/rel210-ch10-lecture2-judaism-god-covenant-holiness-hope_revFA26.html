Section I

One God & Ethical Monotheism

What must God be like for morality to be grounded in the divine?

Understanding This Slide

This section moves from the Shema and divine names to the philosophical and ethical implications of one God. Keep returning to the distinction between divine power and divine goodness.

Teachings of Judaism · One God

One God: The Shema

Hear, O Israel: The Lord is our God, the Lord alone.

Deuteronomy 6:4 (NRSV)

Shema Yisrael, Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai Echad

  • Judaism teaches strict monotheism
  • Shema Yisrael, “Hear, O Israel”: a Jewish prayer that encapsulates the monotheistic essence of Judaism

Understanding This Slide

The Shema is the clearest entry into Jewish monotheism: “Hear, O Israel” is both confession and prayer. Its point is not merely that one deity exists, but that Israel owes undivided loyalty to YHWH.

Observant Jews recite the Shema morning and evening. Connect belief to practice: the doctrine of one God is rehearsed daily, taught to children, written in mezuzot, and embodied in covenant loyalty. Deuteronomy 6:4-9.

The NRSV footnote gives the alternatives “The LORD our God is one LORD,” “The LORD our God, the LORD is one,” and “The LORD is our God, the LORD is one.” The second is the familiar liturgical rendering of the Hebrew Adonai Eloheinu, Adonai echad.

The Divine Name: YHWH

  • Probably built on hayah, “to be” (“I am” / “I will be”); tradition reads it as God’s self-existence

Tetragrammaton

Greek for “four letters”: the Hebrew consonants Y-H-W-H (yod, he, vav, he). Traditional Jews avoid pronouncing the name, saying Adonai (“my Lord”) in prayer and HaShem (“the Name”) in ordinary speech.

Understanding This Slide

This slide continues the previous one. Explain that the Hebrew consonants YHWH are called the Tetragrammaton. Traditional Jews avoid pronouncing the name, substituting Adonai in prayer or HaShem in ordinary speech. The restraint communicates reverence and divine ineffability.

Titles of Deity

El

El Shaddai

El Elyon

Most High God

My Lord

Understanding This Slide

These titles emphasize different aspects of divine relationship and majesty. El is a general Semitic term for God; El Shaddai is traditionally rendered God Almighty; El Elyon means God Most High; Adonai means Lord; HaShem means The Name.

Do not present the list as separate gods. The titles are different ways of addressing or describing the one God.

The meaning of Shaddai is debated. “God Almighty” follows the Latin Vulgate (Deus omnipotens); a scholarly proposal derives it from Akkadian shadû, “mountain.”

Definitions

Ethical Monotheism

Most significant contribution to world religion and history

Monotheism

Belief in one and only one god

Ethical Monotheism

Belief in one and only one god who is the source of morality and demands goodness, i.e., ethical conduct

Understanding This Slide

Define the distinction precisely. Monotheism is belief in one God. Ethical monotheism is belief in one God whose character is morally good and who therefore places moral obligations on human beings.

This is the lecture’s central concept. Ethical conduct is not an optional supplement to worship; it is a response to the nature and will of God.

Ethical Monotheism: One God, One Human Race

  • God places moral obligations on followers
  • God is a law-giver God, not capricious or arbitrary
  • Significant implication: One God means One Human Race
  • No person or group is intrinsically more valuable than any other
  • One moral law for all humanity (the rabbis’ seven Noahide laws); Israel’s covenant adds further commandments

Understanding This Slide

Follow the argument step by step: if one God created all humanity, then the human race forms one moral community. No people possesses a different creator or a separate moral universe.

Chosenness therefore cannot coherently mean that other people lack value. Jewish sources usually interpret chosenness as a distinctive covenantal vocation and burden of obligation.

Rabbinic tradition holds that all descendants of Noah are bound by seven basic laws; Maimonides calls gentiles who keep them “the pious among the gentiles” (Mishneh Torah, Kings 8:11, trans. Touger).

Sources

Mishneh Torah, Kings and Wars 8:11, trans. Eliyahu Touger (Sefaria), checked 2026-10-19: “Anyone who accepts upon himself the fulfillment of these seven mitzvot and is precise in their observance is considered one of ‘the pious among the gentiles’…”

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

What Must God Be Like?

What does God need to be like for this belief to be meaningful?

Contrast the Hebrew God to the polytheistic gods of the day.

Transcendent→Personal→Good→Holy

Understanding This Slide

Use comparison carefully. Many ancient religions associated gods with natural forces, cities, dynasties, and peoples. Israelite religion increasingly described YHWH as sovereign over nature and history rather than one force within them.

Avoid a simplistic progression from “primitive polytheism” to “advanced monotheism.” Polytheistic traditions also developed sophisticated ethics. The distinctive Jewish claim is that a single universal God grounds a unified moral order.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Transcendent

In the beginning when God created the heavens and the earth, …

Genesis 1:1 (NRSV)

  • God is above and separate from Nature (not a part of it)
  • Nature is finite – God is infinite
  • Implication: God is Eternal and Unchanging (Psalm 102:25–27; numbered 102:26–28 in Jewish Bibles)

Understanding This Slide

Transcendence means that God is not identical with nature. Creation depends upon God; God does not depend upon creation. This makes nature meaningful and good without making it divine.

Sources

Genesis 1:1, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 against biblestudytools.com (NRS) and bible.oremus.org (NRSV, © 1989). The verse continues into verse 2, hence the ellipsis. The original slide’s “God created the Heavens and the Earth” was a partial paraphrase.

Psalm 102:25–27 (NRSV numbering) = 102:26–28 in Jewish Bibles, because the Hebrew counts the heading as verse 1. NRSV 102:27: “but you are the same, and your years have no end.”

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

Nature Is Not God

  • Nature is amoral – God is the source of morality
  • Implication: Desacralize nature – foolishness to worship the creation instead of the Creator (Psalm 96:4–5)

For all the gods of the peoples are idols, but the Lord made the heavens.

Psalm 96:5 (NRSV)

×
Desacralize

To stop treating something as divine. In this view nature is created and good, but it is not a god to be worshiped or appeased; moral standards come from its Creator, not from natural power.

Understanding This Slide

The ethical implication is crucial: what happens in nature is not automatically what ought to happen morally. Human beings can resist cruelty, exploitation, and “survival of the fittest” because moral obligation comes from beyond natural power.

Sources

Psalm 96:5, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 against biblestudytools.com (NRS) and bible.oremus.org (NRSV, © 1989). Same verse number in Jewish and Christian Bibles.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Personal

  • God is Personal
  • God cares about His creations
  • Created in His image with ability to know God
  • God is also immanent: present in the world and active in human history

Understanding This Slide

Transcendence does not make God remote. The covenantal God speaks, remembers, judges, forgives, and enters relationship. Jewish prayer assumes that God can be addressed and that human action matters.

Connect this to the image of God: humans possess reason, moral awareness, freedom, and the capacity for relationship.

Sources

Textbook p. 238 (Reading Guide Q6 anchor): God is “transcendent, far above the world and human ability to comprehend God, but God is immanent as well, present in the world and in each human being,” and “God guides not only those who know him, but also the nations and human history.”

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Good

God is Good

  • God rules by moral standards

Power alone cannot ground morality; goodness can.

Understanding This Slide

Ethical monotheism requires more than divine power. If God were arbitrary or morally capricious, divine commands could not establish a trustworthy moral order.

Jewish tradition repeatedly describes God as just, compassionate, faithful, and merciful. Human goodness is therefore measured by resemblance to divine character, not merely by obedience to power.

The takeaway line on the slide restates the first sentence of these notes for students.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism

God Is Holy

  • Definition: spiritually pure, consecrated, devoted to God, set apart
  • Holiness means becoming like God in how we live—created in God’s image, called to be gracious, compassionate, and just

You shall be holy, for I the Lord your God am holy.

Leviticus 19:2 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Holiness means being set apart for God’s purposes, but it also carries ethical content. Leviticus 19 links the command to be holy with obligations concerning parents, workers, the poor, neighbors, strangers, honesty, and justice.

Imago Dei grounds human dignity: every human being bears God’s image. The concept supports moral agency and the obligation to treat other people as persons of sacred worth.

Maimonides summarizes the sages: “Just as God is called ‘Gracious,’ you too must be gracious. Just as God is called ‘Compassionate,’ you too must be compassionate. Just as God is called ‘Holy,’ you too must be holy.” (Mishneh Torah, De’ot 1:6, Sefaria Community Translation)

Sources

Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, De’ot (Human Dispositions) 1:6, Sefaria Community Translation, checked 2026-10-19.

Leviticus 19:2, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 against biblestudytools.com (NRS) and bible.oremus.org (NRSV, © 1989). The original slide’s “Be ye therefore Holy, even as I am holy” is not the wording of any standard translation of Lev 19:2 (the phrasing resembles Matthew 5:48 and 1 Peter 1:16).

Section II

Covenant, Holiness & Moral Obligation

How does belief in one God become a way of life?

Understanding This Slide

Shift from the nature of God to the human response. Covenant joins identity, law, ritual, ethics, memory, and vocation. Ethical monotheism becomes concrete through Torah and holiness.

Covenant People

  • Genesis 17:7
  • Jewish tradition traces its story to Abram (later Abraham)
  • God chose Abram (and his descendants) to establish His covenant

Covenant (Hebrew berit)

Like a contract, it binds two parties with promises. Unlike a contract, it is an ongoing relationship of mutual loyalty between unequal partners—God and Israel—sealed by an oath or a sign.

×
Genesis 17:7 (NRSV)

I will establish my covenant between me and you, and your offspring after you throughout their generations, for an everlasting covenant, to be God to you and to your offspring after you.

Understanding This Slide

Covenant is the relational framework of Jewish theology. It joins divine initiative with human response: God promises presence and blessing; Abraham and his descendants are called to trust, obedience, and a distinctive way of life.

Stress that covenant is more than a commercial contract. It creates an enduring relationship, identity, and vocation.

Abrahamic Covenant: Making the Covenant

  • The Call: Genesis 17:1–2 – a theophany
  • Cut the Covenant: Genesis 15:9–10; Jeremiah 34:18
  • Terms of the Covenant: Genesis 17:1–8
  • Token of the Covenant: Genesis 17:11–12 – every male to be circumcised
×
Theophany

An appearance of God to a human being. In Genesis 17:1 (NRSV) “the LORD appeared to Abram” and begins the covenant speech.

Cutting a covenant

Biblical Hebrew says one “cuts” (karat) a covenant. In Genesis 15:9–10 animals are cut in two, and in 15:17 “a smoking fire pot and a flaming torch passed between these pieces”; Jeremiah 34:18 refers to the same ceremony, “the calf when they cut it in two and passed between its parts” (both NRSV).

Understanding This Slide

Genesis 17 connects covenant with Abraham’s new name, descendants, land, divine protection, and circumcision as a sign. Genesis 12 adds the outward mission: all families of the earth are to be blessed through Abraham.

Jewish theology usually reads election as a calling to Torah, holiness, and responsibility. The prophet Amos even makes it the ground of stricter judgment: “You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities” (Amos 3:2, NRSV).

Leviticus 19:2 · Commentary

Rashi: Holiness as Separation

Be separated from the inappropriate sexual relationships and from sin.

Rashi on Leviticus 19:2 (Sefaria Community Translation)

  • Rashi (1040–1105) links holiness to keeping away from the forbidden sexual relations listed just before (Leviticus 18) and from sin
  • Where the Torah fences off sexual wrongdoing, he notes, it also mentions holiness (e.g., Leviticus 21:7–8)

Understanding This Slide

Rashi connects holiness with separation from forbidden sexual relations and from sin (Hebrew aveirah, transgression). He does not mention idolatry here; the earlier version of this slide said “sexual immorality and idolatry,” which has been corrected.

Sources

Rashi on Leviticus 19:2, Sefaria. Hebrew: hevu perushim min ha-arayot u-min ha-aveirah. Other translations: “keep aloof from the forbidden sexual relations just mentioned and from sinful thoughts” (Rosenbaum & Silbermann, 1929–34); “Separate yourselves from sexual crimes, and from transgression” (Metsudah, 2009).

Leviticus 19:2 · Commentary

Nahmanides: Holiness beyond the Letter of the Law

  • Nahmanides (Ramban, 1194–1270) goes beyond just refraining from forbidden acts
  • The Torah permits food, wine, and marital intimacy; holiness asks for moderation even in what is permitted, and for clean speech
  • Otherwise a person could keep every rule and still be “a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah”
  • “Be holy” is a general command that completes the detailed laws

Understanding This Slide

Nahmanides argues that technical legality is not enough: a person could obey every individual rule while overindulging in permitted food, wine, and sex, or speaking coarsely. His phrase for such a person is naval bi-reshut ha-Torah, “a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah.” The earlier wording (“selfish or exploitative”; “ethical and moral behavior that transcends legalistic adherence”) went beyond what Ramban says on this verse and has been corrected.

Ramban draws the parallel himself: after the detailed laws of honest dealing, Deuteronomy 6:18 adds the general command “Do what is right and good in the sight of the LORD” (NRSV), which he reads as going beyond the letter of the law.

Sources

Ramban on Leviticus 19:2, Sefaria Community Translation (“he would be a scoundrel with the permission of the Torah”); Chavel’s translation renders it “a sordid person within the permissible realm of the Torah.” Checked 2026-10-19.

Leviticus 19:2 · Commentary

Midrash: Why Leviticus 19 Was Said to Everyone

We are hereby taught that this section was stated in the presence of all. Why so? Because most of the major tenets of Torah are inherent in it.

Sifra, Kedoshim 1:1 (trans. Shraga Silverstein); compare Leviticus Rabbah 24:5

  • Rabbi Levi: the Ten Commandments are embedded in this chapter (Leviticus Rabbah 24:5)

The implication: the pursuit of holiness permeates and underlies all aspects of religious observance.

Understanding This Slide

The broader rabbinic point is that holiness demands formation of character, not merely avoidance of prohibited acts.

Correction: the earlier slide called “You shall be holy” “a comprehensive commandment from which many other commandments can be derived.” The midrash actually says the whole section (Leviticus 19) was proclaimed to the entire assembly because most of the Torah’s essential teachings depend on it, and Rabbi Levi matches each of the Ten Commandments to a verse in the chapter. The “implication” line is our interpretation, not a quotation.

Leviticus 19

Holiness in Everyday Life

  • Ethical commandments: the chapter and verses that follow contain a variety of commandments; ethical behavior is intrinsic to the path to holiness
  • Holiness is accessible to every Jew in daily life
  • Radical concept: everybody, not just priests, can be holy in every action they undertake
  • Intertwined nature of ritual, ethics, and spirituality

Understanding This Slide

Leviticus 19 deliberately interweaves ritual and ethics. Sabbath, sacrifices, harvest rules, wages, disability, courts, speech, sexuality, neighbors, and strangers all belong to one holy life.

This resists a modern division between “religious” behavior and “ordinary” morality. Everyday economic and interpersonal choices become arenas of holiness.

The Ten Commandments: An Overview

  • See Exodus 20 (and Deuteronomy 5)
  • Divided between obligation to God and duty to others

Understanding This Slide

The Ten Commandments provide a compact moral architecture rather than the whole of Jewish law. The first obligations order Israel’s relationship with God; the later commands regulate family, life, sexuality, property, truth, and desire.

Jewish numbering differs slightly from some Christian traditions. The opening statement—God who brought Israel out of slavery—is foundational.

The Ten Commandments Begin with Freedom

Then God spoke all these words: I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery; …

Exodus 20:1–2 (NRSV)

  • God is giving these commandments! God delivered the children of Israel → therefore, obligation to God

Understanding This Slide

The commandments begin with liberation: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery” (Exodus 20:2, NRSV). Obedience is therefore a response to freedom, not the price paid to earn it.

The Ten Commandments

  • Committing evil in God’s name: a desecration of the Name
  • Evil committed by religious people does near irreparable damage to the cause of religion

Humans in the Image of God

Two Impulses: Good & Evil

Good Impulse

Yetzer ha-tov: the inclination to do what is right and what God commands.

Evil Impulse

Yetzer ha-ra: self-interest and desire, which can lead to sin.

Were it not for the evil inclination, a man would never build a house, would never marry a wife, would never beget children, and would never engage in commerce.

Genesis Rabbah 9:7 (The Sefaria Midrash Rabbah, 2022)

Both impulses are part of human nature as God made it; Torah helps people direct them.

Understanding This Slide

New slide, added so that Reading Guide Question 8 is taught before the pause that asks it.

Rabbinic teaching holds that God created human beings with two inclinations: the yetzer ha-tov (good impulse) and the yetzer ha-ra (evil impulse). Rav Nahman bar Rav Hisda reads the double yod in vayyitzer, “the LORD God formed” (Genesis 2:7), as a sign that God created both (Berakhot 61a).

Despite its name, the evil impulse is not simply bad. Genesis Rabbah reads “very good” in Genesis 1:31 as including it, because the drives behind building, marriage, family, and work come from it. The moral task is to rule it, not to destroy it.

The Talmud pictures Torah as the remedy: God says, “I created an evil inclination, which is the wound, and I created Torah as its antidote” (Kiddushin 30b). Humans are free and responsible: “you may rule over it” (Genesis 4:7, cited there).

Ask the class: if the evil impulse drives ambition and desire, what would a life with no evil impulse look like? Then connect back to Evil in God’s Name: religion does not remove the impulse; it asks people to govern it.

Ethical Monotheism & Social Justice

  • The Vulnerable: widow, orphan, stranger, poor, laborer, and debtor receive special protection.
  • The Prophets: Amos, Isaiah, and Micah condemn worship that coexists with exploitation and corrupt courts.
  • The Standard: justice, mercy, honesty, and protection of the stranger become covenant obligations.

Ritual without justice is not faithfulness to the God of Israel.

Understanding This Slide

This slide supplies the major gap in the original deck. Ethical monotheism becomes socially concrete in the Torah and prophets. Exodus repeatedly links care for strangers to Israel’s memory of having been strangers in Egypt.

Amos 5 rejects festivals and songs when justice is absent. Isaiah 1 links worship with defending the orphan and widow. Micah 6:8 summarizes the demand as justice, covenant loyalty or mercy, and humble walking with God. The point is not that ritual is unimportant, but that ritual cannot compensate for oppression.

Mashiach (Messiah)

Hebrew mashiach, “anointed one.” In the Hebrew Bible kings and priests were anointed with oil when installed in office, so the title first describes a legitimate ruler.

Understanding This Slide

Messiah means anointed one. In classical Jewish expectation the messiah is a future human ruler from David’s line who restores justice, peace, and the security of Israel.

Normative Judaism does not identify the messiah as God incarnate. Jewish interpretations vary: some retain a personal messiah, while others emphasize a future messianic age created through divine and human action.

Four Biblical “Hells”

Sheol

Hebrew Bible: the grave or shadowy realm of the dead—not necessarily punishment.

Hades

Greek term often used to translate Sheol; broadly, the realm of the dead.

Gehenna

From the Valley of Hinnom; later imagery of divine judgment, punishment, or purification.

Tartarus

Greek prison of rebellious divine beings; used once in the New Testament, not as a standard Jewish afterlife.

Different words, images, and historical settings—not four identical versions of eternal hell.

Understanding This Slide

Begin with a translation warning. English Bibles sometimes render several different words as “hell,” which makes separate concepts look identical. Sheol is the main Hebrew Bible term and usually describes the destination or condition of the dead without a developed doctrine of eternal punishment. Hades is its common Greek counterpart.

Gehenna develops from the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem and becomes an image of divine judgment in later Jewish and Christian literature. Tartarus belongs to Greek mythic vocabulary and appears once in the New Testament for imprisoned rebellious angels; it is not a normal Jewish destination for human souls.

Resurrection & the World to Come

  • Development: early biblical texts emphasize Sheol; Daniel 12 clearly anticipates resurrection and judgment
  • Rabbinic affirmation: techiyat ha-metim, the revival of the dead, becomes a classical Jewish teaching

Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.

Daniel 12:2 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

The Hebrew Bible does not offer one uniform afterlife doctrine. Daniel 12:2 is the clearest early statement that at least some of the dead will awaken for judgment. The belief becomes important in Pharisaic and rabbinic Judaism, while the Sadducees rejected it.

Techiyat ha-metim means revival of the dead. Classical Jewish liturgy praises God as the one who revives the dead, and Maimonides includes resurrection among his principles. Olam Ha-Ba, the world to come, is not always used with one precise meaning: it can refer to the messianic future, resurrected existence, or postmortem spiritual life.

Daniel 12:2, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 against biblestudytools.com (NRS) and bible.oremus.org (NRSV, © 1989).

Resurrection & the World to Come

The World to Come Today

  • Olam Ha-Ba: “the world to come” may refer to resurrected life, the messianic age, or a spiritual afterlife
  • Modern diversity: Orthodox Judaism generally retains bodily resurrection; liberal interpretations often emphasize immortality or symbolism

Resurrection answers a moral question: will death have the final word over justice?

Understanding This Slide

Modern Jewish movements differ. Orthodox Judaism traditionally affirms bodily resurrection. Conservative or Masorti Jews may understand the liturgical language literally, spiritually, or metaphorically. Many Reform, Reconstructionist, Humanistic, secular, and unaffiliated Jews interpret resurrection symbolically, favor ideas such as spiritual immortality or moral legacy, or simply do not affirm a supernatural afterlife. Avoid describing every non-Orthodox position as an explicit rejection: for many Jews, resurrection is simply not emphasized or is left open. Judaism commonly places greater emphasis on faithful conduct in this life than on detailed maps of the next.

Symbols of Jewish Identity

✡Star of David

A comparatively recent communal emblem; adopted by Zionism and placed on Israel’s flag.

Menorah

Ancient Temple lampstand; signifies sacred light and Israel’s vocation as a light to the nations.

חיChai

Hebrew for “life”; expresses Judaism’s affirmation of life and the toast l’chaim.

Understanding This Slide

The menorah is the oldest of these symbols and comes from the Jerusalem Temple. Distinguish its seven branches from the nine-branched Hanukkah lamp.

The six-pointed Star of David became a widespread Jewish emblem only in the early modern and modern periods; Zionism adopted it in 1897. Chai consists of the Hebrew letters chet and yod and means life. Its numerical value is eighteen, which is why gifts in multiples of eighteen are common.

The menorah icon on the slide is drawn with seven branches, as in the Temple lampstand. (The common menorah emoji, U+1F54E, is the nine-branched Hanukkah lamp, so it is not used here.)

Caution: the textbook (p. 225) says the Hanukkah menorah is used for “the nine days of the festival.” Hanukkah lasts eight days; the ninth branch holds the helper light (shamash).

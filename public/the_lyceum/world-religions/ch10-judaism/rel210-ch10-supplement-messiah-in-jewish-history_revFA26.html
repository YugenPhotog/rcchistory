Messiah in Jewish History
From David’s anointing to the hopes of today
An optional companion to Lecture 2, slides AK–AS
Background image: AI-generated painting of dawn breaking over an ancient walled hill city, the last stars fading from an indigo sky as the sun rises over hills on the right; in the foreground a golden seven-branched lampstand with seven lit flames stands on a stone ledge.

This optional mini-lecture expands slides AK (“Messiah: The Anointed King”) and AS (“Messianic Hope Today”) of Lecture 2. It tells the story of one idea across three thousand years: how a title for Israel’s kings became a hope for the future, and how Jews have argued about that hope ever since.

Keep one thread visible: in Judaism the messiah is a human being, a king from David’s family, and Jews have never agreed on one picture of what he will do or when.

The background (AI-generated, also used on Lecture 2 AJ) shows dawn over a city like Jerusalem with a seven-branched lampstand: a mood image for hope, not a historical scene.

Trail I
Kings & Prophets
How did a title for kings become a hope for the future?

This trail starts with the word itself, then tells the story of David, the promise to his family, and what the prophets hoped for after that family lost its throne.

What “Messiah” Means
The word: Hebrew mashiach, “anointed one”; Greek christos
Anointed with oil: kings, and also priests, were installed in office this way
Even a foreigner: Isaiah calls the Persian king Cyrus “his anointed” (Isa 45:1)
Not yet a future savior: the Hebrew Bible never uses the word for an end-time figure
In the Bible, a “messiah” is a real officeholder God has chosen.

Start with the plain meaning. To anoint is to pour oil on someone’s head as a sign that God has chosen them for an office. Saul and David are anointed as kings; the high priest is called “the anointed priest.” The prophet Isaiah even calls Cyrus of Persia, who let the exiles return home, God’s “anointed.”

Britannica makes the key point for students: the Hebrew Bible “never speaks of an eschatological messiah,” and even the passages about a future ideal king “never use the term messiah.” The future-savior meaning grows later, out of the hopes attached to David’s family.

The Greek translation christos is where the Christian title “Christ” comes from.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Isaiah 45:1, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS): “Thus says the LORD to his anointed, to Cyrus, whose right hand I have grasped.” Leviticus 4:3: “If it is the anointed priest who sins…”
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “messiah” (checked 2026-10-19): from Hebrew mashiaḥ, “anointed”; “The Hebrew Bible (Christian Old Testament) never speaks of an eschatological messiah, and even the ‘messianic’ passages that contain prophecies of a future golden age under an ideal king never use the term messiah”; the Greek christos became the Christian title of Jesus.
David: From Shepherd Boy to King
God rejects Saul: Samuel is sent to Bethlehem, to the sons of Jesse
Not the eldest: seven sons pass by; the youngest is out “keeping the sheep”
Anointed: Samuel pours oil on David “in the presence of his brothers”
King: David later unites the tribes and makes Jerusalem his capital

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “King Saul has disobeyed God, and God sends the old prophet Samuel on a secret errand: ‘Fill your horn with oil and set out; I will send you to Jesse the Bethlehemite, for I have provided for myself a king among his sons.’ Jesse brings out his sons one by one. Each looks like a king; none is chosen. ‘Are all your sons here?’ There is one more, the youngest, out with the sheep. They send for him. ‘Rise and anoint him; for this is the one.’ Samuel pours the oil, and from that day the spirit of the LORD comes upon David.”

The last bullet compresses a long story (1 Samuel 16 to 2 Samuel 5): David fights Goliath, flees from Saul, becomes king of Judah at Hebron, then of all Israel, and takes Jerusalem. Students met the monarchy in Lecture 1.

The Promise to David’s House
The setting: David wants to build God a “house,” a temple
The twist: through the prophet Nathan, God promises to build David a “house,” a dynasty

Your house and your kingdom shall be made sure forever before me; your throne shall be established forever.

2 Samuel 7:16 (NRSV)
This promise is the root of every later hope for a king from David’s line.

2 Samuel 7 is a wordplay on “house.” David, now settled in his palace, wants to build a house (a temple) for God. God answers through Nathan: you will not build me a house; I will build you a house (a royal family). David’s son will build the temple, and “I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever.”

For about four hundred years, kings from David’s family ruled in Jerusalem. The next slide asks what happened to the promise when they stopped.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • 2 Samuel 7:12–13, 7:16, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS): “I will raise up your offspring after you… He shall build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever”; 7:16 as quoted.
When the Throne Fell: The Prophets’ Hope

A shoot shall come out from the stump of Jesse, and a branch shall grow out of his roots.

Isaiah 11:1 (NRSV)
A Just King
Jeremiah: God will “raise up for David a righteous Branch” who will “execute justice” (23:5).
Gathering
Isaiah: he will “gather the dispersed of Judah from the four corners of the earth” (11:12).
Peace
Isaiah: “The wolf shall live with the lamb” (11:6).
586 BCE: Babylon ends the line of kings. The promise becomes a hope for the future.

When Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 586 BCE, the last king of David’s line was taken into exile. No descendant of David ever ruled an independent kingdom again. What do you do with a promise of a throne “forever” when there is no throne?

The prophets answer with hope. Isaiah pictures the family tree of Jesse (David’s father) cut down to a stump, and a new shoot growing from it. Jeremiah promises a righteous “Branch” for David. Ezekiel even says, “My servant David shall be king over them” (37:24). These passages never use the word mashiach, but later Jews read them as describing the coming anointed king.

Many scholars think Isaiah 11 first expressed hope for a king of Isaiah’s own time; later readers heard it as a promise about a future king. Mention this only if students ask.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Isaiah 11:1, 11:6, 11:12; Jeremiah 23:5; Ezekiel 37:24, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS). Jer 23:5: “The days are surely coming, says the LORD, when I will raise up for David a righteous Branch, and he shall reign as king and deal wisely, and shall execute justice and righteousness in the land.”
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “messiah”: “After the Babylonian Exile, Jews’ prophetic vision of a future national restoration … became firmly associated with their return to Israel under a scion of David’s house who would be ‘the Lord’s anointed.’”
Pause & Connect
Why would a promise about a king become a hope about the future?

Look for: the promise said David’s throne would last forever; the throne was lost in 586 BCE; so faith in the promise had to look forward, to a king God would raise up later.

Trail II
Hope under Empire
What did Jews expect under Greek and Roman rule?

From the return from exile to the second century CE, Jews lived under Persian, Greek, Hasmonean, and Roman rule. This trail shows that there was no single “Jewish messianic expectation.”

The background (AI-generated, also used in Lecture 1 X and the Roman Judea mini-lecture) imagines Jerusalem burning in 70 CE.

Many Hopes in the Second Temple Era
A king from David: some texts pray for a son of David to drive out foreign rulers
Two messiahs? a Dead Sea Scroll looks for “the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel”
A heavenly figure: some looked for a “Son of Man” from heaven (from Daniel 7)
No messiah at all: many writings of the period hope for God’s rescue with no messiah

Under foreign rule, and under Jewish rulers many people disliked (the Hasmonean priest-kings, then Herod), hopes for a different kind of king grew. But they took many shapes. Present this slide as variety, not a checklist that every Jew held.

Son of David: the Psalms of Solomon (1st century BCE, written after Pompey took Jerusalem) pray for God to raise up a king, a son of David, to purge Jerusalem of foreigners and rule justly. Paraphrased here; not quoted.

Two messiahs (hedge this): the Community Rule from Qumran (1QS 9:11) speaks of a time “until the prophet comes, and the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel” (translation by Florentino García Martínez). Most scholars read this as two figures, a priestly messiah and a royal one; a minority reads “anointed ones” as groups within the community. Say “many scholars think.”

Son of Man: Britannica notes that in some groups “a heavenly being called the ‘Son of Man’ (the term is derived from the Book of Daniel) would descend to save his people.”

Josephus says that what most drove the revolt of 66 CE was “an ambiguous oracle” that “about that time, one from their country should become governor of the habitable earth”; he claims it really meant Vespasian (Jewish War 6.5.4, Whiston).

The image (AI-generated) imagines Herod, whose rule shows why some Jews longed for a different king.

Bar Kokhba and Rabbi Akiva, 132–135 CE
A leader: Simeon bar Kosiba leads a second revolt against Rome
A verse: Rabbi Akiva reads “a star shall come out of Jacob” (Num 24:17) as Bar Kokhba, “son of the star”
“This is the king messiah”: a colleague answers, “Akiva, grass will grow in your cheeks and he still will not have come”
135 CE: Rome crushes the revolt; later rabbis nickname him bar Koziva, “son of the lie”
Mini-lecture: Roman Judea →

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “Sixty years after the Temple burned, a commander named Simeon bar Kosiba leads Judea in revolt against the emperor Hadrian. The greatest sage of the age, Rabbi Akiva, sees him and recalls a verse from Numbers: ‘a star shall come out of Jacob.’ Akiva says: this is the king messiah. Another sage, Rabbi Yohanan ben Torta, answers: ‘Akiva, grass will grow in your cheeks and he still will not have come.’ In other words, you will be long dead and buried first. The revolt is crushed in 135 CE.”

His followers called him Bar Kokhba, “son of the star.” Later rabbinic tradition punned the other way: Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi read the verse’s “star” (kokhav) as “fraudulent” (kozav).

Pause & Connect
Rabbi Akiva backed a messiah who failed. What lesson might later rabbis draw?

Possible answers: be cautious about naming anyone the messiah; judge a claimant by results, not by enthusiasm; do not stake the whole community on a date or a war. Trail III shows how the rabbis and Maimonides drew exactly these lessons.

Trail III
Rabbis & Maimonides
How did the rabbis teach Jews to wait?

After two failed revolts, the rabbis kept the hope but changed how Jews hold it: wait, pray, keep the Torah, and do not calculate the date.

The Rabbis: Wait, but Don’t Calculate
Don’t Calculate
“May those who calculate the end of days be cursed” (Sanhedrin 97b).
Today, If…
Asked “When?”, the messiah answers “Today”: “if you will listen to his voice” (Sanhedrin 98a).
Same World
Shmuel: the only difference will be freedom from foreign rule (Berakhot 34b).
A Dissent
Rabbi Hillel: there is no messiah to come; he came in Hezekiah’s day (Sanhedrin 99a).
The Talmud keeps the hope and the arguments about it side by side.

These four short Talmud passages show the range of rabbinic views.

Don’t calculate: Rabbi Yonatan curses people who predict the date, because when the date passes and the messiah has not come, people say he will never come. “Rather, the proper behavior is to continue to wait for his coming.”

Today, if: a legend tells of Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi finding the messiah “At the entrance of the city of Rome,” sitting among the sick poor, and asking when he will come. “Today,” he says. When he does not come, Elijah explains: he meant “today, if you will listen to his voice” (Psalm 95:7). The coming depends on how people live.

Same world: Shmuel says that “The only difference between this world and the days of the Messiah is with regard to servitude to foreign kingdoms alone.” Maimonides later adopts this sober view.

A dissent: Rabbi Hillel (a later sage, not the famous Hillel the Elder) says the prophecies were already fulfilled in King Hezekiah’s day. Rav Yosef answers, “May the Master forgive Rabbi Hillel,” and points to Zechariah 9:9, a prophecy from after Hezekiah. The Talmud records the minority view and rejects it.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Babylonian Talmud, William Davidson Edition (Sefaria), all checked 2026-10-19: Sanhedrin 97b (“May those who calculate the end of days be cursed [tippaḥ]”); Sanhedrin 98a (“today, if you will listen to his voice” [Psalms 95:7]); Berakhot 34b (Shmuel, as quoted); Sanhedrin 99a (“Rabbi Hillel says: There is no Messiah coming for the Jewish people, as they already ate from him… during the days of Hezekiah”; “May the Master forgive Rabbi Hillel”).
Hope in Daily Prayer

Speedily cause the sprout of David, Your servant, to flourish… We hope all day for Your deliverance.

Weekday Amidah, the blessing for the kingdom of David (Metsudah translation)

I believe with complete faith in the coming of the Messiah, and even though he may tarry, nevertheless, I yearn every day for his coming.

“I believe” (Ani Ma’amin), principle 12 (Metsudah translation)
The hope is said, not just studied: three times a day in the Amidah.

The Amidah (“standing prayer”) is the core of every Jewish prayer service; on weekdays it is said in the morning, afternoon, and evening. One of its blessings asks God to make “the sprout of David” flourish, using the plant language of Isaiah 11 and Jeremiah 23.

The second text is the twelfth line of Ani Ma’amin (“I believe”), a short creed printed after the morning service in many prayer books. It is a later prose summary of Maimonides’ Thirteen Principles (next slide), not Maimonides’ own words. The phrase “even though he may tarry” echoes Habakkuk 2:3, which the Talmud cites: “Though it tarry, wait for it.”

Many Reform prayer books change these wordings (Trail IV).

We hope all day for Your deliverance.”
  • Weekday Siddur Sefard Linear, Thirteen Principles of Faith, The Metsudah Siddur, trans. Avrohom Davis (1981), Sefaria, checked 2026-10-19: principle 12 as quoted (line breaks removed).
  • Sanhedrin 97b (William Davidson Edition): “Though it tarry, wait for it.”
Maimonides: A Human King, Tested by Results
Principle 12 (c. 1168): believe “that he will come and not to think that he is late”; set no date
No miracles needed: Akiva backed Bar Kokhba, and “The Sages did not ask him for any signs or wonders”
The test: a king from David’s house who keeps the Torah may be presumed the messiah; if he rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles, he “is definitely the Mashiach”
Same world: “the world will continue according to its pattern”; the sages longed to be free for “Torah and wisdom”

Moses Maimonides (1135–1204) is the most influential medieval Jewish thinker. He gives the messiah a place among his Thirteen Principles of faith (in his commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin chapter 10) and then, in his law code the Mishneh Torah (Laws of Kings, chapters 11–12), a sober “job description.”

His approach answers the Bar Kokhba problem. The messiah is a human king, judged by what he achieves. If he fails or is killed, “he surely is not the redeemer promised by the Torah.” Nature does not change: Isaiah’s wolf and lamb are “a metaphor and a parable.” The goal is not power over other nations but freedom to study and serve God.

The date “c. 1168”: Britannica says he began the Mishnah commentary at 23 and completed it at 33; born in 1135, that puts completion about 1168.

Trail IV
False Dawns & Modern Paths
How have Jews lived with the hope since?

Britannica: “Almost every generation had its messianic precursors and pretenders—the best-known case being that of the 17th-century pseudo-messiah Shabbetai Tzevi.” This trail tells that story, then surveys modern Jewish views.

Shabbetai Tzvi, 1665–1666
A mystic: a Kabbalist from Smyrna (now İzmir, Turkey) who claims to be the messiah
A prophet: Nathan of Gaza proclaims him and names 1666 as the year of redemption
Mass excitement: in 1665 the movement spreads to Venice, Amsterdam, Hamburg, London, and North Africa
1666: jailed by the Ottomans and threatened with torture, he converts to Islam
Most followers were devastated; a few insisted even the conversion was part of the plan.

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “In 1665 a young scholar called Nathan of Gaza announces that a moody, brilliant Kabbalist named Shabbetai Tzvi is the messiah. Letters fly across the Jewish world. In Smyrna crowds acclaim him, and the movement spreads as far as Amsterdam, Hamburg, and London. At the start of 1666 Shabbetai goes to Constantinople, and the Ottoman authorities arrest him on arrival. In September he is brought before the sultan at Adrianople. Threatened with torture, he converts to Islam and takes the name Mehmed Efendi.”

Most believers were shattered. A faithful minority explained the conversion as a hidden step in the redemption.

Connect back to Maimonides: by his test, a claimant who fails is not the messiah.

The story told here follows the Britannica article; details beyond it are left out on purpose.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Shabbetai Tzevi” (checked 2026-10-19): born 1626 in Smyrna; “steeped himself in… the Kabbala”; Nathan of Gaza “cited 1666 as the apocalyptic year”; in autumn 1665 in Smyrna “he was wildly acclaimed. His movement spread to Venice, Amsterdam, Hamburg, London, and several other European and North African cities”; “At the beginning of 1666, Shabbetai went to Constantinople and was imprisoned on his arrival”; in September, before the sultan in Adrianople, “having been previously threatened with torture, became converted to Islam”; renamed Mehmed Efendi; “All but his most faithful or self-seeking disciples were disillusioned by his apostasy.”
Reform: From a Messiah to a Messianic Age

We recognize, in the modern era of universal culture of heart and intellect, the approaching of the realization of Israel’s great Messianic hope for the establishment of the kingdom of truth, justice, and peace among all men.

Pittsburgh Platform (1885), article 5
1885: no longer “a nation, but a religious community”: no expected return to Palestine or Temple sacrifice
1999: “we are called to help bring nearer the messianic age” through tikkun olam, repairing the world

Nineteenth-century Reform rabbis in Germany and America reinterpreted the hope. Instead of a personal messiah who restores a Jewish kingdom and the Temple, they spoke of a messianic age of justice and peace for all humanity, which people help bring about.

The 1885 Pittsburgh Platform also rejected a return to Palestine. Reform Judaism later changed that position and came to support Zionism and the State of Israel; the 1999 statement keeps the “messianic age” language.

Britannica sums up the modern pattern: “Modernist movements in Judaism have attempted to maintain the traditional faith in an ultimately redeemed world and a messianic future without insisting on a personal messiah figure.”

Zionism and Redemption: A Debate
Political Zionism
Most early Zionist leaders were secular. They sought a national home through politics and settlement, not a messiah.
Religious Zionism
Many religious Zionists pray for Israel as “the first flowering of our redemption.”
Haredi Opposition
Some ultra-Orthodox Jews hold that only God, through the messiah, may end the exile; they cite the Talmud’s “three oaths.”
Is the State of Israel part of the messianic story, unrelated to it, or premature? Jews disagree.

Political Zionism (Theodor Herzl and others, from the 1890s) was largely a secular national movement responding to antisemitism. Students meet Zionism in the textbook, pp. 233–234 (Reading Guide Question 5).

Religious Zionism sees the return to the land as the beginning of redemption brought about through human effort. The Prayer for the State of Israel, recited in many Modern Orthodox and Religious Zionist synagogues, calls Israel “the first flowering of our redemption.”

Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) opposition: some groups reject the state on religious grounds. They point to a Talmud passage in which God makes Israel swear, among other things, that the Jews “should not ascend to Eretz Yisrael as a wall,” that is, en masse, and should not rebel against “the rule of the nations of the world.” Other Haredi Jews live in Israel and take part in its politics without giving the state religious meaning. Keep this card general; positions vary widely.

Orthodox Hope and Chabad
Orthodox Judaism: keeps the classical hope for a human king from David’s line, prayed for every day
Chabad-Lubavitch: a Hasidic movement whose leader, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902–1994), made the messiah’s coming central
Hastening it: he urged Jews to do more good deeds to bring the redemption nearer
A dispute: some followers proclaimed him the messiah; after his death in 1994 the movement divided over that claim
Most Jews, including most Orthodox Jews, do not accept any claimant as the messiah.

Orthodox Judaism keeps the hope much as Maimonides described it and says it daily in the Amidah and the Ani Ma’amin.

Chabad (also called Lubavitch) is one of the largest Hasidic movements, known for its worldwide outreach. Under the seventh Lubavitcher Rebbe, Menachem Mendel Schneerson, it made the coming of the messiah a constant theme; Schneerson taught that acts of goodness and kindness could bring the redemption. Some followers called him the messiah. His own attitude to the claim is debated by scholars.

After his death in 1994 a split developed. Some members argued the movement should stop talking publicly about him as the messiah; others continued to proclaim it. Do not present all Chabad Jews as holding this belief.

The callout is a fair general statement; do not attach a percentage to it.

A promise to one family became a hope for the world. Which change surprised you most?

Walk the flow as one story: a title for kings, a promise to David’s family, a hope when the throne fell, many hopes under foreign rule, a failed revolt, the rabbis’ caution and Maimonides’ sober test, a false messiah, and today’s range of views.

What does “messiah” mean?
Answer: “Anointed one”: in the Bible, a king or priest installed with oil.
Question 2
Why does the hope center on David?
Answer: God promised David that his throne would be established forever (2 Samuel 7:16).
Question 3
What happened with Bar Kokhba?
Answer: Rabbi Akiva called him the king messiah, but Rome crushed his revolt in 135 CE.
Question 4
How does Reform Judaism describe the hope?
Answer: As a messianic age of justice and peace that people help bring nearer.

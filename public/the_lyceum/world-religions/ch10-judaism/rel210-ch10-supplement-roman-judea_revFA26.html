Roman Judea
How Rome Conquered and Governed Judea
From Pompey (63 BCE) to Bar Kokhba (135 CE)
Background image: AI-generated painting of Jerusalem’s Temple Mount as rebuilt by Herod: a tall, boxy white sanctuary with a gold doorway rises above inner courts on a huge platform with massive stone walls and colonnaded porticoes; smoke rises from the altar, crowds climb a monumental stairway, and a stone fortress stands at the left corner, with the city and hills around.

This optional mini-lecture expands slide W of Lecture 1 (“Roman Rule & Rising Tension”). It tells the story of how an independent Jewish kingdom came under Rome, how Rome ruled it first through local kings and then through its own governors, and why the relationship ended in two failed revolts.

Keep two threads visible. First, Rome ruled through local people whenever it could. Second, Jews disagreed sharply about how to live faithfully under Roman power; there was never one “Jewish response.”

Trail I
From Hasmoneans to Rome
How did an independent Jewish kingdom end up under Rome?

Connect back to Lecture 1 (V and AI): the Maccabean revolt produced the Hasmonean dynasty, an independent Jewish state by 142 BCE. About eighty years later, a quarrel inside that family opens the door to Rome.

The background is an AI-generated imagining of the Maccabean revolt, the Hasmoneans’ founding story.

Brothers at War Invite Rome In
Civil war: two Hasmonean brothers, Hyrcanus II and Aristobulus II, fight over the throne
Appeal to Rome: both ask the Roman general Pompey to settle the dispute
63 BCE: Pompey besieges Jerusalem and takes the Temple Mount after three months
Under Rome: Hyrcanus becomes high priest, and Judea now pays tribute to Rome
Josephus: “now we lost our liberty, and became subject to the Romans.”

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “When Queen Salome Alexandra dies, her two sons both want to rule. Hyrcanus is the older; Aristobulus is the stronger. Their fight turns into a civil war. Meanwhile the most powerful general in the world, Pompey, is in Syria reorganizing the East for Rome. Both brothers send him gifts and ask him to decide. Pompey’s answer is to take Jerusalem himself. Supporters of Aristobulus hold out on the Temple Mount for three months. When Roman soldiers break in, Pompey walks into the Holy of Holies, where only the high priest could go. He takes nothing from the treasury, and the next day he orders the Temple cleaned and the sacrifices restored. But the price is clear: Judea now pays Rome, and its coastal cities go to the new Roman province of Syria.”

Josephus, writing much later, blames the brothers: their quarrel cost the nation its independence. The quotation is from William Whiston’s classic English translation.

Why Rome Used Client Kings
Cheaper
A loyal local ruler kept order without a large Roman army on the spot.
Local Know-How
He knew the language, the leading families, and the religious rules.
Loyalty for Favor
Rome gave titles and land; the ruler kept the peace and stayed loyal.
Easy to Replace
A ruler who failed Rome could be removed—as Herod’s son Archelaus was in 6 CE.
Client rule: a local face on Roman power.

A “client king” ruled his own kingdom but owed his throne to Rome. Rome used this arrangement all around its eastern frontier. It was a practical bargain: Rome got stability and loyalty at low cost; the king got Roman backing against rivals.

Britannica’s description of Herod’s son Antipas sums up the deal: as tetrarch, “he was sovereign within his domain, provided he remained loyal to Rome and maintained peace.”

Present the four cards as the way historians usually explain the system, not as a policy Rome wrote down.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Herod Antipas” (quoted above) and “Herod” (Octavian “knew that he was the one man to rule Palestine as Rome wanted it ruled and confirmed him king. He also restored to Herod the land Cleopatra had taken”; Augustus “twice increased Herod’s territory”).
  • Josephus, Antiquities 17.13.2: “in the tenth year of Archelaus’s government” his brothers and the leading men of Judea and Samaria accused him before Caesar, who banished him.
Trail II
Herod & the Client Kings
Why did Rome rule Judea through a local king?

This trail follows one family, Antipater and his son Herod, from advisers to kings, and then Herod’s kingdom divided among his sons.

Antipater & Herod: Rising with Rome
Antipater: an Idumean adviser who backs Hyrcanus, and then Rome
47 BCE: Julius Caesar makes Antipater procurator; his son Herod governs Galilee
40 BCE: Parthians invade; Herod flees to Rome, where the Senate names him king
37 BCE: with Roman troops Herod takes Jerusalem; he rules until 4 BCE

Tell it as a story (about 2 minutes): “Antipater is a rich, clever adviser from Idumea, the region south of Judea whose people had become Jewish under the Hasmoneans. He always backs the winning Roman. When Julius Caesar wins Rome’s civil war, Caesar rewards him with power in Judea, and Antipater puts his young son Herod in charge of Galilee. Then disaster: in 40 BCE the Parthians, Rome’s great rival, invade and put a Hasmonean prince, Antigonus, on the throne. Herod escapes to Rome. Mark Antony takes up his cause, and the Roman Senate does something Herod had not expected: it names him king of Judea. It takes him three years, and Roman legions, to win his kingdom. In 37 BCE he captures Jerusalem.”

Herod practiced Judaism, but his opponents used his Idumean background against him. Josephus reports that Antigonus called him “an Idumean, i.e. a half Jew.” Britannica: Herod “was a practicing Jew.”

Herod the Builder
The Temple: rebuilt on a vast scale from about 20 BCE, on a doubled Temple Mount
Caesarea: a new port city with a great artificial harbor and a temple to Rome and Augustus
Fortresses: palaces at Masada and Herodium, and the Antonia fortress beside the Temple
Two audiences: grandeur for his Jewish subjects and honor for his Roman patrons

Herod was one of the great builders of the ancient world. The Temple was his showpiece. Its retaining walls still stand, including the Western Wall. Caesarea, on the coast, was a Greek-style city named for Caesar Augustus; it later became the Roman governors’ capital (Trail III). Masada, a desert mesa by the Dead Sea, became a royal fortress-palace; it returns at the end of the story.

Ask: why would one king build both a Jewish Temple and a temple to Rome and Augustus? Because his power rested on both audiences.

Image ideas for later: the Caesarea harbor, or an aerial photograph of Masada.

  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Herod”: he built up Caesarea, Sebaste, Herodium, the Antonia, and “His most grandiose creation was the Temple, which he wholly rebuilt.” Encyclopædia Britannica, “Temple of Jerusalem”: rebuilding “began in 20 bce”; “the area of the Temple Mount was doubled.”
Herod the Feared
Fear of rivals: Herod saw plots everywhere, often fed by his own court
His wife: he had Mariamne, a Hasmonean princess, put to death
His sons: two of her sons were strangled; his eldest, Antipater, was killed five days before Herod died
A verdict: Josephus calls him “a man of great barbarity towards all men equally”
Herod’s reign was magnificent and terrifying at the same time.

Herod married Mariamne partly to link himself to the Hasmonean royal family. That made her family a threat. Over the years he had her grandfather, her brother, Mariamne herself, and their sons killed. In his last days, ill and in pain, he ordered his eldest son Antipater killed.

The Gospel of Matthew tells of Herod ordering the killing of Bethlehem’s infants. Britannica notes that this “is not corroborated by any other contemporary witness.” If students raise it, present it as Matthew’s account, which fits Herod’s reputation.

Both slides are true at once: Herod brought prosperity and great buildings, and he ruled by fear.

  • Josephus, Antiquities 15.7.4–5 (Mariamne’s trial and execution), 16.11.7 (“Alexander also and Aristobulus were brought to Sebaste, by their father’s command, and there strangled”), 17.7.1 (Antipater killed), 17.8.1 (“he died, the fifth day after he had caused Antipater to be slain”; “A man he was of great barbarity towards all men equally”), Whiston translation.
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Herod”: “In the end Herod murdered Mariamne, her two sons, her brother, her grandfather, and her mother”; on Matthew 2, as quoted.
One Kingdom, Three Sons (4 BCE)
Judea
Archelaus
Ethnarch of Judea, Samaria, and Idumea. After complaints about his cruelty, Rome removes him in 6 CE.
Galilee
Antipas
Tetrarch of Galilee and Perea until 39 CE. He builds Tiberias and rules Galilee in the Gospels’ story of Jesus.
The northeast
Philip
Tetrarch of the lands northeast of the Sea of Galilee until his death in 33/34 CE.
Augustus, not Herod’s will, had the final say.

Herod’s will divided the kingdom, but it needed the emperor’s approval. Augustus confirmed a modified version and gave none of the sons the title of king. Archelaus was an “ethnarch” (ruler of a people); Antipas and Philip were “tetrarchs” (rulers of part of a kingdom).

Archelaus lasted about ten years. Antipas was exiled in 39 CE after he and his nephew Agrippa I denounced each other before the emperor Caligula; his lands went to Agrippa. Philip died in the twentieth year of Tiberius (33/34 CE).

Pause & Connect
Was Herod a Jewish king, Rome’s man—or both?

Invite a both-and answer. Herod practiced Judaism and rebuilt the Temple on a grand scale. He also owed his crown to the Roman Senate, built a temple to Rome and Augustus, and was resented by many Jews as an outsider and a tyrant.

Trail III
Province & Governors
What changed when Roman officials ruled Judea directly?

From 6 CE (with a short break under Agrippa I, 41–44 CE), Judea was run by a Roman governor. Josephus calls these governors “procurators”; the early ones held the title “prefect.”

6 CE: Who Ran Judea Now?
Roman Governor
A prefect from the equestrian class, based at Caesarea, with the power of life and death. The legate of Syria could step in.
High Priest
Led the Temple, but Roman governors appointed and removed high priests.
Council (Sanhedrin)
Priests and elders handled local matters; it could not meet without the governor’s consent.
Roman Troops
Auxiliary soldiers kept order. At festivals, troops in the Antonia fortress watched the Temple courts.
Jews ran daily religious life; Rome held the sword and the purse.

When Archelaus was removed in 6 CE, his territory (Judea, Samaria, Idumea) became a Roman province. Its first governor, Coponius, was a Roman knight sent with the power of life and death. The governor normally lived in Herod’s city of Caesarea and came up to Jerusalem at festivals with troops.

The governor controlled the high priesthood: Valerius Gratus, Pilate’s predecessor, deposed and appointed several high priests in a few years, and the high priest’s vestments were kept in the Antonia under Roman control for decades.

Careful: historians debate how the council (Greek synedrion, Sanhedrin) worked in this period. Josephus records that when the high priest Ananus convened it on his own in 62 CE, critics told the incoming governor that it was not lawful “to assemble a sanhedrim without his consent,” and Ananus was removed. Use that as evidence of limits, not as a full description.

Counting and Taxing: The Census of 6 CE
6 CE: Quirinius, the Roman governor of Syria, registers people and property for taxes
Most comply: the high priest Joazar persuades many to accept it
Judas the Galilean resists: his “fourth philosophy” held “that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord”
Paying Rome: tribute on land and people; tolls gathered by local toll collectors
Paying Rome raised a religious question: who is Israel’s true king?

A Roman census was a property count for taxation. For many Jews it was a practical burden. For some it was a religious offense: the land belonged to God, and paying tribute meant accepting a foreign master.

Judas the Galilean founded what Josephus calls a “fourth philosophy” (next to Pharisees, Sadducees, and Essenes). Josephus blames it for the later revolutionary movements, but he wrote after the war and had reasons to blame a small radical group. Present the link with care.

Taxes: Josephus mentions tribute and tolls; the toll collectors (Greek telōnai, “publicans”) appear in Josephus and in the Gospels. Keep the detail general.

The Gospel of Luke links Jesus’ birth to a census under Quirinius. Its date is a well-known historical puzzle; leave it aside here.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.1 (Cyrenius [Quirinius] “came at this time into Syria … to take an account of their substance”; the Jews “took the report of a taxation heinously” but most accepted it “by the persuasion of Joazar”; Judas resisted), 18.1.6 (“the fourth sect of Jewish philosophy, Judas the Galilean was the author … they say that God is to be their only Ruler and Lord”), 18.2.1 (taxings in “the thirty-seventh year of Caesar’s victory over Antony at Actium,” i.e. 6 CE); Jewish War 2.14.4 (“John the publican”), Whiston translation.
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Zealot”: “A census of Galilee ordered by Rome in ad 6” spurred resistance.
Pontius Pilate, 26–36 CE
Standards: Pilate brings the emperor’s images into Jerusalem; protesters bare their necks rather than give in, and he backs down
Aqueduct: he spends Temple money on an aqueduct, and soldiers beat the protesters
Recall: after an attack on Samaritans in 36 CE, the governor of Syria sends him to Rome
In stone: an inscription found at Caesarea calls him “prefect” of Judea
A pattern: offense, protest, then retreat or force.

Tell it as a story: “Pilate’s soldiers march into Jerusalem by night carrying standards with images of the emperor, something earlier governors avoided because Jewish law forbids images. A crowd follows Pilate to Caesarea and protests for days. When he surrounds them with soldiers and swords, they throw themselves down and bare their necks: better to die than break the law. Pilate gives in and removes the images.”

Pilate is best known as the governor who ordered the crucifixion of Jesus (Gospels; Britannica). The Josephus stories show the same pattern of a governor who repeatedly offended local religious sensibilities.

Agrippa I: A Jewish King Again
Who: Herod’s grandson, raised in Rome among the imperial family
41 CE: the emperor Claudius gives him Herod’s whole kingdom, Judea included
Popular: he wins favor with many Jews by honoring Jewish law and the Temple
44 CE: he dies at Caesarea; his son is 17, so Rome sends governors again
For three years Judea had a Jewish king; then Roman rule returned.

Agrippa I was the son of one of the sons Herod had strangled. He grew up in Rome as a friend of the imperial household, ran up huge debts, was jailed by Tiberius, and was rescued by his friend Caligula, who made him a king in the north. Claudius then gave him Judea as well.

His death in 44 CE ended the last period of Jewish royal rule over Judea. Under the governors who followed, tensions rose quickly (Trail IV). Acts 12 calls him “Herod” and describes his death at Caesarea.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Josephus, Antiquities 19.5.1 (Claudius “confirmed that kingdom to Agrippa” and added the rest of Herod’s lands), 19.8.2 (death in “the seventh year of his reign”), 19.9.1 (son “in the seventeenth year of his age”); Jewish War 2.11.6 (Claudius “made the country a Roman province, and sent Cuspius Fadus to be its procurator”), Whiston translation.
  • Encyclopædia Britannica, “Herod Agrippa I”: king of Judaea 41–44 CE; “Because his son was only 17 years old, Judaea once more returned to provincial status.”
Pause & Connect
Which Roman decision would have angered you most—and why?

Let students choose: the census, control of the high priest, images in Jerusalem, Temple money spent by a governor, or troops watching the Temple at festivals. Ask them to say whether the problem was money, religion, honor, or all three.

Trail IV
Revolt & Aftermath
Why did Judea go to war with Rome—twice?

Avoid reducing the revolt to one cause. Imperial taxation and violence, conflict among local elites, revolutionary ideas, and competing visions of faithfulness all contribute.

Many Ways to Be Faithful under Rome
Sadducees
Linked to priestly and wealthy families and the Temple; Josephus says they persuaded “none but the rich.”
Pharisees
Popular teachers of the Law and of ancestral traditions not written in it.
Essenes
Disciplined communities that shared their property.
Revolutionaries
The “fourth philosophy,” and later the Sicarii (“dagger men”) and Zealots, rejected Roman rule.
Most Jews belonged to none of these groups, and how the rebel groups were connected is debated.

Josephus describes three “philosophies” (Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes) and adds a fourth begun by Judas the Galilean. These were small, influential circles, not denominations that every Jew joined.

The Sicarii appear in the 50s CE, killing opponents with hidden daggers at festivals. “Zealots” is the name of a faction in Jerusalem during the war itself. Britannica treats the Sicarii as extremists among the Zealots; many historians keep the groups separate. Do not present them as one organized movement from 6 to 73 CE.

Connect to Lecture 1 AB: after 70 CE, the Pharisees’ heirs, the rabbis, shape Judaism’s future.

The Road to War, 64–66 CE
Bad governors: Josephus blames a run of greedy governors, worst of all Gessius Florus (64–66 CE)
Temple money: Florus takes seventeen talents from the Temple treasury
Crackdown: his soldiers plunder Jerusalem; residents are whipped and crucified
66 CE: priests stop the sacrifices offered for the emperor, “the true beginning of our war”
Money, violence, and religion came together in one crisis.

Tell it as a story: “Florus needs money. He takes seventeen talents from the Temple treasury, claiming the emperor wants it. Some Jerusalemites mock him by passing around a basket to collect coins for the ‘poor’ governor. Florus sends in troops; houses are plundered and people, including women and children, are killed. Then Eleazar, son of a former high priest and the Temple’s captain, persuades the priests to accept no more offerings from foreigners. That means no more sacrifices for the emperor, a public break with Rome.”

Josephus also reports that rebels burned the public archives to destroy the records of debts, which shows a social side to the revolt as well.

Josephus was a participant, first as a rebel commander and then on the Roman side, and he writes to blame a few bad governors and a few radical Jews. Treat his account as important but slanted.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Josephus, Antiquities 20.11.1 (Florus “filled Judea with abundance of miseries”); Jewish War 2.14.6 (“seventeen talents out of the sacred treasure”), 2.14.9 (people “chastised with stripes, and then crucified”), 2.17.2 (Eleazar persuaded the priests “to receive no gift or sacrifice for any foreigner. And this was the true beginning of our war with the Romans”), 2.17.6 (debt records burned), Whiston translation.
  • Gessius Florus, procurator 64–66 CE: Wikipedia, “Gessius Florus,” citing Antiquities 20.11.1 and the Jewish Encyclopedia (1906).
War: Jerusalem Falls, 66–73/74 CE
66 CE: rebels defeat the Syrian governor Cestius Gallus’s army as it retreats
Rome strikes back: Vespasian, then his son Titus, lead the reconquest
70 CE: Titus besieges Jerusalem; the Temple burns and the city falls
73/74 CE: Masada falls; Josephus says 960 people died there

Rome’s first response failed: Cestius Gallus, governor of Syria, marched on Jerusalem, then withdrew and was badly beaten on the way out. Nero then sent Vespasian, who reconquered Galilee (where Josephus surrendered) and much of the country. When Vespasian left to become emperor in 69, his son Titus finished the siege.

Josephus dates the Temple’s burning to the tenth of the month Lous (Av). Jewish tradition remembers the destruction on Tisha B’Av, the ninth of Av, as also the date of the First Temple’s fall.

Masada: Josephus says the defenders, led by Eleazar ben Yair, killed themselves rather than be captured, and that two women and five children survived. The date is debated (73 or 74 CE). Connect to Lecture 1 X.

  • Josephus, Jewish War 2.19.9 (Cestius’s flight), 3.1.2 (Nero chooses Vespasian), 6.4.5 (“the tenth day of the month Lous, [Ab,] upon which it was formerly burnt by the king of Babylon”), 6.10.1 (“thus was Jerusalem taken, in the second year of the reign of Vespasian”), 7.8.1 (Flavius Silva besieges Masada, held by the Sicarii under Eleazar), 7.9.1 (“nine hundred and sixty in number”), Whiston translation.

Epilogue: Bar Kokhba, 132–135 CE
A new city: the emperor Hadrian founds a Roman colony, Aelia Capitolina, on the ruins of Jerusalem
132 CE: Bar Kokhba leads a second great revolt
135 CE: Rome crushes it at Bethar; Christian sources say Jews were then barred from Jerusalem
Causes: debated—the colony, limits on Jewish practice, and a harsh governor are all cited

About sixty years after 70 CE, a second war broke out. Cassius Dio says Hadrian built a new city in place of Jerusalem, named Aelia Capitolina, with a temple to Jupiter on the site of the Temple, and that this caused the war. Other sources point to a ban on circumcision. Britannica lists the governor’s misrule, the colony, and restrictions on Jewish observance together.

The revolt’s leader, known from his own letters as Simeon bar Kosiba, was called Bar Kokhba (“son of the star”). After 135 CE the center of Jewish life moved to Galilee and the diaspora, and the rabbis became its leaders (Lecture 1, AB).

Rome ruled first through kings, then through governors. Which choice made revolt more likely—and why?

Use the flow as a story, not an inevitable march. At each step, people made choices: brothers appealed to Rome; Rome chose Herod; Augustus divided the kingdom; governors offended or exploited; radicals and priests broke with Rome.

Possible answers: direct rule removed a Jewish buffer between Rome and the people; governors were short-term outsiders who often enriched themselves; the census and taxes made Roman control visible; but Herod’s rule was also brutal, so neither system was gentle.

×
Presbyter

Greek presbyteros, “elder.” In the earliest churches a council of elders often led a community together; the English word “priest” comes from it.

Understanding This Slide

Story first: around the year 96, news reached Rome that the church in Corinth had pushed out its elders. The Roman Christians answered with a long letter. It does not come from a pope or even from a named bishop: it opens, “The church of God which sojourns at Rome, to the church of God which sojourns at Corinth.” It urges the Corinthians to restore their presbyters and keep peace. Later writers, starting with Dionysius of Corinth around 170, said Clement wrote it, and Clement was later counted as an early bishop of Rome. The letter shows that Rome’s church already felt responsible for other churches, and that others listened.

This is where the history of the papacy begins: not with an office, but with a reputation. Rome was the capital of the empire, its church was large and wealthy enough to send aid to other churches, and Christians remembered that Peter and Paul had died there under Nero in the 60s. Most historians think that for its first century Rome’s many house churches were led by a group of presbyters rather than a single bishop. Ignatius of Antioch wrote to the Roman church around 110 without mentioning a bishop there. A single leading bishop seems to emerge by the middle of the second century.

Around 180, Irenaeus of Lyon, arguing against Gnostic teachers, listed Rome’s bishops from Linus down to Eleutherius in his own day. His point was that true teaching had been handed down publicly, church by church; Rome was his model because it was the best-known example. In the 190s, Bishop Victor of Rome (c. 189–199) tried to excommunicate the churches of Asia Minor, which celebrated Easter on the 14th of the Jewish month of Nisan rather than on a Sunday. Irenaeus and other bishops rebuked him for breaking communion over a custom. Rome mattered, but it could not simply command.

Rise of the Papacy

Rome’s Bishops Claim Peter’s Authority

In 452 Attila’s Huns were in northern Italy. Leo, bishop of Rome, rode out with two senators to meet him, and the Huns turned back. Romans remembered that their bishop, not their emperor, had faced the enemy.

  • 250s: Stephen I claimed authority as Peter’s successor, the first Roman bishop known to do so; Rome later rested this claim on Matthew 16:18. Cyprian of Carthage rejected his rulings.
  • Late 300s: Damasus I called Rome “the apostolic see.” Siricius issued the earliest surviving papal decretal (385) and called himself papa, “father.”
  • 440–461: Leo the Great taught that Peter spoke through Rome’s bishop, and his Tome shaped the Council of Chalcedon (451). Many historians see him as the first pope in the full sense.
×
Decretal

A letter from the pope answering a question from another church, written as a binding ruling, the way an emperor answered officials. Decretals later became a core part of church (canon) law.

In the East, the bishop of Constantinople, the “New Rome,” made a rival claim.

Understanding This Slide

Story first: in 452 Attila the Hun had already sacked Aquileia and was moving through northern Italy. The emperor stayed behind the marshes at Ravenna. Leo, bishop of Rome, went north with two senators, Avienus and Trygetius, and met Attila near the river Mincio. Attila withdrew. Disease and hunger in his army probably mattered as much as Leo’s words, but the story people told was that Rome’s bishop had saved Italy. Three years later, in 455, Leo met the Vandal king Gaiseric and persuaded him to spare the city from burning and slaughter, though it was still plundered.

How did the bishop of Rome get here? In the 250s, Bishop Stephen I (254–257) quarreled with Cyprian of Carthage over whether people baptized by schismatic groups had to be baptized again. Stephen said no and claimed to speak as the successor of Peter. Our evidence comes from his opponents: Firmilian of Caesarea, writing to Cyprian, complained that Stephen boasted of his see and claimed the succession of Peter, “on whom the foundations of the church were laid.” That is the first known appeal by a Roman bishop to Jesus’ words to Peter in Matthew 16:18. Cyprian and the bishops of North Africa refused to give way.

After Constantine, Rome’s bishops borrowed the language of imperial government. Damasus I (366–384) regularly called Rome “the apostolic see.” His successor Siricius (384–399) answered a list of questions from Bishop Himerius of Tarragona in Spain in 385 with a letter of rulings, the earliest surviving papal decretal, and he is the first Roman bishop known to call himself papa. The word, “father,” was used for other bishops too, especially in Alexandria; only much later did the West reserve it for Rome.

Leo I (440–461) put these pieces together. In his sermons he taught that Peter still acted through his successor in Rome, and he claimed a care for the whole church. His letter on the person of Christ, the Tome (449), was read at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, where bishops reportedly cried out, “Peter has spoken through Leo.” Even so, the same council (canon 28) gave Constantinople, the imperial capital and “New Rome,” rank second only to Rome, a decision Leo protested. The bishop of Constantinople would claim his own leading place in the East. That rival claim is where the Eastern Orthodox story on the next slide (C) begins, and it comes to a head in 1054 (slide D).

The Iconoclasm Controversy

  • Emperor Leo III (from the 720s) commands the destruction of images of Jesus, Mary, and the angels (traditionally linked to Muslim criticism of Christianity; the causes were more complex)
  • The action is condemned by the Pope and the western church
  • An eastern council (754) condemns the use of icons as idolatry
  • The Second Council of Nicaea (787) affirms the use of icons

Icons are venerated, not worshiped; that distinction shaped Orthodox worship ever after.

Understanding This Slide

Story first: picture soldiers on ladders taking down a famous image of Christ over the palace gate in Constantinople while a crowd, many of them women, tries to stop them. Later sources tell a story like this about the start of iconoclasm (“image-breaking”) under Leo III; historians debate it. For over a century, emperors and monks fought over whether a painted face of Christ was a window to God or an idol.

Iconoclasm involved theology, imperial politics, monastic influence, and debates over representation; it should not be reduced to a single reaction to Islam. Iconoclasts rejected sacred images, while icon defenders distinguished veneration from worship.

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 affirmed the veneration of icons while reserving worship for God. Icons became especially important in Orthodox spirituality and liturgy.

Popes Govern Rome, Then Challenge Kings

In January 1077, King Henry IV of Germany stood barefoot in the snow for three days outside the castle of Canossa, asking Pope Gregory VII to lift his excommunication.

  • 590–604: Gregory the Great, a former city prefect turned monk, fed Rome, bargained with Lombard invaders, and sent missionaries to England.
  • 1070s: Gregory VII’s reforms attacked the buying of church offices and married clergy, and claimed that a pope could depose an emperor.
  • Who appoints bishops? The Investiture Controversy ended in a compromise, the Concordat of Worms (1122).
×
Investiture Controversy

The struggle (1075–1122) over who could “invest” a new bishop with his ring and staff. Kings saw bishops as their officials and landholders; reforming popes said only the Church could make a bishop.

Understanding This Slide

Back to Rome: we left the bishop of Rome with Leo the Great in the 400s (slide AH), then followed the eastern churches to the break of 1054 (slides C–D). Now the story of the papacy picks up again in the West, where, with no emperor in Rome, the pope became the city’s protector.

Story first (590): plague had just killed the bishop of Rome, the Tiber had flooded, and Lombard armies controlled much of Italy. The man chosen to replace him, Gregory, had once been prefect of the city, its highest civil official, before giving away his family wealth and becoming a monk. As Pope Gregory I (590–604) he organized grain from the church’s estates to feed the city, paid soldiers, and negotiated with the Lombards, because no one else would. In 596 he sent a monk named Augustine with a group of companions to the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Kent; they landed in 597. Gregory called himself “servant of the servants of God,” a title popes still use. With Gregory, the bishop of Rome was also, in practice, the ruler of Rome.

Story second (1077): five centuries later, kings and nobles across Europe treated bishops and abbots as their own appointees, and church offices were often bought and sold. Pope Gregory VII (1073–1085) led a reform movement against both practices and against married clergy. A list of propositions in his register from 1075, the Dictatus Papae, claimed that the pope alone could depose bishops and even emperors. When King Henry IV of Germany had his bishops declare Gregory deposed in January 1076, Gregory excommunicated Henry and released his subjects from their oaths. With his nobles turning against him, Henry crossed the Alps in winter. On 25–28 January 1077 he stood as a penitent outside the castle of Canossa, where Gregory was staying with Countess Matilda of Tuscany, until Gregory absolved him.

Canossa did not end the conflict; Henry later drove Gregory from Rome, and Gregory died in exile in 1085. The fight over investiture was settled in 1122 by the Concordat of Worms: the Church would elect bishops and give them ring and staff, while the emperor could grant them their lands and be present at elections in Germany. By around 1200, under Innocent III (1198–1216), the papacy was the most powerful institution in Western Europe.

Crisis and Definition: Avignon to Vatican I

1309–1377 · Avignon

The popes lived in Avignon, on the Rhone, under heavy French influence; critics called it the “Babylonian Captivity.”

1378–1417 · Great Western Schism

Two, then three, rival popes. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) ended the schism by electing Martin V.

1870 · Vatican I

Defined papal infallibility; that same year the pope lost Rome to the new Kingdom of Italy.

By 1517 the papacy had recovered its unity, and a new pope needed money to rebuild St. Peter’s.

×
Papal infallibility

Catholic teaching, defined in 1870, that when the pope solemnly defines a doctrine of faith or morals for the whole church, speaking ex cathedra (“from the chair”), he is protected from error. It does not mean the pope never sins or is never wrong.

Background image: A fortress-like Gothic palace with square crenellated towers rises on a rocky hill above a river and a long arched stone bridge, while a pope in a white robe and tall white tiara rides under a canopy, escorted by cardinals in red on horseback.

Understanding This Slide

Story first: in 1309 Pope Clement V, a Frenchman elected in 1305, settled in Avignon instead of violent, faction-torn Rome. His successors stayed and built the enormous Palace of the Popes. The papal court there was efficient and expensive, and it raised money through new fees and taxes on church offices. The Italian poet Petrarch called the period the “Babylonian Captivity,” comparing it to Israel’s exile. Catherine of Siena was among those who urged a return, and Gregory XI moved back to Rome in 1377.

Gregory XI died the next year. Under pressure from a Roman crowd, the cardinals elected an Italian, Urban VI, in April 1378. Within months most of the same cardinals declared that election invalid and chose Clement VII, who returned to Avignon. Western Europe split, kingdom by kingdom, between two popes. A council at Pisa in 1409 tried to fix the problem by electing a third. The Council of Constance (1414–1418) finally removed or accepted the resignations of all three claimants and elected Martin V in November 1417. Constance also declared that a general council held its authority directly from Christ, an idea called conciliarism, and it condemned Jan Hus, a follower of Wycliffe’s ideas (slide T), who was burned in 1415.

Jump forward to 1870. At the First Vatican Council, on 18 July 1870, the bishops approved Pastor Aeternus by 533 votes to 2, defining papal primacy and infallibility as Catholic doctrine; a violent thunderstorm broke over St. Peter’s during the session. Two months later, on 20 September, Italian troops entered Rome, ending the Papal States (slide S), and the council was suspended. The pope had lost his territory but gained a sharper definition of his teaching authority. Vatican II (Lecture 6) later set that authority within the shared authority of all the bishops.

Bring the story back to the next slide: after Constance the popes were again one, and Renaissance popes rebuilt Rome on a grand scale. In 1515 Leo X needed money for St. Peter’s Basilica, which is where the indulgence campaign on slide H begins.

A New Theology: Grace, Faith, Scripture

Luther develops a new understanding of salvation, or reconciliation with God. Catholic tradition teaches that grace initiates and enables human response and transformation; Luther stresses justification by grace through faith, apart from works as merit:

Sola gratia

Salvation is, fully and completely, a work of divine grace.

Sola fide

God’s pardon for guilty sinners is granted to and received through faith alone, excluding all works.

Sola scriptura

Luther claims authority through an appeal to scripture alone.

Background image: A young tonsured Augustinian monk sits bent over an open Bible by candlelight in a stone room, quietly taking notes with a quill.

Understanding This Slide

Luther taught justification by grace through faith apart from works as a basis for merit. Catholic and Orthodox traditions also teach the priority of grace, but describe grace-enabled cooperation and transformation differently.

The three solas on the slide are connected: grace is the source, faith the means of receiving God’s promise, and scripture the final norm for judging doctrine. Later Protestants formulated additional solas.

Story first: Luther the monk confessed for hours and still felt he could never be good enough for God. Reading Paul’s letter to the Romans, he got stuck on the line “The one who is righteous will live by faith” (Romans 1:17). Later in life he remembered the moment he understood it as a gift received by faith, not a standard to earn, and said it felt like walking through open gates into paradise. (Luther told this story in 1545, decades afterward; historians debate exactly when the breakthrough happened.)

Magisterium

The teaching authority of the Roman Catholic Church, especially as exercised by bishops or the Pope.

Understanding This Slide

Catholic theology describes sacraments as effective signs and means of grace, ordinarily celebrated through ordained ministry. Luther retained Baptism and the Eucharist as rites clearly instituted by Christ and attached to a divine promise.

Universal priesthood means all baptized Christians have direct access to God and a vocation of witness and service. It does not mean Lutheran communities abolished trained or ordained ministers.

The original footnote (“*the teaching authority of the Roman Catholic Church, especially as exercised by bishops or the Pope”) is now the popup on “magisterium.”

The Lutheran Church

  • Luther excommunicated in 1521; excommunication and political conflict produce separate evangelical church institutions
  • Luther and his movement were promoted by rulers of several German states
  • Translated the Bible into German, still used today
  • Gave community singing a renewed role
  • Composed hymns and a hymn book
  • Example: A Mighty Fortress Is Our God

Understanding This Slide

Luther initially sought reform within the western church. Excommunication, imperial politics, and support from German rulers produced separate evangelical institutions; Lutheran became the enduring external label.

His German Bible and vernacular hymns expanded lay participation. Printing spread sermons, pamphlets, catechisms, scripture, polemic, and music with unprecedented speed.

Story first: in April 1521, at the Diet (imperial assembly) of Worms, the young emperor Charles V demanded that Luther take back his books. Luther refused unless he was shown wrong from scripture and plain reason. The famous line “Here I stand, I can do no other” appears in early printed versions of the speech but may have been added; treat it as tradition. On the way home, friendly soldiers of his prince staged a kidnapping and hid him in Wartburg Castle, where he translated the New Testament into German (printed 1522).

Original bullet “Gave community singing a renewed role” and the hymn bullets are kept in the right column. A Mighty Fortress (German Ein feste Burg) dates from around 1529 and is based on Psalm 46.

Reformation

Reformed and Anglican Christianity

The Reformation quickly became several reformations

Reformed · Zwingli and Calvin

God’s sovereignty and disciplined communal life; simplified worship centered on Scripture and preaching; spread through Switzerland, France, Scotland, and the Netherlands.

Anglican · Church of England

Political break under Henry VIII; Protestant doctrine develops within episcopal and liturgical continuity; a contested “middle way” between Protestant and Catholic inheritances.

The movements differed over worship, authority, church government, and Christ’s presence in Communion.

Background image: A minister preaches from a tall wooden pulpit in a whitewashed Reformed church while a congregation in dark clothes sits on benches.

Understanding This Slide

Zwingli led reform in Zurich and interpreted Communion primarily as a memorial. Calvin organized reform in Geneva, emphasized God’s sovereignty and Christian vocation, and systematized Reformed theology in the Institutes of the Christian Religion.

Henry VIII’s break with Rome began over royal authority and succession rather than Luther’s theology. Under later monarchs, the Church of England adopted Protestant teachings while retaining bishops, the historic creeds, and a formal liturgy. Anglicanism has always included both more Protestant and more Catholic currents.

The Radical Reformation

Anabaptists argued that reform must reach church membership, power, and everyday life.

Believer’s baptism

Baptism follows a conscious profession of faith, rather than occurring in infancy.

Voluntary church

Membership should not be automatic through birth or citizenship.

Separation from coercive power

Many rejected state control of religion and the use of force in matters of faith.

Discipleship

Pacifism, mutual aid, simplicity, and strict moral accountability characterized important branches.

Mennonites, Amish, and related traditions preserve parts of the Anabaptist inheritance.

Understanding This Slide

“Anabaptist” means “rebaptizer,” a label opponents used because they did not recognize infant baptism as valid. Both Catholic and magisterial Protestant governments persecuted Anabaptists, sometimes by execution.

The movement was diverse. Some groups experimented with communal property; many emphasized nonviolence and church-state separation. Menno Simons helped stabilize a pacifist stream that became the Mennonite tradition. Amish communities emerged later from a division among Swiss and Alsatian Anabaptists.

Story first: on the evening of January 21, 1525, a small group met secretly in the Zurich house of Felix Manz, against the city council’s orders. George Blaurock asked Conrad Grebel to baptize him; Grebel poured water over his head, and Blaurock then baptized the others. Two years later, Zurich executed Manz by drowning him in the Limmat River, the first Anabaptist killed by a Protestant government.

Catholic Counter-Reformation

  • Efforts by the Catholic Church against the Protestant Reformation and toward internal renewal
  • Pope Paul III convenes the Council of Trent in 1545
  • Issues the formal Catholic reply to the doctrinal challenges of the Protestant Reformation

Key Doctrinal Clarifications

Liturgy and sacraments: transubstantiation affirmed.

Church Discipline and Reform

Index of Forbidden Books; new religious orders, significantly the Jesuits.

Background image: Bishops in white mitres and cardinals in red sit in facing rows as a theologian addresses the Council of Trent before a candlelit altar.

Understanding This Slide

Key Doctrinal Clarifications: The Council reaffirmed several core Catholic doctrines that were disputed by Protestant reformers. This included the authority of the Pope, the importance of both Scripture and Church tradition, the doctrine of Original Sin, the necessity of both faith and good works for salvation, and the seven sacraments.
Liturgy and Sacraments: The Council standardized the liturgy and reinforced the significance of the sacraments. It affirmed the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist, directly countering Protestant beliefs like those of Zwingli and Luther.
Church Discipline and Reform: Besides doctrinal issues, the Council addressed abuses within the Church, such as the sale of indulgences and moral laxity among the clergy. It introduced reforms to improve clerical education and discipline, established seminaries for the proper training of priests, and mandated a more pastoral approach to ministry.
The Index of Forbidden Books: Another outcome was the establishment of the Index Librorum Prohibitorum, a list of books forbidden for Catholics to read, which included many works by Protestant reformers.

Precision note: the first Index of Forbidden Books was issued by Pope Paul IV in 1559; Trent prepared the revised “Tridentine Index” published in 1564. The council met in three periods between 1545 and 1563.

Society of Jesus (Jesuits)

  • Founded in 1534 by St. Ignatius of Loyola
  • Swore loyalty to the Pope; dedicated to reconstructing church life and teaching
  • Highly educated; prominent agents of Catholic renewal, scholarship, and education
  • Active in missionary efforts throughout the world, especially South America and Asia: St. Francis Xavier in Asia

Understanding This Slide

Ignatius of Loyola and companions founded the Society of Jesus; papal approval came in 1540. Jesuits combined disciplined spiritual formation, education, scholarship, pastoral work, and global mission.

Their missions involved cultural learning and translation as well as entanglement with European expansion. Francis Xavier worked in India, Southeast Asia, and Japan; later Jesuits such as Matteo Ricci pursued accommodation in China.

Story: Ignatius was a Spanish soldier until a cannonball shattered his leg in 1521. Bored during months of recovery, he read the only books in the castle, a life of Christ and lives of the saints, and decided to become a soldier for God instead.

Reform Prior to Luther: John Wycliffe

  • John Wycliffe (c. 1330–1384), English theologian
  • Promoted the first complete translation of the Bible into English
  • Argued the church should give up its possessions and return to evangelical poverty
  • Attacked doctrines such as transubstantiation
×
Transubstantiation

Catholic teaching that in the Eucharist the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ in their substance, while their appearances remain. See Lecture 3.

Understanding This Slide

Reform did not begin with Luther. John Wycliffe criticized clerical wealth and papal power, promoted scripture in the vernacular, and challenged transubstantiation; followers known as Lollards continued some of his ideas.

Jan Hus in Bohemia drew on related reform currents and was executed in 1415. These movements show that institutional criticism, biblical reform, and calls for evangelical poverty predated 1517.

Wycliffe’s birth year is uncertain (hence “c.”).

Sacred Writings

The New Testament

Twenty-seven writings from the first generations of the Christian movement

4 Gospels

Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John interpret the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus.

Acts

A narrative of the Spirit-guided expansion from Jerusalem toward Rome.

21 Letters

Pauline and general epistles address belief, worship, ethics, and community conflict.

Revelation

Apocalyptic visions promise God’s victory amid imperial oppression.

The canon emerged gradually as communities tested texts by apostolic connection, widespread use, and consistency with accepted faith.

Understanding This Slide

The New Testament is a library, not a single kind of book. Paul's undisputed letters are earlier than the written Gospels. Mark is commonly dated around 70 CE; Matthew and Luke somewhat later; John toward the end of the first century, though precise dates remain debated.

The 27-book canon was not decided at one dramatic meeting. Broad agreement developed over several centuries through worship and theological debate. Other early Christian writings, including the Gospel of Thomas, Shepherd of Hermas, and Didache, help historians study Christian diversity even though they are not in the New Testament canon.

Who Interprets Scripture?

Christians share a Bible but differ over the location of interpretive authority

Catholic

Scripture and apostolic Tradition are interpreted within the Church’s teaching office.

Orthodox

Scripture is read within Holy Tradition, especially councils, liturgy, and the Church Fathers.

Protestant

Sola scriptura makes Scripture the final norm, while traditions still guide interpretation.

No reading is context-free: translation, worship, community, and prior assumptions shape interpretation.

Understanding This Slide

All Christian Bibles include the 27 New Testament books, but Old Testament collections differ. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include books that many Protestants call the Apocrypha; Orthodox canons also vary internally. "Hebrew Bible" and "Old Testament" overlap but are not interchangeable labels in every context.

Sola scriptura does not necessarily mean "me and my Bible alone." Classical Reformers used creeds, teachers, and historical interpretation while denying that any later authority could stand above Scripture. The comparison should illuminate family differences without suggesting that one tradition uses the Bible and others do not.

The Floodgates Open

  • Multiple reformers followed Luther: John Calvin, Huldrych Zwingli
  • Gives rise to multiple denominations: Calvinism, Anglican (Church of England), Anabaptists

“33,000 denominations” is a disputed classification, not a count of Protestant denominations.

Understanding This Slide

Huldrych Zwingli (original note, cleaned up from a pasted outline): Zwingli was born on January 1, 1484, in Wildhaus, Switzerland, and studied at Vienna and Basel, where he absorbed classical and humanist learning. Ordained a Roman Catholic priest in 1506, he was deeply influenced by Erasmus and later by Luther. As a pastor in Zurich he preached the Bible as the sole basis of Christian doctrine and rejected practices he found unscriptural, such as indulgences, the veneration of saints, and clerical celibacy. He worked closely with the city council: church and civic authorities should cooperate, but the church should not be subordinate to the state. Under his influence Zurich removed images from its churches and reshaped the Mass around the sermon and scripture reading. Zwingli and Luther agreed on much but split over the Eucharist: Zwingli saw Christ’s presence as symbolic, while Luther held to the Real Presence. Their meeting at the Marburg Colloquy (1529) failed to resolve this, leaving a lasting division between Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism. Zwingli died in battle in 1531 (at Kappel) during a war between Catholic and Protestant cantons. His work shaped the Swiss Reformation and influenced John Calvin. His approach was more practical and political than Luther’s more theological and spiritual focus.

Where the “33,000” comes from (original note, kept and hedged): the number traces to the two-volume World Christian Encyclopedia (Barrett, Kurian, and Johnson; Oxford University Press, 2nd ed. 2001). As it is usually quoted: “World Christianity consists of 6 major ecclesiastico-cultural blocs, divided into 300 major ecclesiastical traditions, composed of over 33,000 distinct denominations in 238 countries” (Vol. I, p. 16). So the figure covers all of world Christianity, not Protestantism alone.

The WCE’s breakdown, as usually reported: Independents 22,000 denominations; Protestants 9,000; Marginals 1,600; Orthodox 781; Catholics 242; Anglicans 168. The WCE also counts any separately organized body in each country as a “denomination,” so the same church in two countries counts twice.

Verification note: the WCE passage could not be checked against the printed volume; it is quoted from secondary sources that cite Vol. I, p. 16, so treat the wording as reported, not verified.

Sources

Barrett, Kurian, and Johnson, World Christian Encyclopedia, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2001), Vol. I, p. 16, as quoted in secondary sources (not checked against the print edition).

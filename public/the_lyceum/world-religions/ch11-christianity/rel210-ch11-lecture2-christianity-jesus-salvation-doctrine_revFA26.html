Christology

“But who do you say that I am?”

Mark 8:29 (NRSV)

Christology: the branch of Christian theology relating to the person, nature, and role of Jesus Christ.

Understanding This Slide

Christology asks who Jesus is and how his identity relates to his work. Historical Christians have answered through scripture, worship, philosophical language, creeds, councils, and continuing debate.

The topic is not abstract for Christians: claims about Jesus shape prayer, salvation, sacraments, ethics, art, and the boundaries of Christian community.

Story first: retell the Caesarea Philippi scene from the title slide. Peter’s answer, “You are the Messiah,” is the first Christology. Every later creed is a longer attempt to answer the same question.

Divine and Human Nature

Hypostatic Union

Jesus is both fully divine and fully human.

Incarnation

God became human in the person of Jesus Christ.

And the Word became flesh and lived among us, and we have seen his glory, the glory as of a father’s only son, full of grace and truth.

John 1:14 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

The hypostatic union is the later doctrinal formula that one person, Jesus Christ, possesses a fully divine nature and a fully human nature. Chalcedon in 451 described the two natures as united without confusion, change, division, or separation.

Incarnation means the eternal Word or Son truly became human. The claim protects both divine initiative and genuine human experience—birth, suffering, death, and embodied life.

Teaching tip: “incarnation” comes from Latin caro, carnis, “flesh” (as in “chili con carne”). John 1:14 is the classic verse: the Word “became flesh.”

Doctrine

Mary and the Logic of the Incarnation

Debates about Mary were also debates about who Jesus is

Ephesus · 431

Theotokos, “God-bearer,” protects the claim that the one born of Mary is truly divine.

Chalcedon · 451

Jesus Christ is one person in two natures, fully divine and fully human, without confusion or separation.

Across Traditions

Catholics and Orthodox venerate Mary strongly; Protestants generally honor her biblical role with less devotional emphasis.

The Immaculate Conception concerns Mary’s conception without original sin; it is not another name for Jesus’s virgin conception.

Understanding This Slide

The title Theotokos was approved at the Council of Ephesus to clarify Christology: Mary did not give birth merely to a human person later joined to God. Chalcedon then described Christ as one person in two complete natures, divine and human. Oriental Orthodox churches historically rejected the Chalcedonian formula, though modern dialogues have found substantial shared faith behind differing terminology.

Distinguish the virgin conception of Jesus, affirmed in the Apostles' Creed and accepted broadly among traditional Christians, from the Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception, which concerns Mary's own conception. Marian doctrines and devotions vary greatly and should not be treated as identical across Christianity.

Titles of Christ

Christ (Greek) / Messiah (Hebrew)

The Anointed One.

King of the Jews

A spiritual king, from the royal lineage of David.

Savior

A spiritual savior, not the political liberator many hoped for: “he will save his people from their sins” (Matt. 1:21).

Savior of All

The Gentile mission carries the Jesus movement beyond Jewish communal boundaries.

Understanding This Slide

Messiah is Hebrew and Christos is its Greek translation; both mean anointed one. Royal expectations drew on Israel’s kings, but early Christians reinterpreted messiahship through Jesus’ suffering, resurrection, and exaltation.

Savior language is spiritual, moral, bodily, and cosmic across Christian traditions. Avoid implying that all first-century Jews expected one identical political messiah.

The Gentile mission made membership possible without full conversion to Judaism. This did not transform an ethnic religion into a universal one; Judaism already included universal moral claims and welcomed converts.

The key Christian development was the claim that Jesus’ saving work applied to Jews and Gentiles alike, creating communities that crossed inherited boundaries.

Titles of Christ: Son of God

Central to Christianity is the divine sonship of Christ.

The angel said to her, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you; therefore the child to be born will be holy; he will be called Son of God.”

Luke 1:35 (NRSV)

Titles of Christ: I AM

In John’s Gospel, Jesus speaks of himself in words that echo God’s name revealed to Moses.

Jesus said to them, “Very truly, I tell you, before Abraham was, I am.”

John 8:58 (NRSV)

Christians read this as a claim to be the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob; the crowd’s angry reaction (John 8:59) shows they heard it as one.

Understanding This Slide

Story first: the argument in John 8 escalates. The crowd says, in effect, “You are not yet fifty years old, and you have seen Abraham?” (John 8:57). Jesus answers with John 8:58, and they pick up stones to throw at him (8:59).

The original note, kept and made more precise: “The term I Am used here in the Greek is identical with the Septuagint usage in Ex. 3:14 which identifies Jehovah.” More precisely: John’s egō eimi (“I am”) echoes the Septuagint (Greek) translation of Exodus 3:14, where God says egō eimi ho ōn, “I am the one who is.” The words are related rather than identical; many interpreters also hear Isaiah’s “I am he” sayings (e.g. Isaiah 43:10). “Jehovah” is an older English rendering of the divine name YHWH.

Why a Savior? Sin and Justification

Sin

Willful disobedience of God’s commandments.

Separation

Sin cuts one off from the presence of God.

Justification

A Savior is needed to justify humanity to God. Justify: to pardon and clear from guilt; to absolve from guilt and merited punishment.

since all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God;

Romans 3:23 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Christian traditions define sin in several related ways: wrongful acts, distorted desire, damaged relationship, bondage, and a condition that individuals cannot repair by their own effort.

Justification is courtroom language for being declared or made right with God. Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions agree that salvation depends on divine grace but explain justification and transformation differently.

Work of Jesus: Soteriology

In his hometown synagogue, Jesus reads Isaiah: “The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to bring good news to the poor…” Then he rolls up the scroll and says:

“Today this scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing.”

Luke 4:21 (NRSV); compare Mark 14:61–62

Soteriology: theology dealing with salvation, especially as effected by Jesus Christ.

Understanding This Slide

Soteriology is the study of salvation: from what, by whom, through what means, and toward what end human beings are saved. Christian answers include forgiveness, reconciliation, liberation, healing, participation in divine life, and resurrection.

Luke 4 emphasizes good news to the poor and liberation; Mark 14 emphasizes Jesus’ messianic identity. The texts show that salvation cannot be reduced to only one metaphor.

Story first: Luke 4:16–21. Jesus comes home to Nazareth, stands up in the synagogue on the sabbath, is handed the scroll of Isaiah, and reads Isaiah 61. Every eye is on him when he sits down and says the passage is fulfilled “today.” The original slide’s point: Jesus declares himself the looked-for Messiah (Luke 4:18–21 and Mark 14:61–62). In Mark 14 the high priest asks, “Are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed One?” and Jesus answers, “I am.”

Definition check: Merriam-Webster defines soteriology as “theology dealing with salvation especially as effected by Jesus Christ” (the original slide said “branch of theology”).

Work of Jesus: Atonement

  • Atonement: the reconciliation of God and humankind through Jesus Christ
  • Passion (from Latin passio, “suffering”): the suffering and death of Jesus
  • Gethsemane (Luke 22:44) → Crucifixion → Resurrection
  • Christus Victor: Jesus’ death and resurrection triumphed over sin, death, and the devil

Understanding This Slide

Atonement names restored relationship between God and humanity through Christ. The Passion includes Gethsemane, arrest, trial, crucifixion, death, and burial; resurrection is inseparable from Christian interpretations of victory and new life.

Christus Victor is one major model: Christ defeats sin, death, and enslaving powers. Later slides compare it with sacrifice or satisfaction and moral-influence approaches.

Story first: the night before he dies, Jesus prays in the garden of Gethsemane. Luke says, “In his anguish he prayed more earnestly, and his sweat became like great drops of blood falling down on the ground” (Luke 22:44). Note: in the NRSV, Luke 22:43–44 is printed in brackets because some of the oldest manuscripts leave these verses out.

Doctrine

How Does the Cross Save?

Atonement is one doctrine expressed through several overlapping models

Sacrifice / Satisfaction

Christ deals with sin and restores a broken relationship between humanity and God.

Christus Victor

Through death and resurrection, Christ defeats sin, death, and enslaving powers.

Moral Influence

Christ reveals divine love and calls people toward repentance and transformed life.

These are not necessarily competing theories; many Christians combine them.

Understanding This Slide

The New Testament uses multiple images: sacrifice, ransom, reconciliation, victory, justification, adoption, healing, and new creation. Later theologians developed these into more systematic explanations.

Anselm’s satisfaction account and later penal-substitution theories are related but not identical. Christus Victor emphasizes liberation from hostile powers. Moral-influence approaches emphasize the transforming disclosure of God’s love. Present the models as a family of metaphors rather than implying that all Christians must choose only one.

Work of Jesus: Salvation for All Nations

  • A Jewish Jesus movement expands into a translocal, predominantly Gentile church
  • Apostles now commanded to teach Gentiles (Matt. 28:19; compare Matt. 10:5–6)
  • Atonement applies to all who believe, repent, and are baptized

Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit,

Matthew 28:19 (NRSV)

Understanding This Slide

Matthew 28’s Great Commission expresses the post-resurrection mission to all nations, while Matthew 10 reflects an earlier focus on Israel. Acts and Paul’s letters show the debates involved in welcoming Gentiles.

Baptism became the initiation rite into the translocal church. Traditions differ over the relation of belief, repentance, baptism, grace, and salvation, so present this slide as one common pattern rather than a universal formula.

Compare: earlier in Matthew, Jesus sends the Twelve with the instruction “Go nowhere among the Gentiles… but go rather to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (Matt. 10:5–6). After the resurrection the circle widens to “all nations.”

Symbols of Christianity

Visual forms carry compact statements about Jesus, salvation, and Christian identity

Fish · Ichthus

An early symbol; the Greek letters became an acronym for “Jesus Christ, God’s Son, Savior.”

Cross and Crucifix

The empty cross stresses resurrection; the crucifix keeps Jesus’s suffering and sacrifice visible.

Chi-Rho and the Sign

The Chi-Rho combines the first Greek letters of Christ; tracing the cross makes belief a bodily practice.

Understanding This Slide

The fish predates widespread public use of the cross. Ichthus is formed from the initial letters of the Greek phrase commonly translated “Jesus Christ, God’s Son, Savior.” It remains especially popular among Protestants.

Crosses vary by tradition and culture: Latin, Greek, Celtic, Russian Orthodox, and others. The cross was slow to become a dominant public symbol because crucifixion was a shameful Roman execution. By the fourth century, after crucifixion had ceased and Christianity received imperial support, the symbol became more prominent.

Nicaea, 325: Defining Orthodoxy

  • 325: the first ecumenical council meets in Nicaea (the Council of Nicaea)
  • Ecumenical: intended to represent the church across the Roman world
  • Proclaims the Nicene Creed (revised in 381); through the Creed, orthodoxy is proclaimed
  • Creeds define orthodoxy against alternatives increasingly labeled heresy or heterodox

Mini-lecture: From Persecution to Nicaea →

Understanding This Slide

Constantine convened Nicaea in 325 amid a dispute over the Son’s relation to the Father. The council’s creed was expanded at Constantinople in 381 and became the most influential doctrinal statement in Christianity.

Story first: in Alexandria around 318, a popular priest named Arius taught that the Son had a beginning. His bishop, Alexander, disagreed; the quarrel spread across the eastern churches. Constantine, who had just reunited the empire, wanted one church with one voice, so in 325 he called bishops to the lakeside town of Nicaea (in modern Turkey) and attended himself.

When Heresy Became a Crime

  • With the establishment of Christianity as Roman state religion, heresy can be viewed as a crime against the state
  • Note: “heresy” is applied anachronistically to theological theories put forth during this time; it was not at all clear which theories would rise as orthodoxy
  • Some theories are still accepted by different branches of Christianity

“Heresy” is a label the winning side gives afterward.

Understanding This Slide

Imperial involvement changed the stakes of theology. Councils sought unity, but emperors also exiled opponents and treated religious division as a threat to public order. Capital punishment for doctrinal dissent was a later and less common development, not Nicaea’s normal procedure.

Example for the last bullet: the Oriental Orthodox churches (Coptic, Ethiopian, Armenian, Syriac) never accepted Chalcedon’s two-natures formula (slide U).

Nicaea
325→Constantinople
381→Ephesus

  • Additional councils debated them; creedal language was written as a direct reaction

Understanding This Slide

Nicaea did not end controversy. Competing councils, emperors, bishops, and regional churches debated terminology for decades before the Nicene position achieved durable imperial support.

The category heresy is retrospective: the winning position defines the boundary. Teach rejected views as attempts to solve real problems, while explaining why councils judged them inadequate.

Christian Heresy: Arianism

Arianism · Monophysitism · Docetism · Monarchianism

  • Arius taught that the Son was created and therefore not coeternal with the Father
  • Denies the full divinity of Jesus Christ
  • Nicaea answered by affirming that the Son is of the same substance as the Father
  • Enjoyed a brief period of ascendancy before it was denounced as heresy

Understanding This Slide

Arius argued that the Son had a beginning and was not coeternal with the Father. His opponents replied that only one who truly shares God’s being can reveal and save humanity fully.

Nicaea used homoousios—of the same substance—to affirm the Son’s full divinity. Later compromise positions used language resembling homoiousios, of like substance; do not collapse every non-Nicene position into one movement.

Story first: Arius was a gifted communicator; ancient writers say he spread his ideas in popular verses. The slogan his opponents remembered was that of the Son “there was when he was not.” The Creed of Nicaea (325) ended by condemning anyone who said this. Christian Heresy: Monophysite / Miaphysite

Arianism · Monophysitism · Docetism · Monarchianism

How are Christ’s full divinity and full humanity united in one incarnate nature?

Chalcedon (451) answered: one person in two natures. Churches that kept “one united nature” became the Oriental Orthodox.

Understanding This Slide

The fifth-century debate asked how complete divinity and complete humanity are united in Christ. Chalcedon’s two-natures formula was accepted by imperial churches but rejected by communities now called Oriental Orthodox.

Miaphysite is the preferred self-description for many Oriental Orthodox traditions: one united incarnate nature from divinity and humanity. It should not be casually equated with a denial of Christ’s humanity.

Christian Heresy: Docetism

Arianism · Monophysitism · Docetism · Monarchianism

  • Docetism: Jesus Christ, as fully divine, did not have a real or natural body during his life on earth, but only an apparent one
  • Associated with Gnosticism, which held that matter was evil and spirit good, and claimed that salvation was attained only through esoteric knowledge (gnosis)

Understanding This Slide

Docetism comes from a Greek verb meaning to seem. It describes views in which Jesus only appeared to possess an ordinary material body or to suffer physically.

Church writers rejected Docetism because it undermined genuine incarnation, suffering, death, and bodily salvation. Associations with Gnosticism are useful, but ancient Gnostic movements were diverse rather than one system.

Ask the class: if Jesus only seemed to have a body, did he really suffer on the cross? That is exactly why church writers rejected Docetism.

Christian Heresy: Monarchianism

Arianism · Monophysitism · Docetism · Monarchianism

Strongly maintained the essential unity of deity.

Adoptionist

Christ, although of miraculous birth, was a mere man until adopted by God.

Patripassionist (“the Father suffers”)

God the Father came to earth and suffered and died under the appearance of the Son. The Trinity is three manifestations of the single divine being.

Understanding This Slide

Monarchian approaches stressed the unity or monarchy of God. Adoptionism described Jesus as a human specially chosen or adopted by God; modalism treated Father, Son, and Spirit as modes of the one divine being.

Patripassianism was a criticism of some modalist views: if Father and Son are simply the same mode, then the Father suffers on the cross. Later Trinitarian doctrine sought to preserve one God while distinguishing three persons.

Nicene Creed: Reading for the Arguments

Read the Creed; identify phrases that are in direct reaction to the “heresies” just discussed.

“begotten, not made, of one Being with the Father”

is in reaction to Arianism.

“was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary and became truly human”

is in reaction to Docetism.

Understanding This Slide

Thus the line “begotten, not made, of one being with the father” is in reaction to Arianism. The line “was incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, and became truly human.” Was in reaction to docetism.

Read the creed as an argument embedded in worship. Begotten, not made and of one being with the Father answer claims that the Son is a creature; became truly human answers views that deny full embodiment.

Creedal language does not explain every mystery. It establishes boundaries within which later theologians continue to interpret the relation of Father, Son, Spirit, incarnation, church, baptism, resurrection, and judgment.

Common Teachings

After the councils: what do most Christians share?

Background image: AI-generated painting of a candlelit domed baptistery with a gold mosaic ceiling and an arcade of round arches: men in long white robes stand around a sunken octagonal pool of water while a bearded bishop in a gold-embroidered blue vestment and a gold mitre raises his hand toward them.

Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed

  • Most influential of all Christian statements of belief
  • “accepted as authoritative by the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and major Protestant churches”
  • Gives authoritative creedal language to Trinitarian doctrine: the Father and Son are consubstantial
  • Fully developed in the Athanasian Creed

Understanding This Slide

The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed is used liturgically by Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and many Protestant churches. It expands the earlier Nicene statement and gives fuller language about the Holy Spirit and the church.

Consubstantial translates the claim that the Son shares the same divine being as the Father. The Athanasian Creed offers a later western formulation and is not attributed to Athanasius by modern scholars.

Story: it is still recited aloud every Sunday in millions of churches, from Orthodox cathedrals to Lutheran parishes, almost word for word as the bishops wrote it in 381.

Sources

Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Nicene Creed”: “accepted as authoritative by the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and major Protestant churches” (checked 2026-10-19).

Doctrine of the Trinity

  • “Neither the word ‘Trinity’ nor the explicit doctrine appears in the New Testament”
  • Early texts describe Father, Son, and Spirit in diverse ways; later interpretations included Arian and Adoptionist views
  • Doctrine develops while keeping Jewish monotheism and affirming the divinity of Son and Spirit; some early texts present Jesus as subordinate to the Father
  • Formulated in the 4th–5th centuries and codified in the Creeds: monotheism kept by stating three persons of the same substance
  • Uses Greek philosophical terms to clarify claims rooted in scripture and worship

Understanding This Slide

The New Testament contains triadic patterns and high claims about Jesus, but not the later technical formula one essence in three persons. Early Christian texts express the relation of Father, Son, and Spirit in diverse ways.

Fourth-century doctrine sought to preserve monotheism, the full divinity of the Son and Spirit, and real distinction among Father, Son, and Spirit. It developed through scripture, worship, controversy, and philosophical vocabulary—not simply by purging pagan elements.

The original bullet “Jesus subordinate to the Father” is folded into the third bullet as a description of some early texts (it is not the later creedal position, as the original note explains).

Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Trinity”: “Neither the word “Trinity” nor the explicit doctrine appears in the New Testament” (verbatim, checked 2026-10-19).

The Holy Trinity: Father and Son

God the Father

All powerful in heaven and on earth; there is nothing outside his power. A living being, not a force or a cosmic principle.

God the Son

Through him heaven and earth were created. Jesus is the crucified and exalted Lord, and the Son of God. Interpretations of Jesus: the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.

Understanding This Slide

Trinitarian doctrine does not teach three gods or three parts of God. It speaks of one divine being eternally existing as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

The Son is understood as the eternal Word through whom creation exists and as Jesus Christ, crucified and risen. Gospel portraits contribute different emphases without becoming four different Christs.

The original bullet “Created heaven and earth” for the Son is reworded to match the Creed: “through him all things were made.”

The Holy Trinity: Holy Spirit

  • Called Lord, the term also used for the Father and the Son
  • The foundational view of the Spirit is sketched in a section of the Nicene Creed:

We believe in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life, who proceeds from the Father [and the Son]. With the Father and the Son he is worshiped and glorified. He has spoken through the Prophets.

Nicene Creed, ecumenical English text (BCP 1979); [and the Son] = the western filioque

Powerful, uncontrollable, and unpredictable

Understanding This Slide

The Holy Spirit is described as giver of life, active in creation, prophecy, incarnation, the church, sanctification, and spiritual gifts. Calling the Spirit Lord affirms full divinity.

The creed of 381 says the Spirit proceeds from the Father. The western addition and the Son—the filioque—became a major dispute with eastern Christianity. Keep the original and western forms distinct.

Quote check: the original slide omitted “and the Son” without marking it. The BCP text includes it (“who proceeds from the Father and the Son”); the 381 original and Orthodox usage omit it. The brackets show students where the filioque (Lecture 1, Filioque slide) sits.

Scripture, Grace & Reformation

When Christians disagree about salvation, who has authority to interpret scripture and reform the church?

Understanding This Slide

Begin with the New Testament canon and competing models of interpretive authority. Then present the Reformation as interconnected debates about grace, faith, scripture, ministry, sacraments, church institutions, and political power.

About the background (AI-generated image): an imagined printing workshop of the 1500s. Sacred Writings

The New Testament

Twenty-seven writings from the first generations of the Christian movement

4 Gospels

Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John interpret the life, death, and resurrection of Jesus.

Acts

A narrative of the Spirit-guided expansion from Jerusalem toward Rome.

21 Letters

Pauline and general epistles address belief, worship, ethics, and community conflict.

Revelation

Apocalyptic visions promise God’s victory amid imperial oppression.

The canon emerged gradually as communities tested texts by apostolic connection, widespread use, and consistency with accepted faith.

Understanding This Slide

The New Testament is a library, not a single kind of book. Paul's undisputed letters are earlier than the written Gospels. Mark is commonly dated around 70 CE; Matthew and Luke somewhat later; John toward the end of the first century, though precise dates remain debated.

The 27-book canon was not decided at one dramatic meeting. Broad agreement developed over several centuries through worship and theological debate. Other early Christian writings, including the Gospel of Thomas, Shepherd of Hermas, and Didache, help historians study Christian diversity even though they are not in the New Testament canon.

Sacred Writings

Who Interprets Scripture?

Christians share a Bible but differ over the location of interpretive authority

Catholic

Scripture and apostolic Tradition are interpreted within the Church’s teaching office.

Orthodox

Scripture is read within Holy Tradition, especially councils, liturgy, and the Church Fathers.

Protestant

Sola scriptura makes Scripture the final norm, while traditions still guide interpretation.

No reading is context-free: translation, worship, community, and prior assumptions shape interpretation.

Understanding This Slide

All Christian Bibles include the 27 New Testament books, but Old Testament collections differ. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include books that many Protestants call the Apocrypha; Orthodox canons also vary internally. "Hebrew Bible" and "Old Testament" overlap but are not interchangeable labels in every context.

Sola scriptura does not necessarily mean "me and my Bible alone." Classical Reformers used creeds, teachers, and historical interpretation while denying that any later authority could stand above Scripture. The comparison should illuminate family differences without suggesting that one tradition uses the Bible and others do not.

A New Theology: Grace, Faith, Scripture

Luther develops a new understanding of salvation, or reconciliation with God. Catholic tradition teaches that grace initiates and enables human response and transformation; Luther stresses justification by grace through faith, apart from works as merit:

Sola gratia

Salvation is, fully and completely, a work of divine grace.

Sola fide

God’s pardon for guilty sinners is granted to and received through faith alone, excluding all works.

Sola scriptura

Luther claims authority through an appeal to scripture alone.

Understanding This Slide

Luther taught justification by grace through faith apart from works as a basis for merit. Catholic and Orthodox traditions also teach the priority of grace, but describe grace-enabled cooperation and transformation differently.

The three solas on the slide are connected: grace is the source, faith the means of receiving God’s promise, and scripture the final norm for judging doctrine. Later Protestants formulated additional solas.

Story first: Luther the monk confessed for hours and still felt he could never be good enough for God. Reading Paul’s letter to the Romans, he got stuck on the line “The one who is righteous will live by faith” (Romans 1:17). Later in life he remembered the moment he understood it as a gift received by faith, not a standard to earn, and said it felt like walking through open gates into paradise. (Luther told this story in 1545, decades afterward; historians debate exactly when the breakthrough happened.)

Sources

Romans 1:17, NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS): “For in it the righteousness of God is revealed through faith for faith; as it is written, ‘The one who is righteous will live by faith.’”

Luther’s recollection: Preface to the Complete Edition of his Latin Writings (1545); paraphrased, not quoted.

  • Catholic teaching presents sacraments as means of grace, ordinarily entrusted to ordained ministry
  • Luther’s sola gratia / sola fide is a direct attack on the magisterium
  • All baptized Christians share a “universal priesthood”; ordained ministry remains, but is not a separate spiritual class
  • Sacraments limited to the two found in the Bible: Baptism and Eucharist

Magisterium

The teaching authority of the Roman Catholic Church, especially as exercised by bishops or the Pope.

Understanding This Slide

Catholic theology describes sacraments as effective signs and means of grace, ordinarily celebrated through ordained ministry. Luther retained Baptism and the Eucharist as rites clearly instituted by Christ and attached to a divine promise.

Universal priesthood means all baptized Christians have direct access to God and a vocation of witness and service. It does not mean Lutheran communities abolished trained or ordained ministers.

The Lutheran Church

  • Luther excommunicated in 1521; excommunication and political conflict produce separate evangelical church institutions
  • Luther and his movement were promoted by rulers of several German states
  • Translated the Bible into German, still used today
  • Gave community singing a renewed role
  • Composed hymns and a hymn book
  • Example: A Mighty Fortress Is Our God

Understanding This Slide

Luther initially sought reform within the western church. Excommunication, imperial politics, and support from German rulers produced separate evangelical institutions; Lutheran became the enduring external label.

His German Bible and vernacular hymns expanded lay participation. Printing spread sermons, pamphlets, catechisms, scripture, polemic, and music with unprecedented speed.

Story first: in April 1521, at the Diet (imperial assembly) of Worms, the young emperor Charles V demanded that Luther take back his books. Luther refused unless he was shown wrong from scripture and plain reason. The famous line “Here I stand, I can do no other” appears in early printed versions of the speech but may have been added; treat it as tradition. On the way home, friendly soldiers of his prince staged a kidnapping and hid him in Wartburg Castle, where he translated the New Testament into German (printed 1522).

Original bullet “Gave community singing a renewed role” and the hymn bullets are kept in the right column. A Mighty Fortress (German Ein feste Burg) dates from around 1529 and is based on Psalm 46.

The Floodgates Open

  • Multiple reformers followed Luther: John Calvin, Huldrych Zwingli
  • Gives rise to multiple denominations: Calvinism, Anglican (Church of England), Anabaptists

“33,000 denominations” is a disputed classification, not a count of Protestant denominations.

Understanding This Slide

Huldrych Zwingli (original note, cleaned up from a pasted outline): Zwingli was born on January 1, 1484, in Wildhaus, Switzerland, and studied at Vienna and Basel, where he absorbed classical and humanist learning. Ordained a Roman Catholic priest in 1506, he was deeply influenced by Erasmus and later by Luther. As a pastor in Zurich he preached the Bible as the sole basis of Christian doctrine and rejected practices he found unscriptural, such as indulgences, the veneration of saints, and clerical celibacy. He worked closely with the city council: church and civic authorities should cooperate, but the church should not be subordinate to the state. Under his influence Zurich removed images from its churches and reshaped the Mass around the sermon and scripture reading. Zwingli and Luther agreed on much but split over the Eucharist: Zwingli saw Christ’s presence as symbolic, while Luther held to the Real Presence. Their meeting at the Marburg Colloquy (1529) failed to resolve this, leaving a lasting division between Lutheran and Reformed Protestantism. Zwingli died in battle in 1531 (at Kappel) during a war between Catholic and Protestant cantons. His work shaped the Swiss Reformation and influenced John Calvin. His approach was more practical and political than Luther’s more theological and spiritual focus.

Where the “33,000” comes from (original note, kept and hedged): the number traces to the two-volume World Christian Encyclopedia (Barrett, Kurian, and Johnson; Oxford University Press, 2nd ed. 2001). As it is usually quoted: “World Christianity consists of 6 major ecclesiastico-cultural blocs, divided into 300 major ecclesiastical traditions, composed of over 33,000 distinct denominations in 238 countries” (Vol. I, p. 16). So the figure covers all of world Christianity, not Protestantism alone.

The WCE’s breakdown, as usually reported: Independents 22,000 denominations; Protestants 9,000; Marginals 1,600; Orthodox 781; Catholics 242; Anglicans 168. The WCE also counts any separately organized body in each country as a “denomination,” so the same church in two countries counts twice.

Verification note: the WCE passage could not be checked against the printed volume; it is quoted from secondary sources that cite Vol. I, p. 16, so treat the wording as reported, not verified.

Sources

Barrett, Kurian, and Johnson, World Christian Encyclopedia, 2nd ed. (Oxford University Press, 2001), Vol. I, p. 16, as quoted in secondary sources (not checked against the print edition).

Reformation

Reformed and Anglican Christianity

The Reformation quickly became several reformations

Reformed · Zwingli and Calvin

God’s sovereignty and disciplined communal life; simplified worship centered on Scripture and preaching; spread through Switzerland, France, Scotland, and the Netherlands.

Anglican · Church of England

Political break under Henry VIII; Protestant doctrine develops within episcopal and liturgical continuity; a contested “middle way” between Protestant and Catholic inheritances.

The movements differed over worship, authority, church government, and Christ’s presence in Communion.

Understanding This Slide

Zwingli led reform in Zurich and interpreted Communion primarily as a memorial. Calvin organized reform in Geneva, emphasized God’s sovereignty and Christian vocation, and systematized Reformed theology in the Institutes of the Christian Religion.

Henry VIII’s break with Rome began over royal authority and succession rather than Luther’s theology. Under later monarchs, the Church of England adopted Protestant teachings while retaining bishops, the historic creeds, and a formal liturgy. Anglicanism has always included both more Protestant and more Catholic currents.

The Radical Reformation

Anabaptists argued that reform must reach church membership, power, and everyday life.

Believer’s baptism

Baptism follows a conscious profession of faith, rather than occurring in infancy.

Voluntary church

Membership should not be automatic through birth or citizenship.

Separation from coercive power

Many rejected state control of religion and the use of force in matters of faith.

Discipleship

Pacifism, mutual aid, simplicity, and strict moral accountability characterized important branches.

Mennonites, Amish, and related traditions preserve parts of the Anabaptist inheritance.

Understanding This Slide

“Anabaptist” means “rebaptizer,” a label opponents used because they did not recognize infant baptism as valid. Both Catholic and magisterial Protestant governments persecuted Anabaptists, sometimes by execution.

The movement was diverse. Some groups experimented with communal property; many emphasized nonviolence and church-state separation. Menno Simons helped stabilize a pacifist stream that became the Mennonite tradition. Amish communities emerged later from a division among Swiss and Alsatian Anabaptists.

Story first: on the evening of January 21, 1525, a small group met secretly in the Zurich house of Felix Manz, against the city council’s orders. George Blaurock asked Conrad Grebel to baptize him; Grebel poured water over his head, and Blaurock then baptized the others. Two years later, Zurich executed Manz by drowning him in the Limmat River, the first Anabaptist killed by a Protestant government.

About the image (AI-generated): an adult baptism by pouring, by candlelight, in a plain farmhouse room. It echoes the first baptism of January 1525, held secretly in a house in Zurich, but the room and the people are imagined.

Sources

Global Anabaptist Mennonite Encyclopedia Online (GAMEO), articles “Grebel, Conrad” and “Manz, Felix” (standard accounts of the January 21, 1525 baptism and Manz’s execution on January 5, 1527).

Catholic Counter-Reformation

  • Efforts by the Catholic Church against the Protestant Reformation and toward internal renewal
  • Pope Paul III convenes the Council of Trent in 1545
  • Issues the formal Catholic reply to the doctrinal challenges of the Protestant Reformation

Key Doctrinal Clarifications

Liturgy and sacraments: transubstantiation affirmed.

Church Discipline and Reform

Index of Forbidden Books; new religious orders, significantly the Jesuits.

Understanding This Slide

Key Doctrinal Clarifications: The Council reaffirmed several core Catholic doctrines that were disputed by Protestant reformers. This included the authority of the Pope, the importance of both Scripture and Church tradition, the doctrine of Original Sin, the necessity of both faith and good works for salvation, and the seven sacraments.
Liturgy and Sacraments: The Council standardized the liturgy and reinforced the significance of the sacraments. It affirmed the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist, directly countering Protestant beliefs like those of Zwingli and Luther.
Church Discipline and Reform: Besides doctrinal issues, the Council addressed abuses within the Church, such as the sale of indulgences and moral laxity among the clergy. It introduced reforms to improve clerical education and discipline, established seminaries for the proper training of priests, and mandated a more pastoral approach to ministry.
The Index of Forbidden Books: Another outcome was the establishment of the Index Librorum Prohibitorum, a list of books forbidden for Catholics to read, which included many works by Protestant reformers.

Precision note: the first Index of Forbidden Books was issued by Pope Paul IV in 1559; Trent prepared the revised “Tridentine Index” published in 1564. The council met in three periods between 1545 and 1563.

Society of Jesus (Jesuits)

  • Founded in 1534 by St. Ignatius of Loyola
  • Swore loyalty to the Pope; dedicated to reconstructing church life and teaching
  • Highly educated; prominent agents of Catholic renewal, scholarship, and education
  • Active in missionary efforts throughout the world, especially South America and Asia: St. Francis Xavier in Asia

Understanding This Slide

Ignatius of Loyola and companions founded the Society of Jesus; papal approval came in 1540. Jesuits combined disciplined spiritual formation, education, scholarship, pastoral work, and global mission.

Their missions involved cultural learning and translation as well as entanglement with European expansion. Francis Xavier worked in India, Southeast Asia, and Japan; later Jesuits such as Matteo Ricci pursued accommodation in China.

Story: Ignatius was a Spanish soldier until a cannonball shattered his leg in 1521. Bored during months of recovery, he read the only books in the castle, a life of Christ and lives of the saints, and decided to become a soldier for God instead.

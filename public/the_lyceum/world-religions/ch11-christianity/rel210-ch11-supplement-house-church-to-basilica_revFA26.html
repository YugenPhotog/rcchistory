Trail I
The Dining Room
What did the first Christians do when they met in homes?
Background image: AI-generated painting of an early Christian gathering in a plain room of a house at night: men, women, and two small children sit on floor cushions around a low table holding a plate of bread, clay cups, and a single small oil lamp, with a dark doorway behind them.

For about 250 years there were almost no church buildings. Christians met in houses, often a wealthier member’s home.

Background: AI-generated imagined house church.

Trail I · 30s–50s CE
“The Church in Their House”
In the New Testament, “church” means people, not a building

Day by day, as they spent much time together in the temple, they broke bread at home and ate their food with glad and generous hearts,

Acts 2:46 (NRSV)

Greet also the church in their house.

Romans 16:5 (NRSV), about Prisca and Aquila
The Greek ekklēsia means “assembly”: the gathered people.

Tell it as a story: Prisca and Aquila were a married couple of tentmakers who worked with Paul. Wherever they lived (Corinth, Ephesus, Rome), their house became a meeting place; Paul sends greetings to “the church in their house” (Romans 16:5; compare 1 Corinthians 16:19).

Picture a Roman dining room that held perhaps a few dozen people. The meal was a real meal: in 1 Corinthians 11 Paul scolds the Corinthians because the rich ate first while the poor went hungry.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Acts 2:46; Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; NRSV (1989), checked 2026-10-19 on biblestudytools.com (NRS).
Trail I · c. 100 CE
A Prayer over Broken Bread
The Didache (“Teaching”), an early church manual

Even as this broken bread was scattered over the hills, and was gathered together and became one, so let Your Church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into Your kingdom

Didache 9
  • Instructions for baptism, fasting, and the thanksgiving meal (Eucharist, “thanksgiving”)
  • Only the baptized may share the Eucharist (Didache 9)

Tell it as a story: the Didache was lost for centuries and rediscovered in 1873 in a library in Constantinople, in a manuscript copied in 1056. It reads like a handbook for a small community: how to baptize (in running water if possible), when to fast, and what to pray over the cup and bread.

The image of grain scattered on hillsides and gathered into one loaf is a picture of the church: scattered people made one. Connect to Lecture 3, slide AD (Baptism and Eucharist).

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Didache 9, New Advent (Ante-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim. Rediscovery (1873, Bryennios; manuscript dated 1056): standard account, not re-checked this session.
Trail I · c. 155 CE
A Sunday Gathering
Justin Martyr explains Christian worship to the emperor

on the day called Sunday, all who live in cities or in the country gather together to one place, and the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time permits

Justin Martyr, First Apology 67

Read

Scripture: the “memoirs of the apostles” (the Gospels) and the prophets.

Preach & Pray

The leader “instructs, and exhorts”; then all stand and pray.

Share

Bread, wine, and water are brought; the wealthy help the needy.

Tell it as a story: Justin was a philosopher who became a Christian and wrote an open letter to Emperor Antoninus Pius defending Christians against rumors (cannibalism, secret crimes). To prove they had nothing to hide, he simply described a Sunday service. He was later executed in Rome (about 165).

Students will recognize the order: readings, sermon, prayers, communion, offering. Most churches still follow it.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Justin Martyr, First Apology 67, New Advent (Ante-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim.
Pause & Connect
Pliny saw a hymn before dawn and a shared meal; Justin described readings, a sermon, prayers, and bread and wine. What stayed the same, and what was added?

Look for: a fixed day, singing or prayer, a shared meal or Eucharist, care for the poor. Added by Justin’s time: Sunday, scripture readings, a sermon by a “president.” See the Persecution to Nicaea mini-lecture, slide D, for Pliny.

Trail II
The Remodeled House
What does the oldest known church building look like?
Background image: AI-generated painting of a narrow catacomb corridor cut into rock, burial niches cut into its walls; a plastered panel shows painted symbols of a fish, an anchor, and a dove with an olive twig, while four people in Roman tunics and mantles walk away down the passage holding small clay oil lamps.

Third century: some communities owned property and remodeled houses for worship. Catacombs also appear in this period, but as cemeteries.

Trail II · c. 240s–256 CE
Dura-Europos: The Oldest Known House Church
A Roman frontier town on the Euphrates River, in modern Syria
  • An ordinary house with a courtyard was remodeled for Christian use: two rooms knocked into one meeting hall
  • A small room became a baptistery, with a basin under a canopy and painted walls: the Good Shepherd, the women at the tomb, Jesus walking on water
  • In 256 the town buried the street along its wall under earth to strengthen it against a Persian siege, which preserved the house; the town fell anyway
Found by Yale and French archaeologists in the early 1930s; the baptistery paintings are now at the Yale University Art Gallery.

Tell it as a story: Dura-Europos was a Roman garrison town that was abandoned after the Persians captured it around 256 and never rebuilt. Because the defenders had packed earth against the inside of the city wall, the buildings along it, including a church, a synagogue (see the Ch10 Temple to Synagogue mini-lecture), and temples, survived almost untouched for 1,700 years.

Hedge on dates: the house was built earlier; it was remodeled as a church around the 240s (a date often given is 232/233, from an inscription in the house, but scholars debate exactly when the remodeling happened).

The point for students: even the oldest church building we know is a house.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Dura-Europus”; Yale University Art Gallery, Dura-Europos collection: standard accounts; dates hedged (not re-checked line by line this session).
Trail II · Myth Check
Did Christians Hide in the Catacombs?
The catacombs of Rome: miles of underground cemeteries

What they were

Burial galleries dug into soft rock outside the city, since Roman law banned burial inside it.

What happened there

Funerals, memorial meals, and visits to martyrs’ graves, decorated with fish, anchors, doves, and the Good Shepherd.

What did not

They were not secret hideouts or regular Sunday churches; that picture comes mainly from later legends and novels.

A favorite student myth. The Roman authorities knew exactly where the cemeteries were; burial grounds were legally protected, and gatherings there were not secret.

The symbols are the ones on Lecture 2, slide O (Symbols of Christianity). The AI image on Lecture 1, slide AB and on this mini-lecture’s Trail II divider shows a catacomb.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Encyclopaedia Britannica, “catacomb”: standard account of the Roman catacombs as burial places; the “hiding place” idea is described by historians as a later legend (paraphrase, hedged).
Trail III
The Basilica
What changed when the emperor started building churches?
Background image: AI-generated painting of the interior of an early basilica: rows of marble columns with carved capitals line the nave under a timber roof, a gold apse mosaic shows Christ enthroned among standing saints, and a crowd of worshippers in late-Roman tunics and colorful mantles stands facing the altar.

After 313, Constantine and his family paid for huge churches in Rome, Constantinople, and the Holy Land.

Background (AI-generated, also on Lecture 1, slide Z): an imagined early basilica with a columned nave and a gold apse mosaic of Christ enthroned among saints; the building and people are invented.

Trail III · After 313 CE
A Law Court Becomes a Church
The basilica: a Roman public hall, borrowed for worship
  • A Roman basilica was a long hall with columns, used for law courts and business, with a raised platform at one end
  • Christians did not want temples (those were for statues of gods), so they borrowed the hall: room for thousands
  • Constantine’s churches: the Lateran in Rome (begun c. 313), Old St. Peter’s over Peter’s grave (begun c. 320s), the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem (dedicated 335)
The bishop sat where the Roman judge had sat; the altar stood in front of him.

Tell it as a story: imagine a congregation that had met in a dining room for thirty people walking into Constantine’s Lateran basilica, with gold, marble, and space for a crowd. That is the change Lecture 3, slide W describes.

Dates are approximate and given with “c.”; Old St. Peter’s was replaced by the present St. Peter’s in the 1500s–1600s.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Encyclopaedia Britannica, “basilica,” “Saint John Lateran,” “Old Saint Peter’s Basilica,” “Church of the Holy Sepulchre”: standard dates, given as approximate (not re-checked line by line this session).
Trail III · What Changed, What Stayed
Bigger Rooms, the Same Meal
Lecture 3, slide W, in one picture

Changed

Scale (thousands, not dozens); clergy in robes modeled on Roman officials’ dress; processions, choirs, and a yearly calendar; worship became public and connected to civic life.

Stayed

Bread and cup; scripture read and preached; prayer; baptism; care for the poor; Sunday.

House churches, chapels, and monasteries continued alongside the great basilicas.

This summarizes the original Lecture 3 notes for slide W (“Large buildings changed scale and social relationships but did not simply erase intimacy”).

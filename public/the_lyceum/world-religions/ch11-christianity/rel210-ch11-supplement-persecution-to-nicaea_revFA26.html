How a hunted movement ended up writing a creed with an emperor in the room

This optional mini-lecture expands Lecture 1, slides AB (“Persecution and Martyrs”), AE (“Constantine at the Milvian Bridge”), and AF (“From Toleration to Imperial Religion”), and leads into Lecture 2, Section II (Nicaea and the creeds). It tells one story across about 270 years: from a governor who did not know what to do with Christians (c. 112) to an emperor who made Nicene Christianity the official faith (380).

Story before analysis: each trail opens with a person (Pliny, Perpetua, Constantine, Arius).

Trail I
Suspects & Martyrs
Why did Rome punish Christians, and why did it not work?
Background image: AI-generated painting of a narrow catacomb corridor cut into rock, burial niches cut into its walls; a plastered panel shows painted symbols of a fish, an anchor, and a dove with an olive twig, while four people in Roman tunics and mantles walk away down the passage holding small clay oil lamps.

This trail covers about a century of local, on-and-off persecution: Pliny and Trajan (c. 112), Perpetua (203), and Tertullian’s famous boast.

Background (AI-generated, also on Lecture 1, slide AB): a Roman catacomb lit by clay lamps. Catacombs were burial places, not hideouts; see the House Church to Basilica mini-lecture.

Trail I · c. 112 CE
A Governor Writes for Advice
Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus (northern Turkey), to the emperor Trajan
  • People were being accused of being Christians, and Pliny was unsure what the crime even was
  • He asked each suspect three times; those who kept saying “yes” were executed for their “stubbornness”
  • What he found out about their meetings:

they were accustomed to meet on a fixed day before dawn and sing responsively a hymn to Christ as to a god

Pliny, Letters 10.96
Trajan’s answer: “They are not to be sought out”; punish them only if accused and proven (Letters 10.97).

Tell it as a story: Pliny is a careful bureaucrat in a province far from Rome. Anonymous lists of names are showing up; neighbors are accusing neighbors. He writes to the emperor because he honestly does not know whether the crime is the name “Christian” itself or some crime connected with it. He has two enslaved women “who were called deaconesses” questioned under torture and finds only “depraved, excessive superstition” (do not dwell on the torture with students; mention it only if asked).

Point out what this outsider tells us: Christians met before dawn on a fixed day, sang to Christ “as to a god,” took an oath not to steal or commit adultery, and later shared “ordinary and innocent food.” This is one of the earliest non-Christian descriptions of Christian worship.

Trajan’s reply set the pattern for the next 150 years: no hunting for Christians, no anonymous accusations, but punishment if someone is accused and refuses to sacrifice to the gods. That is why persecution in this period was local and sporadic, not empire-wide.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Pliny, Letters 10.96–97, English translation on Fordham University’s Internet History Sourcebooks (sourcebooks.fordham.edu/source/pliny1.asp); quotations verbatim from that translation.
Trail I · 203 CE
Perpetua’s Prison Diary
Carthage, North Africa: a young mother writes down her own story
  • Vibia Perpetua, about 22, well-born, with a baby son, is arrested with other new Christians, including the enslaved woman Felicity
  • Her father begs her to give up the name; she points to a water jug:

‘Can it be called by any other name than what it is?’ And he said, ‘No.’ ‘Neither can I call myself anything else than what I am, a Christian.’

The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity 1.2
One of the earliest surviving texts written by a Christian woman.

Tell it as a story: Perpetua kept a diary in prison. She worries about her baby, who is brought to nurse; she has vivid dreams of climbing a ladder to a garden. An editor finished the account after her death in the arena at Carthage in 203. Keep the ending brief and non-graphic: she and her companions were executed at public games.

Why it matters for Lecture 1, slide AB: martyrs (Greek martys, “witness”) were remembered by name, and their stories were read aloud in church. Persecution produced heroes, and heroes produced converts.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • The Passion of the Holy Martyrs Perpetua and Felicitas 1.2, New Advent (Ante-Nicene / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim.
Trail I · c. 197 CE
The Blood of Christians Is Seed
Tertullian of Carthage answers the persecutors

The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.

Tertullian, Apology 50

Courage

Onlookers saw ordinary people, women and enslaved people too, refuse to give in.

Community

Churches cared for prisoners, widows, and the poor, which outsiders noticed.

Limits

Persecution was local and occasional, so most Christians were never arrested.

This is the quotation on Lecture 1, slide AB. Tertullian wrote it a few years before Perpetua died in his own city.

The three cards are historians’ common explanations for why persecution failed. The third matters: before 250, there was no empire-wide campaign, so the church could keep growing between local outbreaks.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Tertullian, Apology 50, New Advent (Ante-Nicene / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim.
Pause & Connect
Pliny, Perpetua, and Tertullian all saw the same thing from different sides. Why did persecution make the movement stronger instead of weaker?

Look for: martyrs as public witnesses; stories retold in church; community care; local rather than empire-wide persecution (Trajan’s rule).

Trail II
The Great Persecution & Constantine
How did the worst persecution end with a Christian emperor?
Background image: AI-generated painting of two Roman soldiers seen from behind, in segmented plate armor, dark red cloaks, and gold-crested helmets, carrying oval dark-blue shields painted with a gold Chi-Rho, as they look across a river toward a long arched stone bridge at dusk under an indigo and gold sky.

From 303 to 313 the empire tried, for the first time, to wipe out the church everywhere at once. Ten years later a Christian-friendly emperor ruled the West.

Background (AI-generated): soldiers with the Chi-Rho on their shields (Lactantius, Deaths of the Persecutors 44).

Trail II · 303 CE
The Great Persecution
Emperor Diocletian tries to end Christianity across the whole empire
  • February 23, 303: at dawn, officials force open the church in Nicomedia, across from the imperial palace; they burn the scriptures and tear the building down
  • The next day: an edict strips Christians of rank and legal rights; later edicts target clergy and order sacrifice to the gods
  • For about ten years, worst in the East, churches were destroyed, books burned, and many Christians killed
The persecution ended in failure: in 311 the emperor Galerius, its strongest supporter, issued an edict of toleration.

Tell it as a story: Lactantius, a Christian teacher of Latin who lived in Nicomedia, says Diocletian and his junior emperor Galerius watched from the palace as soldiers broke into the church, which stood in plain view on rising ground. They argued over whether to set it on fire and decided against it only because the fire might spread to the city. Lactantius chose the date on purpose: it was the feast of Terminus, the god of boundaries, as if to “terminate” Christianity.

The edict of toleration of 311 is also preserved by Lactantius (Deaths of the Persecutors 34). Keep the violence general with students.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors 12–13 (church at Nicomedia; edict), 34 (Galerius’ edict, 311), New Advent (Ante-Nicene / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19. The date (“the festival of the god Terminus… the sevens of the kalends of March”, i.e. February 23) is from chapter 12.
Trail II · October 28, 312
A Sign Before the Battle
Constantine marches on Rome against his rival Maxentius

he saw with his own eyes the trophy of a cross of light in the heavens, above the sun, and bearing the inscription, Conquer by this.

Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.28, from what Constantine told him “long afterwards”

Constantine was directed in a dream to cause the heavenly sign to be delineated on the shields of his soldiers

Lactantius, Deaths of the Persecutors 44, written soon after
Two Christian writers, two versions: a vision in the sky, or a dream. Both agree the sign went on the shields, and Constantine won.

Tell it as a story: Maxentius held Rome; Constantine had the smaller army. Lactantius says Constantine was told in a dream to mark his soldiers’ shields with Christ’s sign (the Chi-Rho). Eusebius, writing decades later, says Constantine swore to him that he saw a cross of light in the sky with the words “Conquer by this” (the Latin in hoc signo vinces is a later tradition). Maxentius’ army crossed the Tiber and was driven back across the Milvian Bridge; Maxentius drowned.

Ask students why the two accounts differ, and which they would trust more: the one written soon after, or the one claiming to come from the emperor himself?

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Eusebius, Life of Constantine 1.28; Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors 44; New Advent (Ante-Nicene / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim.
Trail II · 313 CE
The “Edict of Milan”
Constantine and his fellow emperor Licinius agree on religious freedom

it was proper that the Christians and all others should have liberty to follow that mode of religion which to each of them appeared best

Letter of Constantine and Licinius, as preserved by Lactantius, Deaths of the Persecutors 48
  • Freedom of worship for everyone, not only Christians
  • Confiscated churches and property returned to the Christians
  • Not really an “edict” and not issued at Milan: the emperors met in Milan, and Licinius posted the letter in Nicomedia in June 313

This expands Lecture 1, slide AF (“313 Edict of Milan: legal toleration and restoration of Christian property”).

Irony worth pointing out: the text that ended the persecution was posted in Nicomedia, the same city where the Great Persecution began ten years earlier.

The textbook-caution file notes that the textbook says Christianity was “tolerated in 312”; the agreement is usually dated 313.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors 48, New Advent (Ante-Nicene / Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translations), newadvent.org, checked 2026-10-19; quoted verbatim.
Pause & Connect
In ten years Christians went from having their churches torn down to having them given back. What changed: the Christians, or the emperors?

Look for: the change was political (a new emperor and civil war), not a change in Christian belief; Constantine hoped the Christian God would protect the empire; the letter of 313 granted freedom to all religions.

Trail III
One Empire, One Creed?
Why did the emperor care what bishops believed about the Son?
Background image: AI-generated painting of a council in a columned hall hung with purple drapery: a young, clean-shaven emperor wearing a gold laurel wreath and purple robes sits on a raised throne and gestures as he speaks, while bearded bishops in dark blue and gold robes and caps sit on either side, some reaching toward him in debate.

Constantine now wanted a united church to support a united empire. Instead he found the bishops arguing.

Trail III · 318–325 CE
A Quarrel in Alexandria Goes Imperial
The priest Arius versus Bishop Alexander, and the Council of Nicaea

Arius

The Son is God’s first and greatest creation, but had a beginning: “there was when he was not.”

Alexander (and Athanasius)

The Son is eternal and truly God; only God can save.

Nicaea, 325

Constantine calls the bishops to Nicaea; the creed says the Son is homoousios, “of the same being,” as the Father.

Arius was exiled, but the argument went on for another fifty years.

Tell it as a story: Arius was a popular priest in Alexandria, and ancient writers say his ideas spread through catchy verses. His bishop, Alexander, condemned him; Alexander’s young assistant Athanasius later became the great defender of Nicaea and was exiled five times for it. Constantine first sent a letter telling both sides the quarrel was trivial. When that failed, he summoned bishops (later tradition says 318) to Nicaea in 325 and attended in person.

The creed of 325 ended by condemning those who said of the Son “there was when he was not” (Lecture 2, slide T). The word homoousios was controversial because it is not in the Bible.

Lecture 2, slides Q–X, cover the results: orthodoxy, heresy, and the creed.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Creed of Nicaea (325), closing anathemas: “there was when he was not” (standard English rendering; wording varies by translation). Number of bishops: traditionally 318; ancient estimates vary (about 250–300). Athanasius’ five exiles: standard account (e.g., Britannica, “Saint Athanasius”), not re-checked this session.
Trail III · 380–381 CE
From Tolerated to Official
Theodosius I and the Council of Constantinople
  • After Nicaea, emperors who favored Arian-leaning bishops ruled for decades; Nicene bishops like Athanasius were exiled
  • 380: Theodosius I orders all his peoples to hold the faith of the bishops of Rome and Alexandria, the Nicene faith; others are labeled heretics
  • 381: the Council of Constantinople expands the Nicene Creed, adding more about the Holy Spirit
In under 80 years: from a church torn down in Nicomedia (303) to the empire’s only approved faith (380).

This is the last node of Lecture 1, slide AF (“380 Theodosius I makes Nicene Christianity the empire’s official religion”) and leads to Lecture 2, slide Z (Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed).

The edict of 380 is known by its opening words, Cunctos populos (“all peoples”), and is preserved in the Theodosian Code 16.1.2. It is paraphrased here, not quoted. Then point students back to Lecture 2, slide R: once the faith was official, “heresy” could be treated as a crime against the state.

Sources (checked 2026-10-19):

  • Edict Cunctos populos (February 27, 380), Theodosian Code 16.1.2: paraphrased from the standard summary (e.g., Britannica, “Theodosius I”); not quoted.

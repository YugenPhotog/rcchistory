The Romans renamed the region of Judea to "Syria Palaestina" in the 2nd century CE. This change is traditionally linked to the aftermath of the Bar Kokhba Revolt (132–136 CE), a major Jewish uprising against the Roman Empire. After the revolt was suppressed, the Roman Emperor Hadrian implemented a series of measures aimed at suppressing Jewish nationalism and religion. Renaming Judea to "Syria Palaestina" was part of this effort, as it sought to minimize Jewish ties to the land and associate it instead with the Philistines, an ancient enemy of the Israelites. The name "Palaestina" itself is derived from "Philistia," the ancient Greek and Roman term for the land of the Philistines.

Matthew’s Antitheses: The Higher Law
“Ye have heard of old . . . But I say”

Matthew's Antitheses refer to a series of teachings by Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew, specifically in Matthew 5:21-48, which are part of the Sermon on the Mount. In these passages, Jesus contrasts the traditional Jewish laws (often referred to as the Law of Moses) with his own authoritative teachings. Each antithesis follows a formulaic pattern:
"You have heard that it was said…"
"But I say to you…"
Purpose of the Antitheses
The antitheses serve to deepen the moral and spiritual demands of the Law. Rather than simply following the external commandments, Jesus calls for a transformation of the heart and mind, emphasizing inner righteousness and the spirit of the law over mere outward compliance.
Key Antitheses Examples
Anger and Murder
"You have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not murder…' But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:21-22).
Lust and Adultery
"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28).
Divorce
"It was also said, 'Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.' But I say to you that everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery" (Matthew 5:31-32).
Oaths
"Again you have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not swear falsely…' But I say to you, Do not take an oath at all…" (Matthew 5:33-34).
Retaliation
"You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil…" (Matthew 5:38-39).
Love for Enemies
"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:43-44).
Theological Significance
Fulfillment of the Law: Jesus states in Matthew 5:17, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them." The antitheses demonstrate how Jesus fulfills and transcends the Mosaic Law.
Inner Transformation: The teachings emphasize the importance of internal purity and integrity, not just external compliance.
Higher Ethical Standard: They present a radical call to a higher moral and spiritual standard, reflecting the values of the Kingdom of Heaven.
Interpretations
Christian scholars and theologians often debate whether Jesus is:
Intensifying the Law: Making it more stringent.
Reinterpreting the Law: Offering a new perspective on its true intent.
Superseding the Law: Establishing a new covenant that surpasses the old one.
The antitheses highlight the transformative nature of Jesus' teaching and his unique authority as the interpreter of God's will.

Introduction to Constantine (306–337 CE)
Overview of Reign: Constantine served as Emperor of Rome during a transformative era in Roman history, overseeing major shifts in political and religious structures. His reign marked the pivotal transition of Christianity from a persecuted minority faith to the dominant religion of the empire.
Background: Born in Naissus (modern-day Serbia), Constantine rose to power amidst political turmoil, becoming the sole ruler of the Roman Empire by defeating his rivals.


Key Events in Constantine’s Religious Impact
312 CE – Constantine’s Dream
On the eve of the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, Constantine reportedly experienced a vision or dream in which he saw the symbol of the Chi-Rho (☧), a Christian symbol representing Christ.
Accompanying the vision, Constantine heard the words, “In this sign, you will conquer” (Latin: In hoc signo vinces).
Interpreting this as divine favor, Constantine instructed his soldiers to mark their shields with the symbol and went on to secure a decisive victory.
313 CE – Edict of Milan
Co-authored with Licinius, the Edict of Milan proclaimed religious toleration throughout the empire, ending centuries of persecution against Christians.
It granted Christians the freedom to worship openly, restored confiscated church properties, and set a precedent for imperial support of the Christian Church.
The edict marks the beginning of Christianity’s transformation from a marginalized faith to an integral part of Roman governance and identity.


The Union of Church and State
Political Use of Christianity:
Constantine understood the potential of Christianity as a unifying force within the empire. By aligning himself with Christianity, he sought to consolidate his power and legitimize his rule under a divine mandate.
His famous phrase, "One God, one Church, and one Emperor," encapsulated this vision: a unified religious and political structure under his authority.
Creation of Orthodoxy:
To wield Christianity effectively as a political tool, Constantine needed a unified Church with a consistent doctrine. Disputes over theological issues (e.g., the nature of Christ, the Trinity) threatened the stability he sought.
Constantine convened the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, which:
Established the Nicene Creed, affirming foundational Christian beliefs.
Condemned heretical views like Arianism, which denied the full divinity of Christ.
Laid the groundwork for the concept of orthodoxy (orthos doxa = “correct teaching”).
Orthodoxy provided the Church with a clear and unified theological identity, aligned with the emperor's authority.


Christianity as the State Religion
While the Edict of Milan legalized Christianity, Constantine went further by actively promoting the faith:
Building significant Christian structures (e.g., the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem).
Granting privileges to Christian clergy and integrating Christian symbols into imperial iconography.
Long-Term Consequences:
Christianity’s rise to dominance under Constantine reshaped the cultural, social, and political fabric of the empire.
The symbiotic relationship between Church and state set a precedent that would influence Western civilization for centuries.


Relevance to Contemporary Students
Religious and Political Alliances: Discuss parallels in history where religious ideologies are used to legitimize political power.
Unity and Diversity: Highlight the tension between the desire for unity (e.g., orthodoxy) and the reality of diversity within religious traditions.
Personal Conviction vs. Public Policy: Reflect on the implications of Constantine’s personal faith journey on broader societal structures.


Conclusion
Constantine’s reign marks a turning point in the history of both Christianity and the Roman Empire. His political acumen, combined with his embrace of Christianity, created a legacy of intertwined religious and state power—a legacy that continues to shape discussions on the role of religion in governance today.

Augustinian thought, stemming from the ideas of Augustine of Hippo (354-430 AD), a prominent early Christian theologian and philosopher, has profoundly influenced both Christian theology and Western philosophy. His work continues to be a subject of study and debate. Here are key elements of his thought:
Human Nature and Original Sin: Augustine believed that humanity is spiritually wounded due to original sin, the sin inherited from Adam and Eve. This sin has damaged human nature, making it difficult for people to do good and easy to do evil. This concept became a central tenet in Christian theology regarding human nature and the necessity of divine grace for salvation.
Divine Grace and Free Will: Augustine maintained that divine grace is essential for salvation, and without God's grace, it is impossible for humans to be saved. He debated Pelagius, who argued that human beings could achieve salvation through their own efforts. Augustine emphasized the necessity of God's grace while also acknowledging the role of free will. This balance between grace and free will has been a significant topic in Christian theology.
The City of God: Augustine's work "The City of God" was a response to the sack of Rome in 410 AD. He argued that there are two cities: the City of God and the City of Man. The City of God is characterized by love of God and is eternal, while the City of Man is characterized by love of self and is temporal. His analysis delves into the nature of the church and its relationship to secular authority, influencing Christian perspectives on politics and society.
Confessions: In his autobiographical work "Confessions," Augustine explores his own spiritual journey, including his conversion to Christianity. This work is not only a narrative of his life but also a philosophical and theological exploration of memory, time, and the nature of God. It is one of the earliest autobiographies in Western literature and has had a lasting impact on Christian spirituality and philosophy.
The Problem of Evil: Augustine grappled with the problem of evil, questioning how a good and omnipotent God could allow evil to exist. He proposed that evil is not a thing in itself but a corruption or privation of good, a concept that has been influential in discussions about the nature of evil and suffering.
Influence on Christian Doctrine: Augustine's thought has been instrumental in shaping many doctrines of the Western Christian Church, particularly in areas like the nature of the Trinity, the role of the church, and the interpretation of Scripture.
Philosophical Contributions: Augustine is also remembered for his contributions to philosophy, especially in the realms of metaphysics, ethics, and the philosophy of mind. His explorations of concepts such as time, memory, and the nature of being have had a lasting impact on philosophical thought.
Augustine's ideas have been subject to various interpretations and debates throughout history, and they continue to be influential in both theological and secular contexts. His synthesis of Christian doctrine with Neoplatonic ideas created a framework that has shaped Western thought for centuries.

Huldrych Zwingli was a key figure in the Protestant Reformation and played a pivotal role in the early reformation movements, particularly in Switzerland. Here are some significant aspects of his life and contributions: 1. **Early Life and Education**: Zwingli was born on January 1, 1484, in Wildhaus, Switzerland. He received a thorough education, studying in universities such as Vienna and Basel. He was well-versed in the classics and showed a deep interest in humanist studies. 2. **Priesthood and Shift to Reformation Ideas**: Initially ordained as a Roman Catholic priest in 1506, Zwingli's exposure to the works of Erasmus and the teachings of Martin Luther greatly influenced his theological development. He began to question the practices and doctrines of the Catholic Church. 3. **Reform in Zurich**: Zwingli's most significant impact was in Zurich, where he was appointed as a pastor. He started preaching ideas of reform, emphasizing the importance of the Bible as the sole basis of Christian doctrine and rejecting practices not grounded in scripture, such as indulgences, the veneration of saints, and clerical celibacy. 4. **Political and Social Reforms**: Zwingli was also involved in the political sphere, advocating for the reform of the church and state. He believed in a strong relationship between the church and civic authorities but emphasized that the church should not be subordinate to the state. 5. **Iconoclasm and Liturgical Changes**: Under Zwingli's influence, Zurich saw significant liturgical changes. These included the removal of images from churches and changes in the Mass, emphasizing the sermon and scripture reading. 6. **Differences with Luther and Other Reformers**: Although Zwingli and Martin Luther shared many reformist ideas, they famously disagreed on the nature of the Eucharist. Zwingli saw the presence of Christ in the Eucharist as symbolic, whereas Luther held to the doctrine of Real Presence. 7. **The Marburg Colloquy**: In 1529, Zwingli and Luther attempted to resolve their differences at the Marburg Colloquy. However, they could not agree on the Eucharist issue, leading to a lasting division between Lutheran and Zwinglian branches of Protestantism. 8. **Death and Legacy**: Zwingli died in battle in 1531, during a conflict between Catholic and Protestant cantons in Switzerland. His legacy continued through his followers, influencing the Swiss Reformation and the broader Protestant movement. Zwingli's teachings also had a significant impact on John Calvin, another prominent Reformation figure. Zwingli's approach to reform was characterized by a pragmatic and sometimes political approach, distinct from Luther's more theological and spiritual focus. His life and work significantly shaped the course of the Reformation, especially in Switzerland, and had lasting impacts on Protestant theology and practice.

Key Doctrinal Clarifications: The Council reaffirmed several core Catholic doctrines that were disputed by Protestant reformers. This included the authority of the Pope, the importance of both Scripture and Church tradition, the doctrine of Original Sin, the necessity of both faith and good works for salvation, and the seven sacraments.
Liturgy and Sacraments: The Council standardized the liturgy and reinforced the significance of the sacraments. It affirmed the doctrine of transubstantiation in the Eucharist, directly countering Protestant beliefs like those of Zwingli and Luther.
Church Discipline and Reform: Besides doctrinal issues, the Council addressed abuses within the Church, such as the sale of indulgences and moral laxity among the clergy. The Index of Forbidden Books: Another outcome was the establishment of the Index Librorum Prohibitorum, a list of books forbidden for Catholics to read, which included many works by Protestant reformers.

ecumenism , Movement toward unity or cooperation among the Christian churches. The first major step in the direction of ecumenism was the International Missionary Conference of 1910, a gathering of Protestants. Several Protestant denominations inaugurated a Life and Work Conference (on social and practical problems) in 1925 and a Faith and Order Conference (on church doctrine and governance) in 1927. After World War II the World Council of Churches (WCC) was established; the International Missionary Conference joined it in 1961. The Roman Catholic church also has shown strong interest in improving interchurch relations since the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) and, with the patriarch of Constantinople, has lifted the excommunication of 1054. The Eastern Orthodox church was active in the movement since 1920 and joined the WCC at its inception. The more conservative or fundamentalist Protestant denominations have generally refrained from involvement. Another important factor in 20th-century ecumenism was the creation of united churches that reconcile splintered sects, such as the United Church of Christ (1957) and the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (1988).

Paul and the Gentile Mission
A Jewish follower of Jesus helps transform a local movement into a trans-Mediterranean religion
  • Conversion and calling: Paul changes from persecutor to missionary after an experience of the risen Christ.
  • Gentile inclusion: Non-Jews may join without circumcision or full observance of Jewish identity markers.

Paul was born and remained Jewish. His letters are the earliest surviving Christian writings, predating the written Gospels. In Galatians and Romans, he argues that Gentiles enter God's covenant community through Christ without first becoming Jews.

The Jerusalem meeting described in Acts 15 and Galatians 2 reflects real debate about circumcision, food practices, and fellowship. "Justification by faith" later became central to Protestant theology, but in Paul's historical context it also answered a communal question: on what basis can Jews and Gentiles belong at one table?

Missionaries created schools, hospitals, translations, and new Christian communities, but missions could also accompany colonial rule and attacks on Indigenous cultures. Death, Resurrection, and Christian Hope
Christian hope centers on resurrection and renewed creation, not only an immortal soul

Resurrection

God raises the dead; embodied life is transformed rather than merely discarded.

Judgment and Afterlife

Christian traditions agree on resurrection in the creeds while differing over the sequence, imagery, and details of the afterlife.

The Apostles' and Nicene Creeds affirm resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Popular Christianity often speaks mainly of souls going to heaven, but classical Christian teaching culminates in bodily resurrection and the renewal of creation.

Mary and the Logic of the Incarnation
Debates about Mary were also debates about who Jesus is

Ephesus · 431

Theotokos, "God-bearer," protects the claim that the one born of Mary is truly divine.

Chalcedon · 451

Jesus Christ is one person in two natures, fully divine and fully human, without confusion or separation.

Across Traditions

Catholics and Orthodox venerate Mary strongly; Protestants generally honor her biblical role with less devotional emphasis.

The Immaculate Conception concerns Mary's conception without original sin; it is not another name for Jesus's virgin conception.

The title Theotokos was approved at the Council of Ephesus to clarify Christology: Mary did not give birth merely to a human person later joined to God. Chalcedon then described Christ as one person in two complete natures, divine and human. Oriental Orthodox churches historically rejected the Chalcedonian formula, though modern dialogues have found substantial shared faith behind differing terminology.

Distinguish the virgin conception of Jesus, affirmed in the Apostles' Creed and accepted broadly among traditional Christians, from the Catholic dogma of the Immaculate Conception, which concerns Mary's own conception. Marian doctrines and devotions vary greatly and should not be treated as identical across Christianity.

Rome and Constantinople were major centers—but never the whole Christian world.

Armenian and Ethiopian Christianity developed distinctive liturgies, art, biblical traditions, and church structures. Coverage Addition · Medieval Christianity
The Western Medieval Church
Institutions of worship, learning, and authority expand after Rome’s political collapse

Monasteries

The medieval Church was simultaneously a spiritual community, political power, landholder, school system, and provider of social care.

After imperial government collapsed in the West, bishops and monasteries often supplied continuity. Papal influence grew unevenly and was frequently contested by kings, emperors, local bishops, and reform movements.

Scholasticism used disciplined questioning to reconcile authorities and examine objections. Thomas Aquinas’s synthesis of Christian theology and Aristotle became especially influential in Catholic thought. Mendicant orders such as Franciscans and Dominicans responded to growing towns through preaching, teaching, and service rather than rural monastic stability.

Crusades and Renaissance
Two developments transformed Christian relations with power, culture, and other religions

  • Armed pilgrimages promoted by popes and rulers
  • Violence against Muslims, Jews, and Eastern Christians
  • A long legacy in Christian-Muslim relations

Christian institutions could inspire learning and beauty while also becoming entangled with coercion and political ambition.

Zwingli led reform in Zurich and interpreted Communion primarily as a memorial. Calvin organized reform in Geneva, emphasized God’s sovereignty and Christian vocation, and systematized Reformed theology in the Institutes of the Christian Religion.

Henry VIII’s break with Rome began over royal authority and succession rather than Luther’s theology. Under later monarchs, the Church of England adopted Protestant teachings while retaining bishops, the historic creeds, and a formal liturgy. Anglicanism has always included both more Protestant and more Catholic currents.

Coverage Addition · Reformation
The Radical Reformation
Anabaptists argued that reform must reach church membership, power, and everyday life
  • Believer’s baptism: Baptism follows a conscious profession of faith, rather than occurring in infancy.
  • Voluntary church: Membership should not be automatic through birth or citizenship.
  • Separation from coercive power: Many rejected state control of religion and the use of force in matters of faith.
  • Discipleship: Pacifism, mutual aid, simplicity, and strict moral accountability characterized important branches.
Mennonites, Amish, and related traditions preserve parts of the Anabaptist inheritance.

“Anabaptist” means “rebaptizer,” a label opponents used because they did not recognize infant baptism as valid. Both Catholic and magisterial Protestant governments persecuted Anabaptists, sometimes by execution.

The movement was diverse. Some groups experimented with communal property; many emphasized nonviolence and church-state separation. Menno Simons helped stabilize a pacifist stream that became the Mennonite tradition. Amish communities emerged later from a division among Swiss and Alsatian Anabaptists.

Coverage Addition · Doctrine
How Does the Cross Save?
Atonement is one doctrine expressed through several overlapping models

Sacrifice / Satisfaction

Christ deals with sin and restores a broken relationship between humanity and God.

Christus Victor

Through death and resurrection, Christ defeats sin, death, and enslaving powers.

Moral Influence

Christ reveals divine love and calls people toward repentance and transformed life.

These are not necessarily competing theories; many Christians combine them.

The New Testament uses multiple images: sacrifice, ransom, reconciliation, victory, justification, adoption, healing, and new creation. Later theologians developed these into more systematic explanations.

Anselm’s satisfaction account and later penal-substitution theories are related but not identical. Christus Victor emphasizes liberation from hostile powers. Moral-influence approaches emphasize the transforming disclosure of God’s love. Present the models as a family of metaphors rather than implying that all Christians must choose only one.

Rabbit Hole · Modern Theology
Karl Barth and Neo-Orthodoxy
A twentieth-century recovery of divine transcendence and revelation

The Crisis

World War I shattered confidence that culture, reason, and moral progress would steadily build God’s kingdom.

Barth’s Response

Christian faith begins with God’s self-revelation in Jesus Christ—not with human religious achievement.

  • Neo-orthodoxy: renewed engagement with Reformation themes without simply repeating premodern theology.
  • Political consequence: Barth resisted attempts to subordinate the church to Nazi ideology.

Karl Barth reacted against liberal Protestant confidence in human progress after his theological teachers supported German war policy in 1914. His Commentary on Romans and later Church Dogmatics emphasized the radical difference between God and humanity and the priority of divine grace.

“Neo-orthodoxy” has no connection to Eastern Orthodoxy. It describes a varied Protestant movement that recovered themes of sin, judgment, revelation, and grace while engaging modern criticism. Barth helped draft the Barmen Declaration of 1934 against Nazi control of German churches.

Rabbit Hole · Modern Catholicism
Vatican II “Opens the Windows”
The Second Vatican Council, 1962-1965

Liturgy

Vernacular worship and fuller congregational participation

Laity and Scripture

Expanded lay vocation and encouragement of biblical study

Religious Liberty

Freedom of conscience and rejection of religious coercion

Other Faiths

Ecumenism and a transformed Catholic relationship with Judaism

Vatican II pursued renewal within continuity—not the creation of a different Catholic Church.

Pope John XXIII convened the council, and Pope Paul VI brought it to completion. Major documents include Sacrosanctum Concilium on liturgy, Dei Verbum on revelation, Dignitatis Humanae on religious liberty, Unitatis Redintegratio on ecumenism, and Nostra Aetate on relations with non-Christian religions.

Nostra Aetate rejected collective Jewish guilt for Jesus’s death and condemned antisemitism. Popular summaries sometimes say Vatican II ordered priests to face the people or abolished Latin; the actual reforms were more complex. Latin remained permitted, while vernacular liturgy and congregational participation expanded greatly.

Rabbit Hole · Global Christianity
Christianity Shifts South
The demographic and cultural center of Christianity is no longer concentrated in Europe
  • Africa, Asia, and Latin America contain many of Christianity’s fastest-growing and most youthful communities.
  • Local churches reinterpret inherited traditions through their own languages, music, politics, and social realities.
  • Migration carries these forms of Christianity into Europe and North America.
  • Global differences reshape debates over authority, sexuality, wealth, mission, and church government.
Reserved image · Global Christianity map

From “Western” to Worldwide

European history remains important, but it no longer defines Christianity’s demographic center.

Use “Global South” cautiously: it is a broad demographic label, not a claim that African, Asian, and Latin American churches are culturally or theologically uniform. Many are Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Protestant, Pentecostal, or independent.

The movement is not merely numerical. Global South theologians and church leaders increasingly shape biblical interpretation, mission, ethics, and denominational politics. If current percentages are added later, verify them against recent demographic research rather than copying the textbook’s dated figures.

Rabbit Hole · Global Christianity
African Independent Churches and Pentecostalism
Local agency produces new Christian forms rather than simple copies of European churches

African Independent / Initiated Churches

  • Founded and governed by Africans
  • Integrate healing, prophecy, community, and local cultural forms
  • Often emerged in resistance to missionary control

Pentecostal and Charismatic Christianity

  • Emphasizes direct experience of the Holy Spirit
  • Tongues, prophecy, healing, energetic worship, and testimony
  • Exists in independent churches and older denominations
These movements are internally diverse and now circulate through worldwide media, migration, and mission.

“African Independent Churches” and “African Initiated Churches” both appear in scholarship. Examples vary enormously, from Zionist and prophetic churches to institutions that resemble historic denominations. Avoid portraying African Christianity as a return to a single traditional religion.

Pentecostalism began as a modern global movement in the early twentieth century, with important interracial beginnings, and spread rapidly through local leadership. Charismatic renewal brought similar spiritual gifts into Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and mainline Protestant settings.

Rabbit Hole · North American Christianity
Three Influential North American Streams
Overlapping categories—not three uniform churches

Mainline Protestant

Historic denominations often associated with ecumenism, formal institutions, and greater openness to modern scholarship.

Evangelical

Emphasizes conversion or being “born again,” biblical authority, personal faith, and evangelism.

African American Churches

Distinctive worship and community institutions shaped survival, abolition, civil rights, and political leadership.

Race, immigration, region, class, and politics complicate every denominational label.

Mainline and evangelical are networks and tendencies rather than perfect denominational boxes; some denominations contain both. Evangelicalism has roots in the Reformation, Pietism, revivalism, and global mission. “Born again” refers to a personal experience of conversion and new life in Christ.

African American churches developed partly because segregation excluded Black Christians from white-controlled congregations. They became centers of education, mutual aid, music, political organization, and civil-rights activism. Martin Luther King Jr. exemplifies—but does not exhaust—their public leadership.

Rabbit Hole · New Religious Movements
Restorationist Movements
The goal: recover the authentic church believed to have been lost or corrupted

Latter-day Saints

Joseph Smith’s nineteenth-century movement adds new scriptures, prophetic authority, temple rituals, and a distinctive account of God and human destiny.

Jehovah’s Witnesses

A restorationist Bible movement rejecting the Trinity, emphasizing God’s name Jehovah, evangelism, and an imminent transformed world.

Both identify as Christian; most Nicene Christian churches classify their doctrines as outside historic Christian orthodoxy.

Use self-designations respectfully. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints prefers its full name or “Latter-day Saints” rather than “Mormon Church.” Its additional scriptures include the Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price.

Jehovah’s Witnesses emerged from the nineteenth-century Bible Student movement associated with Charles Taze Russell. They reject the Trinity, decline military service and blood transfusions, and are known for door-to-door evangelism. The final sentence distinguishes self-identification from classification by churches whose boundaries are defined by the Nicene Creed.

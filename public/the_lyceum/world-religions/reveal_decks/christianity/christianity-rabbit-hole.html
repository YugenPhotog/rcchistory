Path I
Control, Dissent & Modern Reform
How do institutions defend inherited truth while responding to intellectual, social, and political change?

This path begins with coercive boundary enforcement, then moves to twentieth-century fundamentalism, ecumenism, and Vatican II. The recurring issue is who controls change and what counts as faithful continuity.

Roman Inquisition
Establishment : Founded in 1542 by Pope Paul III, responding to the spread of Protestantism and heretical ideas.
Purpose : To maintain Catholic orthodoxy and counteract the Protestant Reformation. Aimed to uphold Church doctrine and combat heresy within Papal territories.
Structure : Operated under the authority of the Holy Office, led by a group of cardinals and officials appointed by the Pope.
Methodology : Focused on identifying, trying, and sentencing individuals accused of heresy. Used interrogation and sometimes torture for extracting confessions.

The Roman Inquisition was established in 1542 as a permanent papal institution for defending Catholic orthodoxy, especially in territories under direct or allied Catholic authority. It belonged to a broader early modern world in which governments commonly enforced religious conformity.

Proceed carefully with sensational images. Tribunals used legal investigation, censorship, imprisonment, penance, and in some cases torture; procedures and severity varied by time and place. Coercion was real, but the institution was not identical to every earlier medieval inquisition.

Roman Inquisition
Key Activities :
Censorship of books and literature, including maintaining the Index of Forbidden Books.
Prosecution of notable figures, such as Giordano Bruno and Galileo Galilei, for teachings contrary to Church doctrine.
Oversight of religious conformity within Catholic territories, ensuring adherence to orthodox practices.
Impact : Influenced cultural and intellectual life in Europe, enforcing religious conformity but also contributing to censorship and suppression of scientific and philosophical advancements.
Legacy : Part of the broader Counter-Reformation effort, its actions and consequences remain subjects of historical and ethical debate.
 

Giordano Bruno was executed in 1600 after a lengthy heresy trial involving multiple theological claims, not simply one scientific discovery. Galileo’s 1633 condemnation centered on heliocentrism, biblical interpretation, authority, and the politics of his relationship with church officials.

The legacy is neither science versus religion in every case nor harmless misunderstanding. These trials reveal the dangers of coercive orthodoxy, censorship, and institutions that make theological conformity a condition of intellectual permission.

Modern Christianity
1900-Present

The period 1900 to the present contains several simultaneous Christian stories: fundamentalism and liberal theology, ecumenism, Catholic renewal, decolonization, Pentecostal growth, migration, secularization, and demographic movement toward the Global South.

Because this material changes quickly and is not part of the core classroom lectures, the Rabbit Hole treats it as optional exploration rather than a single linear narrative.

ecumenism , Movement toward unity or cooperation among the Christian churches. The first major step in the direction of ecumenism was the International Missionary Conference of 1910, a gathering of Protestants. Several Protestant denominations inaugurated a Life and Work Conference (on social and practical problems) in 1925 and a Faith and Order Conference (on church doctrine and governance) in 1927. After World War II the World Council of Churches (WCC) was established; the International Missionary Conference joined it in 1961. The Roman Catholic church also has shown strong interest in improving interchurch relations since the Second Vatican Council (1962–65) and, with the patriarch of Constantinople, has lifted the excommunication of 1054. The Eastern Orthodox church was active in the movement since 1920 and joined the WCC at its inception. The more conservative or fundamentalist Protestant denominations have generally refrained from involvement. Another important factor in 20th-century ecumenism was the creation of united churches that reconcile splintered sects, such as the United Church of Christ (1957) and the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (1988).

Vatican II was the twenty-first ecumenical council recognized by the Roman Catholic Church. Convened by John XXIII and completed under Paul VI, it issued sixteen documents on liturgy, revelation, the church, religious liberty, ecumenism, and relations with non-Christian religions.

The council expanded vernacular worship and active lay participation but did not ban Latin. It pursued aggiornamento—renewal or bringing up to date—while presenting that renewal as continuity with Catholic tradition.

When does adaptation count as faithful reform—and when does a community experience it as surrender to modernity?

Compare fundamentalism, ecumenism, and Vatican II. Each responds to modern conditions but identifies different threats, authorities, and forms of continuity.

Rabbit Hole · Modern Theology
Karl Barth and Neo-Orthodoxy
A twentieth-century recovery of divine transcendence and revelation

The Crisis

World War I shattered confidence that culture, reason, and moral progress would steadily build God’s kingdom.

Barth’s Response

Christian faith begins with God’s self-revelation in Jesus Christ—not with human religious achievement.

  • Neo-orthodoxy: renewed engagement with Reformation themes without simply repeating premodern theology.
  • Political consequence: Barth resisted attempts to subordinate the church to Nazi ideology.

Karl Barth reacted against liberal Protestant confidence in human progress after his theological teachers supported German war policy in 1914. His Commentary on Romans and later Church Dogmatics emphasized the radical difference between God and humanity and the priority of divine grace.

“Neo-orthodoxy” has no connection to Eastern Orthodoxy. It describes a varied Protestant movement that recovered themes of sin, judgment, revelation, and grace while engaging modern criticism. Barth helped draft the Barmen Declaration of 1934 against Nazi control of German churches.

Rabbit Hole · Modern Catholicism
Vatican II “Opens the Windows”
The Second Vatican Council, 1962-1965

Liturgy

Vernacular worship and fuller congregational participation

Laity and Scripture

Expanded lay vocation and encouragement of biblical study

Religious Liberty

Freedom of conscience and rejection of religious coercion

Other Faiths

Ecumenism and a transformed Catholic relationship with Judaism

Vatican II pursued renewal within continuity—not the creation of a different Catholic Church.

Pope John XXIII convened the council, and Pope Paul VI brought it to completion. Major documents include Sacrosanctum Concilium on liturgy, Dei Verbum on revelation, Dignitatis Humanae on religious liberty, Unitatis Redintegratio on ecumenism, and Nostra Aetate on relations with non-Christian religions.

Nostra Aetate rejected collective Jewish guilt for Jesus’s death and condemned antisemitism. Popular summaries sometimes say Vatican II ordered priests to face the people or abolished Latin; the actual reforms were more complex. Latin remained permitted, while vernacular liturgy and congregational participation expanded greatly.

Rabbit Hole · Global Christianity
Christianity Shifts South
The demographic and cultural center of Christianity is no longer concentrated in Europe
  • Africa, Asia, and Latin America contain many of Christianity’s fastest-growing and most youthful communities.
  • Local churches reinterpret inherited traditions through their own languages, music, politics, and social realities.
  • Migration carries these forms of Christianity into Europe and North America.
  • Global differences reshape debates over authority, sexuality, wealth, mission, and church government.
Reserved image · Global Christianity map

From “Western” to Worldwide

European history remains important, but it no longer defines Christianity’s demographic center.

Use “Global South” cautiously: it is a broad demographic label, not a claim that African, Asian, and Latin American churches are culturally or theologically uniform. Many are Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Protestant, Pentecostal, or independent.

The movement is not merely numerical. Global South theologians and church leaders increasingly shape biblical interpretation, mission, ethics, and denominational politics. If current percentages are added later, verify them against recent demographic research rather than copying the textbook’s dated figures.

Rabbit Hole · Global Christianity
African Independent Churches and Pentecostalism
Local agency produces new Christian forms rather than simple copies of European churches

African Independent / Initiated Churches

  • Founded and governed by Africans
  • Integrate healing, prophecy, community, and local cultural forms
  • Often emerged in resistance to missionary control

Pentecostal and Charismatic Christianity

  • Emphasizes direct experience of the Holy Spirit
  • Tongues, prophecy, healing, energetic worship, and testimony
  • Exists in independent churches and older denominations
These movements are internally diverse and now circulate through worldwide media, migration, and mission.

“African Independent Churches” and “African Initiated Churches” both appear in scholarship. Examples vary enormously, from Zionist and prophetic churches to institutions that resemble historic denominations. Avoid portraying African Christianity as a return to a single traditional religion.

Pentecostalism began as a modern global movement in the early twentieth century, with important interracial beginnings, and spread rapidly through local leadership. Charismatic renewal brought similar spiritual gifts into Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, and mainline Protestant settings.

Pause & Reflect
Does Christianity’s shift toward the Global South make the religion less Western—or reveal that it was never only Western?

The best answers should distinguish historical European power from Christianity’s earlier Middle Eastern and African roots and its present global diversity. The demographic shift is real, but it is also a re-centering of a tradition that has long crossed regions.

Path III
North American & Restorationist Movements
How should scholars classify communities whose self-understanding and inherited creedal boundaries do not align?

Use careful category language. Mainline, evangelical, and African American churches overlap; Restorationist groups identify as Christian while Nicene churches often place them outside historic orthodoxy. Description and classification are different tasks.

Rabbit Hole · North American Christianity
Three Influential North American Streams
Overlapping categories—not three uniform churches

Mainline Protestant

Historic denominations often associated with ecumenism, formal institutions, and greater openness to modern scholarship.

Evangelical

Emphasizes conversion or being “born again,” biblical authority, personal faith, and evangelism.

African American Churches

Distinctive worship and community institutions shaped survival, abolition, civil rights, and political leadership.

Race, immigration, region, class, and politics complicate every denominational label.

Mainline and evangelical are networks and tendencies rather than perfect denominational boxes; some denominations contain both. Evangelicalism has roots in the Reformation, Pietism, revivalism, and global mission. “Born again” refers to a personal experience of conversion and new life in Christ.

African American churches developed partly because segregation excluded Black Christians from white-controlled congregations. They became centers of education, mutual aid, music, political organization, and civil-rights activism. Martin Luther King Jr. exemplifies—but does not exhaust—their public leadership.

Rabbit Hole · New Religious Movements
Restorationist Movements
The goal: recover the authentic church believed to have been lost or corrupted

Latter-day Saints

Joseph Smith’s nineteenth-century movement adds new scriptures, prophetic authority, temple rituals, and a distinctive account of God and human destiny.

Jehovah’s Witnesses

A restorationist Bible movement rejecting the Trinity, emphasizing God’s name Jehovah, evangelism, and an imminent transformed world.

Both identify as Christian; most Nicene Christian churches classify their doctrines as outside historic Christian orthodoxy.

Use self-designations respectfully. The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints prefers its full name or “Latter-day Saints” rather than “Mormon Church.” Its additional scriptures include the Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price.

Jehovah’s Witnesses emerged from the nineteenth-century Bible Student movement associated with Charles Taze Russell. They reject the Trinity, decline military service and blood transfusions, and are known for door-to-door evangelism. The final sentence distinguishes self-identification from classification by churches whose boundaries are defined by the Nicene Creed.

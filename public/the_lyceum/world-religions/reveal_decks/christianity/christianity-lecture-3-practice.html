Following Jesus:
Ethics, Worship & Practice
Discipleship · Ritual · Sacred Time · Community

Lecture 3 moves from belief to lived religion. It examines Jesus’ kingdom ethics, Christian moral formation, diverse worship traditions, sacraments, sacred time, and hope in resurrection.

Emphasize practice as formative: Christians do not only express beliefs through rituals and ethics; repeated actions also teach communities what to believe, value, remember, and hope for.

Three Dimensions of Christian Practice

1 · A Way of Life

Kingdom ethics shape character, relationships, inclusion, enemy-love, and public responsibility.

2 · A Worshiping Community

Liturgy, preaching, saints, music, and spiritual gifts organize communal encounter with God.

3 · A Sacred Rhythm

Sacraments and the liturgical year carry Christians from initiation through death and resurrection hope.

Guiding question: How do repeated practices turn Christian beliefs into a lived identity?

Preview ethics, worship, and sacred rhythm as overlapping rather than separate boxes. Eucharist has ethical implications; the calendar teaches doctrine; moral commitments are rehearsed in prayer and community.

Ask students to observe both broad continuity and denominational variation throughout the lecture.

Section I
The Way of Jesus
What kind of person—and what kind of community—does the kingdom of God call into being?

This section links Jesus’ kingdom proclamation, Beatitudes, antitheses, parables, love commands, inclusion at the margins, Pauline virtues, and public ethics. Present ethics as formation in a way of life, not merely rule memorization.

The Romans renamed the region of Judea to "Syria Palaestina" in the 2nd century CE. This change is traditionally linked to the aftermath of the Bar Kokhba Revolt (132–136 CE), a major Jewish uprising against the Roman Empire. After the revolt was suppressed, the Roman Emperor Hadrian implemented a series of measures aimed at suppressing Jewish nationalism and religion. Renaming Judea to "Syria Palaestina" was part of this effort, as it sought to minimize Jewish ties to the land and associate it instead with the Philistines, an ancient enemy of the Israelites. The name "Palaestina" itself is derived from "Philistia," the ancient Greek and Roman term for the land of the Philistines.

Theological Significance
Counter-Cultural Values: The Beatitudes emphasize qualities that often contradict worldly values, such as power, wealth, and self-sufficiency.
Kingdom Ethics: They outline the attitudes and behaviors expected of those who belong to God's Kingdom.
Present and Future Blessing: The blessings described in the Beatitudes have both a present and eschatological (future) dimension, offering hope for both now and eternity.
Application
The Beatitudes serve as a blueprint for Christian living, calling believers to embody these virtues in their daily lives. They encourage reliance on God, compassionate action, and perseverance through trials.

Matthew’s Antitheses: The Higher Law
“Ye have heard of old . . . But I say”

Matthew's Antitheses refer to a series of teachings by Jesus in the Gospel of Matthew, specifically in Matthew 5:21-48, which are part of the Sermon on the Mount. In these passages, Jesus contrasts the traditional Jewish laws (often referred to as the Law of Moses) with his own authoritative teachings. Each antithesis follows a formulaic pattern:
"You have heard that it was said…"
"But I say to you…"
Purpose of the Antitheses
The antitheses serve to deepen the moral and spiritual demands of the Law. Rather than simply following the external commandments, Jesus calls for a transformation of the heart and mind, emphasizing inner righteousness and the spirit of the law over mere outward compliance.
Key Antitheses Examples
Anger and Murder
"You have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not murder…' But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother will be liable to judgment" (Matthew 5:21-22).
Lust and Adultery
"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall not commit adultery.' But I say to you that everyone who looks at a woman with lustful intent has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:27-28).
Divorce
"It was also said, 'Whoever divorces his wife, let him give her a certificate of divorce.' But I say to you that everyone who divorces his wife, except on the ground of sexual immorality, makes her commit adultery" (Matthew 5:31-32).
Oaths
"Again you have heard that it was said to those of old, 'You shall not swear falsely…' But I say to you, Do not take an oath at all…" (Matthew 5:33-34).
Retaliation
"You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye and a tooth for a tooth.' But I say to you, Do not resist the one who is evil…" (Matthew 5:38-39).
Love for Enemies
"You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor and hate your enemy.' But I say to you, Love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:43-44).
Theological Significance
Fulfillment of the Law: Jesus states in Matthew 5:17, "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them." The antitheses demonstrate how Jesus fulfills and transcends the Mosaic Law.
Inner Transformation: The teachings emphasize the importance of internal purity and integrity, not just external compliance.
Higher Ethical Standard: They present a radical call to a higher moral and spiritual standard, reflecting the values of the Kingdom of Heaven.
Interpretations
Christian scholars and theologians often debate whether Jesus is:
Intensifying the Law: Making it more stringent.
Reinterpreting the Law: Offering a new perspective on its true intent.
Superseding the Law: Establishing a new covenant that surpasses the old one.
The antitheses highlight the transformative nature of Jesus' teaching and his unique authority as the interpreter of God's will.

Parables
Often taught in Parables
“without a parable spake he not unto them” (Mark 4:34)
Greek origin, meaning setting side by side, a comparison
Narrative, metaphor, brevity
Parables demand interpretation
 

Most teachers, especially those in the Middle East, have used some form of parable in their instruction, but none so exclusively as Jesus at one period of His ministry. During part of the Galilean ministry the record states that “without a parable spake he not unto them” (Mark 4:34).

The word parable is Greek in origin and means a setting side by side, a comparison. In parables divine truth is presented by comparison with material things. The Hebrew word, mashal, which parable is used to translate, has a wider significance, and is applied to the balanced metrical form in which teaching is conveyed in the poetical books of the Old Testament. Jesus joins Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18 to summarize devotion to God and responsibility toward the neighbor. The teaching is deeply rooted in Judaism rather than a rejection of it.

Enemy-love and nonretaliation extend the ethical demand beyond reciprocity. Christian communities disagree about whether turn the other cheek requires absolute pacifism, nonviolent resistance, personal restraint, or another application.

Ethical Foundations

This transition moves from Jesus’ teachings to broader Christian moral formation. Ethical traditions draw on Hebrew scripture, Jesus, apostolic writings, natural law, virtues, conscience, community, and changing historical problems.

There is no single Christian position on every public issue. The shared task is discerning how love of God and neighbor should shape character, institutions, and responsibility.

Pauline ethics does not treat moral conduct as a way to purchase salvation. Virtues are the fruit of life in Christ and in the Spirit, expressed through communities learning fidelity, hope, peace, mutual service, and love.

Paul also adapts inherited commandments to mixed Jewish-Gentile congregations. His letters address concrete conflicts, so students should distinguish enduring principles from first-century social settings.

Connect personal ethics to institutional ethics. Catholic social encyclicals, ecumenical organizations, Protestant reform movements, Orthodox social teaching, and Christian relief agencies represent different ways churches apply moral traditions beyond individual conduct.

Liberation theology asks how Scripture sounds when read from the standpoint of people experiencing poverty or oppression. Black and feminist theologians similarly expose the way supposedly universal theology can overlook race or gender. Present these as influential Christian approaches, not as positions accepted uniformly by all churches.

Section II
Worship & Community
How do words, bodies, buildings, music, and spiritual experience make a community Christian?

Trace worship from Jewish settings and house gatherings into diverse public liturgies. Compare Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Pentecostal, and charismatic emphases without placing them on a ladder from primitive to advanced.

Christian Worship and Ritual
The first believers worshiped in the Jerusalem temple and synagogues
The Roman Catholic Church built itself around seven sacraments
Eastern Orthodox churches preserve shared liturgical traditions across regional branches
Protestant reforms, Catholic renewal, and Vatican II each reshaped the work of the people

Christian worship begins within Jewish sacred space and practice: Temple, synagogue, scripture, prayer, psalms, festivals, and meals. Distinct Christian forms develop around baptism, the Lord’s Supper, Sunday gathering, and proclamation of Jesus.

Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant worship should not be portrayed as a simple progression. Each contains internal diversity and multiple reform movements, while the phrase work of the people captures the participatory meaning of liturgy.

Christian Worship and Ritual
Christian worship became increasingly public and elaborate after legal toleration and imperial patronage in the 300s
Formal church buildings were erected, and church officials dressed for worship in dress modeled after Roman government garb
Liturgical calendar was developed
Church buildings were designed for the community
Large basilicas changed the scale and social character of Christian gatherings
 

Legal toleration and imperial patronage enabled public basilicas, processions, professional clergy, councils, and an increasingly elaborate calendar. These developments made worship more visible and connected to civic life.

Large buildings changed scale and social relationships but did not simply erase intimacy. House churches, monasteries, chapels, parish communities, and domestic devotions continued alongside monumental worship.

Cult of the saints uses cultus in the older sense of devotional veneration, not worship of saints as gods. Catholic and Orthodox traditions ask saints to intercede; most Protestants sharply limit or reject this practice.

Eucharistic liturgies varied across regions long before the Reformation. Many Protestants emphasized vernacular scripture and preaching and recognized Baptism and Communion as the two rites clearly instituted by Christ.

Other Worship Practices
Charismatic movement : Stressed the use of supernatural gifts of the Holy Spirit
Charismatic - Groups inside Roman Catholic, Orthodox, and mainline Protestant churches
Pentecostal - Protestant churches where these practices are the norm

Pentecostal churches place gifts such as tongues, prophecy, healing, testimony, and spontaneous prayer near the center of communal worship. Charismatic renewal brings similar practices into older denominations.

These movements are global and internally diverse. Their worship often stresses direct experience of the Holy Spirit, lay participation, music, embodied response, and expectation of divine action.

Gregorian Chant

The two embedded examples allow students to compare chant settings. Gregorian chant is a western plainchant tradition: normally unaccompanied, monophonic, and shaped by Latin liturgical texts.

The label Gregorian does not mean Pope Gregory I personally composed the repertoire. The tradition developed over centuries through oral practice, regional exchange, notation, and liturgical standardization.

Pause & Reflect
What changes when worship moves from a house gathering to a public institution—and what might remain continuous?

Possible changes include scale, leadership, architecture, visibility, music, ritual elaboration, and political relationships. Continuities may include scripture, prayer, teaching, meals, baptism, praise, charity, and communal identity.

Section III
Sacraments, Sacred Time & Christian Hope
How does ritual carry the Christian story from initiation through death—and beyond death?

Treat sacraments and calendar as embodied theology. Water, bread, wine, touch, seasons, colors, fasting, feasting, burial, and remembrance connect individual lives with the story of Christ and the church.

Coverage Addition · Worship and Ritual
Baptism and Eucharist
The two practices recognized across nearly all Christian traditions

Baptism

  • Initiation into Christian life
  • Water signifies cleansing, death and rebirth, and incorporation into the Church
  • Infant or believer's baptism; immersion or pouring

Eucharist / Communion

  • Remembers Jesus's Last Supper
  • Bread and wine proclaim participation in Christ
  • Understood as sacrifice, real presence, spiritual presence, or memorial
Common ritual forms express Christian unity, while their interpretation reveals major denominational differences.

Baptism is associated with forgiveness, reception of the Spirit, union with Christ's death and resurrection, and membership in the Church. Traditions disagree over infant baptism, the proper mode, and whether baptism itself conveys grace or testifies to faith already present.

For Catholics, transubstantiation names the change of bread and wine into Christ's body and blood while appearances remain. Orthodox churches strongly affirm mystery and real presence without making one scholastic explanation universally definitive. Protestant views range from Lutheran sacramental presence to Reformed spiritual presence and memorial interpretations.

Orthodox Christianity also celebrates these sacred mysteries; most Protestants recognize baptism and Communion as the rites directly instituted by Jesus.

A sacrament is commonly defined as a visible sign through which divine grace is communicated. Catholic teaching groups the seven as sacraments of initiation, healing, and service of communion. Eastern Orthodox Christians often call them mysteries and do not always emphasize an exhaustive numerical list in the same way.

Many Protestant traditions reserve "sacrament" for baptism and the Lord's Supper. Others prefer the word "ordinance" to emphasize obedience and testimony rather than a rite that conveys grace. Avoid implying that churches with two sacraments have only two important rituals.

The calendar teaches doctrine through repeated practices, colors, readings, fasting, and feasting.

The liturgical year is most formal in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, and some Reformed traditions, but Christmas and Easter shape worship across much of Christianity. A lectionary coordinates readings from Scripture so congregations move through the biblical story over time.

Eastern and Western calendars do not always place feasts on the same civil dates because of different calendar systems and calculations. "Ordinary Time" does not mean unimportant time; the term refers to numbered weeks devoted to Jesus's teaching and the Church's ongoing life.

Coverage Addition · Worship and Belief
Death, Resurrection, and Christian Hope
Christian hope centers on resurrection and renewed creation, not only an immortal soul

Resurrection

God raises the dead; embodied life is transformed rather than merely discarded.

Judgment and Afterlife

Traditions teach heaven and hell differently; Catholic teaching also includes purgatory.

Funeral Practice

Prayer, Scripture, remembrance, burial or cremation, and hope in Christ's victory over death.

Christian traditions agree on resurrection in the creeds while differing over the sequence, imagery, and details of the afterlife.

The Apostles' and Nicene Creeds affirm resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Popular Christianity often speaks mainly of souls going to heaven, but classical Christian teaching culminates in bodily resurrection and the renewal of creation.

Purgatory in Catholic teaching is purification of those destined for communion with God, not a second chance after final rejection. Orthodox teaching uses different categories, and Protestants generally reject purgatory. Funeral customs vary culturally, but they typically combine mourning with proclamation of resurrection hope.

Christianity
Lecture 1 · Origins & Historical Development

Lecture 1 traces Christianity from Jesus and the earliest church through the Roman, medieval, Reformation, and early modern periods.

Frame the organizing question: how did a Jewish movement centered on Jesus become a global family of churches? Emphasize that the historical path was contested rather than inevitable.

Three Historical Transformations

1 · Jesus Movement

A Jewish renewal movement becomes a missionary church centered on the crucified and risen Jesus.

2 · Empire & Institution

Persecuted communities grow, spread beyond Rome, and become entwined with imperial power.

3 · Christendom & Reform

Eastern and western churches diverge; medieval institutions face reform, fragmentation, and modernity.

Guiding question: How did a Jewish movement around Jesus become a global family of churches?

Preview the lecture as a sequence of transformations rather than an inevitable march toward the present. Christianity repeatedly adapts to new languages, cultures, political systems, and institutional crises.

Ask students to track both continuity and change: claims about Jesus remain central, while authority, worship, organization, and relations with political power take many forms.

Section I
From Jesus Movement to Earliest Church
How did Jesus’ life, death, and resurrection become the center of a new missionary community?

This section begins within first-century Judaism. It follows the remembered ministry, execution, and resurrection of Jesus, then examines Pentecost, leadership in Jerusalem, Paul’s Gentile mission, and the gradual distinction between church and synagogue.

Christianity emerged from Judaism and shares Israel’s scriptures, belief in one God, and many ethical traditions. The term Judeo-Christian can name shared inheritance, but it can also blur real differences between the two religions.

Christ is the Greek translation of the Hebrew title Messiah, meaning anointed one. The earliest Christian claim was not that Jesus had the surname Christ, but that Jesus was the promised Messiah.

The Christian Present as Shaped by Its Past

This transition slide establishes the lecture’s method: contemporary Christianity is the result of accumulated historical choices rather than a timeless, uniform institution.

Invite students to watch for recurring tensions—Jewish roots and Gentile expansion, charisma and institution, marginality and power, unity and diversity, continuity and reform.

The Life, Death, and Resurrection of Jesus Christ
(ca. 4 B.C. – 30 A.D.)

Introduce the first historical movement. Dates for Jesus’ life are approximate; the conventional BCE/CE calendar was calculated centuries later and contains no year zero.

The lecture treats Jesus as a historical first-century Jewish teacher and also explains the theological claims Christians make about him. Keep those two kinds of inquiry distinct.

The Life, Death, and Resurrection of Jesus Christ (ca. 4 BC – 30 AD )
Primary sources of knowledge of Jesus are the four Gospels
Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John
Historians place Jesus’s birth around 4 B.C.E. and his death around the years 29 to 30
Beginning of Jesus‘s public ministry is marked by his baptism

The canonical Gospels are the primary narrative sources for Jesus. They preserve earlier traditions, but each author arranges the material to communicate theological meaning to a particular community.

The Synoptic Gospels—Matthew, Mark, and Luke—share much material; John follows a distinct pattern. Jesus’ baptism by John marks the beginning of his public ministry in the Gospel narratives.

The Life, Death, and Resurrection of Jesus Christ
Gathered twelve disciples who were called apostles by the church
 

The Twelve symbolize the restoration of Israel’s twelve tribes. Calling them apostles emphasizes being sent as authorized witnesses, although the New Testament uses disciple and apostle in more than one way.

The group included people of different backgrounds and temperaments. Their prominence should not obscure the women and wider circle of followers who also supported Jesus’ ministry and became resurrection witnesses.

Use this visual slide to discuss Jesus’ baptism by John. In the Gospel accounts, the descent of the Spirit and the heavenly voice identify Jesus and inaugurate his public vocation.

Baptism also locates Jesus within a wider Jewish movement of repentance and expectation. Later Christian baptism develops from this setting but acquires additional meanings tied to Jesus’ death and resurrection.

Passion comes from the Latin word for suffering. The final journey to Jerusalem brings Jesus into conflict with religious and political authorities during Passover, a festival already charged with memories of liberation.

Frame the coming media sequence as interpretation as well as illustration. Every film makes choices about chronology, dialogue, characterization, responsibility, and violence.

The Last Supper
John 13: 1-35

The Last Supper provides the narrative foundation for the Eucharist or Lord’s Supper. The Synoptic Gospels connect the meal explicitly with Passover; John emphasizes foot washing and the command to love one another.

Christian traditions differ over how Christ is present in the bread and wine, a topic developed in Lecture 3. Here, emphasize memory, covenant, service, and communal identity.

Jesus is Condemned Before Pilate
Matt 27: 1-2, 11-25

Pontius Pilate was the Roman prefect of Judea. Crucifixion was a Roman imperial punishment, especially associated with rebels, enslaved people, and those judged a threat to public order.

The Gospel accounts also portray conflict with some Jewish leaders, but collective blame against Jews is historically false and has fueled antisemitism. Keep responsibility specific to the actors and institutions described.

Jesus Is Scourged and Crucified
Matt 27: 26-50

Crucifixion was deliberately public, painful, and humiliating. Christians later transformed the instrument of Roman execution into their most recognizable symbol.

Do not try to settle the meaning of the cross on this slide. Preview that Christians explain salvation through several overlapping models—sacrifice, victory over evil, reconciliation, moral transformation, and others.

Jesus is Laid in a Tomb
Matt 27: 57-60

Burial narratives link Jesus’ public execution to the proclamation of an empty tomb. Joseph of Arimathea appears as the figure who requests the body and provides a burial place.

This slide functions as the narrative hinge between crucifixion and resurrection. Ask students what expectations the image creates before moving forward.

Jesus is Resurrected
John 20: 3-18

Resurrection is the central claim of earliest Christian proclamation: God raised Jesus from the dead. Christians understand this as transformation and vindication, not merely a return to ordinary mortal life.

The Gospels vary in details about the tomb and appearances. Their shared claim creates the basis for Christian hope, worship on the first day of the week, and the conviction that death does not have the final word.

Pentecost and the Earliest Church
Acts presents the Holy Spirit as empowering a new community

Pentecost

During the Jewish festival of Shavuot, disciples experience wind, fire, and inspired speech.

Proclamation

Peter announces Jesus's resurrection and calls listeners to repentance and baptism.

Community

Believers gather for teaching, prayer, shared meals, mutual aid, and "breaking bread."

Pentecost is often called the "birthday of the Church," although Jesus's followers still understood themselves as Jews.

Acts 2 locates Pentecost fifty days after Passover, at Shavuot. The multilingual scene symbolizes a mission able to cross linguistic and ethnic boundaries. Christians also read it as a reversal of Babel: difference remains, but communication becomes possible.

Do not describe the event as the instant creation of a completely separate religion. The first followers worshiped in the Jerusalem Temple and participated in Jewish life. Peter and James emerge as leaders of the Jerusalem community; the later Gentile mission transforms the movement.

Coverage Addition · Origins of the Church
Paul and the Gentile Mission
A Jewish follower of Jesus helps transform a local movement into a trans-Mediterranean religion
  • Conversion and calling: Paul changes from persecutor to missionary after an experience of the risen Christ.
  • Gentile inclusion: Non-Jews may join without circumcision or full observance of Jewish identity markers.
  • Justification by faith: Salvation is God's gift, received through trust rather than earned status.
  • Networks of churches: Paul's journeys and letters connect house congregations across the Roman world.
Paul did not think he was abandoning Israel's God; he believed Israel's promises were opening to the nations.

Paul was born and remained Jewish. His letters are the earliest surviving Christian writings, predating the written Gospels. In Galatians and Romans, he argues that Gentiles enter God's covenant community through Christ without first becoming Jews.

The Jerusalem meeting described in Acts 15 and Galatians 2 reflects real debate about circumcision, food practices, and fellowship. "Justification by faith" later became central to Protestant theology, but in Paul's historical context it also answered a communal question: on what basis can Jews and Gentiles belong at one table?

Avoid presenting a single clean "parting of the ways." Separation occurred differently across regions and continued for centuries. The destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 CE forced multiple Jewish groups, including Jesus-following communities and the ancestors of rabbinic Judaism, to redefine themselves.

Some New Testament polemics arose from disputes among Jews, but later Gentile Christians read them as attacks on Judaism as a whole. Explicitly reject collective Jewish responsibility for Jesus's death. This history helps students understand why responsible Christian interpretation today emphasizes the Jewish identity of Jesus and the apostles.

Pause & Reflect
Which mattered more for Christianity’s early expansion: the message about Jesus—or the decision to welcome Gentiles without requiring full conversion to Judaism?

Invite students to defend either choice, then show why the alternatives are inseparable. The message supplied the theological center; the Gentile mission reshaped the movement’s social boundaries and demographic future.

Section II
From Marginal Movement to Imperial Religion
What did Christianity gain—and risk—when it moved from persecution to political power?

Trace growth across social networks and regions, including communities beyond the Roman West. Constantine is a turning point, not an instantaneous conversion of the empire; imperial patronage creates new opportunities and new conflicts over authority.

Ancient Period
100 – 500 AD

The label Ancient Period is a teaching convention, not a boundary that all regions experienced in the same way. The next slides move from local post-apostolic communities to legal toleration and imperial patronage.

Keep the geographic lens broad. Christianity also expanded eastward and southward beyond the territories that later became Latin western Europe.

Growth of Church
As the apostles died, local churches developed lasting structures of leadership
Christianity had established itself in every large and midsize city in the eastern Roman Empire
Established local leadership – Episkopos (overseer or bishop) and presbyter (elder)
Did not have authority to speak for whole church or declare doctrine
Growth of Christianity challenged Roman empire
Led to increased persecutions and martyrdom of Christians
But failed to wipe out movement
 

As the first generation died, offices such as bishop, presbyter or elder, and deacon became more regular. Authority remained locally organized, though bishops increasingly communicated through regional networks and councils.

Roman persecution was real but varied by place and period; it was not uniformly continuous for three centuries. Martyr stories nevertheless became powerful models of witness and identity.

Why Did Christianity Grow?
No single cause explains expansion across the Roman world

Networks

Roads, ports, common languages, and urban travel connected house churches.

Belonging

Congregations crossed some ethnic, gender, and social boundaries.

Mutual Care

Members supported the sick, poor, widowed, imprisoned, and displaced.

Hope

Resurrection and eternal life offered meaning amid suffering and death.

Growth came through ordinary relationships and resilient communities as much as through famous missionaries.

The textbook highlights moral teaching, happiness and meaning, eternal life, improved standing for women and enslaved people, and social support. These explanations should not imply that the church erased Roman hierarchy: Christians still lived within patriarchal and slaveholding societies.

House churches met in private homes and were linked through travelers, merchants, missionaries, and letters. Their small scale could produce intimacy and strong mutual obligation. Conversion often moved through families and social networks rather than through isolated individual decisions.

Reserved image · Early Christian expansion map

A Multicentered Religion

Rome and Constantinople were major centers—but never the whole Christian world.

Armenian and Ethiopian Christianity developed distinctive liturgies, art, biblical traditions, and church structures. Christian communities in India trace their origins to the apostle Thomas, though the precise historical beginnings are debated; their longstanding Syriac connections are well documented.

Cyril and Methodius are associated with ninth-century missions among Slavic peoples and translation into a Slavic literary language. Orthodox Christianity subsequently became central in Bulgaria, Kievan Rus, and Russia. After Constantinople fell in 1453, Moscow sometimes imagined itself as a “Third Rome.”

Constantine and the Christianization of the Roman Empire
306 – 337 Constantine reigns as Emperor of Rome
312 Constantine’s Dream – “In this sign, you will conquer”

Introduction to Constantine (306–337 CE)
Overview of Reign: Constantine served as Emperor of Rome during a transformative era in Roman history, overseeing major shifts in political and religious structures. His reign marked the pivotal transition of Christianity from a persecuted minority faith to the dominant religion of the empire.
Background: Born in Naissus (modern-day Serbia), Constantine rose to power amidst political turmoil, becoming the sole ruler of the Roman Empire by defeating his rivals.


Key Events in Constantine’s Religious Impact
312 CE – Constantine’s Dream
On the eve of the Battle of the Milvian Bridge, Constantine reportedly experienced a vision or dream in which he saw the symbol of the Chi-Rho (☧), a Christian symbol representing Christ.
Accompanying the vision, Constantine heard the words, “In this sign, you will conquer” (Latin: In hoc signo vinces).
Interpreting this as divine favor, Constantine instructed his soldiers to mark their shields with the symbol and went on to secure a decisive victory.
313 CE – Edict of Milan
Co-authored with Licinius, the Edict of Milan proclaimed religious toleration throughout the empire, ending centuries of persecution against Christians.
It granted Christians the freedom to worship openly, restored confiscated church properties, and set a precedent for imperial support of the Christian Church.
The edict marks the beginning of Christianity’s transformation from a marginalized faith to an integral part of Roman governance and identity.


The Union of Church and State
Political Use of Christianity:
Constantine understood the potential of Christianity as a unifying force within the empire. By aligning himself with Christianity, he sought to consolidate his power and legitimize his rule under a divine mandate.
His famous phrase, "One God, one Church, and one Emperor," encapsulated this vision: a unified religious and political structure under his authority.
Creation of Orthodoxy:
To wield Christianity effectively as a political tool, Constantine needed a unified Church with a consistent doctrine. Disputes over theological issues (e.g., the nature of Christ, the Trinity) threatened the stability he sought.
Constantine convened the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, which:
Established the Nicene Creed, affirming foundational Christian beliefs.
Condemned heretical views like Arianism, which denied the full divinity of Christ.
Laid the groundwork for the concept of orthodoxy (orthos doxa = “correct teaching”).
Orthodoxy provided the Church with a clear and unified theological identity, aligned with the emperor's authority.


Christianity as the State Religion
While the Edict of Milan legalized Christianity, Constantine went further by actively promoting the faith:
Building significant Christian structures (e.g., the Church of the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem).
Granting privileges to Christian clergy and integrating Christian symbols into imperial iconography.
Long-Term Consequences:
Christianity’s rise to dominance under Constantine reshaped the cultural, social, and political fabric of the empire.
The symbiotic relationship between Church and state set a precedent that would influence Western civilization for centuries.


Relevance to Contemporary Students
Religious and Political Alliances: Discuss parallels in history where religious ideologies are used to legitimize political power.
Unity and Diversity: Highlight the tension between the desire for unity (e.g., orthodoxy) and the reality of diversity within religious traditions.
Personal Conviction vs. Public Policy: Reflect on the implications of Constantine’s personal faith journey on broader societal structures.


Conclusion
Constantine’s reign marks a turning point in the history of both Christianity and the Roman Empire. His political acumen, combined with his embrace of Christianity, created a legacy of intertwined religious and state power—a legacy that continues to shape discussions on the role of religion in governance today.

Augustinian Thought
Original Sin
Divine Grace

Augustinian thought, stemming from the ideas of Augustine of Hippo (354-430 AD), a prominent early Christian theologian and philosopher, has profoundly influenced both Christian theology and Western philosophy. His work continues to be a subject of study and debate. Here are key elements of his thought:
Human Nature and Original Sin: Augustine believed that humanity is spiritually wounded due to original sin, the sin inherited from Adam and Eve. This sin has damaged human nature, making it difficult for people to do good and easy to do evil. This concept became a central tenet in Christian theology regarding human nature and the necessity of divine grace for salvation.
Divine Grace and Free Will: Augustine maintained that divine grace is essential for salvation, and without God's grace, it is impossible for humans to be saved. He debated Pelagius, who argued that human beings could achieve salvation through their own efforts. Augustine emphasized the necessity of God's grace while also acknowledging the role of free will. This balance between grace and free will has been a significant topic in Christian theology.
The City of God: Augustine's work "The City of God" was a response to the sack of Rome in 410 AD. He argued that there are two cities: the City of God and the City of Man. The City of God is characterized by love of God and is eternal, while the City of Man is characterized by love of self and is temporal. His analysis delves into the nature of the church and its relationship to secular authority, influencing Christian perspectives on politics and society.
Confessions: In his autobiographical work "Confessions," Augustine explores his own spiritual journey, including his conversion to Christianity. This work is not only a narrative of his life but also a philosophical and theological exploration of memory, time, and the nature of God. It is one of the earliest autobiographies in Western literature and has had a lasting impact on Christian spirituality and philosophy.
The Problem of Evil: Augustine grappled with the problem of evil, questioning how a good and omnipotent God could allow evil to exist. He proposed that evil is not a thing in itself but a corruption or privation of good, a concept that has been influential in discussions about the nature of evil and suffering.
Influence on Christian Doctrine: Augustine's thought has been instrumental in shaping many doctrines of the Western Christian Church, particularly in areas like the nature of the Trinity, the role of the church, and the interpretation of Scripture.
Philosophical Contributions: Augustine is also remembered for his contributions to philosophy, especially in the realms of metaphysics, ethics, and the philosophy of mind. His explorations of concepts such as time, memory, and the nature of being have had a lasting impact on philosophical thought.
Augustine's ideas have been subject to various interpretations and debates throughout history, and they continue to be influential in both theological and secular contexts. His synthesis of Christian doctrine with Neoplatonic ideas created a framework that has shaped Western thought for centuries.

Christian monasticism began before Benedict and developed especially strongly in Egypt, Syria, and the eastern Mediterranean. Benedict’s sixth-century Rule became exceptionally influential in the Latin West.

Section III
Christendom, Division, Reform & Modernity
How did shared Christian roots produce distinct eastern, western, Protestant, and modern trajectories?

This section compares Byzantine and Latin Christian worlds, the disputes that hardened division, medieval western institutions, the Reformation crisis, and the new conditions created by confessional states, global expansion, science, and Enlightenment criticism.

Byzantine, Medieval, and Renaissance Christianity
500 – 1500 ad

This thousand-year periodization is intentionally broad. Western Europe, Byzantium, the Middle East, Africa, and Asia followed connected but different historical paths.

The next slides focus on eastern and western institutions and then the Reformation. Remind students that medieval Christianity was never culturally or institutionally uniform.

Constantinople’s rise shifted political and ecclesiastical gravity eastward. Primus inter pares means first among equals and describes the Ecumenical Patriarch’s place among Orthodox bishops, not authority identical to the Roman papacy.

The western empire ended in the fifth century, while the Byzantine Empire endured until 1453. Greek and Latin languages, political settings, and theological customs gradually differentiated the churches.

Iconoclasm Controversy
Held on to the fiction of one universal church, at least until the Pope and the Patriarch excommunicated each other!
Papacy assumed supremacy as the successor to Peter; Patriarch assumed supremacy as the Bishop of the Imperial Capital
Iconoclasm – Emperor Leo III commands the destruction of all images of Jesus, Mary, and the Angels (in reaction to Muslim criticism of Christianity)
Action condemned by Pope and western church
Eastern church convenes council which condemns use of icons as idolatry
2 nd Council of Nicaea affirms use of icons

Iconoclasm involved theology, imperial politics, monastic influence, and debates over representation; it should not be reduced to a single reaction to Islam. Iconoclasts rejected sacred images, while icon defenders distinguished veneration from worship.

The Second Council of Nicaea in 787 affirmed the veneration of icons while reserving worship for God. Icons became especially important in Orthodox spirituality and liturgy.

Filioque Controversy
Eastern Bishops rejected the filioque (“and by the son”) inserted into the Creeds
Schism of 1054 – the Pope and the Patriarch mutually excommunicate each other
Schism between Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic is complete

Filioque means and the Son. Western churches added the phrase to the creed’s statement about the Holy Spirit; eastern churches objected both to the theology and to changing an ecumenical creed without an ecumenical council.

The mutual excommunications of 1054 are a symbolic date, not an instant and complete break. Estrangement developed gradually and was deepened by later conflict, especially the Fourth Crusade’s sack of Constantinople in 1204.

The Western Medieval Church

Papacy

Monasteries

New Institutions

Universities, cathedrals, and mendicant orders reshape urban religious life.

The medieval Church was simultaneously a spiritual community, political power, landholder, school system, and provider of social care.

After imperial government collapsed in the West, bishops and monasteries often supplied continuity. Papal influence grew unevenly and was frequently contested by kings, emperors, local bishops, and reform movements.

Scholasticism used disciplined questioning to reconcile authorities and examine objections. Thomas Aquinas’s synthesis of Christian theology and Aristotle became especially influential in Catholic thought. Mendicant orders such as Franciscans and Dominicans responded to growing towns through preaching, teaching, and service rather than rural monastic stability.

Coverage Addition · Medieval and Renaissance Christianity
Crusades and Renaissance
Two developments transformed Christian relations with power, culture, and other religions

The Crusades · from 1095

  • Armed pilgrimages promoted by popes and rulers
  • Violence against Muslims, Jews, and Eastern Christians
  • A long legacy in Christian-Muslim relations

The Renaissance · 1300s-1500s

  • Humanism returns to classical texts and original languages
  • Church patronage produces major art and architecture
  • Printing, nationalism, and criticism prepare the way for reform
Christian institutions could inspire learning and beauty while also becoming entangled with coercion and political ambition.

The First Crusade was launched after Pope Urban II’s appeal in 1095. Motives included devotion, pilgrimage, defense, political ambition, land, and wealth. Crusaders massacred Jewish communities in Europe and Muslim and Jewish residents of Jerusalem; the Fourth Crusade’s sack of Constantinople deepened the Catholic-Orthodox rupture.

Renaissance humanists emphasized ad fontes—“to the sources”—encouraging study of Greek and Hebrew as well as Latin. Printing accelerated the spread of scripture and controversy. Humanism did not simply replace Christianity; many humanists sought Christian renewal.

Reform did not begin with Luther. John Wycliffe criticized clerical wealth and papal power, promoted scripture in the vernacular, and challenged transubstantiation; followers known as Lollards continued some of his ideas.

Jan Hus in Bohemia drew on related reform currents and was executed in 1415. These movements show that institutional criticism, biblical reform, and calls for evangelical poverty predated 1517.

Martin Luther
Born to a wealthy family in Germany
Educated at most distinguished university in Germany
Following a terrifying experience in a thunderstorm, he abandoned the study of law and joined a monastery
Earned Doctor of Theology in 1512
Became professor of biblical studies
 

Luther trained in law before entering the Augustinian order after a thunderstorm vow. His education, monastic discipline, and biblical teaching shaped his concern with sin, grace, faith, and assurance.

Avoid presenting the Reformation as the achievement of one isolated hero. Printing, universities, German politics, church finance, humanism, and older reform movements all made his challenge consequential.

The Indulgences Controversy
Luther objected to teachings that purchase of indulgences granted forgiveness of sins
Drafted of set of propositions for the purpose of opening academic debate on indulgences;
Nailed his 95 Thesis to the door of Wittenberg Castle Church on October 31, 1517
Attacks not only the corruption, but the underlying doctrine as well

Indulgences concerned the remission of temporal penalties associated with sin after absolution; popular fundraising could make the practice sound like salvation for sale. Johann Tetzel’s campaign helped provoke Luther’s response.

Luther sent the Ninety-five Theses to Archbishop Albrecht in 1517; the famous account of nailing them to the Wittenberg church door is possible but historically debated. The controversy quickly expanded from abuse to deeper questions about authority and salvation.

This slide supplies the missing bridge between the sixteenth-century Reformation and twentieth-century fundamentalism. Confessionalization linked religious identity with governments and schools. The devastation associated with European religious conflict encouraged arguments for toleration, though the process was slow and incomplete.

Pietism arose within Lutheranism but influenced Methodism, evangelicalism, missions, and reform. It shifted emphasis from correct doctrine alone to a transformed heart and active Christian life. The Enlightenment did not simply destroy Christianity; Christian thinkers adopted, negotiated, or resisted its methods in different ways.

Christianity Confronts Modernity
The nineteenth century produced both adaptation and resistance
  • Historical criticism: Scholars analyze biblical texts through language, sources, authorship, and historical setting.
  • Science: Geology and evolutionary theory challenge some traditional readings of creation.
  • Reform: Christians organize movements concerning slavery, poverty, education, labor, and temperance.
  • Global missions: Christianity expands worldwide, often entangled with European and American imperial power.
Modern Christianity is shaped by arguments over what may change and what must remain authoritative.

Historical criticism asks how biblical documents arose in particular settings. Some Christians saw it as a threat; others used it to deepen interpretation. Likewise, responses to Darwin ranged from rejection to acceptance of evolution within a theology of creation.

Missionaries created schools, hospitals, translations, and new Christian communities, but missions could also accompany colonial rule and attacks on Indigenous cultures. Emphasize local agency: converts did not merely receive European Christianity; they translated, selected, resisted, and transformed it.

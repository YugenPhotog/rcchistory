Hebrew, any member of an ancient northern Semitic people that were the ancestors of the Jews. Biblical scholars use the term Hebrews to designate the descendants of the patriarchs of the Hebrew Bible (Old Testament)—i.e., Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (also called Israel [Genesis 33:28])—from that period until their conquest of Canaan (Palestine) in the late 2nd millennium BCE. Thenceforth these people are referred to as Israelites until their return from the Babylonian Exile in the late 6th century BCE, from which time on they became known as Jews.

In the Bible the patriarch Abraham is referred to a single time as the ivri, which is the singular form of the Hebrew-language word for Hebrew (plural ivrim, or ibrim). But the term Hebrew almost always occurs in the Hebrew Bible as a name given to the Israelites by other peoples, rather than one used by themselves. For that matter, the origins of the term Hebrew itself are uncertain. It could be derived from the word eber, or ever, a Hebrew word meaning the “other side” and conceivably referring again to Abraham, who crossed into the land of Canaan from the “other side” of the Euphrates or Jordan River. The name Hebrew could also be related to the seminomadic Habiru people, who are recorded in Egyptian inscriptions of the 13th and 12th centuries BCE as having settled in Egypt.

Code of Justinian (527 C.E)
Contained discriminatory legislation against the Jews
Influenced European legal systems for centuries and contributed to anti-Semitism
 

The Code of Justinian (Corpus Juris Civilis) included provisions that specifically targeted Jews and their practices, reflecting and institutionalizing prejudices of the time. While the primary aim of the Code was to consolidate and streamline the vast expanse of Roman law, it also embedded various societal biases into law. Here are some aspects of the Code that can be seen as anti-Semitic:
 
1. Restrictions on Synagogue Construction: The Code prohibited the construction of new synagogues. Existing synagogues could be renovated or repaired, but building new ones was banned.
 
2. Forced Conversion: The Code allowed for the forced conversion of Jewish children if they were under the guardianship of Christians.
 
3. Limitations on Office Holding: Jews were barred from holding certain public offices, ensuring they didn't have authority over Christians.
 
4. Prohibition of Testimony: Jews were forbidden from giving testimony against Christians, which limited their legal rights and protections.
 
5. Marriage Restrictions: Marriages between Jews and Christians were prohibited, and any Christian woman who married a Jew could have her property confiscated.
 
6. Rituals and Traditions: The Code targeted Jewish practices that were misunderstood or seen as threatening by Christian authorities. For instance, it prohibited the celebration of Passover before Easter, to ensure the primacy of the Christian holiday.
 
7. Economic Restrictions: In certain instances, Jews were restricted from certain economic activities, further limiting their societal integration.
 
The above provisions did not originate with Justinian or his Code but were a continuation of prior Roman policies, many of which became harsher following the Christianization of the Empire. While not every regulation was necessarily enforced consistently across the vast Byzantine Empire, their presence in the Code ensured a legal foundation for prejudice and discriminatory actions.
 
It's worth noting that anti-Semitic provisions in legal codes and societal norms weren't unique to the Byzantine Empire. Throughout history, many societies, especially in Medieval Europe, implemented similar or even harsher measures against Jewish communities. The impact of these measures in the Justinian Code, however, was long-lasting, as the Code influenced legal thought and systems for centuries to come.

Leviticus 19:2 – "You shall be holy, for I, the LORD your God, am holy" – is a foundational verse in the Torah, and it has been a focal point of Jewish exegesis, ethics, and theology for millennia. This command is significant not only for its theological implications but also for its ethical ramifications, as the chapter that follows this verse is replete with various commandments governing interpersonal relations and moral behavior. Jewish commentators have engaged with this verse in rich and multifaceted ways:
 
1. **Rashi**: One of the most famous Jewish commentators, Rashi (Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki), commenting on this verse, links holiness to abstaining from sexual immorality and idolatry, based on the surrounding context in Leviticus. His commentary suggests that refraining from transgressions leads to holiness.
 
2. **Nahmanides (Ramban)**: Expanding on Rashi, Ramban (Rabbi Moses ben Nahman) emphasizes that the commandment goes beyond just refraining from forbidden actions. He writes that a person might technically obey the Torah's laws while still acting "within the limit of the law" in a gluttonous, lustful, or unethical manner. Thus, the call to holiness requires ethical and moral behavior that transcends mere legalistic adherence.
 
3. **Midrash**: The Midrashic literature, a collection of ancient rabbinic commentaries, explores the verse in various ways. One perspective in the Sifra, a halakhic midrash on the book of Leviticus, posits that "You shall be holy" is a comprehensive commandment from which many other commandments can be derived. The implication is that the pursuit of holiness permeates and underlies all aspects of religious observance.
 
4. **Ethical Commandments**: The context of Leviticus 19:2 is illuminating. The chapter that follows includes a variety of commandments, from honoring parents and observing the Sabbath to not stealing, lying, or bearing grudges. By positioning these commandments immediately after the call to holiness, the Torah seems to be emphasizing that ethical behavior is intrinsic to the path of holiness.
 
5. **Holiness in Everyday Life**: Jewish thinkers have often pointed out that the holiness described in Leviticus is not limited to the Temple or the priesthood. Instead, it's a holiness accessible to every Jew in their daily life. This idea revolutionizes the notion of sanctity, democratizing it and placing it within reach of every individual, in every action they undertake.
 
6. **Kedushah in Jewish Liturgy**: The concept of holiness (Kedushah) is also echoed in the daily Jewish prayers, especially in the Kedushah section of the Amidah prayer. This liturgical emphasis reinforces the Torah's call for holiness in daily life.
 
In essence, Leviticus 19:2 and its call for holiness has been understood in Jewish exegesis as both a call to ethical behavior and a deeper spiritual aspiration. It underscores the intertwined nature of ritual, ethics, and spirituality in Jewish life.

The interaction between Moses and God where God reveals His name as "I AM" occurs in the book of Exodus. It's a profound moment in the biblical narrative, rich in theological and philosophical implications. Let's explore the context and some interpretations:

**Context**:
Moses encounters God in the form of a burning bush on Mount Horeb. When Moses asks God what he should tell the Israelites when they ask for the name of the God who sent him, God responds in Exodus 3:14 (English Standard Version):

> God said to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM.” And he said, “Say this to the people of Israel: ‘I AM has sent me to you.’”

Here are some interpretations and reasons for this revelation:

1. Eternal Self-Existence: The name "I AM" suggests a being who exists in and of Himself, one who is eternal and unchanging. In other words, God's existence doesn't depend on anything else. He is the uncaused cause.

2. Ineffability of God: The name can be seen as a refusal to be defined. In many ancient cultures, knowing and using a deity's name could imply some measure of control or access to that deity. By declaring His name as "I AM," God might be emphasizing His transcendence and mystery.

3. Active Presence: While the name suggests eternality and unchangeability, it also implies a dynamic and active presence. "I AM" is present tense, suggesting a God who is always present with His people.

4. Hebrew Linguistic Roots: The name given, often transliterated as "YHWH" (and sometimes vocalized as "Yahweh"), is closely related to the Hebrew verb "to be." It's important to note that ancient Hebrew didn't have a future tense in the same way modern English does. So, "I AM" can also imply "I WILL BE," reinforcing the idea of God's continuous and future presence with Israel.

5. Contrast with Pagan Deities: Unlike the gods of the surrounding nations, who were often associated with specific aspects of nature or human endeavor, the God of Israel, by using the name "I AM," sets Himself apart as the one true God who encompasses all of reality.

The revelation of the name "I AM" to Moses is foundational for Judeo-Christian theology, emphasizing both the transcendence and immanence of God.

The interaction between Moses and God where God reveals His name as "I AM" occurs in the book of Exodus. It's a profound moment in the biblical narrative, rich in theological and philosophical implications. Let's explore the context and some interpretations:

**Context**:
Moses encounters God in the form of a burning bush on Mount Horeb. When Moses asks God what he should tell the Israelites when they ask for the name of the God who sent him, God responds in Exodus 3:14 (English Standard Version):

> God said to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM.” And he said, “Say this to the people of Israel: ‘I AM has sent me to you.’”

Here are some interpretations and reasons for this revelation:

1. Eternal Self-Existence: The name "I AM" suggests a being who exists in and of Himself, one who is eternal and unchanging. In other words, God's existence doesn't depend on anything else. He is the uncaused cause.

2. Ineffability of God: The name can be seen as a refusal to be defined. In many ancient cultures, knowing and using a deity's name could imply some measure of control or access to that deity. By declaring His name as "I AM," God might be emphasizing His transcendence and mystery.

3. Active Presence: While the name suggests eternality and unchangeability, it also implies a dynamic and active presence. "I AM" is present tense, suggesting a God who is always present with His people.

4. Hebrew Linguistic Roots: The name given, often transliterated as "YHWH" (and sometimes vocalized as "Yahweh"), is closely related to the Hebrew verb "to be." It's important to note that ancient Hebrew didn't have a future tense in the same way modern English does. So, "I AM" can also imply "I WILL BE," reinforcing the idea of God's continuous and future presence with Israel.

5. Contrast with Pagan Deities: Unlike the gods of the surrounding nations, who were often associated with specific aspects of nature or human endeavor, the God of Israel, by using the name "I AM," sets Himself apart as the one true God who encompasses all of reality.

The revelation of the name "I AM" to Moses is foundational for Judeo-Christian theology, emphasizing both the transcendence and immanence of God.

Topic 1 of 6
From Rabbinic Survival
to Modern Diversity
How did a Temple-centered tradition become a global, text-centered way of life?

Transition from slide 60: the destruction of the Second Temple ended the sacrificial system described on the previous slides, but it did not end Judaism. The center of religious life shifted toward Torah study, prayer, synagogue communities, and rabbinic interpretation.

Frame the next slides around adaptation rather than decline. Jewish communities repeatedly preserved continuity while responding creatively to life under new political powers and in new cultural settings. Textbook connection: sections 10-2e through 10-2g.

Jews under Islamic & Christian Rule
Muslim lands: protected but subordinate status; important centers flourished in Babylonia, North Africa, and Spain
Christian Europe: restrictions, ghettos, expulsions, and recurring violence varied by place and period
Diaspora communities: synagogues, schools, courts, charity, and kosher institutions sustained communal life
Distinct cultures: Sephardic and Ashkenazi traditions developed different languages, customs, and histories

Stress variation. Jewish life under Muslim rule was neither a uniform golden age nor unbroken persecution. Jews generally held a protected but unequal status, and some communities became major centers of scholarship, medicine, trade, and philosophy. Under Christian rule, conditions also varied, but restrictions on residence and occupations, forced displacement, and anti-Jewish violence were recurrent.

Define Sephardic broadly as traditions rooted in Iberia and later much of the Mediterranean and Middle East; define Ashkenazi as traditions rooted in Central and Eastern Europe. These are cultural-historical families, not separate religions. Textbook connection: section 10-2f.

Reason, Mysticism & Rabbinic Culture
Talmud: preserved debate over how Torah should guide daily life
Maimonides: joined Jewish law with philosophical reason in the medieval Mediterranean
Kabbalah: explored creation, divine presence, evil, and restoration through mystical symbols
Hasidism: emphasized joyful devotion, community, and experiencing God in ordinary life

The Babylonian Talmud became the central record of the oral Torah: generations of legal reasoning, stories, disagreements, and applications. Emphasize that rabbinic debate is part of the tradition, not evidence of a missing consensus.

Moses Maimonides (1135-1204) wrote both a major code of Jewish law and The Guide for the Perplexed, which addressed tensions between revelation and philosophy. Kabbalah developed several forms; the Zohar became especially influential. Eighteenth-century Hasidism, associated with the Baal Shem Tov, brought mystical ideas into a communal, emotionally expressive form. Textbook connection: section 10-2f and the “Closer Look: Kabbalah” feature.

Emancipation Transforms Jewish Identity
After 1800, civil rights opened education, professions, and citizenship to many European Jews
Assimilation: reduce or leave Jewish distinctiveness to enter majority society
Acculturation: participate in national culture while maintaining Jewish religion and community
Legal equality expanded, yet modern racial antisemitism and nationalist exclusion intensified

Emancipation meant freedom from older legal restrictions and communal controls. It created opportunity but also a difficult question: could Jews become full citizens of European states while remaining visibly and religiously Jewish?

Distinguish assimilation from acculturation. Assimilation can mean absorption into the majority culture and the weakening or abandonment of Jewish identity. Acculturation means adopting parts of the surrounding culture while retaining a distinct Jewish identity. Moses Mendelssohn is a useful example of engagement with European intellectual life while remaining Jewish. Close by noting the paradox: legal emancipation did not eliminate antisemitism. Textbook connection: section 10-2g.

Topic 2 of 6
Modern Jewish Movements
Reform
Tradition develops through history; ethical teachings and adaptation to modern life receive strong emphasis.
Orthodox
Written and oral Torah remain binding; modern life should be lived within halakhic tradition.
Conservative / Masorti
Jewish law remains authoritative while scholarship reveals historical development and permits measured change.
Shared question: What must remain binding—and what may change?

Present these as broad families, not three internally uniform camps. Reform developed first in nineteenth-century Germany. Modern Orthodoxy combined commitment to halakhah with selective engagement in modern society. Conservative Judaism, called Masorti in many countries, claimed a middle path that accepted both continuity and historical development.

Each movement contains internal diversity, and many Jews do not identify with any movement. Reconstructionist, Humanistic, Renewal, and other forms will appear on the North America slide. The teaching goal is comparison: authority of Torah and Talmud, openness to historical change, and patterns of worship and observance. Textbook connection: sections 10-2g and 10-6b.

Zionism & Jewish National Revival
Zionism: modern movement for Jewish national self-determination in the ancestral homeland
European antisemitism and nationalism gave urgency to Theodor Herzl’s 1897 Zionist Congress
Jewish migration and institution-building grew during Ottoman and British rule
Israel’s founding in 1948 realized Jewish statehood amid war, Palestinian displacement, and competing national claims

Define Zionism carefully as a family of Jewish nationalist movements, not as a synonym for Judaism or for every policy of the modern State of Israel. Zionists disagreed about religion, socialism, territory, culture, and the nature of the future state. Some religious Jews initially opposed secular Zionism; other Jews embraced it in religious or cultural forms.

The land was not empty. Arab Palestinians had longstanding communities and their own developing national movement. Israel’s establishment in 1948 brought sovereignty and refuge for many Jews while the war produced mass Palestinian displacement, remembered by Palestinians as the Nakba. Keep the distinction among Judaism, Jewish peoplehood, Zionism, and Israeli government policy visible throughout discussion. Textbook connection: section 10-2g.

Topic 3 of 6
The Holocaust / Shoah
Nazi racial ideology defined Jews as an enemy to be excluded, dispossessed, and destroyed
Persecution escalated from discriminatory laws to ghettos, mass shootings, deportations, and killing centers
Nazi Germany and its collaborators murdered approximately six million Jews
Jewish responses included flight, mutual aid, spiritual resistance, armed resistance, and survival under impossible conditions

Shoah, meaning catastrophe or destruction, is a common Hebrew term. Explain the escalation without making genocide sound automatic or inevitable: laws and propaganda normalized exclusion; war created opportunities for mass violence; governments, institutions, collaborators, and individuals made choices at each stage.

Do not reduce Jewish experience to passive victimhood. Resistance included preserving learning and worship, documenting events, smuggling food, organizing aid, escaping, joining partisan units, and armed uprisings such as Warsaw. Avoid asking why victims did not resist; focus instead on the overwhelming coercive power facing them and the many forms resistance took. Textbook connection: section 10-2h.

Jewish Responses after the Holocaust
Crisis of faith: How can covenant, divine justice, and human freedom be understood after Auschwitz?
Plural responses: protest against God, rejection of belief, renewed commitment, lament, and theological reconstruction
Memory: testimony, museums, ritual remembrance, and education resist denial and restore individual lives to history
Continuity: rebuilding communities became a response to the attempt to erase Jewish life

There is no single Jewish theology of the Holocaust. Richard Rubenstein argued that inherited concepts of an active covenantal God could not remain unchanged. Emil Fackenheim insisted that Jews must not grant Hitler a posthumous victory by abandoning Jewish existence. Other thinkers emphasized divine hiddenness, human freedom, protest, lament, or the limits of all explanation.

Avoid presenting the Holocaust as divine punishment; that framing can blame victims and cannot represent the diversity of Jewish responses. Emphasize memory as an ethical practice: preserving names, testimony, communities, and choices prevents victims from becoming an anonymous number. Textbook connection: section 10-2h.

Topic 4 of 6
Worship in the Synagogue
Ark: sacred cabinet holding Torah scrolls, oriented toward Jerusalem when possible
Service: blessings, hymns, Hebrew prayer, Torah reading, interpretation, and communal response
Leadership: cantor leads musical prayer; rabbi teaches and interprets; community participation remains central
Minyan: prayer quorum traditionally counted as ten adult Jewish men; many communities count adults of all genders

The synagogue is simultaneously a house of prayer, study, and community. The Torah scroll is treated with great care: worshippers stand when it is removed from the ark, readers chant from it, and a pointer helps avoid touching the parchment.

Practices differ across communities. Orthodox synagogues generally separate men and women and traditionally count men for a minyan. Conservative, Reform, Reconstructionist, and many other communities generally include women equally, though practice varies. A rabbi is primarily a teacher and legal-religious guide, not a priest who performs sacrifice. Textbook connection: section 10-5a.

Shabbat as Lived Practice
Shabbat extends from Friday sunset to Saturday night
Home rituals may include candle lighting, blessings over wine, challah, a festive meal, and song
Synagogue worship centers prayer, Torah reading, study, and community
Rest from ordinary labor creates sacred time for God, family, dignity, and renewal

Connect this slide to slides 55-57: the earlier slides explain the commandment; this slide shows how the commandment is embodied. Practices range from strict halakhic observance to selective family traditions.

Traditional restrictions on work are detailed and interpreted through rabbinic law. Rather than presenting Shabbat as merely a list of prohibitions, stress its positive meaning: welcoming sacred time, freedom from production, communal belonging, and a weekly rehearsal of creation and liberation. The home is as important as the synagogue. Textbook connection: section 10-5b.

Jewish holidays begin at sunset and follow a lunisolar calendar, so their dates move relative to the Gregorian calendar. Avoid treating every festival as equally important: Yom Kippur is the most solemn holy day, while Passover is especially central in home ritual and collective memory.

The Passover seder uses foods, questions, storytelling, and ritual actions to make participants experience the Exodus as their own story. Hanukkah is culturally prominent in North America but is not the Jewish equivalent of Christmas. The three groupings on the slide organize the calendar thematically rather than chronologically. Topic 5 of 6
Kashrut: Making Food Sacred
Kosher means fit or proper according to Jewish dietary law
Permitted land animals chew the cud and have split hooves; aquatic animals require fins and scales
Meat requires prescribed slaughter and preparation; meat and dairy are kept separate
Passover adds restrictions on leavened grain products and often requires separate preparation

Correct a common misconception: rabbis do not make food kosher by blessing it. Certification verifies ingredients and preparation. Kashrut concerns species, slaughter, processing, utensils, kitchens, and combinations of food.

Observance varies widely. Some Jews maintain fully separate meat and dairy kitchens; some avoid only pork and shellfish; others do not observe dietary law. Present the system as a way of making ordinary consumption intentional and connected to covenant, not as an ancient hygiene code. Passover kashrut is stricter because leavened grain products are excluded. Textbook connection: section 10-5d.

Life Cycle: Covenant, Community & Family
Brit milah / naming: welcomes a child into covenant and community; practices differ by family and movement
Bar or bat mitzvah: marks responsibility for the commandments and participation in communal worship
Marriage: covenant formalized under a canopy, often with a ketubah and the breaking of a glass
Rituals connect personal transitions to Torah, historical memory, family, and the Jewish people

Brit milah is traditionally performed for boys on the eighth day and recalls the Abrahamic covenant. Jewish communities also use naming ceremonies for girls and have developed additional inclusive practices. Avoid implying that one ritual pattern represents every Jewish family.

Bar/bat mitzvah literally describes becoming responsible for commandments, not merely having a party. The young person may chant Torah, lead prayers, and offer interpretation after extensive preparation. A Jewish wedding may include a ketubah, blessings beneath a chuppah, and breaking a glass. Interpretations of the glass vary; remembrance of the destroyed Temple is traditional. Textbook connection: sections 10-5e through 10-5g.

Death, Mourning & Memory
Burial is traditionally prompt, simple, and equal in dignity
Shiva: seven-day period in which mourners withdraw from ordinary life and receive the community
Kaddish: prayer praising God, recited during an extended period of mourning
Yahrzeit and memorial practices keep the deceased present in family and communal memory

Jewish mourning practices serve the living while honoring the dead. Traditional burial avoids display and emphasizes equality. Shiva structures the first intense period of grief and shifts ordinary obligations so the community can care for mourners.

The Mourner’s Kaddish does not describe death; it magnifies and sanctifies God’s name. This makes it both an affirmation and, for some mourners, a difficult act of faith. Mourning decreases in stages rather than ending abruptly. Practices vary among movements and families, particularly regarding cremation, autopsy, organ donation, gender roles, and the length of observance. Textbook connection: section 10-5h.

Topic 6 of 6
Judaism in Israel Today
Jewish Israelis commonly describe identity along a spectrum: Haredi, religious, traditional, and secular
Religious practice may remain meaningful even for people who identify as secular
Orthodox institutions retain major influence over marriage, divorce, conversion, and public religious life
Debates over religion and state reveal competing definitions of Jewish identity and democratic citizenship

Israeli categories do not map neatly onto North American denominations. Many people describe themselves by degree of observance rather than as Reform, Conservative, or Orthodox. A secular Israeli may still attend a Passover seder, fast on Yom Kippur, light Shabbat candles, or choose an Orthodox synagogue for occasional worship.

Religion-and-state debates concern marriage, divorce, conversion, military service, public transportation and commerce on Shabbat, synagogue recognition, and funding. Avoid presenting Israeli Jews as a single political or religious bloc. Source context: textbook section 10-6a and the Israel Democracy Institute’s 2024 Religion and State statistical report.

Judaism in North America Today
Communities include Reform, Conservative, Orthodox, Reconstructionist, Humanistic, Renewal, and unaffiliated Jews
Jewish identity may be religious, ethnic, cultural, familial—or several at once
Pew’s 2020 survey: 27% identified as Jews of no religion; 32% identified with no denominational branch
Intermarriage and continuity debates coexist with Orthodox growth and new forms of cultural participation

North American denominational labels are more common than Israeli ones, but they still do not describe everyone. Pew’s 2020 survey found 37% of U.S. Jewish adults identifying as Reform, 17% Conservative, 9% Orthodox, about 4% with smaller movements, and 32% with no particular branch. The same study classified 27% as Jews of no religion.

Do not equate “no religion” with “no Jewish identity.” Respondents may understand Jewishness through ancestry, family, ethics, food, language, memory, or peoplehood. Pew also found high intermarriage among recently married non-Orthodox Jews alongside a younger and growing Orthodox population. Source context: textbook section 10-6b; Pew Research Center, Jewish Americans in 2020.

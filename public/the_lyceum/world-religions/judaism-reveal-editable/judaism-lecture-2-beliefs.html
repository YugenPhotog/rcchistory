Beliefs, doctrines, ethics, and symbols in Judaism

Open by locating this lecture within the three-deck sequence. Lecture 1 follows historical development; this lecture asks what theological and ethical claims hold the tradition together.

Tell students that ethical monotheism is the organizing spine: one God, one humanity, covenantal obligation, holiness, justice, and hope.

Section I
One God & Ethical Monotheism
What must God be like for morality to be grounded in the divine?

This section moves from the Shema and divine names to the philosophical and ethical implications of one God. Keep returning to the distinction between divine power and divine goodness.

Teachings of Judaism – One God
Deut 6:4 "Hear, O Israel: YHWH is our God, YHWH is one"
Tetragrammaton – YHWH
Related to verb hayah – “to be;” essence of Hebrew deity expressed in name, existence, self-existence, uncreated
Observant Jews do not pronounce nor read it aloud
Instead they substitute titles, Adonai, usually translated as lord
Judaism teaches strict monotheism
Shema Yisrael: “Hear O Isreal” Jewish Prayer and encapsulates the monotheistic essence of Judaism
 
“Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is One”

The Shema is the clearest entry into Jewish monotheism: “Hear, O Israel” is both confession and prayer. Its point is not merely that one deity exists, but that Israel owes undivided loyalty to YHWH.

Observant Jews recite the Shema morning and evening. Connect belief to practice: the doctrine of one God is rehearsed daily, taught to children, written in mezuzot, and embodied in covenant loyalty. Deuteronomy 6:4-9.

Teachings of Judaism – One God
Why the cryptic name?
Names for God are an important aspect of Jewish teaching about God
Eternal Self-Existence
Ineffability of God
Active Presence
Hebrew Linguistic Roots
Contrast to Pagan Deities
 

Exodus 3 resists treating the divine name like the name of a manageable local deity. “I AM” or “I WILL BE” suggests self-existence, continuing presence, and freedom from human control.

Explain that the Hebrew consonants YHWH are called the Tetragrammaton. Traditional Jews avoid pronouncing the name, substituting Adonai in prayer or HaShem in ordinary speech. The restraint communicates reverence and divine ineffability.

Titles of Deity
El – God
El Shaddai (God of the Mountain or God Almighty)
El Elyon (Most High God)
 
Adonai (My Lord)
 
HaShem (The Name)
 
Hakadosh Baruch Hu (The Holy One, blessed be He)
 

These titles emphasize different aspects of divine relationship and majesty. El is a general Semitic term for God; El Shaddai is traditionally rendered God Almighty; El Elyon means God Most High; Adonai means Lord; HaShem means The Name.

Do not present the list as separate gods. The titles are different ways of addressing or describing the one God.

Ethical Monotheism
Most significant contribution to world religion and history
Definitions
 
Belief in one and only one god
Monotheism
Belief in one and only one god who is the source of morality and
demands goodness, i.e., ethical conduct
Ethical Monotheism

Define the distinction precisely. Monotheism is belief in one God. Ethical monotheism is belief in one God whose character is morally good and who therefore places moral obligations on human beings.

This is the lecture’s central concept. Ethical conduct is not an optional supplement to worship; it is a response to the nature and will of God.

Ethical Monotheism
God places moral obligations on followers
God is a law-giver God, not capricious or arbitrary
Significant implication is
One God means One Human Race
No person or group is intrinsically more valuable than any other
One moral standard for all people (Universal morality)

Follow the argument step by step: if one God created all humanity, then the human race forms one moral community. No people possesses a different creator or a separate moral universe.

Chosenness therefore cannot coherently mean that other people lack value. Jewish sources usually interpret chosenness as a distinctive covenantal vocation and burden of obligation.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism
What does God need to be like for this belief to be meaningful? Contrast the Hebrew God to the polytheistic gods of the day

Use comparison carefully. Many ancient religions associated gods with natural forces, cities, dynasties, and peoples. Israelite religion increasingly described YHWH as sovereign over nature and history rather than one force within them.

Avoid a simplistic progression from “primitive polytheism” to “advanced monotheism.” Polytheistic traditions also developed sophisticated ethics. The distinctive Jewish claim is that a single universal God grounds a unified moral order.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism
God is Transcendent -- “God created the Heavens and the Earth” (Genesis 1:1)
God is above and separate from Nature (not a part of it)
Nature is finite – God is infinite
Implication: God is Eternal and Unchanging (Psalms 102:25-27.)
Nature is amoral – God is the source of morality
Implication: Desacralize nature – foolishness to worship the creation instead of the Creator (Psalms 96:4-5.)

Transcendence means that God is not identical with nature. Creation depends upon God; God does not depend upon creation. This makes nature meaningful and good without making it divine.

The ethical implication is crucial: what happens in nature is not automatically what ought to happen morally. Human beings can resist cruelty, exploitation, and “survival of the fittest” because moral obligation comes from beyond natural power.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism
God is Personal
God cares about His creations
Created in His image with ability to know God
 

Transcendence does not make God remote. The covenantal God speaks, remembers, judges, forgives, and enters relationship. Jewish prayer assumes that God can be addressed and that human action matters.

Connect this to the image of God: humans possess reason, moral awareness, freedom, and the capacity for relationship.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism
God is Good
God rules by moral standards

Ethical monotheism requires more than divine power. If God were arbitrary or morally capricious, divine commands could not establish a trustworthy moral order.

Jewish tradition repeatedly describes God as just, compassionate, faithful, and merciful. Human goodness is therefore measured by resemblance to divine character, not merely by obedience to power.

Nature of Ethical Monotheism
God is Holy
Definition: spiritually pure, consecrated, devoted to God, set apart
Holiness is the elevation of the human being from his animal nature to his divine nature – created in God’s image
“Be ye therefore Holy, even as I am holy” (Lev. 19:2)

Holiness means being set apart for God’s purposes, but it also carries ethical content. Leviticus 19 links the command to be holy with obligations concerning parents, workers, the poor, neighbors, strangers, honesty, and justice.

Imago Dei grounds human dignity: every human being bears God’s image. The concept supports moral agency and the obligation to treat other people as persons of sacred worth.

Pause & Reflect
If one God created every person in the divine image, what obligations follow?

Give students time to move from theology to implication. Listen for universal dignity, one moral standard, care for strangers, limits on power, and the rejection of intrinsic racial or national superiority. Then transition: Judaism expresses those implications through covenant.

Section II
Covenant, Holiness & Moral Obligation
How does belief in one God become a way of life?

Shift from the nature of God to the human response. Covenant joins identity, law, ritual, ethics, memory, and vocation. Ethical monotheism becomes concrete through Torah and holiness.

Covenant People
Genesis 17: 7
History of Judaism begins with Abram
God chose Abram (and his descendants) to establish His covenant
Literally, a contract. In the Bible, an agreement between God and his people; two-way promise
Covenant

Covenant is the relational framework of Jewish theology. It joins divine initiative with human response: God promises presence and blessing; Abraham and his descendants are called to trust, obedience, and a distinctive way of life.

Stress that covenant is more than a commercial contract. It creates an enduring relationship, identity, and vocation.

Genesis 17 connects covenant with Abraham’s new name, descendants, land, divine protection, and circumcision as a sign. Genesis 12 adds the outward mission: all families of the earth are to be blessed through Abraham.

Present these promises alongside their obligations. The covenant calls Abraham’s descendants to worship, obedience, justice, and holiness.

Chosen People
Deuteronomy 10: 15
Both sides of phrase important
People implies Judaism is both a religion and an ethnicity
And an important part of Jewish religion and identity is the concept of “Chosenness” or covenant
Being chosen brings a call to be holy
Choice is based on God’s love
Helped sustain themselves throughout a long and difficult history

Both words matter: chosen people. The phrase names a people formed by covenant, memory, and obligation—not simply individual believers sharing doctrines.

Chosenness is easily misunderstood as superiority. In Jewish theology it normally means being chosen for Torah, holiness, service, and responsibility. Amos 3:2 even treats election as the basis for stricter judgment.

Be ye therefore Holy, even as I am holy
Imitatio Dei (Imitation of God)
Rashi – links holiness to abstaining from sexual immorality and idolatry
Nahmanides – goes beyond just refraining from forbidden acts; the call to holiness requires ethical and moral behavior that transcends legalistic adherence
Midrash - "You shall be holy" is a comprehensive commandment from which many other commandments can be derived. The implication is that the pursuit of holiness permeates and underlies all aspects of religious observance.
Holiness in Everyday Life -- radical concept; everybody not just priests, can be holy in every action they undertake 
 

Imitatio Dei means imitating God. Rashi connects holiness with separation from idolatry and sexual wrongdoing. Nahmanides argues that technical legality is not enough: a person can obey individual rules while remaining selfish or exploitative.

The broader rabbinic point is that holiness demands formation of character, not merely avoidance of prohibited acts.

Holiness is accessible to every Jew in daily life
radical concept; everybody not just priests, can be holy in every action they undertake 
Intertwined nature of ritual, ethics, and spirituality

Leviticus 19 deliberately interweaves ritual and ethics. Sabbath, sacrifices, harvest rules, wages, disability, courts, speech, sexuality, neighbors, and strangers all belong to one holy life.

This resists a modern division between “religious” behavior and “ordinary” morality. Everyday economic and interpersonal choices become arenas of holiness.

The Ten Commandments
See Exodus 20
Divided between obligation to God and duty to others
 

The Ten Commandments provide a compact moral architecture rather than the whole of Jewish law. The first obligations order Israel’s relationship with God; the later commands regulate family, life, sexuality, property, truth, and desire.

Jewish numbering differs slightly from some Christian traditions. The opening statement—God who brought Israel out of slavery—is foundational.

And God spake all these words, saying,
 
I am the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.

The commandments begin with liberation: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of Egypt.” Obedience is therefore a response to freedom, not the price paid to earn it.

Develop the political implication: a free society requires moral self-government. People liberated from Pharaoh must learn not to become oppressors of one another.

The Ten Commandments
Hebrew original, “do not carry the name of the Lord thy God in Vain” or “do not misuse the name of the Lord thy God.”
God will not forgive violation of this commandment
Committing evil in God’s name

Thou shalt not take the name of the Lord thy God in vain; for the Lord will not hold him guiltless that taketh his name in vain.

The Hebrew command can mean not carrying, invoking, or using God’s name falsely. It includes perjury, manipulation, and attaching divine authority to wrongdoing.

This is an important ethical-monotheist claim: evil committed in God’s name contradicts the character of the God being invoked and damages religion’s moral witness.

Ethical Monotheism & Social Justice
The Vulnerable
Widow, orphan, stranger, poor, laborer, and debtor receive special protection.
The Prophets
Amos, Isaiah, and Micah condemn worship that coexists with exploitation and corrupt courts.
The Standard
Justice, mercy, honesty, and protection of the stranger become covenant obligations.
Ritual without justice is not faithfulness to the God of Israel.

This slide supplies the major gap in the original deck. Ethical monotheism becomes socially concrete in the Torah and prophets. Exodus repeatedly links care for strangers to Israel’s memory of having been strangers in Egypt.

Amos 5 rejects festivals and songs when justice is absent. Isaiah 1 links worship with defending the orphan and widow. Micah 6:8 summarizes the demand as justice, covenant loyalty or mercy, and humble walking with God. The point is not that ritual is unimportant, but that ritual cannot compensate for oppression.

Pause & Reflect
Is ethical monotheism primarily a belief—or a practice? Defend your answer.

The strongest answer recognizes that the distinction is artificial in Judaism. Belief about God generates covenantal conduct; conduct testifies to the character of God. Use student responses to reinforce the integration of ritual, ethics, and spirituality.

Section III
Hope, the World to Come & Symbolism
How does Judaism express hope and preserve identity across time?

The final section is shorter. It adds textbook coverage missing from the original deck: afterlife and major symbols, while retaining the messianic material.

Teachings of Judaism – Messiah
Lit. “anointed one”
A savior and redeemer of the Jewish people; future king from the Davidic line
See Isaiah 9:6-7; 11:1-9
“government upon his shoulder”
“upon the throne of David”
Jews looking for a political savior; one who will re-assert Jewish sovereignty
 

Messiah means anointed one. In classical Jewish expectation the messiah is a future human ruler from David’s line who restores justice, peace, and the security of Israel.

Normative Judaism does not identify the messiah as God incarnate. Jewish interpretations vary: some retain a personal messiah, while others emphasize a future messianic age created through divine and human action.

Four Biblical “Hells”
Sheol
Hebrew Bible: the grave or shadowy realm of the dead—not necessarily punishment.
Hades
Greek term often used to translate Sheol; broadly, the realm of the dead.
Gehenna
From the Valley of Hinnom; later imagery of divine judgment, punishment, or purification.
Tartarus
Greek prison of rebellious divine beings; used once in the New Testament, not as a standard Jewish afterlife.

Different words, images, and historical settings—not four identical versions of eternal hell.

Begin with a translation warning. English Bibles sometimes render several different words as “hell,” which makes separate concepts look identical. Sheol is the main Hebrew Bible term and usually describes the destination or condition of the dead without a developed doctrine of eternal punishment. Hades is its common Greek counterpart.

Gehenna develops from the Valley of Hinnom near Jerusalem and becomes an image of divine judgment in later Jewish and Christian literature. Tartarus belongs to Greek mythic vocabulary and appears once in the New Testament for imprisoned rebellious angels; it is not a normal Jewish destination for human souls.

The embedded video is useful comparative biblical context, but remind students that it crosses from Jewish scripture into Christian scripture. Its four terms should not be presented as one uniform Jewish doctrine of the afterlife.

Resurrection & the World to Come
Development: early biblical texts emphasize Sheol; Daniel 12 clearly anticipates resurrection and judgment
Rabbinic affirmation: techiyat ha-metim, the revival of the dead, becomes a classical Jewish teaching
Olam Ha-Ba: “the world to come” may refer to resurrected life, the messianic age, or a spiritual afterlife
Modern diversity: Orthodox Judaism generally retains bodily resurrection; liberal interpretations often emphasize immortality or symbolism
Resurrection answers a moral question: will death have the final word over justice?

The Hebrew Bible does not offer one uniform afterlife doctrine. Daniel 12:2 is the clearest early statement that at least some of the dead will awaken for judgment. The belief becomes important in Pharisaic and rabbinic Judaism, while the Sadducees rejected it.

Techiyat ha-metim means revival of the dead. Classical Jewish liturgy praises God as the one who revives the dead, and Maimonides includes resurrection among his principles. Olam Ha-Ba, the world to come, is not always used with one precise meaning: it can refer to the messianic future, resurrected existence, or postmortem spiritual life.

Modern Jewish movements differ. Orthodox Judaism traditionally affirms bodily resurrection. Conservative or Masorti Jews may understand the liturgical language literally, spiritually, or metaphorically. Many Reform, Reconstructionist, Humanistic, secular, and unaffiliated Jews interpret resurrection symbolically, favor ideas such as spiritual immortality or moral legacy, or simply do not affirm a supernatural afterlife. Avoid describing every non-Orthodox position as an explicit rejection: for many Jews, resurrection is simply not emphasized or is left open. Judaism commonly places greater emphasis on faithful conduct in this life than on detailed maps of the next.

Symbols of Jewish Identity

Menorah
Ancient Temple lampstand; signifies sacred light and Israel’s vocation as a light to the nations.

Hebrew for “life”; expresses Judaism’s affirmation of life and the toast l’chaim.

The menorah is the oldest of these symbols and comes from the Jerusalem Temple. Distinguish its seven branches from the nine-branched Hanukkah lamp.

The six-pointed Star of David became a widespread Jewish emblem only in the early modern and modern periods; Zionism adopted it in 1897. Chai consists of the Hebrew letters chet and yod and means life. Its numerical value is eighteen, which is why gifts in multiples of eighteen are common.

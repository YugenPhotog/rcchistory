Q4 story · Beijing · December 1915

In December 1915 the President of China Accepted the Throne

On 12 December 1915 Yuan Shikai, president of the republic, agreed to become emperor.
A hand-picked assembly of 1,993 delegates had voted for monarchy without a single no.
His reign was to be called Hongxian, "Grand Constitution," starting 1 January 1916.
Coins were struck with his face, but within three months he gave the throne up.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Ernest P. Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: Tianjin mint, 1916.

Start with the strangest moment. Four years after the last emperor abdicated, the president of the Republic of China agreed to become emperor himself. Ebrey says: "When in August 1915 he announced that he would become emperor, the educated and politically aware elite were outraged" (Ebrey, p. 419).

The dates were a little different. In August 1915 Yuan's supporters formed a society to promote monarchy, and his American adviser, the political scientist Frank Goodnow, wrote that monarchy might suit China better than a republic. In the autumn a convention of delegates, chosen under Yuan's control, voted 1,993 to none for monarchy. On 12 December 1915 Yuan accepted. The new reign title was Hongxian, "Grand Constitution."

The coin shows Yuan in his plumed field marshal's hat with the imperial dragon on the back. This lecture asks how a republic came to this, and what happened next. Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419) frames the first half.

Source beyond Ebrey: Ernest P. Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: Yuan's throne showed how little the 1911 revolution had settled about who would rule China, and how.

Check yourself: Why was the vote of 1,993 to none not a sign of real support?

Recap · Yuan Shikai before 1912

Recap: In 1898 Yuan Helped Cixi Crush the Reformers, and Then He Built China's Best Army

In 1898 the reformers asked Yuan to help them; instead he warned Cixi's side · Ch. 18 L4 · the coup ↗
After 1901 he commanded the Northern Army and ran the Baoding Military Academy · Ch. 24 L1 · Yuan's army ↗
In 1911 the court and the revolutionaries both needed him · Ch. 24 L1 · the abdication ↗
Ebrey: he wanted "a strong China but not a republican one."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 418–419; Ch. 18 L4 and Ch. 24 L1. Image: Fu Sheng, Tianjin, about 1900.

You have met Yuan Shikai twice already. In 1898, during the Hundred Days of reform, the reformers hoped Yuan's troops would protect the Guangxu emperor against the Empress Dowager. Yuan went to Cixi's side instead, and the reform movement was crushed. The first link takes you back to that coup in Chapter 18.

After 1901 Yuan became the most powerful general in China, as you saw in the Pierian Spring of the last lecture. His officers owed their careers to him. In 1911 the court called him back, and he bargained with both sides until the emperor abdicated and he became president.

The portrait shows him around 1900, in the dragon robe of a high Qing official. Ebrey's judgment is the key to this lecture: Yuan "had strong credentials as a reformer of the old, self-strengthening type," but "he was committed to a strong China but not a republican one" (Ebrey, p. 419).

Why this matters: The man chosen to lead the republic had spent his career serving a throne.

Q4 story · Nanjing and Beijing · 1912

Yuan Made the Deal: The Emperor Abdicated, Sun Stepped Aside, and Yuan Became President

The revolutionaries feared foreign intervention and could not beat Yuan's army.
So they bargained: if Yuan ended the monarchy, he would be president.
Puyi abdicated on 12 February 1912; Sun resigned the next day.
On 10 March 1912 Yuan took office in Beijing, not in the revolutionaries' capital, Nanjing.

Names in This Story

Yuan Shikai general and president, 1859–1916; pronounced "ywen shir-kye"

Sun Yatsen revolutionary leader, provisional president Jan.–Feb. 1912

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Images: K. T. Thompson studio, 1911; unknown photographer, 1912.

How did a Qing general become the president of a revolutionary republic? Ebrey explains: "The biggest fear of the revolutionaries was foreign intervention; to avoid that, they were willing to compromise. In the end, agreement was reached to establish a republic with Yuan as president" (Ebrey, p. 419).

Sun Yatsen had been sworn in at Nanjing as provisional president on 1 January 1912. But Yuan controlled the strongest army, and the foreign powers trusted him. So the deal was simple: Yuan would persuade the court to abdicate, and Sun would hand him the presidency. The abdication edict came on 12 February, and Sun resigned the next day.

The revolutionaries tried to make Yuan come south to Nanjing, away from his army. He refused, and on 10 March he took the oath in Beijing. The right-hand photograph shows him with his officers just afterward. The left shows Sun, who had worked for this revolution for seventeen years.

Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419) asks why the revolution failed to produce a stable republic. Part of the answer is here: the revolutionaries gave the presidency to a man who did not share their goals.

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: The republic began as a bargain with the man who held the guns.

Q4 story · The first elections · 1912–1913

In 1913 the Nationalists Won the Elections, and Their Leader Song Jiaoren Was Shot

Winter 1912–13: about 40 million men, roughly a tenth of the population, could vote.
Sun's new Nationalist Party won 269 of 596 seats in the House and 123 of 274 in the Senate.
Its organizer, Song Jiaoren, aged 30, expected to become prime minister and check Yuan's power.
On 20 March 1913 a gunman shot him at the Shanghai railway station; he died two days later.

Names in This Story

Song Jiaoren Nationalist organizer, 1882–1913; "sung jyow-run"

Nationalist Party (Guomindang) Sun's party, founded 1912; "gwo-min-dahng"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: unknown photographer, about 1912.

The republic held real elections. "Yuan did not prevent parliamentary elections from being held in 1913. However, when Sun Yatsen's new Nationalist Party won a plurality of the seats, Yuan was unwilling to accept the outcome" (Ebrey, p. 419). Voters had to be men with property or schooling, about 40 million of them.

The Nationalist Party, or Guomindang, had grown out of Sun's Revolutionary Alliance. Its real builder was Song Jiaoren, pronounced "sung jyow-run." He wanted a cabinet responsible to parliament, so that the prime minister, not the president, would run the government. With a plurality in both houses, he was the obvious choice for prime minister.

On the evening of 20 March 1913, as Song waited for the train to Beijing, a hired gunman shot him. He died on 22 March. "The shocked public assumed Yuan was responsible." The trail led to Yuan's own prime minister, though Yuan himself was never charged.

This is the first step in Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419): how Yuan began to subvert the republic.

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: The first party to win a national election in China lost its leader within weeks.

Q4 story · Beijing · April 1913

Yuan Borrowed £25 Million from Foreign Banks Without Asking Parliament

In April 1913 Yuan's cabinet signed a £25 million loan with banks from five powers.
Ebrey calls it "a $100 million loan from a foreign consortium."
It ran 47 years at 5 percent, secured on China's salt taxes, which foreigners would help collect.
Nationalist leaders protested that only parliament could approve it; the bankers signed anyway.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, doc. 197. Image: Deutsch-Asiatische Bank, 1913.

Next Yuan needed money, and he got it without parliament. "Then Yuan, without consulting the national assembly, negotiated a $100 million loan from a foreign consortium" (Ebrey, p. 419). The contract, signed in Beijing on the night of 26–27 April 1913, was for £25 million, roughly $120 million at the time.

The lenders were a group of British, French, German, Russian, and Japanese banks. The United States had pulled out a month earlier. The loan ran for 47 years at 5 percent and was secured on the salt tax, which foreign inspectors would help administer. After fees and old debts, Yuan received much less than the face value.

The new parliament had just met. The American minister reported that the Nationalist leaders of the Senate went to the bank that night to protest that a loan without the assembly's approval was unconstitutional. The bankers refused to listen.

The bond on the slide is one of thousands sold to European investors. For Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419), the point is that foreign money freed Yuan from parliament.

Source beyond Ebrey: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1913, doc. 197.

Why this matters: With foreign loans, Yuan could pay his army without the people's representatives.

Q4 story · The Yangzi provinces · July–September 1913

Seven Provinces Rose Against Yuan in 1913, and His Armies Crushed Them in Two Months

In June 1913 Yuan dismissed three Nationalist governors in the south.
In July seven provinces declared independence; Nationalists called it the Second Revolution.
Yuan's Northern Army beat them in weeks; Nanjing fell on 1 September.
Sun, Huang Xing, and other leaders fled once more to Japan.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: unknown photographer, before 1906.

After Song's murder and the loan, the Nationalists decided to fight. "By summer the Nationalist Party was organizing open revolt against Yuan, and seven provincial governments declared their independence. This second revolution ended in military rout, and Sun Yatsen and other Nationalist leaders once more fled to Japan" (Ebrey, p. 419).

Yuan moved first. In June 1913 he removed the Nationalist military governors of Jiangxi, Guangdong, and Anhui. In July the governor of Jiangxi, Li Liejun, rose against him, and Huang Xing, the man in the photograph, took command at Nanjing. Other southern provinces followed.

It was not a close fight. Yuan's divisions, paid partly with the new loan, moved down the railways. By early September Nanjing had fallen, and the revolt was over. Huang Xing, who had commanded the revolutionaries in 1911, fled to Japan with Sun.

For Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419), notice that the republic's founders now depended on armies too. When they lost on the battlefield, they lost in politics.

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: After 1913 the question of who ruled China was settled by soldiers, not by elections.

Q4 story · Beijing · 1913–1914

Yuan Outlawed the Nationalist Party and Abolished Every Assembly Down to the County

October 1913: with troops around the building, parliament elected Yuan president for five years.
November 1913: he outlawed the Nationalist Party and expelled its members from parliament.
January 1914: he dissolved parliament; in February the provincial assemblies.
A new constitution in May 1914 gave the president almost unlimited power.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: Sir Alexander Hosie collection, 1914.

With the rebels gone, Yuan took apart the republic's institutions one by one. Ebrey sums it up: "Yuan outlawed the Nationalist Party; in 1914 he abolished all assemblies down to the county level, trying to nip participatory democracy in the bud" (Ebrey, p. 419). The guide asks you to find the sentence beginning "Yuan outlawed the."

First Yuan made his own position legal. In October 1913 parliament elected him president, after several ballots, while crowds sent by his supporters surrounded the building. Then he banned the Nationalists and expelled them, which left parliament without a quorum. In January 1914 he dissolved it, and in February the provincial assemblies, the ones that had brought down the Qing.

In May 1914 a new "constitutional compact" gave the president sweeping powers, and a law that December made his term ten years, renewable, and let him name his own successor. The photograph shows him that year, in full uniform.

This is the heart of the "how" half of Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419).

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: Within two years of the abdication, China had no elected body left at any level.

Q4 concept · Autocracy

Concept: Autocracy Is One Man's Rule, Unchecked by Parliament, Parties, or Courts

Concept

Autocracy

A system of government in which one ruler holds supreme power, and no elected body, party, court, or law can effectively limit him.

Yuan kept the title of president, but by 1914 he ruled alone.
Ebrey: Yuan wanted "careful central planning" on the German and Japanese model.
He also extended schools, suppressed opium, and reformed the courts.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419.

You need one word for what Yuan built. An autocracy is a government in which one person holds supreme power, and nothing, not a parliament, a party, a court, or a written law, can stop him. The Qing emperors had been autocrats in this sense. So were the Russian tsars.

Yuan kept the outward forms of a republic. He was still called president, and he ruled under a constitution, but one he had written himself. Ebrey explains his thinking: "He believed in careful central planning of the sort Germany and Japan had shown could be effective... If local or provincial assemblies were empowered to act as they liked, how could China move rapidly toward a modern nation-state?" (Ebrey, p. 419).

That is why the verdict on Yuan is mixed. "Yuan did undertake some progressive projects, extending elementary education, suppressing opium cultivation, and promoting judicial reform." An autocrat can modernize; what he cannot do is share power.

Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419) asks how Yuan "consolidated his autocratic rule." Use this word in your answer.

Why this matters: Yuan's rule raised a question China would face again: can a strong state be built without democracy?

Check yourself: Name one good thing and one bad thing about Yuan's autocratic rule.

Q4 story · Beijing · January–May 1915

In 1915 Japan Presented Twenty-One Demands, and on 9 May Yuan Accepted Most of Them

In 1914 Japan, Britain's ally, seized Germany's holdings in Shandong.
On 18 January 1915 Japan's minister handed Yuan twenty-one secret demands in five groups.
Group 5 would have put Japanese advisers in China's government and police; after protests Japan dropped it.
On 7 May Japan sent an ultimatum; on 9 May Yuan accepted the rest.

Names in This Story

Twenty-One Demands Japan's secret demands on China, January 1915

National Humiliation Day 7 or 9 May, remembered from 1915

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, doc. 154. Image: Bain News Service, Library of Congress, undated.

While Yuan was crushing his rivals at home, Japan saw its chance abroad. "In 1915, when the European powers were preoccupied with their war, Japan took steps to strengthen its hand in China. It presented Yuan Shikai's government with the Twenty-One Demands, most of which entailed economic privileges in various regions of China" (Ebrey, p. 425).

The fifth group "would have made China, in effect, a protectorate of Japan by requiring that Japanese advisers be attached to key organs of the Chinese government, even the police." Yuan's government leaked the demands, and protests and boycotts swept China. "Japan dropped the last group but gave Yuan an ultimatum to accept the rest."

Ebrey says Yuan accepted on May 7. In fact Japan's ultimatum came on 7 May 1915, and Yuan accepted on 9 May, as the American records show. Both days were later remembered as National Humiliation Day. The man in the photograph, Hioki Eki, delivered the demands to Yuan in person.

You will meet these demands again in Lecture 4, where they lead to May Fourth. For Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419), notice that Yuan's strong state could not stand up to Japan.

Source beyond Ebrey: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, doc. 154.

Why this matters: Yuan's surrender to Japan cost him the patriotic support he needed for his next step.

Q4 story · The Temple of Heaven · 23 December 1914

Yuan Restored the Worship of Confucius and Offered the Emperor's Sacrifice at the Temple of Heaven

In 1914 Yuan restored the state rites to Confucius, which the republic had dropped.
Ebrey: he "announced that Confucianism would be made the state religion."
At the winter solstice of 1914 he offered the sacrifice to Heaven, a rite only emperors had performed.
Young people who had cheered the republic saw the old order coming back.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: unknown photographer, 1914.

Yuan also used old rituals to make his rule feel legitimate. Ebrey writes that "he was out of touch with the mood of younger people, especially when he announced that Confucianism would be made the state religion" (Ebrey, p. 419). Conservatives such as Kang Youwei's Confucian Society were campaigning for that, and in 1914 Yuan ordered officials to sacrifice to Confucius again.

Then came the most imperial rite of all. For centuries, only the Son of Heaven could offer the great sacrifice at the Temple of Heaven at the winter solstice. You saw the temple, occupied by foreign troops, in Chapter 18. On 23 December 1914 Yuan drove there in an armored car and performed the rite in robes designed for the occasion.

The photograph shows him at the far left, at the altar. The robes in the title photograph come from the same ceremony.

For Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419), this is how Yuan prepared the ground for the throne. For the young, it was a warning.

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: By borrowing the emperor's rituals, Yuan told everyone what he intended to become.

Q4 story · Yunnan to Beijing · December 1915–June 1916

The Provinces Revolted, Yuan Gave Up the Throne in March 1916, and in June He Died

On 25 December 1915 Yunnan, led by General Cai E, declared independence to protect the republic.
Guizhou, Guangxi, and other southern provinces joined; Yuan's own generals held back.
Japan and the European powers advised Yuan to give up the plan.
On 22 March 1916 he cancelled the empire; on 6 June he died, aged 56.

Names in This Story

Cai E general who led the Yunnan revolt, 1882–1916; "tsai uh"

Hongxian Yuan's reign title, 1916: "Grand Constitution"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977). Image: unknown photographer, before 1916.

The throne lasted less than a hundred days. Ebrey writes that the elite "were outraged, their protests dying down only after Yuan died unexpectedly in June 1916" (Ebrey, p. 419). The protests were also armed.

On 25 December 1915 the southwestern province of Yunnan declared independence. Its leaders, among them the young general Cai E, the man in the photograph, called their army the National Protection Army: it would protect the republic. Guizhou and Guangxi joined, then more provinces.

What sank Yuan was that his own side would not fight for him. His senior generals, including Duan Qirui and Feng Guozhang, hung back. Japan, Britain, and other powers warned him against the throne. On 22 March 1916 Yuan cancelled the empire and went back to being president. It was too late. He died of kidney failure on 6 June 1916.

Ebrey's "August 1915" is when the campaign for monarchy began; Yuan accepted the throne in December. This slide completes Reading Guide Q4 (p. 419).

Source beyond Ebrey: Young, The Presidency of Yuan Shih-k'ai (1977).

Why this matters: Yuan's fall left no one with the power to hold China together.

Recap · Armies and their generals · since the 1850s

Recap: Since the Taiping War, Chinese Armies Had Been Loyal to Their Own Generals

In the 1850s the Qing's own armies could not beat the Taiping.
Officials like Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang raised armies whose men were loyal to them · Ch. 18 L2 · regionalism ↗
Yuan Shikai's Northern Army grew from Li's army, and its officers were loyal to Yuan.
When Yuan died in 1916, each of his generals still had an army.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Ch. 18 L2 for the regional armies. Image: unknown photographer, 1871.

To understand what happened after June 1916, go back sixty years. In the 1850s the Qing's regular armies failed against the Taiping. The court let trusted officials such as Zeng Guofan, in the photograph, raise new armies in their home provinces. The soldiers were recruited through personal ties and paid by their commanders, so they were loyal to the men who led them. You studied this as regionalism in Chapter 18; the link takes you back.

The pattern lasted. Li Hongzhang's Huai Army passed its officers on to Yuan Shikai's Northern Army. Yuan's own officers, as you saw in Lecture 1, owed their careers to him. As long as Yuan lived, he held them together.

Then he died. Ebrey writes: "Without a central strongman, commanders in Yuan's old army, governors of provinces, and even gangsters built their own power bases" (Ebrey, p. 419). Reading Guide Q5 (p. 420) begins here.

Why this matters: China's warlords were the last stage of a sixty-year shift of military power from the throne to generals.

Q5 concept · Warlord

Concept: A Warlord Was a General Who Ruled a Territory with His Own Army

Concept

Warlord

A military commander who controls a territory through an army loyal to him personally, collects its taxes, and acts independently of any central government.

Zhang Zuolin, a former bandit, ruled Manchuria from Mukden (Shenyang).
Wu Peifu, a degree-holder turned officer, led the Zhili army in central China.
Each needed land to tax, railways to move troops, and foreign friends to supply guns.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 419–420; James E. Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975). Images: unknown photographer, before 1925; Time, 1924.

The word for these men is warlord. A warlord was a general who ruled a territory through an army that was loyal to him personally. He collected its taxes and made his own deals, whatever the government in Beijing said. Between 1916 and 1928 hundreds of commanders, large and small, fit that description.

Two of the biggest are on the slide. Zhang Zuolin, on the left, rose from leading a band of irregulars to rule all three provinces of Manchuria. Wu Peifu, on the right, had passed the lowest level of the old examinations before joining the army; he led the Zhili clique and was famous enough abroad to make the cover of Time in 1924.

Warlords were not all alike. Some built schools and roads; some were simply predators. Historians such as James Sheridan stress that all of them depended on armies, and armies depended on money. Reading Guide Q5 (p. 420) asks you to describe the conditions they created.

Source beyond Ebrey: James E. Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975).

Why this matters: After 1916 power in China belonged to whoever commanded soldiers.

Q5 story · Beijing · 1916–1928

In Beijing, Six Presidents and Twenty-Five Cabinets Came and Went as Warlords Fought for the Capital

Whoever held Beijing claimed to be the government; foreign powers recognized it.
Ebrey counts six presidents and twenty-five cabinets.
In July 1917 a general even put Puyi back on the throne, for twelve days.
The northern cliques fought major wars in 1920, 1922, and 1924; in 1923 Cao Kun bought the presidency from parliament.

Names in This Story

Duan Qirui premier, Anhui clique, 1865–1936; "dwan chee-ray"

Wu Peifu Zhili clique general, 1874–1939; "woo pay-foo"

Zhang Zuolin warlord of Manchuria, 1875–1928; "jahng dzwaw-lin"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 419–420; Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975). Image: unknown photographer, 1913.

There was still a government in Beijing, and foreign countries still dealt with it. But "a government of sorts was maintained in Beijing under the domination of whichever warlord held the region. It was hardly stable, however, with six different presidents and twenty-five successive cabinets" (Ebrey, p. 420).

At first the strongman was Duan Qirui, in the photograph, Yuan's most senior general, who served as premier and borrowed heavily from Japan. In July 1917 another general, Zhang Xun, whose soldiers still wore queues, marched into Beijing and restored the boy emperor Puyi. The restoration lasted twelve days.

After that the northern generals formed rival cliques. "For a while, the key struggle seemed to be for control of the north, as the strongest warlords waged highly destructive wars across north China." Duan's Anhui clique lost to Wu Peifu's Zhili clique in 1920; Wu fought Zhang Zuolin in 1922 and again in 1924. In 1923 Cao Kun, Wu's patron, made himself president by paying members of parliament about 5,000 silver dollars each for their votes.

Source beyond Ebrey: Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975).

Why this matters: The national government became a prize for the strongest army, not a power over the armies.

Q5 story · The villages · 1916–1928

Armies Lived Off the Land: Soldiers Seized Grain, Animals, and Men from the Villages

Armies grew from about half a million men in 1916 to well over a million by the mid-1920s.
Soldiers were often unpaid, so they took food, carts, and animals wherever they marched.
They seized farmers as porters and sometimes killed those who resisted.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975), p. 91. Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

For ordinary people, the warlord era meant soldiers. Ebrey writes that warlords "caused havoc in the countryside because the armies lived off the land, looting wherever they moved" (Ebrey, p. 420). Armies grew far beyond what any province could pay for, and unpaid soldiers fed themselves.

The quotation on the slide comes from a warlord officer, cited by the historian James Sheridan and by Ebrey. Read it closely. It describes a routine, "the classic way of raiding a village," told almost casually. The shots before dawn were meant to frighten. Other accounts that Sheridan collected tell of villagers beaten or killed when they resisted, and of young men taken to carry loads for the army.

The picture is an AI-generated illustration made for this deck, an interpretation, not a historical source: soldiers leading away a farmer's ox and his grain while the family watches. No photographer recorded a raid like this. Compare the picture with the officer's own words.

This is the "peasantry" half of Reading Guide Q5 (p. 420).

Source beyond Ebrey: Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975), p. 91.

Why this matters: In the warlord years, the people who paid for the wars were farmers who had no part in them.

Q5 story · Money and guns · 1916–1928

Warlords Invented New Taxes, Bought Foreign Guns, and Forced Peasants to Grow Opium

To buy weapons, warlords added new taxes and surcharges on land, trade, and goods.
In Sichuan some districts collected land tax years, even decades, in advance.
Foreign powers sold arms and backed their favorites, despite a 1919 arms embargo.
Opium, nearly wiped out in many provinces, came back when warlords made farmers plant poppies for revenue.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975). Image: unknown photographer, 1920s.

Armies cost money. "Because they also needed money to buy weapons, warlords instituted all sorts of new taxes. Foreign countries were more than willing to sell modern arms to the warlords, often backing their own favorite contender" (Ebrey, p. 420).

The taxes took many forms: surcharges on the land tax, tolls on goods moving by road or river, and forced loans from merchants. In Sichuan, the most divided province, rival commanders collected the land tax for future years; by the late 1920s some districts had paid many years ahead. The foreign powers agreed in 1919 not to ship arms to China, but the guns kept coming. Japan backed Duan Qirui and later Zhang Zuolin, and others had their own favorites.

Then there was opium. "Opium cultivation had been nearly eradicated in many places until the warlords entered the scene and forced peasants to grow it as a revenue source." The late Qing and Yuan had suppressed it. Now poppies paid for armies again.

The soldier in the photograph holds a German Mauser pistol, one of the most common imported weapons of the period. This slide answers the "social conditions" part of Reading Guide Q5 (p. 420).

Source beyond Ebrey: Sheridan, China in Disintegration (1975).

Why this matters: Warlord rule pushed the costs of war onto farmers and merchants and brought back opium.

Q5 story · Railways and rivers · 1916–1928

Warlords Wrecked the Railways and Let the Yellow River Dikes Decay

Armies seized trains to move troops, so trade and mail stopped for weeks.
Rail lines, bridges, and stations were cut or wrecked in the fighting.
No one maintained the dikes on the Yellow River, and floods followed.
When drought struck north China in 1920–21, about half a million people died.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Lillian M. Li, Fighting Famine in North China (2007). Image: Shenbao, 1921.

Reading Guide Q5 (p. 420) asks about infrastructure, and Ebrey's answer is blunt: "Warlords, not surprisingly, did little to maintain infrastructure or advance modernization. They disrupted rail lines and allowed the dikes on the Yellow River to deteriorate, leading to some catastrophic floods" (Ebrey, p. 420). The guide asks you to find the sentence beginning "Warlords, not surprisingly."

Railways were the warlords' main weapon, because they moved armies fast. So commanders seized locomotives and cars, stopped civilian traffic, and blew up track to slow their enemies. Merchants could not ship goods, and grain could not reach the cities. The Yellow River needed constant work on its dikes; when no government paid for it, the river broke through.

The map comes from Shenbao, China's leading newspaper, in 1921. A drought, not the warlords, caused that famine, but the historian Lillian Li shows how war and broken transport made relief harder. Chinese and foreign charities, not the government, organized most of it.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lillian M. Li, Fighting Famine in North China (2007).

Why this matters: The warlords spent China's resources on war and let the systems that protected people from disaster fall apart.

Check yourself: Why were railways so important to the warlords, and what did that mean for everyone else?

Q5 story · The frontiers · 1911–1924

Tibet and Mongolia Broke Away, and Japan Gained Ground in Manchuria

December 1911: Outer Mongolia declared independence under the Bogd Khan, with Russian backing.
1912–13: Tibet expelled the Chinese garrison, and the Dalai Lama declared independence.
Ebrey: Tibet fell "under British sway and Mongolia under Russian."
In Manchuria, Japan ran the South Manchuria Railway and dealt with Zhang Zuolin.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 419–420; Melvyn C. Goldstein, A History of Modern Tibet (1989). Images: Th. Parr, 1910; Marzan Sharav, before 1924.

Fragmentation reached the edges of the old empire too. "The outer regions of the Qing Empire, such as Tibet and Mongolia, declared their independence. Tibet soon fell under British sway and Mongolia under Russian. Manchuria was more and more dominated by Japan" (Ebrey, pp. 419–420).

Remember the Qing empire was a Manchu empire, and Mongols and Tibetans had been tied to the Qing emperor, not to "China." When the emperor fell, their leaders said the tie was broken. In December 1911 Mongol princes made the Bogd Khan, the leading lama of Mongolia, their ruler. In Tibet, Chinese troops were driven out in 1912, and in 1913 the Thirteenth Dalai Lama, back from exile in British India, declared Tibet independent.

Neither became fully independent in the eyes of the world. Russia and Britain each preferred an autonomous region under loose Chinese "suzerainty." Mongolia became a Soviet-aligned people's republic in 1924. In Manchuria, Japan's railway zone and army grew stronger, and Zhang Zuolin relied on Japanese goodwill.

Source beyond Ebrey: Melvyn C. Goldstein, A History of Modern Tibet (1989).

Why this matters: A weak, divided China could not hold the empire's frontiers, a problem every later government tried to reverse.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · Shanghai · 1872

Recap: China's First Modern Companies, Like Li Hongzhang's Steamship Line, Were Run by Officials

In 1872 Li Hongzhang founded the China Merchants Steam Navigation Company · Ch. 18 L3 · Li's projects ↗
Merchants put up money, but officials supervised and took the profits.
Ebrey: these were "not true capitalist" ventures.
Private Chinese industry stayed small until the 1900s.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Ch. 18 L3 for self-strengthening. Image: China Merchants Steam Navigation Company, 1901.

Start with what came before. "China had opened some modern enterprises as early as 1872, when Li Hongzhang had started the China Merchant Steamship Navigation Company; however, those were government-supervised and -supported ventures, not true capitalist ones" (Ebrey, p. 422).

You met Li's projects in Chapter 18; the link takes you back. The formula was "official supervision and merchant management." Merchants invested, but officials appointed the managers, used the firms for government business, and expected a share of the profits. The steamship company competed with British lines on the Yangzi and the coast, and Li added coal mines, a telegraph company, and a cotton mill.

The photograph shows the company's head office on the Shanghai Bund in 1901. It was a real modern business, but it depended on official favor.

Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422) asks what changed in the 1910s and 1920s. To answer it, you need this baseline: before 1900, modern industry in China was small and mostly in official hands.

Why this matters: China's first modern firms grew up under officials, so private capitalism started late.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · The treaty ports · 1895–1914

After 1895 Japan Could Build Factories in China, and the Other Powers Followed

The Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895) let Japanese open factories in the treaty ports · Ch. 18 L4 · Shimonoseki ↗
Under most-favored-nation clauses, Britain and the others gained the same right.
Chinese labor was very cheap by world standards, so foreign mills spread.
By 1914 a Chinese bourgeoisie of merchants, bankers, industrialists, and compradors had formed.

Names in This Story

bourgeoisie the urban middle and business class; "boor-zhwah-zee"

comprador Chinese manager for a foreign firm

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Ch. 18 L4 for the Treaty of Shimonoseki. Image: Arnold Wright, ed., 1908.

The second step came from defeat. "In 1895, Japan won the right to open factories in China. Other imperialist powers leaped at the chance to set up factories as well because labor costs in China were very low by international standards" (Ebrey, p. 422). The right was written into the Treaty of Shimonoseki, which you studied in Chapter 18; the link takes you back.

Because of the most-favored-nation clauses in the older treaties, whatever Japan won, Britain, France, Germany, and the rest won too. Foreign cotton mills, cigarette factories, and shipyards went up in Shanghai and other treaty ports. The photograph shows the works of one Japanese firm, published in 1908.

Chinese businessmen built factories too, and a new class formed. "By the eve of World War I, China had an emerging bourgeoisie made up of merchants, bankers, industrialists, compradors working for foreign firms, and overseas Chinese engaged in import-export." A comprador was a Chinese manager who handled a foreign firm's business with Chinese customers.

Why this matters: Modern industry came to China partly through imperialism, which is one reason later nationalists distrusted it.

Then came the lucky break. "World War I gave China's businesses and industries a chance to flourish. Britain, France, Germany, and Russia were preoccupied with what was happening in Europe and no longer had spare goods to export. Imports from the West thus dropped dramatically, giving Chinese manufacturers a chance to sell more profitably. At the same time, the demand for products from China increased, helping China's export industries" (Ebrey, p. 422). The guide asks you to find the sentence beginning "World War I gave."

The numbers are on the slide. "The number of Chinese textile mills increased from 22 in 1911 to 109 in 1921. Tonnage of coal produced grew from 13 million tons to 20 million tons between 1913 and 1919. Modern banking took off: between 1912 and 1923, the number of modern banks soared from 7 to 131." Electric light and telephones came "not only in major cities but also in county seats and even in market towns" (p. 424).

Notice the word "inadvertently" in Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422). No one planned this. A war on the other side of the world did for Chinese industry what no Chinese government had managed.

Why this matters: China's industrial take-off began during the worst years of its politics.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · The cities · 1904–1920s

Chambers of Commerce Gave the New Bourgeoisie a Voice, and Some Ran Their Cities

After 1904 the Qing allowed chambers of commerce, and they spread to cities large and small.
They spoke for merchants and industrialists to officials and the press.
After 1915, as the national government decayed, many ran city services: sanitation, schools, and police.
Shanghai's chamber opened a grand new hall in 1916.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Marie-Claire Bergère, The Golden Age of the Chinese Bourgeoisie, 1911–1937 (1989). Image: unknown photographer, 1916.

The new class organized. "In the first decade of the century, more and more chambers of commerce had been established in cities large and small, giving this bourgeoisie more of a voice in politics. With the deterioration of the national government after 1915, often the chambers of commerce took over running cities, seeing to sanitation, education, and police" (Ebrey, p. 422).

The Qing had authorized chambers of commerce in 1904 as part of its New Policies, hoping to harness merchant wealth. They became something more: bodies that elected their own leaders, petitioned governments, organized boycotts, and in hard times kept order. The photograph shows Shanghai's chamber opening its new hall in 1916.

The historian Marie-Claire Bergère called the years from about 1911 to 1927 the "golden age" of the Chinese bourgeoisie. With Beijing weak and the warlords busy, businessmen had more freedom than before or after.

This is the "social classes" part of Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422): a business class with its own institutions and voice.

Source beyond Ebrey: Marie-Claire Bergère, The Golden Age of the Chinese Bourgeoisie, 1911–1937 (1989).

Why this matters: Where the state failed, merchants and industrialists stepped in to govern their cities.

Ebrey includes a small table that says a lot about the new class. It shows what the Commercial Press, the great Shanghai publisher of textbooks and magazines, paid its editors each month between 1912 and 1927, depending on where they had studied (Ebrey, p. 422).

A graduate of a Chinese college started at 80 yuan a month. A graduate of a Japanese college got 100 to 120, and of a Japanese imperial college 150. A Western college degree was worth 200, and Harvard, Yale, Oxford, or Cambridge 250, more than three times the Chinese rate.

Ebrey explains: "Many of those who returned from study abroad took jobs in modern enterprises, where their foreign degrees brought prestige and often higher salaries." Remember the Boxer Indemnity scholarships that sent students to America; this is where many of them ended up.

For Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422), the table shows a new elite whose status came from modern, often foreign, education rather than the old examinations.

Why this matters: The new urban elite measured itself by foreign learning, a sign of how far China had moved from the exam system.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · Wuxi and Shanghai · 1901–1920

The Rong Brothers Built a Flour and Cotton Empire from Wuxi

The Rongs came from a merchant family in Wuxi, west of Shanghai.
They opened a flour mill in 1901 and another in 1913.
From 1914 to 1920 they built eight new factories and moved into cotton spinning.
People called them China's kings of flour and cotton.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 424; Sherman Cochran, Encountering Chinese Networks (2000). Images: unknown photographers; Rong Desheng, 1949.

The boom made new fortunes. "For instance, the Rong brothers, from a family of merchants in Wuxi, built a flour mill in 1901 and another in 1913. As opportunities opened up, they built eight new factories between 1914 and 1920, expanding into textiles" (Ebrey, p. 424).

Rong Zongjing and his younger brother Rong Desheng started with money from a small banking business. Their first flour mill, in Wuxi, used imported machinery. When the war cut off foreign flour, their Shanghai mills could hardly grind fast enough. In 1915 they opened their first cotton mill, Shenxin, in Shanghai.

They ran the business as a family firm, with relatives and men from Wuxi in key posts. The historian Sherman Cochran shows how firms like theirs used family and native-place networks to compete with foreign companies.

The younger brother's photograph dates from 1949. He stayed in China after the Communist victory, and his son Rong Yiren later became vice-president of the People's Republic. For Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422), the Rongs are your example of the new industrial bourgeoisie.

Source beyond Ebrey: Sherman Cochran, Encountering Chinese Networks (2000).

Why this matters: Chinese entrepreneurs, not only foreigners, built modern industry during the war years.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · The mills · 1910s–1920s

Children Worked Twelve-Hour Days, Seven Days a Week, in the New Mills

Ebrey: conditions were as bad as in Britain a century earlier.
Workers put in twelve-hour days, seven days a week; children worked, especially in textile mills.
Labor contractors recruited village girls and kept them in debt, with minimal food and housing.
Many mills were foreign-owned, increasingly Japanese, which sharpened anger.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 424; Emily Honig, Sisters and Strangers: Women in the Shanghai Cotton Mills, 1919–1949 (1986). Image: Duran, Raw Silk, 1921.

The boom had a price. "Industrialization had its predictable costs as well. Conditions in China's factories in the 1910s were as bad as they had been a century earlier in Britain, with twelve-hour days, seven-day weeks, and widespread child labor, especially in textile mills" (Ebrey, p. 424).

"Labor contractors often recruited in the countryside and kept laborers in conditions of debt slavery, providing the most minimal housing and food." The historian Emily Honig, who interviewed former mill women in Shanghai, describes how contractors paid poor families a small sum for a daughter's labor for several years, then kept most of her wages.

The photograph, published in an American buyer's handbook in 1921, shows a Chinese silk filature, where girls stood all day over basins of hot water to unwind cocoons. Look at the faces of the two girls in front; they are young teenagers or younger. The handbook printed the picture to show efficiency, not hardship.

"That many of the factories were foreign owned (increasingly Japanese owned) added to management-labor friction." Those tensions will explode in Lecture 4. For Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422), this is the working class the boom created.

Source beyond Ebrey: Emily Honig, Sisters and Strangers: Women in the Shanghai Cotton Mills, 1919–1949 (1986).

Why this matters: China's industrial growth rested on the cheap labor of village girls and children.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · Shanghai · 1917

Shanghai's Great World Offered Six Floors of Opera, Acrobats, and Peep Shows

In 1917 Huang Chujiu, who had grown rich selling patent medicines, opened the Great World.
It was billed as the Crystal Palace and Coney Island in one, but its customers were mostly Chinese.
Floors held magicians, sing-song girls, barbers, earwax extractors, acupuncturists, storytellers, and a temple.
On top: tightrope walkers, mahjong, lottery tickets, and marriage brokers.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 421. Image: unknown publisher, 1930s.

The new money made a new kind of city life. Ebrey's Material Culture feature describes it: "In 1917 an entrepreneur who had made his fortune in medicine built the Great World, a six-story amusement park touted as the Crystal Palace and Coney Island rolled into one" (Ebrey, p. 421). The entrepreneur was Huang Chujiu, who sold a famous "brain tonic."

From outside it looked European, "an agglomeration of European building motifs with columns holding up a decorative tower." Inside, "it catered more to Chinese tastes." The first floor had "gaming tables, slot machines, magicians, acrobats, and sing-song girls"; others had restaurants, opera troupes, "midwives, barbers, and earwax extractors," masseurs, acupuncturists, "peep shows, scribes who composed love letters, and a temple." On the roof were "tightrope walkers, places to play mahjong, lottery tickets, and marriage brokers."

The postcard shows the building in the 1930s, with its tiered tower. For Reading Guide Q8 (p. 422), think of the Great World as the leisure side of the boom: a mass audience with money to spend.

Why this matters: Modern consumer culture in China began in places like this, mixing the foreign and the familiar.

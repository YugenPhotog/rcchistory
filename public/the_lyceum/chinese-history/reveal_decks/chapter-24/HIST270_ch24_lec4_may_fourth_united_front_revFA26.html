The Day the Students Marched

May Fourth, the Soviet Alliance, and the March North

1914–1927 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome to Lecture 4 of Chapter 24, the last lecture of the chapter. It runs from 1914 to 1927, from a war in Europe to an army marching north (Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 425, 428–429).

There are two parts. The first is the May Fourth Incident of 1919, when Beijing students protested a peace treaty. The second is the alliance Sun Yatsen made with Soviet Russia and the small Chinese Communist Party, and the campaign it launched.

A short Pierian Spring follows.

Q9 story · Beijing · Sunday, 4 May 1919

On 4 May 1919, About Three Thousand Students Gathered at Tiananmen to Protest a Peace Treaty

Word came from Paris: Germany's rights in Shandong would go to Japan, not back to China.
About three thousand students from thirteen Beijing colleges met in front of the palace gate.
Their banners read "Return our Qingdao" and "Cancel the Twenty-One Demands."
Many were readers of New Youth · Ch. 24 L3 · New Youth ↗

Names in This Story

May Fourth the 4 May 1919 protest; later the name for the whole movement

Tiananmen "Gate of Heavenly Peace," the front gate of the old palace

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Chow Tse-tsung, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 4. Image: Beijing, 4 May 1919.

Begin with the day itself. "On May 4, 1919, when word arrived that the decision had gone in favor of Japan, there was an explosion of popular protest. Some three thousand Beijing students assembled at Tiananmen Square in front of the old palace shouting patriotic slogans and trying to arouse spectators to action" (Ebrey, p. 425).

In 1919 there was no great square. "Tiananmen" meant the Gate of Heavenly Peace and the walled space in front of it, which is where the students stood. Chow Tse-tsung, who wrote the standard history of the movement, counts students from thirteen colleges, led by Beijing University. They carried white banners with slogans: return Qingdao, cancel the Twenty-One Demands, punish the traitors.

In the last lecture you met New Youth and its call to the young; the link takes you back. Many of these students had read it. Now their anger had a target.

The rest of this part asks why the news from Paris hit so hard. The answer starts in 1898.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chow Tse-tsung, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 4.

Why this matters: This protest gave its name to a whole era of Chinese history.

Q9 story · Recap · Shandong · 1897–1898

Recap: In 1898 Germany Forced China to Lease It a Port in Shandong

In 1897 two German missionaries were killed in Shandong · Ch. 18 L4 · Shandong 1898 ↗
Germany seized Jiaozhou Bay and in 1898 forced a 99-year lease.
It built the port of Qingdao and a railway inland, and won mining rights.
Other powers then grabbed their own ports · Ch. 18 L4 · the scramble ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 328; Ch. 24, p. 425. Image: H. C. O'Neill, 1914.

To see why Shandong mattered, go back to Chapter 18. In November 1897 two German Catholic missionaries were murdered in Shandong. Germany used the killings as a pretext: its navy occupied Jiaozhou Bay on the south coast, and in March 1898 the Qing signed a 99-year lease. Germany built a modern port at Qingdao, ran a railway to the provincial capital Jinan, and claimed mining rights along it (Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 328).

That seizure set off the scramble for concessions you studied there, when Russia, Britain and France demanded ports of their own. The two links take you back to those slides.

Shandong was not just any province. It was the home of Confucius and Mencius. When China's diplomats later argued for it in Paris, they called it "the cradle of Chinese civilisation" and "a Holy Land for the Chinese." So the lease stood for everything humiliating about the years after 1895.

Ebrey's chapter now picks up the story: "In 1914, Japan as an ally of Britain and France seized German territories in China" (Ebrey, p. 425).

Why this matters: The fight of 1919 was over a foreign claim that was already twenty years old.

Q9 story · Qingdao · August–November 1914

In 1914 Japan Joined the War Against Germany and Took Its Holdings in Shandong

In August 1914, as Britain's ally, Japan declared war on Germany.
Japanese troops landed in Shandong, outside the leased zone, and besieged Qingdao.
The German garrison surrendered on 7 November 1914.
Japan kept the port, the railway, and the mines.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Frederick R. Dickinson, War and National Reinvention: Japan in the Great War, 1914–1919 (1999). Image: Qingdao, 1914.

Ebrey puts it in one sentence: "In 1914, Japan as an ally of Britain and France seized German territories in China" (Ebrey, p. 425). Japan had been Britain's ally since 1902. When war broke out in Europe in August 1914, Tokyo saw an opening: Germany's small Asian colonies were far from home and almost undefended.

Japan declared war on Germany on 23 August. Its troops landed on the north coast of Shandong, well outside the German zone, and marched across Chinese territory, which was neutral. A small British force joined them. After a two-month siege the German garrison at Qingdao surrendered on 7 November. The historian Frederick Dickinson shows how Japanese leaders treated the war as a chance to settle their position in China while Europe was busy.

The photograph shows the victory parade nine days later. Japan now held the port, the Jiaozhou–Jinan railway and the mines, and kept troops along the line.

China had declared itself neutral and could do nothing. The next step came only weeks later, in Beijing.

Source beyond Ebrey: Frederick R. Dickinson, War and National Reinvention: Japan in the Great War, 1914–1919 (1999).

Why this matters: Japan, not Germany, became the power China had to face in Shandong.

Q9 story · Recap · Beijing · 1915

Recap: In 1915 Japan's Twenty-One Demands Confirmed Its Hold on Shandong

In January 1915 Japan handed Yuan Shikai twenty-one demands · Ch. 24 L2 · the demands ↗
Group 1 made China accept whatever Japan and Germany later agreed about Shandong.
Yuan gave in on 9 May; patriots called the date National Humiliation Day.
Students who were teenagers in 1915 were in college in 1919.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, doc. 154. Image: Beijing, 1915.

You met the Twenty-One Demands in Lecture 2; the link takes you back. Ebrey reminds us that some of them "confirmed Japan's position in the former German leasehold in Shandong" (Ebrey, p. 425). The first group of demands required China to accept in advance whatever Germany and Japan later agreed about Shandong.

"When a wave of anti-Japanese protests swept China, Japan dropped the last group but gave Yuan an ultimatum to accept the rest." Ebrey says the day he gave in, May 7, "was in later years called National Humiliation Day." In fact the ultimatum came on 7 May and Yuan accepted on 9 May, as the American diplomatic record shows, and both dates were later remembered; 9 May was the more common one.

Think about who was marching in 1919. A student of twenty had been sixteen when Yuan gave in. Schools held humiliation days every May. The slogans of 4 May, "Cancel the Twenty-One Demands" and "Return our Qingdao," came straight out of 1915.

Source beyond Ebrey: Foreign Relations of the United States, 1915, doc. 154.

Why this matters: The demands turned Shandong into a symbol of national shame years before Paris.

Q9 story · France · 1917–1919

China Joined the Allies in 1917 and Sent 140,000 Laborers to France

In August 1917 China declared war on Germany.
About 140,000 Chinese laborers went to France, most of them from Shandong.
They unloaded ships, dug trenches, and repaired roads and railways; thousands died.
Wilson's talk of self-determination made China expect a fair peace.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Xu Guoqi, Strangers on the Western Front: Chinese Workers in the Great War (2011). Image: Imperial War Museum, 1918.

China came into the war hoping to win a seat at the peace table. "In 1917, the Republic of China joined the allied war effort, and although China sent no combatants, it did send some 140,000 laborers to France, where they unloaded cargo ships, dug trenches, and otherwise provided manpower of direct use to the war effort" (Ebrey, p. 425).

The historian Xu Guoqi tells their story. Most of the men were recruited by the British and French in North China, a great many of them from Shandong itself. Recruiting began in 1916, before China declared war. They signed three-year contracts, sailed across the Pacific and Canada or around Africa, and worked behind the lines in Britain's Chinese Labour Corps and in French service. Several thousand died of disease, accidents and shelling. The photograph shows a camp festival at New Year, 1918.

"China was thus expecting some gain from the Allies' victory, particularly in light of the stress placed on national self-determination by the U.S. president, Woodrow Wilson." Students in Beijing read Wilson's speeches and believed them.

Source beyond Ebrey: Xu Guoqi, Strangers on the Western Front: Chinese Workers in the Great War (2011).

Why this matters: China had paid for a seat at the peace conference with its workers' labor.

Check yourself: Why did China send workers rather than soldiers, and what did it expect in return?

Q9 story · London, Paris, Rome, Beijing · 1917–1918

But Britain, France, and Italy Had Secretly Promised Shandong to Japan

In early 1917 Britain, France, and Italy secretly agreed to back Japan's claim to Shandong.
In return Japan sent warships to help hunt German submarines.
In September 1918 Beijing's warlord government also accepted Japan's position, in exchange for loans.
So Wilson's principles ran into promises already made.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Margaret MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001), ch. 23. Image: US Army Signal Corps, Paris, 1919.

Here is the catch. "Unfortunately for China, Japan had reached secret agreements with Britain, France, and Italy to support Japan's claim to German rights in Shandong. Japanese diplomats had also won the consent of the warlord government that held Beijing in 1918" (Ebrey, p. 425).

Margaret MacMillan's history of the conference explains both deals. In February and March 1917 the British, French and Italians agreed to support Japan's claims at the peace table; in return Japan sent destroyers to the Mediterranean against German submarines. In September 1918 the government of Duan Qirui, which depended on Japanese loans, signed notes that "gladly agreed" to Japan's arrangements for the Shandong railway. That note would haunt the Chinese delegation.

The photograph shows the four leaders who finally decided the question in April 1919. Japan threatened to walk out and stay out of the new League of Nations unless it got Shandong. Wilson, who wanted the League above all, gave way.

Source beyond Ebrey: Margaret MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001), ch. 23.

Why this matters: The decision against China had been prepared by secret diplomacy and by China's own warlord government.

Q9 story · Paris · January–April 1919

At Paris, China's Delegates Asked for Shandong Back and Were Turned Down

China had two seats at the conference; its delegation was led by Lu Zhengxiang.
On 28 January 1919 Wellington Koo, aged 30, argued China's case before the Council of Ten.
He called Shandong "the cradle of Chinese civilisation" and "a Holy Land for the Chinese."
On 30 April the Big Three gave Germany's rights to Japan.

Names in This Story

Wellington Koo Gu Weijun, diplomat, 1888–1985; pronounced "goo way-jyun"

Lu Zhengxiang head of China's delegation, 1871–1949; pronounced "loo juhng-shyahng"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Foreign Relations of the United States, Paris Peace Conference 1919, vol. 3, doc. 50; MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001). Images: Library of Congress, 1919.

Ebrey writes that "at Versailles the Chinese representatives were not even admitted, while those from Japan were seated at the table with the Western powers" (Ebrey, p. 425). The American record shows otherwise. China was one of the smaller Allied states with two seats at the full conference. Its delegation was led by the foreign minister, Lu Zhengxiang, with Wellington Koo, Alfred Sze and C. T. Wang. What is true is that China, like the other small powers, had no place on the Council of Ten, where the great powers decided.

But China was heard there. On 28 January 1919 Koo, a day short of his 31st birthday and holding a Columbia doctorate, spoke for half an hour without notes. The minutes, published in Foreign Relations of the United States, record him saying he spoke for "400 millions, one quarter of the human race," and that Shandong was "the cradle of Chinese civilisation, the birthplace of Confucius and Mencius, and a Holy Land for the Chinese."

It was not enough. Japan produced the 1917 and 1918 agreements, and on 30 April Wilson, Lloyd George and Clemenceau awarded Germany's rights to Japan.

Source beyond Ebrey: Foreign Relations of the United States, Paris Peace Conference 1919, vol. 3, doc. 50; MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001).

Why this matters: China lost in Paris not because it went unheard, but because the great powers had other priorities.

Check yourself: What arguments did Koo use, and why did they fail?

Q9 story · Zhaojialou, Beijing · Afternoon of 4 May 1919

The Marchers Burned a Cabinet Minister's House and Beat a Pro-Japanese Official

Turned away from the foreign legations, the marchers went to the house of Cao Rulin.
Cao had helped negotiate the 1915 treaties and arranged Japanese loans.
Students climbed in, set the house on fire, and beat the envoy Zhang Zongxiang, who was hiding there.
Police arrested 32 students that evening.

Names in This Story

Cao Rulin minister of communications, 1877–1966; pronounced "tsow roo-lin"

Zhang Zongxiang China's minister to Japan; pronounced "jahng dzung-shyahng"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 4. Image: undated, before 1925.

The march did not stay peaceful. Ebrey says that "some students broke through police lines to beat up a pro-Japanese official and set fire to the home of a cabinet minister" (Ebrey, p. 425). Chow Tse-tsung reconstructs the afternoon. The students first went to the Legation Quarter to hand petitions to the Allied ministers, but it was Sunday and police kept them out. Angry, they turned toward the house of Cao Rulin, the minister of communications, in a lane called Zhaojialou.

Cao had helped negotiate the treaties of 1915 as vice foreign minister and arranged the Japanese loans of 1917–1918. The students called him, with Zhang Zongxiang, China's minister to Japan, and Lu Zongyu, a "traitor." Some climbed through a window and opened the gate. Cao escaped over a back wall. Zhang, who was visiting, was found and beaten badly, though he survived. Someone set the house on fire.

The police arrived and arrested thirty-two students. This is the one violent scene of the day, and the government used it to call the students rioters.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 4.

Why this matters: The attack made the protest a direct challenge to the government, not just a petition.

Q9 story · Beijing and Shanghai · May–June 1919

Strikes Spread to More Than Two Hundred Cities, and the Government Gave In

Teachers, merchants, the press, and workers took the students' side; Japanese goods were boycotted.
Student strikes closed schools in more than 200 cities.
On 3–4 June police arrested about 1,150 student speakers and held them in Beijing University.
Shanghai merchants and workers struck; on 10 June the three officials were dismissed, and the cabinet fell.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 5. Images: Sidney D. Gamble, 4 June 1919; Tsinghua, 1919.

The arrests of 4 May made things worse for the government. "These actions set off a wave of protests around the country in support of the students and their cause. Everyone, it seemed, was on the students' side: teachers, workers, the press, the merchants, Sun Yatsen, and the warlords. Japanese goods were boycotted. Soon strikes closed schools in more than two hundred cities" (Ebrey, p. 425).

In early June the students went back onto the streets of Beijing to give speeches. "The Beijing warlord government finally arrested 1,150 student protesters, turning parts of Beijing University into a jail." Chow Tse-tsung gives the same total, about 400 on 3 June and some 700 on 4 June. The American sociologist Sidney Gamble photographed one group being marched away.

Then Shanghai joined. From 5 June merchants closed their shops and tens of thousands of workers struck. "Patriotic sympathy strikes, especially in Shanghai, soon forced the government to release them." On 10 June Cao Rulin, Zhang Zongxiang and Lu Zongyu were dismissed, and the cabinet resigned days later.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960), ch. 5.

Why this matters: For the first time, students, merchants and workers acted together, and they won.

Check yourself: Why did the Shanghai strikes force the government's hand when the student strikes had not?

Q9 story · Versailles · 28 June 1919

On 28 June 1919, China's Delegates Stayed Away and Did Not Sign the Treaty

China asked to sign with a reservation on Shandong; the powers refused.
Chinese students and workers in Paris surrounded Lu Zhengxiang's residence.
On 28 June China's chairs in the Hall of Mirrors stayed empty.
Japan held Shandong until the Washington Conference of 1922 returned it to China.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 425; MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001); Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960). Image: William Orpen, 1919.

Ebrey's sentence is short: "The cabinet fell, and China refused to sign the Versailles Treaty. The students were ebullient" (Ebrey, p. 425).

Behind it lies a hard choice. Beijing wavered: some officials thought China must sign to join the League of Nations. The delegation in Paris asked to sign with a written reservation on Shandong, and the great powers said no. Later accounts describe Chinese students and workers in Paris gathering outside Lu Zhengxiang's residence on the last night to make sure no one left to sign. On 28 June 1919, when the German delegates signed in the Hall of Mirrors, the seats reserved for China were empty. Orpen's painting shows the room; China is not in it.

It was the first time a Chinese government had refused to accept a treaty the powers had drafted. Shandong itself came back by another route: at the Washington Conference in 1922, Japan agreed to return the leasehold and sell back the railway.

Source beyond Ebrey: MacMillan, Paris 1919 (2001); Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960).

Why this matters: Public pressure, not the government's own resolve, decided China's foreign policy.

Check yourself: What did China gain, and what did it risk, by refusing to sign?

Q9 concept · May Fourth nationalism

Concept: May Fourth Turned Nationalism into a Mass Movement of Students, Merchants, and Workers

Concept

May Fourth Movement

The wave of patriotic protest that began on 4 May 1919, and, more broadly, the years of cultural and political ferment around it, when nationalism spread from a few reformers to students, merchants, and workers across China.

Before 1919, nationalism belonged to small circles of reformers and revolutionaries.
After 1919 it had a mass base, new tactics (strikes, boycotts), and young leaders.
Many May Fourth students later led the Nationalist and Communist parties.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 425–426. Image: 1919.

Historians use "May Fourth" in two ways. The narrow meaning is the incident of 4 May 1919 and the protests that followed. The broad meaning, which Chow Tse-tsung made standard, covers the whole period from about 1915 to the early 1920s, joining the New Culture Movement of the last lecture to the political protest.

Ebrey keeps the two strands apart: "The personal and intellectual goals of the New Culture movement were pursued along with, and sometimes in competition with, the national power goals of the May Fourth movement" (Ebrey, p. 425).

Why call it a turning point in nationalism? Compare it to 1911. The revolution against the Qing was made by small groups of soldiers, secret societies and exiles. In 1919 the actors were thousands of students, women among them, then merchants and workers in many cities. They used new tools: strikes, boycotts, lecture teams, newspapers. And they won a concrete victory against their own government.

Two of the students' teachers, Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao, helped found the Chinese Communist Party in 1921.

Why this matters: After 1919 any party that wanted to rule China had to speak for the nation.

Check yourself: What was new about the people who protested in 1919?

Q11 story · Guangzhou · 1917–1923

Sun Went to Guangzhou to Build an Army, and Russia's Revolution Offered a Model

Yuan Shikai had shown the revolutionaries they needed their own army · Ch. 24 L1 · Three Principles ↗
In 1917 Sun set up a rival government in Guangzhou, but local warlords twice drove him out.
The same year the Bolsheviks seized power in Russia.
Russia was big, poor, and peasant, like China; perhaps its revolution could work here.

Names in This Story

Sun Yatsen leader of the Nationalist Party, 1866–1925; pronounced "soon yaht-sen"

Chiang Kaishek Sun's officer, later his successor, 1887–1975; pronounced "jyahng ky-shek"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Marie-Claire Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: 1923.

The second half of this lecture follows Sun Yatsen. You met him in Lecture 1 as the founder of the Revolutionary Alliance and the author of the Three Principles of the People; the link takes you back. By 1917 he had a party but no power. "The ease with which Yuan Shikai had pushed the revolutionaries out of power demonstrated to them that they needed their own army. Sun Yatsen in 1917 went to Guangzhou, then controlled by warlords, to try to form a military government there" (Ebrey, p. 428).

It went badly. His biographer Marie-Claire Bergère describes how Sun depended on southern generals who used him and dropped him. He was pushed out in 1918, came back in 1920, and was driven out again in June 1922, when his own commander turned his guns on Sun's headquarters. Chiang Kaishek, a young officer, stayed by his side on a gunboat for weeks.

Meanwhile, "that year, the Bolshevik Revolution succeeded in Russia, and Sun began to think that Russia might offer a better model for political change than Japan." Ebrey asks the question Sun asked: "Why shouldn't the sort of revolution that worked in Russia also work in China?"

Source beyond Ebrey: Marie-Claire Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: Sun turned to Moscow because the West and the warlords had failed him.

Q11 concept · The Comintern

Concept: The Comintern Was Lenin's Organization for Spreading Revolution Around the World

Concept

Comintern

The Communist International, founded by Lenin in Moscow in 1919 to link communist parties in every country and to promote revolution, including national revolutions against imperialism in Asia.

In 1920 the Comintern decided communists should back national movements in colonies and semi-colonies.
In China, that meant backing Sun's Nationalists against warlords and the powers.
Moscow also feared a weak China would let Japan expand toward Siberia.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Eugene W. Wu, "Divergence in Strategic Planning: Chiang Kai-shek's Mission to Moscow, 1923," Republican China 16:1 (1990). Image: Moscow, 1920.

Ebrey defines the term in passing: "the Comintern (short for Communist International, the organization Lenin had founded to promote communist revolution throughout the world)" (Ebrey, p. 428).

Why would Communists help a nationalist like Sun? Ebrey explains the theory: "In Marxist-Leninist theory, socialist revolution would occur by stages, and because China had not yet gone through a bourgeois, capitalist stage, a victory by the Nationalist revolutionaries who would overthrow the imperialists appeared to be the next stage for China." The Second Congress of 1920, shown here, adopted Lenin's theses telling communists to support such national movements. And there was a practical motive: "a weak China might invite the expansion of Japan, the Soviet Union's main worry to the east."

Ebrey writes that "in 1920, Sun turned to the Comintern." The first meeting did come in November 1920, when the Comintern agent Grigori Voitinsky called on Sun in Shanghai, as Eugene Wu shows. But at first Sun only asked questions; real help came in 1923.

Source beyond Ebrey: Eugene W. Wu, "Divergence in Strategic Planning: Chiang Kai-shek's Mission to Moscow, 1923," Republican China 16:1 (1990).

Why this matters: The Comintern gave Sun what no Western power would: money, weapons and advisers.

Check yourself: Why would a communist organization support a non-communist party in China?

Q11 story · Shanghai and Guangzhou · 1923–1924

In 1923 Sun Made a Deal with Moscow, and Borodin Came to Rebuild His Party

26 January 1923: the Sun–Joffe statement promised Soviet help to unify China.
6 October 1923: Mikhail Borodin arrived in Guangzhou as adviser.
He drafted a new party constitution with a strict chain of command.
January 1924: the party's First Congress adopted it and admitted Communists.

Names in This Story

Mikhail Borodin Comintern adviser to Sun, 1884–1951

Adolph Joffe Soviet envoy, 1883–1927

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; C. Martin Wilbur and Julie Lien-ying How, Missionaries of Revolution: Soviet Advisers and Nationalist China, 1920–1927 (1989). Images: Guangzhou, 1923; Agence Rol, 1923.

Ebrey compresses these years: "The Comintern sent advisers to Sun, most notably Michael Borodin, who drafted a constitution for the Nationalist Party, giving it a more hierarchical chain of command" (Ebrey, p. 428). The dates come from Wilbur and How's study of the Soviet advisers.

On 26 January 1923, in Shanghai, Sun and the Soviet envoy Adolph Joffe issued a joint statement. It said communism was not suited to China, but that China's most urgent tasks were unity and independence, and Russia would help. On 6 October Borodin, an experienced Comintern agent who spoke English after years in Chicago, reached Guangzhou. Three days later Sun gave him the dinner in the photograph.

In January 1924 the party's First National Congress adopted Borodin's constitution, modeled on the Soviet party, with cells, discipline, and a central executive committee. When members complained it looked too Communist, Sun answered that "the capitalist countries will never be sympathetic to our Party. Sympathy can only be expected from Russia, the oppressed nations, and the oppressed peoples."

Source beyond Ebrey: C. Martin Wilbur and Julie Lien-ying How, Missionaries of Revolution: Soviet Advisers and Nationalist China, 1920–1927 (1989).

Why this matters: Sun's party took the organization of a Leninist party, though not its goals.

Q11 concept · The First United Front

Concept: In the United Front, Communists Joined the Nationalist Party as Individuals

Concept

First United Front

The alliance of 1923–1927 between Sun's Nationalist Party and the small Chinese Communist Party, backed by the Soviet Union. Communists joined the Nationalist Party as individual members while keeping their own party.

The Communist Party, founded in 1921, had only a few hundred members.
Its members took Nationalist Party cards and posts, and kept meeting on their own.
Both parties aimed at the same enemies: warlords and imperialism.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Wilbur and How, Missionaries of Revolution (1989). Image: Guangzhou, January 1926.

Ebrey describes the arrangement: "In 1922, on Comintern urging, the two parties formed a united front, as a consequence of which members of the Communist Party joined the Nationalist Party as individuals but continued separate Communist Party activities on the side" (Ebrey, p. 428).

The date needs care. In August 1922 the Comintern's agent pressed Communist leaders to accept the idea, and the Communist Party's congress approved it in June 1923. The alliance became formal only at the Nationalist congress of January 1924, when Communists, including Li Dazhao, were seated on its central committee. Ebrey's 1922 marks the start of the talks.

Why did both sides agree? "Sun Yatsen endorsed this policy, confident that the Nationalist Party would not be threatened by a small number of Communists and eager to tap all possible resources for building a strong state." The Communists, few and young, gained a legal mass organization to work inside. Ebrey stresses that "at no time in the 1920s or 1930s did it have nearly as many members or supporters as the Nationalist Party."

Source beyond Ebrey: Wilbur and How, Missionaries of Revolution (1989).

Why this matters: This was a "party within a party," and that tension shaped everything that followed.

Check yourself: What risks did each party take by joining the united front?

Q11 story · Whampoa Island, near Guangzhou · 1923–1924

At Whampoa in 1924, Chiang Kaishek Trained Nearly Five Hundred Cadets with Soviet Help

In autumn 1923 Chiang led a three-month study mission to Moscow.
The academy opened on 16 June 1924 with Chiang as commandant and Soviet money and instructors.
Nearly 500 cadets, aged 17 to 24, studied Sun's Three Principles as well as tactics.
The Communist Zhou Enlai worked in its political department.

Names in This Story

Whampoa Huangpu, the island academy near Guangzhou; pronounced "hwahng-poo"

Zhou Enlai Communist, later premier of the PRC, 1898–1976; pronounced "joe un-lie"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Eugene W. Wu, "Divergence in Strategic Planning" (1990); Wilbur and How (1989); Jon Jacobson, When the Soviet Union Entered World Politics (1994). Image: Whampoa, 1924.

Sun now built the army he had lacked. Ebrey writes that Chiang Kaishek "was sent there for four months' training in 1923" (Ebrey, p. 428). Eugene Wu's study of the trip shows it was a three-month study mission, from 2 September to 29 November 1923, at the head of a four-man delegation. Chiang met Trotsky and other leaders and came home suspicious of Soviet aims.

"On Chiang's return, Borodin helped him set up the Whampoa Military Academy near Guangzhou, and the Soviet Union made a substantial contribution to its costs." It opened on 16 June 1924. "The communist Zhou Enlai, recently returned from France, became deputy head of this academy's political education department." "The first class was admitted in 1924 with nearly five hundred cadets, ages seventeen to twenty-four."

Ebrey counts "about one thousand Russian military advisers" by 1925. Wilbur and How, whom she cites, describe dozens. Jon Jacobson counts fifty-eight attached to the army in 1926; Russian archival work puts the total for 1925–1927 at about 150.

Source beyond Ebrey: Eugene W. Wu, "Divergence in Strategic Planning" (1990); Wilbur and How (1989); Jon Jacobson, When the Soviet Union Entered World Politics (1994).

Why this matters: The cadets became the core of a party army, "fiercely loyal to Chiang."

Q11 story · Shanghai · 30 May 1925

On 30 May 1925, Settlement Police Fired on Demonstrators in Shanghai

On 15 May a Chinese worker was killed in a strike at a Japanese-owned mill.
On 30 May students and workers protested on Nanking Road, in the foreign-run Settlement.
A British inspector ordered his police to fire. Ebrey counts eleven dead; reports range from nine to thirteen.
Strikes and boycotts against British and Japanese firms spread across China.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Richard W. Rigby, The May 30 Movement: Events and Themes (1980); Shanghai Municipal Council, Annual Report 1925. Image: Shanghai, 30 May 1925.

"Nationalism continued to grow during the 1920s as one incident after another served to remind people of China's subjection to the imperialist powers. On May 30, 1925, police in the foreign-run International Settlement of Shanghai fired on unarmed demonstrators, killing eleven" (Ebrey, p. 428).

Richard Rigby's study traces how it began. In mid-May a worker named Gu Zhenghong was shot during a strike at a Japanese-owned cotton mill. Students organized his memorial and planned protests in the Settlement. On Saturday 30 May police arrested several student speakers. A crowd gathered outside the Louza police station on Nanking Road, and Inspector Edward Everson ordered his Sikh and Chinese constables to fire.

How many died depends on the source and on what is counted. The Settlement's own report gives four killed on the spot and several more who died in hospital; Chinese accounts list thirteen by name. The photograph shows the street soon afterward, with police and soldiers on guard.

Within days Shanghai's students, merchants and workers struck, as in 1919, and the movement spread to dozens of cities.

Source beyond Ebrey: Richard W. Rigby, The May 30 Movement: Events and Themes (1980); Shanghai Municipal Council, Annual Report 1925.

Why this matters: The shooting made "imperialism" a word everyone understood, and both parties grew fast.

Check yourself: How was 30 May 1925 like 4 May 1919, and how was it different?

Q11 story · Beijing and Guangzhou · 1925

In March 1925 Sun Died, and His Party Began to Split into Left and Right

Sun had gone north for unity talks; he died of cancer in Beijing on 12 March 1925.
His will told the party: "The revolution is not yet complete."
The left, close to the Communists, rallied around Wang Jingwei.
The right thought Borodin had too much power and the Communists formed "a party within the party."

Names in This Story

Wang Jingwei leader of the party's left, 1883–1944; pronounced "wahng jing-way"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: Beijing, 1925.

The man who held the alliance together did not live to see its campaign. "In 1925, before the planned Northern Expedition to reunify the country could be mounted, Sun Yatsen died of cancer" (Ebrey, p. 428). He had traveled to Beijing in late 1924 to negotiate with the northern generals and fell ill on the way. He died there on 12 March 1925, aged 58. His short political will, read at every party meeting for decades, began with his forty years of struggle and ended, "The revolution is not yet complete; comrades, you must continue to strive."

Without him the party divided. "The recently reorganized Nationalist Party soon suffered strain between the leftists, who shared many of the goals of the communists, and the rightists, who thought that Borodin had too much power and the communists were acting like a party within the party." In August 1925 the leftist leader Liao Zhongkai was assassinated in Guangzhou, and that autumn a group of senior rightists met in Beijing to demand the Communists' expulsion.

Chiang, with the army behind him, stood between the factions.

Source beyond Ebrey: Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: Sun's death left the alliance without the one leader all sides accepted.

Check yourself: Why did the right wing of the party distrust the Communists?

Q11 story · Guangzhou to the Yangzi · July–December 1926

In July 1926 the Northern Expedition Marched North Under Chiang

In July 1926 Chiang, as commander, led the army out of Guangdong; Soviet advisers helped plan.
Communists and the party's left went ahead, organizing peasant and worker unions.
Changsha fell in July, Wuhan in October, Nanchang in November.
Many warlords switched sides; others were beaten · Ch. 24 L2 · warlords ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428, and Map 24.1, p. 416; Donald A. Jordan, The Northern Expedition: China's National Revolution of 1926–1928 (1976). Image: CC BY-SA 4.0.

Now the army marched. "Nevertheless, in July 1926, the two-pronged Northern Expedition was finally launched, with Chiang Kaishek as military commander and Russian advisers helping with strategy" (Ebrey, p. 428). Chiang took his oath as commander in Guangzhou on 9 July. The chief Soviet adviser, Vasily Blyukher, known in China as Galen, helped plan the campaign.

Follow the blue arrows. Donald Jordan's history of the campaign describes the main thrust into Hunan, where Changsha fell in July, and on to the Yangzi, where Hankou and Hanyang fell in September and the walled city of Wuchang after a siege in October. A second push went east into Jiangxi, taking Nanchang in November. The map runs on to 1928, when the expedition reached Beijing; Ebrey's own Map 24.1 (p. 416) dates the campaign from Chiang's appointment as commander on 5 June 1926 to the end of December 1928.

"Communists and members of the left wing of the Nationalist Party formed an advanced guard, organizing peasants and workers along the way to support the revolution. Many warlords joined the cause; others were defeated." The colored zones show the warlords you met in Lecture 2; the link takes you back.

Source beyond Ebrey: Donald A. Jordan, The Northern Expedition: China's National Revolution of 1926–1928 (1976).

Why this matters: Within six months the party army had fought its way from Guangzhou to the Yangzi.

Q11 story · Wuhan and Shanghai · December 1926–March 1927

By Early 1927 the Government Sat in Wuhan, and the Army Stood Outside Shanghai

At the end of 1926 the Nationalist government moved from Guangzhou to Wuhan.
In Wuhan the party's left and the Communists, with Borodin, held the upper hand.
Chiang kept his headquarters at Nanchang and turned east.
In March 1927 his army reached Shanghai, where Communist-led unions had risen to meet it.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 429; Jordan, The Northern Expedition (1976). Image: Louis Fischer, 1930.

Victory brought the alliance's tensions into the open. "By the end of 1926, the Nationalist government was moved from Guangzhou to Wuhan, where the left wing of the party became dominant" (Ebrey, p. 429). The photograph shows its leaders: the foreign minister Eugene Chen, the finance minister T. V. Soong, Wang Jingwei, and Borodin. Wuhan's streets were full of union rallies, and in January 1927 crowds took over the British concession at Hankou.

Chiang did not move to Wuhan. He kept his headquarters at Nanchang, and the party's more conservative leaders and Shanghai's bankers looked to him. Jordan shows how the rivalry between Wuhan and Nanchang grew through the winter.

"By early 1927, the army was ready to attack Shanghai." In March, as the Nationalist troops approached, Communist-led workers rose and took over most of the Chinese city; the army entered on 22 March. China's richest city was in the hands of the alliance, and its two halves now had to decide who would rule it.

Ebrey ends here: "This would mark the end of the United Front, a topic taken up in Chapter 25."

Source beyond Ebrey: Jordan, The Northern Expedition (1976).

Why this matters: The coalition had won, and success made the split almost certain.

Check yourself: Why might Chiang have stayed away from Wuhan?

Chapter 24 · Looking back · 1900 and 1927

China in 1927 Was Not the China of 1900

In 1900 an emperor ruled; by 1927 two thousand years of monarchy were over · Ch. 24 L1 · 1912 ↗
Nationalism had become a mass force, and political parties fought to lead it.
Schools, magazines, and study abroad had spread new ideas; Confucianism was no longer "an obvious good" · Ch. 24 L3 · New Culture ↗
Students, women, and factory workers had become political actors.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 429.

Ebrey closes the chapter with a question: "How different was China in 1927 compared to 1900?" (Ebrey, p. 429). Her answer is a list, and these four lectures have walked through it.

"Two thousand years of monarchical government had come to an end." That was Lecture 1, the fall of the Qing. "Nationalism had become a powerful force. Political parties had come into existence." That was the Revolutionary Alliance, the Nationalists and, in this lecture, the Communists and May Fourth.

"Through the spread of modern schools, the outpouring of new publications, and much more extensive study abroad, a much larger proportion of the population knew something of Western countries and Western ideas. Confucianism was no longer taken to be an obvious good." That was Lecture 3 and New Youth.

"Young people with modern educations had become important political actors as protesters and agitators. Women had come to play much more public roles in society. An urban proletariat had come into existence with the growth of factories in the major cities." You have seen all three on the streets of Beijing in 1919 and Shanghai in 1925. Her last sentence: "China was changing very rapidly."

Why this matters: The chapter is about how fast a very old political order came apart.

Pierian Spring · Story · Paris, Moscow, Guangzhou · 1919–1927

In Guangzhou, a Young Vietnamese Named Nguyen Ai Quoc, Later Ho Chi Minh, Worked for the Comintern

In 1919, in Paris, he petitioned the peace conference for Vietnamese rights; no one answered.
He joined the French Communist Party in 1920 and worked for the Comintern in Moscow.
Late in 1924 he reached Guangzhou as "Ly Thuy," interpreter for Borodin.
In June 1925 he founded the Vietnamese Revolutionary Youth League there.

Names in This Story

Ho Chi Minh Vietnamese revolutionary, 1890–1969, then called Nguyen Ai Quoc

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; William J. Duiker, Ho Chi Minh: A Life (2000). Images: Marseille, 1921; Guangzhou, 1925.

Ebrey gives him a short paragraph: "Among those the Comintern sent to Guangzhou was Ho Chi Minh, a Vietnamese who had become a communist in France and had gone to Moscow to work at Comintern headquarters. Ho spent much of the next twenty years in China and Hong Kong organizing a Vietnamese communist movement among Vietnamese patriots in exile in south China" (Ebrey, p. 428).

His story touches this whole lecture. William Duiker's biography tells it. In June 1919, the same spring as May Fourth, the young man then called Nguyen Ai Quoc, "Nguyen the Patriot," sent the peace conference a petition asking for basic rights for the Vietnamese under French rule. Like China, he got no answer. He joined the French Communist Party at its founding in 1920 and went to Moscow in 1923.

Late in 1924 he arrived in Guangzhou under the name Ly Thuy, officially an interpreter in Borodin's office. There he gathered young exiles, sent some to study at Whampoa, and in June 1925 founded the Vietnamese Revolutionary Youth League, the ancestor of Vietnam's Communist Party. The second photograph shows his recruits at the grave of a Vietnamese patriot.

Source beyond Ebrey: William J. Duiker, Ho Chi Minh: A Life (2000).

Why this matters: Guangzhou's united front trained revolutionaries for all of Asia.

Pierian Spring · Story · Guangzhou and Hong Kong · June 1925–October 1926

After Troops Fired on a March in Guangzhou, Hong Kong's Workers Walked Out for Sixteen Months

In mid-June 1925 Hong Kong's workers began to strike in sympathy with Shanghai.
On 23 June a march in Guangzhou passed the foreign enclave of Shamian, and British and French troops opened fire.
Chinese counts gave 52 dead; British counts were lower.
A strike and boycott of Hong Kong lasted until October 1926.

Names in This Story

Shamian the British and French enclave island at Guangzhou; pronounced "shah-myen"

Su Zhaozheng seamen's union and strike leader, 1885–1929

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 428; Chan Lau Kit-ching, China, Britain and Hong Kong, 1895–1945 (1990); Wilbur and How (1989). Images: Guangzhou, 1925.

Ebrey's account is brief: "Three weeks later, a sympathy protest in Guangzhou led foreign troops to open fire, killing fifty-two demonstrators. A fifteen-month boycott of British goods and trade with Hong Kong followed" (Ebrey, p. 428).

The strike began first. In mid-June, organized by Communist labor leaders such as the seaman Su Zhaozheng, Hong Kong workers began walking out and moving to Guangzhou. On 23 June a great march of workers, students and Whampoa cadets, like those in the first photograph, passed the island of Shamian, where the British and French had their concessions. Shots were fired, each side said the other fired first, and the troops on Shamian fired into the crowd. Chinese investigators counted 52 dead; British reports gave lower figures.

The historian Chan Lau Kit-ching describes what followed. Tens of thousands of workers left Hong Kong. A strike committee in Guangzhou fed and housed them, and its armed pickets blocked trade with the colony. Hong Kong's economy suffered badly until the boycott ended in October 1926, when the Northern Expedition no longer needed it.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chan Lau Kit-ching, China, Britain and Hong Kong, 1895–1945 (1990); Wilbur and How (1989).

Why this matters: The strike showed what the united front could mobilize, and it gave the Communists a mass base.

Background essay: the historians’ debates behind this lecture ↗

Everything in the story slides comes from Ebrey, Chapter 24 (pp. 425–429), and each slide's source line gives the page. Where a slide or note adds something from sources beyond Ebrey, the source line or the notes name it, and the works above are where those facts come from.

Four points differ from Ebrey, each on the strength of a named source. China's delegates were admitted at Paris, and Wellington Koo argued the Shandong case before the Council of Ten (the American record, Foreign Relations). The alliance with Moscow dates from 1923 and was formalized in January 1924. Chiang's trip to Moscow was a three-month study mission, not four months' training (Eugene Wu). And the Soviet advisers numbered in the dozens, not about a thousand (Wilbur and How; Jacobson). The May 30 death toll varies by source, so the slide gives Ebrey's figure and the range.

Every picture in this deck is a real historical image or a modern map. Portraits show people as they wished to be seen, and Orpen's painting is an artist's view of the signing.

Why this matters: Knowing where a fact comes from is how historians decide how much to trust it.

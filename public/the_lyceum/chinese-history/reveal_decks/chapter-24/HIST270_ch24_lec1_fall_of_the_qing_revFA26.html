Q1 and Q3 story · Wuchang · October 1911

In October 1911 a Revolutionary's Bomb Went Off by Accident in Wuchang

Revolutionaries in the Wuhan cities were making bombs for a rising.
On 9 October one went off by accident, and the police found their lists of members.
Soldiers of the New Army named on the lists rose the next night rather than wait to be executed.
Within a day they held Wuchang. How had the Qing come to this?

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Joseph W. Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976). Image: photographer unknown, 1911.

Start at the end. Wuchang, Hankou, and Hanyang, three cities facing each other across the Yangzi, held one of the new Qing army divisions, and many of its soldiers had joined secret revolutionary groups. "A bomb accidentally exploded in the headquarters of a revolutionary group. When the police came to investigate, they found lists of the revolutionaries, including many officers of the new army division located there" (Ebrey, p. 418).

The accident happened on 9 October 1911 in Hankou. Facing arrest and execution, soldiers in Wuchang mutinied on the evening of 10 October. "The local officials fled, and the army took over the city in less than a day."

The photograph shows the new revolutionary government of Hubei a few weeks later, guarded by soldiers in Western-style uniforms under the new flag.

Sun Yatsen, the most famous revolutionary, was not there. He was in the United States raising money. This lecture works back to explain why a small accident could topple a dynasty. Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416) and Q3 (p. 417) frame the story.

Source beyond Ebrey: Joseph W. Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976).

Why this matters: The Qing fell because years of failed reform had left almost no one willing to defend it.

Recap · The Boxer Protocol · 1901

Recap: The Boxer Protocol Charged China 450 Million Taels

In 1900 Cixi declared war on eight foreign powers, and their armies took Beijing.
The Protocol of 1901 punished officials and let foreign troops guard the capital.
It also charged China 450 million taels of silver · Ch. 18 L4 · protocol ↗
The court came home in 1902 to a treasury it could not fill.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; Ch. 18 L4 for the Boxer war and the Protocol.

Recall how Chapter 18 ended. In June 1900 the Empress Dowager Cixi backed the Boxers and declared war on eight foreign powers at once. Their armies relieved the besieged legations, took Beijing, and sent the court fleeing to Xi'an. The link takes you back to the Protocol slide in the last lecture.

The settlement of 1901 had many parts: executions and punishments for pro-Boxer officials, foreign troops stationed between Beijing and the sea, and a suspension of the examinations in rebel areas. The part that shaped the next ten years was the money.

Ebrey describes "a staggering indemnity of 450 million silver dollars, twice as large as the one exacted by Japan a few years earlier and nearly twice the government's annual revenues" (Ebrey, p. 416). The Protocol actually set the sum in taels, the Chinese unit of silver, so that is the word on the slide.

Keep the number in mind. Almost every story in this lecture begins with a government that had no money.

Why this matters: The indemnity turned a military defeat into a lasting fiscal crisis.

Check yourself: How did the indemnity compare with the court's yearly income?

Q1 story · The fiscal crisis · 1901–1911

The Indemnity Came Due in 39 Yearly Installments, and the Court Squeezed the Provinces

China was to pay the 450 million taels over 39 years, with interest.
With older foreign loans, the debts swallowed all of the customs revenue.
Little was left to run the government, much less to modernize it.
The court pushed the costs onto the provinces, and they passed them on as new taxes.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; Douglas R. Reynolds, China, 1898–1912 (1993). Image: Alfons Mumm von Schwarzenstein (attributed), 1901.

The indemnity was not paid at once. "It was to be paid from customs revenue in thirty-nine annual installments, with interest. When interest on existing foreign loans was added, the debts absorbed all of the customs revenue. Little was left for the ordinary operation of the government, much less investment in modernization" (Ebrey, p. 416). With interest, the final bill came to nearly a billion taels.

The customs were collected by foreigners under the Qing's own Maritime Customs Service, and the money went straight to the creditors. To pay its other bills, Beijing assigned each province a share and told it to find the money. Governors raised taxes on land, salt, wine, and trade, and ordinary people felt the squeeze.

In the photograph, the men who imposed the settlement sit at the same table as the Qing officials who had to sign it.

Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416) begins here: the fiscal crisis after the Protocol. Watch what it led to, first among local elites and then at court.

Source beyond Ebrey: Douglas R. Reynolds, China, 1898–1912 (1993).

Why this matters: A government that cannot pay its bills has to ask its people for more just when it can offer them least.

Q1 story · Local activism · 1901–1910

Local Elites Opened Schools, and Periodicals Rose Tenfold Between 1901 and 1910

With Beijing broke, local gentry and merchants paid for change themselves.
They founded new schools that taught science, geography, and foreign languages.
They started newspapers and magazines, which rose about tenfold between 1901 and 1910.
They also began to ask how other nations had grown rich and strong.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416. Image: Popular Science Monthly, vol. 67, 1905.

The first result of the crisis was a burst of local energy. "Forced to look after their own interests, local elites increasingly took on modernization projects. They set up new schools and started periodicals, which by one estimate increased tenfold from 1901 to 1910" (Ebrey, p. 416). The guide asks you to copy the sentence that begins "Forced to look."

These elites were the same gentry families who had once prepared their sons for the examinations. Now they raised money for schools with science and foreign languages, chambers of commerce, police forces, and railway companies. Newspapers gave them a place to argue about the nation's future.

The photograph shows girls at a new school in Zhenjiang, on the Yangzi, in 1905. Schools for girls were among the newest and most debated of these projects.

This is the first half of Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416): the fiscal crisis spurred local elite activism. Keep the word "local" in mind. These men were building power that did not depend on Beijing.

Why this matters: The people who built the new schools and papers would later run the provincial assemblies that turned against the court.

Q1 story · Nationalist protest · 1905–1907

In 1905 Merchants and Students Boycotted American Goods

American law barred Chinese workers from entering the United States.
Chinese visitors to the 1904 St Louis World's Fair were harassed at the border and at the fair.
From 1905 to 1907, merchants and students refused to buy American goods.
Others raised money to buy back railways built by foreign firms.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; Guanhua Wang, In Search of Justice: The 1905–1906 Chinese Anti-American Boycott (2001). Image: Samuel Cupples Envelope Co., 1904.

Interest in Western ideas did not mean liking the Western powers. "Between 1905 and 1907, there were boycotts of the United States for its immigration restriction law and its mistreatment of Chinese at the 1904 World's Fair in Saint Louis" (Ebrey, p. 416).

Since 1882 American law had shut out Chinese laborers, and in 1904 the treaty that allowed it came up for renewal. Merchants in Shanghai and Guangzhou called on people not to buy American flour, cotton, or kerosene. Students and newspapers spread the call. It was one of the first times ordinary Chinese acted together as a nation.

The postcard shows the China Building at the St Louis fair. The Qing took part officially, even as Chinese travelers were treated as suspects.

Ebrey adds that activists raised funds to buy back foreign-built railways, and some even talked of "their own opium war" against Britain. All of this belongs to Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416): local activism grew more nationalist.

Source beyond Ebrey: Guanhua Wang, In Search of Justice: The 1905–1906 Chinese Anti-American Boycott (2001).

Why this matters: A boycott showed that Chinese citizens could put pressure on a foreign power without the court.

Q1 story · Court reform · 1901–1905

After 1901 the Court Ordered New Schools, and in 1905 It Abolished the Exams

In 1901 Cixi, once the enemy of reform, announced a national school system.
Questions on foreign government and science were added to the exams.
In 1905 the court abolished the civil service examinations altogether · Exams video ↗ Ch. 14 L3 · exams ↗
New military academies trained new armies with German or Japanese instructors.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418. Image: unknown photographer, published 1899.

Now the court. The woman who had crushed the reformers of 1898 became a reformer herself. "Empress Dowager Cixi in 1901 announced the establishment of a national school system and called for putting questions about foreign government and science on the civil service examinations. In 1905 she took the momentous step of abolishing the civil service examination system altogether" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Ebrey adds that the exams "had set the framework for relations between the government and the elite for a millennium." You studied how they worked in Chapter 14; the links take you to the video and that lecture.

The photograph shows the long rows of cells in Beijing where candidates once wrote for days. After 1905 such halls stood empty, and many were torn down.

Abolishing the exams cut the old tie between the throne and the gentry. Ambitious young men now went to new schools, military academies, or Japan, where they met new ideas. This is the second half of Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416): the court's own reform efforts.

Why this matters: Ending the exams removed one of the strongest reasons for the elite to stay loyal to the dynasty.

Q1 concept · Constitutional monarchy

Concept: A Constitutional Monarchy Kept the Throne but Shared Power with an Elected Assembly

Concept

Constitutional monarchy

A form of government in which a hereditary monarch remains head of state, but a written constitution sets the rules and an elected assembly shares in making laws.

In Britain the constitution grew from Parliament and custom.
In Japan the emperor granted the constitution himself in 1889, and his powers stayed large.
For the Qing, the question was how much power the throne would share · Ch. 14 L1 · concept-despotism ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418.

Before the next step, you need one idea. A constitutional monarchy keeps a king or emperor, but a written constitution sets limits and an elected assembly shares in making laws. Britain, Germany, and Japan were all constitutional monarchies in 1905, but very different ones.

In Britain, Parliament held real power and the monarch reigned rather than ruled. In Japan, the Meiji emperor "bestowed" the constitution of 1889 as a gift to his subjects. Sovereignty stayed with the emperor, and the elected house had limited powers.

For a court that wanted to survive, the Japanese version was attractive. It promised the energy of popular participation without giving the people sovereignty. Ebrey's phrase for the choice is exact: a constitution the monarchy would "bestow" on the country, "rather than a constitution that made the people sovereign" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416) asks about the court's reform efforts. A constitution was the biggest of them. Remember how much power the throne had gathered since the Ming founder abolished the chancellor; the link on the red beat takes you back to that idea, despotism, in Chapter 14.

Why this matters: Whether power came from the throne or the people was the central question of the last Qing years.

Check yourself: In a constitutional monarchy, who shares power with the monarch?

Q1 story · The constitutional mission · 1905–1907

A Mission Toured Abroad and Chose Japan's Constitution as China's Model

In 1905 Japan, a constitutional monarchy, defeated autocratic Russia.
That year Cixi sent officials abroad to study constitutions.
In 1906 they recommended the Japanese model, with the throne granting the constitution.
In 1907 the court promised assemblies and a full constitution by 1917 · Ch. 18 L4 · reform after 1901 ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 417–418.

Why did a Qing empress turn to constitutions? Partly because of a war. "When Japan defeated Russia in 1905, some reformers drew the inference that Japan's constitutional form of government had enabled it to best autocratic Russia" (Ebrey, p. 417).

"In 1905, Cixi approved sending a mission abroad to study constitutional forms of government. On its return the next year, the commission recommended the Japanese model, which retained the monarchy and had it bestow the constitution on the country" (p. 418). The commissioners visited Japan, the United States, Britain, and Germany, among others.

"In 1907 plans for national and provincial assemblies were announced, with a full constitution to be in place by 1917." Ten years was a long time to wait. Critics suspected the court wanted to look modern while keeping power in Manchu hands.

The link takes you back to the end of Chapter 18, where the court first began reforms after the Boxer war. This is still Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416): the court's reform efforts, and why they came too slowly.

Why this matters: The court chose the model that promised change while keeping the throne's power.

Check yourself: Why did Japan's victory over Russia make constitutions look attractive?

Q1 story · Beijing · November 1908

In November 1908 the Emperor Died, Cixi a Day Later, and a Two-Year-Old Took the Throne

On 14 November 1908 the Guangxu emperor died at 37; Cixi died the next day.
She had named his two-year-old nephew, Puyi, as the next emperor.
The boy's father, Prince Chun, ruled as regent.
The regents soon dismissed Yuan Shikai, the most powerful general.

Names in This Story

Guangxu emperor 1875–1908

Puyi the last emperor, 1906–1967; pronounced "poo-yee"

Prince Chun (Zaifeng) Puyi's father and regent, 1908–1911

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Edward J. M. Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000). Image: unknown photographer, 1909.

Then the two people at the top died within a day of each other. "The seventy-three-year-old Cixi died (the thirty-three-year-old Guangxu emperor had died suspiciously the day before). She had arranged for a three-year-old to succeed her" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Two corrections. Guangxu, born in 1871, was 37, not 33. And Puyi was two by Western count; "three" is the Chinese way of counting, in which a child is one at birth. In 2008 a Chinese forensic study reported arsenic poisoning in his remains, so "suspiciously" was apt.

In the photograph Puyi stands beside his father, Prince Chun, who holds his younger brother. The prince was in his twenties and had little experience. "His regents did not prove to be particularly effective leaders and soon dismissed Yuan Shikai." Remember Yuan; he will be back.

For Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416), this is where the reforms start to look "doomed."

Source beyond Ebrey: Edward J. M. Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

Why this matters: In its last years the Qing was led by a child and an inexperienced regent.

Q1 story · The provinces · 1909–1910

In 1909 Provincial Assemblies Met, Chosen by Fewer Than 1 Percent of the People

In 1909 each province elected an assembly; fewer than 1 percent of the people could vote.
The voters were men with property or degrees, the same local elites who ran the new schools.
The assemblies sent three petitions for a parliament now; the last claimed 25 million signatures.
In 1910 a national assembly met: 100 members elected, 100 named by the court.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418. Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

The court kept part of its promise. "In 1909 assemblies met in each province and sent representatives to Beijing. Although less than 1 percent of the population had been allowed to vote, the elections generated excitement about participatory government" (Ebrey, p. 418).

To vote, a man needed property, a degree, or experience in office. So the assemblies were filled with exactly the local elites you met earlier: gentry, merchants, and graduates of the new schools. They wanted more power, faster.

"The provincial assemblies circulated three petitions calling for the immediate convening of the national assembly, the last reportedly signed by 25 million people. In 1910 the provisional national assembly met, with one hundred members elected by the provincial assemblies and one hundred appointed by the court."

The court had created the very institutions that would organize opposition to it. In 1911, provincial assembly leaders would help take their provinces out of the empire.

The picture is an AI-generated illustration made for this deck, an interpretation, not a historical source: an assembly of gowned gentry in a new Western-style hall.

This is the heart of Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416): the court's reforms gave local elites a stage.

Why this matters: Elected assemblies turned scattered local activism into an organized political force.

Petitions Asked for a Parliament, and in 1911 the Court Named a Cabinet of Eight Manchus

In May 1911 the court named its first cabinet, as a constitution required.
Of its thirteen ministers, eight were Manchus, one a Mongol, and only four Chinese.
Critics called it the "princes' cabinet," and anti-Manchu anger rose.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000). Image: published by Joseph Kürschner, 1901.

Then the court made its worst mistake. "Anti-Manchu feelings rose, however, when in May 1911 the court announced the formation of a cabinet with eight Manchu, one Mongol, and only four Chinese members" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Several of the Manchus were imperial princes, and the prime minister was Yikuang, Prince Qing, the man in the photograph. Critics called it the "princes' cabinet" or the "imperial clan cabinet." A real constitutional cabinet was supposed to answer to parliament. This one answered to the regent, and it looked like a Manchu family business.

Think about how it looked to the assembly leaders who had gathered millions of signatures for a parliament. The court seemed to be using reform to tighten Manchu control, not to share power.

For Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416), this is the key to "ultimately doomed." Five months later, the soldiers rose at Wuchang.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

Why this matters: The cabinet convinced many moderate reformers that the Qing would never share power.

Q2 story · Tokyo · 1903–1906

By 1906 Some 10,000 Chinese Students Were Studying in Tokyo

After 1905, a Japanese school was the new road to a career.
By 1906, ten thousand of the thirteen thousand Chinese students abroad were in Japan.
Living abroad, they felt China's weakness as a personal shame.
They formed clubs and papers to ask how Japan had modernized so fast.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; Reynolds, China, 1898–1912 (1993), p. 62. Image: unknown photographer, Tokyo, 1903.

With the exams gone, ambitious young men needed a new path, and Japan was close, cheap, and used Chinese characters. "Japan served as an incubator of Chinese nationalism. By 1906, of the thirteen thousand students studying abroad, ten thousand were in Tokyo" (Ebrey, p. 416).

"The experience of living in a foreign country, where they felt humiliated by China's weakness and backwardness, aroused nationalistic feelings in the students, who often formed groups to discuss how Japan had modernized so rapidly and what could be done in China." One student paper joked, "Japanese schools are as numerous as our opium dens, Japanese students as numerous as our opium addicts."

The photograph shows four of those students in 1903. The one standing at the right in the back row came to study medicine. Later, as Lu Xun, he became China's most famous writer, and you will meet him again in this chapter.

Reading Guide Q2 (p. 416) asks how intellectuals interpreted Japanese modernization. Tokyo is where many of them did it.

Source beyond Ebrey: Reynolds, China, 1898–1912 (1993), p. 62.

Why this matters: The leaders of China's next generation, reformers and revolutionaries alike, were formed abroad.

Q2 story · Translating the West · 1898–1909

Yan Fu Said the West's Strength Came from Freeing the Individual

Yan Fu studied at a British naval college in the 1870s, one of the first Chinese to study in England.
He translated Mill's On Liberty (1903) and Montesquieu's The Spirit of Laws (1909).
Western governments, he argued, freed individual energy and channeled it toward national goals.
Only 30 percent of China's troubles, he said, came from foreigners; the rest were its own fault.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; Benjamin Schwartz, In Search of Wealth and Power: Yen Fu and the West (1964). Image: unknown photographer, 1907.

Yan Fu (pronounced "yen foo") was the great translator of his age. Ebrey calls him "one of the first to study in England." He trained at the Royal Naval College in Greenwich in the late 1870s and came home asking why Britain was strong.

His answer is the sentence the guide asks you to copy: "Yan Fu argued that the Western form of government freed the energy of the individual, which could then be channeled toward national goals. As he saw it, the West had achieved wealth and power through a complex package, a key part of which was a very differently conceived nation-state" (Ebrey, p. 416).

Notice what he valued in liberty. Freedom mattered because it made the nation strong. The historian Benjamin Schwartz made that point famous.

And he blamed China, too: "only 30 percent of China's troubles were caused by foreigners; the rest were its own fault and could be remedied by its own actions."

This is Reading Guide Q2 (p. 416): how did intellectuals interpret Western power, and how did they diagnose China's weakness?

Source beyond Ebrey: Benjamin Schwartz, In Search of Wealth and Power: Yen Fu and the West (1964).

Why this matters: Yan Fu taught a generation to see Western power as a whole system, not just guns.

Q2 concept · Social Darwinism

Concept: Social Darwinism Taught That Nations Fought to Survive and the Weak Would Perish

1 / 5

Concept

Social Darwinism

The idea, drawn loosely from Darwin's theory of evolution, that nations and races compete for survival like species, so that the strong win and the weak are conquered or die out.

Yan Fu's On Evolution (1898) made "survival of the fittest" famous in China.
Students read China's plight as a race to avoid extinction.
Western cartoons rarely named Darwin, but drew the same ranking of races.
Their "civilizing mission" said the strong had a right, even a duty, to rule the weak.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 417; Schwartz, In Search of Wealth and Power (1964). Images: Judge, Puck and Pasquino, 1898–1900.

Social Darwinism applied Darwin's theory about species, loosely, to nations and races: the fittest survive, the weak are conquered. Yan Fu's Tianyan lun (On Evolution, 1898) made "survival of the fittest" a Chinese student slogan. "The language of Social Darwinism, with its talk of countries in desperate competition for survival, seemed to many to describe China's plight accurately" (Ebrey, p. 417).

Click through five Western cartoons. In "Some One Must Back Up" (1900), Uncle Sam's cart of Progress faces a Boxer on a China dragon. In "The White Man's Burden" (1899), Britain and America haul colonized peoples, China among them, toward Civilization. In "The Pigtail Has Got to Go" (1898), Civilization cuts China's queue. In "School Begins" (1899), Uncle Sam scolds colonized "children" while a Chinese boy waits at the door. In the Italian cartoon (1900), Civilization rallies Europe's armies against a monstrous yellow face.

None of them names Darwin. They show the racial hierarchy and "civilizing mission" that traveled with Social Darwinism. They are racist, and they are here because this is how Chinese abroad saw themselves drawn.

Reading Guide Q2 (p. 416) asks how intellectuals diagnosed China's weakness.

Source beyond Ebrey: Schwartz, In Search of Wealth and Power (1964).

Why this matters: Social Darwinism made reform feel like national life or death.

Q2 story · Liang Qichao · 1903

Liang Qichao Toured America in 1903 and Came Home Wanting a Strong State First

In exile in Japan, Liang wrote that China could grow strong through "democracy" · Ch. 18 L4 · Liang ↗
By "democracy" he meant a government that drew its strength from the people.
In 1903 he toured the United States and disliked what he saw of its politics.
He came home preferring the strong states and constitutional monarchies of Japan and Germany.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 416–417; Hao Chang, Liang Ch'i-ch'ao and Intellectual Transition in China (1971). Image: unknown photographer, 1903.

You met Liang Qichao at the end of Chapter 18 as Kang Youwei's brilliant student; the link takes you back. After 1898 he fled to Japan and became the most widely read writer in Chinese.

"Liang promoted the idea that China could become strong through 'democracy,' which to him meant a government that drew its strength from the people, but not necessarily a representative government or one that defended individual rights" (Ebrey, p. 416).

In 1903 he traveled across the United States, speaking to Chinese communities from Vancouver to New Orleans; the photograph was taken in Portland. He found "the American form of populist democracy unsatisfactory. He preferred the statist ideas and constitutional monarchies of Japan and Germany" (p. 417). He worried that the Chinese he met abroad were not ready to govern themselves.

Compare him with Yan Fu. Both wanted freedom and participation mainly because they would make the nation strong. That is Reading Guide Q2 (p. 416).

Source beyond Ebrey: Hao Chang, Liang Ch'i-ch'ao and Intellectual Transition in China (1971).

Why this matters: Liang showed that admiring the West did not mean wanting Western democracy.

Q2 story · Shanghai · 1903

An Eighteen-Year-Old, Zou Rong, Called for Wiping Out the Manchus

In 1903 Zou Rong, back from Japan, published The Revolutionary Army in Shanghai.
It called for an army to "wipe out the five million barbarian Manchus."
It praised the "sacred Han race, descendants of the Yellow Emperor."
Arrested in the foreign settlement, he died in prison in 1905, aged twenty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 417; Michael Gasster, in The Cambridge History of China, vol. 11, pt. 2 (1980), p. 482; Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000). Image: unknown photographer.

Some young Chinese drew a different lesson: the root of China's problems was the Manchus. "In 1903 the nineteen-year-old Zou Rong published an inflammatory tract calling for the creation of a revolutionary army to 'wipe out the five million barbarian Manchus, wash away the shame of two hundred and sixty years of cruelty and oppression, and make China clean once again'" (Ebrey, p. 417).

Zou Rong was born in 1885, so he was eighteen by Western count; "nineteen" is the Chinese count. He called the Chinese the "sacred Han race, descendants of the Yellow Emperor," enslaved by the Manchus and in danger of extinction. That is Social Darwinism turned into race hatred.

The Qing demanded his arrest. Because he was in the foreign International Settlement in Shanghai, he was tried in its court, sentenced to two years, and died in prison in April 1905. His book sold in the hundreds of thousands.

Remember his words. In 1911 some revolutionaries acted on them. For Reading Guide Q2 (p. 416), Zou Rong is the third diagnosis: the problem was Manchu rule.

Source beyond Ebrey: Michael Gasster, in The Cambridge History of China, vol. 11, pt. 2 (1980), p. 482; Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

Why this matters: Anti-Manchu nationalism gave the revolution its most powerful and most dangerous slogan.

Check yourself: How did Zou Rong use the language of race?

Q3 story · Hawaii and Hong Kong · 1879–1892

Sun Yatsen Grew Up in Hawaii and Trained as a Doctor in Hong Kong

Sun was born in 1866 in Guangdong, to a farming family, not a family of scholars.
At thirteen he joined his elder brother in Hawaii and went to a mission school.
He studied Western medicine in Hong Kong and finished in 1892.
There he and his friends began to talk about a republic.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 417; Marie-Claire Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: K. T. Thompson studio, Shanghai, 1911.

The man later honored as the founder of the Chinese republic came from outside the old elite. "Like Hong Xiuquan, Kang Youwei, and Liang Qichao before him, Sun came from Guangdong province. Unlike them, he was neither from a literati family nor trained in the Confucian classics" (Ebrey, p. 417). The guide asks you to copy that sentence, which begins "Like Hong Xiuquan."

"Several of his close relatives had emigrated, and in 1879 he was sent to join a brother in Hawaii." He went to school in Honolulu, learned English, and was drawn to Christianity. "Later he went to Hong Kong to study Western medicine, completing his degree in 1892."

The portrait shows him in 1911, at forty-five, short-haired and in a Western suit, the year he became the revolution's leader. Few Chinese of his time had an education like his.

In Chinese he is usually called Sun Zhongshan ("soon juhng-shahn"); Sun Yatsen is the Cantonese form of another of his names. Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417) asks you to evaluate his strategies and ideas. Start with where he came from.

Source beyond Ebrey: Marie-Claire Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: Sun's foreign education made him an outsider at home and an insider among overseas Chinese.

Q3 story · Revive China Society · 1894–1896

Sun Founded the Revive China Society, Cut His Queue, and Turned to the Secret Societies

Sun's friends decided the way to topple the Manchus was through the secret societies · Ch. 16 L4 · secret societies ↗
He founded the Revive China Society in Hawaii in 1894, then a branch in Hong Kong.
Its plots failed, and he cut off his queue, the sign of loyalty to the Manchus · Ch. 16 L1 · the queue order ↗
Like Hong Xiuquan, he looked for followers outside the elite · Ch. 18 L2 · Taiping ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 417–418; Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: unknown photographer, Hong Kong.

In Hong Kong, Sun and his friends reasoned that "the best way to overthrow the Manchus... would be to ally with the secret societies so pervasive in south China. Groups like the Triads were anti-Manchu, had large mass followings, and had an organizational base reaching from one province to another" (Ebrey, p. 417). You met the Triads in Chapter 16; the first link takes you back.

"Sun set up the first two chapters of the Revive China Society in Hawaii and Hong Kong. The society's efforts to instigate an uprising with secret society members as the muscle never got very far" (p. 418). A plot to seize Guangzhou in 1895 was discovered, and Sun fled.

"In 1896, Sun cut off his queue and began wearing Western clothes." Most biographers place this in Japan in late 1895, just after he fled. The queue had been required of every man since the Manchu conquest; the second link goes to that order.

For Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417), notice his first strategy: secret societies as the muscle.

Source beyond Ebrey: Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: Cutting the queue was a public declaration of revolution.

Q3 story · Tokyo · 1905

In Tokyo in 1905, Students Joined Sun's Revolutionary Alliance

In London in 1896 Sun saw Westerners criticizing their own societies and arguing for socialism.
He began to think China could skip ahead to a more progressive government.
In Tokyo in 1905, with Japanese help, he joined radical students to found the Revolutionary Alliance.
Students from educated families now followed a doctor from a farming village.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

Sun's second strategy was to win over the students. First, he traveled. "He spent time in England, where he discovered that many Westerners saw flaws in their own institutions and were advocating a variety of socialist solutions. Sun began to think that China could skip ahead of the West by going directly to a more progressive form of government" (Ebrey, p. 418). In London in 1896 the Qing legation kidnapped him, and British protests won his release; the affair made him famous.

"In Japan in 1905 some Japanese helped Sun join forces with the more radical of the student revolutionaries to form the Revolutionary Alliance." In Chinese it is the Tongmenghui ("toong-mung-hway"). Its oath called for driving out the Manchus, restoring China to the Chinese, founding a republic, and equalizing land.

"Despite the difference in social background, the students from educated families were excited by Sun's promise of quick solutions to China's problems."

The picture is an AI-generated illustration made for this deck, an interpretation, not a historical source: students in Tokyo, many with queues cut, listening to Sun.

For Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417), notice the shift: from secret societies to a party of educated young people.

Source beyond Ebrey: Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: The Alliance joined Sun's fame and money to the students' energy.

Check yourself: Why did educated students follow Sun?

Q3 concept · The Three People's Principles

Concept: The Three People's Principles — Nationalism, Democracy, the People's Livelihood

Concept

The Three People's Principles

Sun Yatsen's program: nationalism (an end to Manchu rule and foreign domination), democracy (elections and a constitution), and the people's livelihood (a mild socialism that would equalize land and limit capital).

Nationalism opposed both the Manchus and the foreign powers.
Democracy meant elections and a constitution.
The people's livelihood was, in Ebrey's words, "a vague sort of socialism."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418.

In these years Sun boiled his ideas down to three slogans. In Chinese they are the Sanmin zhuyi ("sahn-meen joo-ee"). Ebrey defines them precisely:

"Nationalism (which opposed both rule by Manchus and domination by foreign powers), democracy (which to Sun meant elections and a constitution), and the 'people's livelihood,' a vague sort of socialism with equalization of landholdings and curbs on capital" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Notice how each principle answered a diagnosis you have already met. Nationalism took up Zou Rong's anger at the Manchus, but also the boycotters' anger at foreigners. Democracy went further than Liang's statism. The people's livelihood drew on the socialism Sun had seen in Europe.

The program was broad enough to unite very different people, and vague enough to mean different things to each. That vagueness helped in 1905 and caused trouble later.

Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417) asks you to evaluate Sun's ideological framework. This is it.

Why this matters: The Three Principles remained the official ideology of the Nationalist Party for the rest of the century.

Check yourself: What were the two enemies of Sun's nationalism?

Q3 concept · Political tutelage

Concept: Political Tutelage Meant the Party Would Rule Until the People Were Ready

Concept

Political tutelage

A period of guided rule in which the revolutionary party would govern under a provisional constitution while teaching the people to vote, starting with local elections, before full democracy began.

Sun admitted most Chinese had no experience of politics.
So the revolutionaries would rule first, under a temporary constitution.
People would learn democracy by electing local officials.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418.

One more idea from Sun, and it mattered for a long time. "Sun admitted that the Chinese people were unaccustomed to political participation; nevertheless, he believed that they could be guided toward democracy through a period of political tutelage, during which the revolutionaries would promulgate a provisional constitution and people would begin electing local officials" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Tutelage means guardianship, the way a teacher guides a pupil. The revolution would happen in stages: first military rule, then tutelage by the party, and only then constitutional government.

Compare this with Liang Qichao, who also doubted the people were ready. Both men wanted participation, and both wanted a strong guide to come first.

Ask the obvious question: who decides when the pupils are ready? Later Nationalist leaders used tutelage to justify one-party rule for decades.

Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417) asks you to evaluate Sun's framework. Tutelage is the part to weigh most carefully.

Why this matters: Tutelage tied democracy to a long period of rule by a single party.

Check yourself: Why did Sun think China needed a period of tutelage?

Q3 story · Southeast Asia and beyond · 1905–1911

Seven or Eight Uprisings Failed, and Sun Raised Money from Overseas Chinese

The Alliance sponsored seven or eight risings in south China; all failed.
Sun spent most of these years abroad, looking for money and foreign backers.
Overseas Chinese merchants and workers in Southeast Asia and America paid for the plots.
Inside China, revolutionaries quietly recruited soldiers in the New Army.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998). Image: unknown photographer, Singapore, 1907.

The Alliance did not wait for theory. "This alliance sponsored seven or eight attempts at uprisings over the next few years. Sun himself continued to spend most of his time abroad in search of funds and foreign backers, especially overseas Chinese" (Ebrey, p. 418).

The risings were small and badly coordinated, mostly in Guangdong, Guangxi, and Yunnan near the borders. The last, at Guangzhou in April 1911, cost the lives of dozens of young revolutionaries, whom the republic later honored as martyrs.

The photograph shows Sun with the Alliance branch in Singapore. Communities like this one, from Malaya to San Francisco, funded the plots. Many had left China poor and resented a government that did nothing for them abroad.

Meanwhile a new strategy grew up inside China: recruiting soldiers of the New Army, the modern units the court itself had created. That is where the revolution finally came from.

For Reading Guide Q3 (p. 417), weigh this record. The strategies kept failing, yet the cause kept growing.

Source beyond Ebrey: Bergère, Sun Yat-sen (1998).

Why this matters: The revolution was paid for abroad but carried out by the Qing's own soldiers.

Q1 and Q3 story · The provinces · October–November 1911

Police Found the Rebels' Lists, the Soldiers Rose, and Fifteen Provinces Left the Qing

The rebels made a brigade commander, Li Yuanhong, head of a new military government.
They telegraphed the other provinces and asked them to declare independence.
New Army officers and provincial assembly leaders took province after province out of the empire.
Within six weeks, fifteen provinces had seceded.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 418; Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976). Image: unknown photographer, 1911.

Back to Wuchang. The mutineers needed a leader with rank, so they turned to a brigade commander, Li Yuanhong, who agreed to head the new Hubei Military Government. Then "the revolutionaries telegraphed the other provinces asking them to declare their independence. Within six weeks, fifteen provinces had seceded" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Now you can see why it spread so fast. In province after province, the same two groups the court had created acted together: officers of the New Army, and the gentry leaders of the provincial assemblies. The historian Joseph Esherick showed how, in Hunan and Hubei, reformist elites took over the revolution and kept order on their own terms.

The photograph shows revolutionary troops at Hankou, which government forces retook and burned in November. There was real fighting, but most provinces changed sides with little bloodshed between Chinese.

This slide answers both Reading Guide Q1 (p. 416), on why reform was doomed, and Q3 (p. 417): Sun's long campaign prepared the ground, but the rising came from the New Army.

Source beyond Ebrey: Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976).

Why this matters: The dynasty fell when the elites and soldiers it had empowered stopped defending it.

Story · The garrison cities · October–December 1911

In the Garrison Cities, Revolutionaries Killed Thousands of Manchu Men, Women, and Children

In Wuchang, soldiers hunted down Manchus; hundreds of banner people died in three days.
In Xi'an the walled Manchu quarter fell on 24 October; thousands were killed or took their own lives.
There were killings in other garrison cities too, among them Taiyuan, Zhenjiang, and Nanjing.
The imperial family was spared; many ordinary banner families were not · Ch. 16 L2 · garrisons ↗ Ch. 18 L4 · bannermen ↗

Sources: Edward J. M. Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000), ch. 4; Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976); Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 417, 419.

Ebrey says the royal family "suffered neither executions nor humiliations" (p. 419). Ordinary Manchus fared worse. Most lived as banner families in walled garrison quarters, recognizable by their homes, speech, names, and the women's unbound feet.

Wuchang, 10–13 October 1911: the rebel soldiers' cry was "Slay the Manchu officials and the banner people!" Soldiers made suspects say "six hundred sixty-six," which Manchus pronounced differently. The new government's spokesman counted 400–500 banner people killed in three days; a Reuters reporter guessed 800. Foreign consuls protested, and the killing stopped.

Xi'an, 22–25 October: the Manchu quarter, perhaps 20,000 people, fell on the 24th. The British missionary J. C. Keyte, an outsider's account, wrote of "not less than ten thousand" killed or dead by suicide; Japanese consular and Chinese estimates run from about 2,000 to 5,000. The garrison general drowned himself in a well.

Why: Zou Rong's call to "wipe out the five million barbarian Manchus" (p. 417) was, Edward Rhoads writes, "no mere rhetorical flourish." Rhoads (Manchus and Han, 2000, ch. 4) notes this violence "has been minimized in nearly all the retrospective accounts" and calls the victims "essentially victims of genocide"; Joseph Esherick says Wuchang "approached racial slaughter." The numbers remain uncertain.

Source beyond Ebrey: Edward J. M. Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000), ch. 4; Esherick, Reform and Revolution in China (1976).

Why this matters: The revolution that ended Manchu rule also killed thousands of Manchu civilians.

Check yourself: How could revolutionaries identify Manchus?

Story · Beijing · February 1912

In February 1912 the Boy Emperor Abdicated, and Two Thousand Years of Monarchy Ended

On 1 January 1912 Sun Yatsen became provisional president of a republic at Nanjing.
The court called back Yuan Shikai, and he bargained with both sides.
On 12 February 1912 the court issued the abdication; in March, Yuan became president.
Men cut off their queues, and soldiers forced others to.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000). Image: Bain News Service, about 1915–1920.

The court did not give up at once. "In desperation it turned to Yuan Shikai, whom the court had dismissed only a few years before, and asked him to mount a military campaign against the revolutionaries. Yuan went back and forth between the court and the revolutionaries, seeing what he could get from each" (Ebrey, p. 419).

Sun Yatsen hurried home and was sworn in at Nanjing on 1 January 1912 as provisional president. But the revolutionaries feared foreign intervention and needed Yuan's army, so they agreed that Yuan would be president if he ended the monarchy.

On 12 February 1912 the Empress Dowager Longyu, Guangxu's widow, issued the edict of abdication in the name of the six-year-old Puyi. "In February 1912, the last Qing emperor abdicated, and in March Yuan Shikai took over as president. As a mark of solidarity with the revolutionaries, men cut off their queues, the symbol of their subordination to the Manchus."

Ebrey's photo caption adds that "soldiers often forced men to cut off their queues." The title of this lecture comes from that moment. The photograph shows Puyi several years later, still living in the palace as a titled ex-emperor.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

Why this matters: The monarchy that had ruled China since 221 BCE ended by negotiation.

Pierian Spring · Story · Reformers in exile · 1899–1911

Kang and Liang Wrote from Exile in Japan, and Liang Explained "Democracy"

After 1898, with prices on their heads, Kang and Liang settled in Japan.
In 1899 they founded the Protect the Emperor Society, with branches across the Chinese diaspora.
Liang's magazines, printed in Japan, were smuggled into China and read everywhere.
Reformers and Sun's revolutionaries competed for the same overseas Chinese money.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 416; L. Eve Armentrout Ma, Revolutionaries, Monarchists, and Chinatowns (1990); Hao Chang (1971). Image: Los Angeles Times, about 1905.

After the coup of 1898, Cixi's government put prices on the heads of the two leading reformers. "The two best-known reformers, Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao, had settled in Japan" (Ebrey, p. 416).

In 1899, in Canada, they founded the Protect the Emperor Society (Baohuanghui), which aimed to restore the Guangxu emperor and make him a constitutional monarch. It opened branches from Vancouver to Singapore. Kang traveled the world for years; the photograph was taken during his visit to the United States.

Liang did the writing. "In Chinese magazines published in Japan, Liang promoted the idea that China could become strong through 'democracy,' which to him meant a government that drew its strength from the people."

In the same Chinatowns, Sun Yatsen's revolutionaries asked for money too. From 1905 to 1907, the reformers' and the revolutionaries' magazines in Japan argued in print over reform or revolution. Overseas Chinese were courted by both.

Source beyond Ebrey: L. Eve Armentrout Ma, Revolutionaries, Monarchists, and Chinatowns (1990); Hao Chang (1971).

Why this matters: The first Chinese political parties were built abroad.

Pierian Spring · Story · The Northern Army · 1901–1909

Yuan Shikai Built the Strongest Army in China

After Li Hongzhang died in 1901, Yuan became the most powerful general in China.
He commanded the Northern Army and headed the Baoding Military Academy.
As governor-general, he also reformed schools, police, and commerce.
His officers owed their careers to him, not to the throne.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 418–419; Stephen R. MacKinnon, Power and Politics in Late Imperial China (1980). Image: Popular Science Monthly, 1905–06.

Every army in this lecture traces back to one man. "With the death of Li Hongzhang in 1901, Yuan Shikai emerged as the most powerful general, serving as both commander of the Northern Army and head of the Baoding Military Academy" (Ebrey, p. 418).

Yuan was governor-general of Zhili, the province around Beijing, from 1901 to 1907. "While governor, he had initiated reforms in education, commerce, and industry, and his army not only was equipped with modern weapons but also was trained along lines established by German and Japanese advisers" (p. 419). The magazine caption on the slide calls him China's "chief educational reformist."

By 1905 his Northern Army had six modern divisions. Its officers came out of his academies, and many owed him their promotions. When the regents dismissed him in 1909, he went home to Henan, but the officers stayed loyal.

So in 1911 the court had to beg him to return, and the revolutionaries had to deal with him. His army was the strongest card on the table.

Source beyond Ebrey: Stephen R. MacKinnon, Power and Politics in Late Imperial China (1980).

Why this matters: Personal loyalty in the army let one general decide the fate of the dynasty.

Check yourself: Why did both the court and the revolutionaries need Yuan in 1911?

Pierian Spring · Story · The Forbidden City · 1912–1924

The Abdication Terms Let the Boy Emperor Keep His Palace and His Title

Under the Articles of Favorable Treatment, Puyi kept the title of emperor.
He stayed in the inner Forbidden City with a yearly allowance of four million dollars.
Separate articles promised Manchus, Mongols, Muslims, and Tibetans equal rights.
Puyi lived in the palace until 1924, when a warlord's soldiers expelled him.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 419; Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

The deal of 1912 was generous to the emperor. "The emperor would abdicate, but he and his entourage would be allowed to remain in the Forbidden City, receive generous allowances, and keep much of their property. Thus, unlike the Bourbons in France or the Romanovs in Russia, the Manchu royal family suffered neither executions nor humiliations when it was deposed" (Ebrey, p. 419).

The terms, called the Articles of Favorable Treatment, let Puyi keep his title and be treated like a foreign monarch. The republic promised four million dollars a year, though it rarely paid in full. Other articles promised Manchus, Mongols, Muslims, and Tibetans equal rights and protection of their property, under the new slogan of "five races in one republic."

Compare that promise with what had happened in Xi'an and Wuchang a few months earlier. The new republic needed the non-Han peoples, and their lands, to stay.

Puyi lived in the inner palace, with eunuchs and tutors, until 1924, when the warlord Feng Yuxiang's soldiers expelled him. You will meet him again.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rhoads, Manchus and Han (2000).

Why this matters: The gentle end of the monarchy left the last emperor alive and available for others to use.

Background essay: the historians’ debates behind this lecture ↗

Everything in the story slides comes from Ebrey, Chapter 24 (pp. 416–419), and each slide's source line gives the page. Where a slide or note adds something from sources beyond Ebrey, the source line or the notes name it, and the books above are where those facts come from.

The title cartoon is an American view, and the five cartoons in the Social Darwinism slideshow are American and Italian, all openly racist; the St Louis postcard was made for American fairgoers, and the magazine photographs were taken for American readers.

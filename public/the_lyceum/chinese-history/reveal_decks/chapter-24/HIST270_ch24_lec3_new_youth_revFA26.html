Youth Is Like Early Spring

The New Culture Movement, Lu Xun, and the Women's Movement

1900–1927 · Study Buddy edition

Welcome to Lecture 3 of Chapter 24. While warlords fought over China, a small magazine and a university in Beijing set out to change how young Chinese thought (Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 420–427).

This lecture has three parts: the New Culture Movement, the writer Lu Xun, and the women's movement. Lu Xun's story "Diary of a Madman" is this chapter's primary-source reading, and the lecture prepares you for it.

Q6 story · Shanghai · September 1915

In 1915 a New Magazine Told Young Chinese, "Youth Is Like Early Spring"

On 15 September 1915 Chen Duxiu launched Youth Magazine in Shanghai.
That same autumn Yuan Shikai was preparing to make himself emperor · Ch. 24 L2 · the throne ↗
Chen wrote: "Youth is like early spring, like the rising sun … like a newly sharpened blade."
In September 1916 the magazine took a new name: New Youth.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Chow Tse-tsung, The May Fourth Movement (1960). Image: Qingnian zazhi, vol. 1, no. 1, 1915.

Start with a magazine. In September 1915 a former revolutionary named Chen Duxiu began publishing a monthly in Shanghai. Ebrey quotes his opening essay: "Youth is like early spring, like the rising sun, like the trees and grass in bud, like a newly sharpened blade" (Ebrey, p. 420). Ebrey sets the scene: "In the first issue of New Youth in 1915, Chen challenged the long-standing Confucian value of deference toward elders." He told readers not to waste their "fleeting time in arguing with the older generation."

Ebrey calls the first issue New Youth. In fact the magazine was called Youth Magazine (Qingnian zazhi) in its first year. It took the name New Youth (Xin qingnian) with the first issue of volume 2 in September 1916, the cover you saw on the title slide. Both covers carry the French title La Jeunesse, "youth."

Think about the timing. That autumn Yuan Shikai was arranging his own coronation, as you saw in the last lecture; the link takes you back. Young readers who had cheered the 1911 revolution watched the republic turn back toward monarchy. Chen's answer was that politics would never change until the culture changed.

Reading Guide Q6 (p. 420) asks what this movement wanted.

Why this matters: A cheap magazine became the voice of a generation that felt the 1911 revolution had failed.

Check yourself: Why might Chen compare youth to "a newly sharpened blade"?

Q6 story · The editor · 1879–1915

Its Editor, Chen Duxiu, Had Passed the First Level of the Old Exams, Then Turned Against the Old Learning

Born 1879 in Anhui, he passed the county examination in 1896, at age 17 · Ch. 14 L3 · the exams ↗
In 1897 he failed the provincial exam in Nanjing and gave up on the system.
He studied in Japan several times (Ebrey adds France) and took part in the 1911 revolution.
By 1915 he was, Ebrey writes, "a zealous advocate of individual freedom."

Names in This Story

Chen Duxiu editor of New Youth, 1879–1942; pronounced "chuhn doo-shyow"

New Youth Xin qingnian, the magazine; Youth Magazine in 1915–16

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Howard L. Boorman, ed., Biographical Dictionary of Republican China (1967); Yves Chevrier, "De l'occidentalisme à la solitude," Études chinoises 3 (1984). Image: undated.

Who was this editor? "Chen had received a traditional education and had taken the civil service examinations before studying in Japan and France. A participant in the 1911 revolution, he became a zealous advocate of individual freedom" (Ebrey, p. 420). His years in Japan are well documented; whether he also studied in France is disputed. Boorman's Biographical Dictionary of Republican China says he did, some time between 1907 and 1910, but the historian Yves Chevrier finds no stay in France at all.

The details make the point sharper. Chen was born in Anqing, Anhui, in 1879, into a gentry family, and was raised by a strict grandfather. At seventeen he passed the county-level examination and ranked first in his district. The next year he went to Nanjing for the provincial examination, failed, and later wrote with disgust about the days he spent there among the candidates. You can revisit how the examinations worked through the link to Chapter 14.

So the man who attacked Confucian learning knew it from the inside. He had memorized the classics and written the essays. After 1900 he studied in Tokyo, ran a vernacular newspaper in Anhui, and joined the revolutionaries.

Why this matters: The leaders of the New Culture Movement were not outsiders; they had been trained in the tradition they rejected.

Q6 story · The attack on Confucius · 1915–1919

Chen Told the Young to Throw Off "Slavish Morality" and Choose Science and Democracy

Chen's first essay urged the young to be "independent, not servile" and "scientific, not imaginative."
He called "loyalty, filial piety, chastity, and righteousness" nothing but "a slavish morality."
Confucianism, he wrote, was incompatible with "the new society, and the new state."
In 1919 he summed up his program in two names: "Mr. Democracy" and "Mr. Science."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Teng and Fairbank, China's Response to the West (1971); Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960).

Chen's opening essay, "Call to Youth," listed six choices for the young. In Teng and Fairbank's translation, they should be independent, not servile; progressive, not conservative; aggressive, not retiring; cosmopolitan, not isolationist; utilitarian, not formalistic; and scientific, not imaginative.

Then he went after Confucius. Ebrey quotes him: "We must be thoroughly aware of the incompatibility between Confucianism and the new belief, the new society, and the new state." To him, "loyalty, filial piety, chastity, and righteousness" were nothing but "a slavish morality" (Ebrey, p. 420). Those four virtues were the heart of the old family and the old state: obey the ruler, obey your parents, and, for women, stay faithful even to a dead husband.

In January 1919, answering critics, Chen wrote that the magazine stood for two gentlemen, "Mr. Democracy" and "Mr. Science," and that defending them meant opposing Confucianism.

Ebrey describes the result: young people "began challenging the authority of their parents to make decisions for them about school, work, and marriage."

Source beyond Ebrey: Teng and Fairbank, China's Response to the West (1971); Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960).

Why this matters: Chen turned the old virtues into the enemy, which is the "sever" in Reading Guide Q6 (p. 420).

Check yourself: Which of the four virtues do you think young readers found easiest to reject, and why?

Q6 concept · The New Culture Movement

Concept: The New Culture Movement Set Out to Replace Confucian Values with Modern Ones

Concept

New Culture Movement

The campaign of the late 1910s, led by teachers and writers at Beijing University, to replace Confucian values and the classical written language with science, democracy, individual freedom, and writing in everyday speech.

Its base was Beijing University; its voice was New Youth.
Its goals: science, democracy, individual freedom, and the vernacular.
Its enemies: Confucian family rule, "blind adherence to tradition," and the classical language.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 420, 426. Image: unknown photographer, Beijing, 1920.

You need a name for all this. The New Culture Movement was the campaign of the late 1910s and early 1920s to remake Chinese culture. Ebrey writes that "the newly reorganized Beijing University played a central role in this New Culture movement" (Ebrey, p. 420).

Its goals and its enemies are listed on page 426: "Nationalism, patriotism, progress, science, democracy, and freedom were the goals; imperialism, feudalism, warlordism, autocracy, patriarchy, and blind adherence to tradition were the evils to be opposed."

The photograph shows four of its leaders in Beijing in 1920. Cai Yuanpei was the university's president; Hu Shi taught philosophy; Li Dazhao ran the library and was soon one of the first Chinese Marxists; Jiang Menglin was an educator trained, like Hu, at Columbia. All four had studied abroad: Cai in Germany, Li in Japan, and Hu and Jiang in the United States.

Historians often treat this movement and the May Fourth protests of 1919, the subject of the next lecture, as two halves of one story.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q6 (p. 420) asks for the movement's "core intellectual objectives"; this slide is your list.

Check yourself: Name two goals and two "evils" of the New Culture Movement.

Q6 story · Beijing University · 1917

At Beijing University, Cai Yuanpei Made Chen Dean, and the Radicals Found a Home

In January 1917 Cai Yuanpei, a top examination graduate, became president of Beijing University.
He opened it to all schools of thought, from old-style classicists to anarchists.
Within weeks he made Chen Duxiu dean of letters, and New Youth moved to Beijing.
Hu Shi, Li Dazhao, and Lu Xun's brother Zhou Zuoren joined the faculty or wrote for the magazine.

Names in This Story

Cai Yuanpei president of Beijing University from 1917; pronounced "tsye ywen-pay"

Beijing University Beida, also called Peking University; founded 1898

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 420; Timothy B. Weston, The Power of Position: Beijing University, Intellectuals, and Chinese Political Culture (2004). Image: Bain News Service, about 1910.

Ebrey says that "the newly reorganized Beijing University" was the center of the movement and that Chen "was appointed dean of letters" (Ebrey, p. 420). The man who reorganized it was Cai Yuanpei, who is not named in Ebrey.

Cai had earned the highest examination degree, the jinshi, in the 1890s, then joined the revolutionaries and studied in Germany. He became the university's president in January 1917. He wanted a real university, not a training school for officials, and he hired scholars of every kind. Old-style classicists who still wore queues taught in the same buildings as radicals.

Cai visited Chen at his Beijing hotel again and again until Chen agreed to become dean of letters. Chen brought New Youth with him, and the magazine's editors and the university's professors became nearly the same people. Students read it, and soon they were writing for magazines of their own.

Source beyond Ebrey: Timothy B. Weston, The Power of Position: Beijing University, Intellectuals, and Chinese Political Culture (2004).

Why this matters: The movement had an institution behind it: China's leading university gave the radicals jobs, students, and prestige.

Check yourself: Why did it matter that the movement was based at a university and not only in a magazine?

Q6 story · Hu Shi · 1917

Hu Shi Said, "A Dead Language Can Never Produce a Living Literature"

Hu Shi studied in the United States from 1910 to 1917, at Cornell and then Columbia.
In January 1917 New Youth printed his "modest proposals" for reforming literature.
He argued that Chinese should write the language people actually spoke.
That autumn, aged 25, he joined the Beijing University faculty.

Names in This Story

Hu Shi scholar and champion of the vernacular, 1891–1962; pronounced "hoo shir"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 420–421; Jerome B. Grieder, Hu Shih and the Chinese Renaissance (1970). Image: 1922.

The second leader was Hu Shi. Ebrey writes that "the leader of the movement to write in the vernacular was Hu Shi," appointed to the Beijing University faculty "after he returned from seven years studying philosophy in the United States." His slogan: "A dead language," Hu declared, "can never produce a living literature" (Ebrey, p. 420).

Hu had gone to Cornell in 1910 on a Boxer Indemnity scholarship, first to study agriculture, and then to Columbia, where he studied with the philosopher John Dewey. From America he sent New Youth an essay in January 1917 with eight proposals. Among them: do not imitate the ancients, avoid stale classical phrases, and do not avoid the words of everyday speech.

Ebrey points out how radical this was. Chinese civilization had been tied so closely to the classical language that "Hu's assertions came dangerously close to declaring Chinese civilization dead" (Ebrey, p. 421).

Source beyond Ebrey: Jerome B. Grieder, Hu Shih and the Chinese Renaissance (1970).

Why this matters: Hu turned the attack on Confucius into a practical program: change the way China writes.

Check yourself: What did Hu mean by calling classical Chinese "a dead language"?

Q6 concept · The vernacular

Concept: Vernacular Writing Meant Writing the Way People Spoke

Concept

Vernacular (baihua)

The written form of everyday spoken Chinese, as opposed to classical Chinese (wenyan), the terse literary language of the classics that only the educated could read.

Classical: 吾欲往 (wu yu wang). Vernacular: 我要去 (wo yao qu). Both mean "I want to go." · Ch. 14 L4 · vernacular ↗
Classical Chinese was the same across China but sounded like no one's speech.
Hu Shi argued that a national literature in the vernacular would create a standard spoken language, as in France and Germany.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 420–421; John DeFrancis, The Chinese Language: Fact and Fantasy (1984).

You need this word. The vernacular, baihua or "plain speech," is written Chinese that follows the way people talk. Classical Chinese, wenyan, was the compressed literary language of the classics, the histories, and the examinations. For two thousand years it was "the mark of the educated person," as Ebrey puts it (Ebrey, p. 420).

The example on the slide shows the difference. The classical sentence uses older words that no one used in speech by 1900. The vernacular sentence is what a person in Beijing would actually say. Reading classical Chinese took years of training; reading the vernacular needed only literacy. Vernacular writing was not new in 1917: in Chapter 14 you saw Ming novels, stories and plays written in it; the link on the first beat takes you back.

Ebrey notes that Hu saw a real cost. Classical Chinese "had allowed speakers of mutually unintelligible dialects to communicate with each other and thus had been a source of unity." His answer was that a vernacular literature would spread one standard dialect, "much as standard vernaculars had gained hold in France and Germany" (Ebrey, p. 421).

Source beyond Ebrey: John DeFrancis, The Chinese Language: Fact and Fantasy (1984).

Why this matters: Changing the written language meant opening reading, and politics, to far more people.

Check yourself: Who gained, and who lost, when the vernacular replaced classical Chinese?

Q6 story · The vernacular wins · 1918–1922

By 1918 the Magazine Was in the Vernacular, and in 1920 the Schools Followed

From January 1918 New Youth was written entirely in the vernacular.
By 1919, hundreds of new magazines for young readers had appeared.
In January 1920 the Ministry of Education ordered vernacular textbooks for the first two grades.
By 1922 the classical textbooks were to be phased out of the elementary schools.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 421–422; Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960). Image: Xin qingnian, vol. 4, no. 1, 1918.

The vernacular won fast. "Chen Duxiu concurred with Hu, and soon New Youth was written entirely in vernacular Chinese" (Ebrey, p. 421). The change came with the first issue of volume 4, in January 1918, shown here.

"By 1919 New Youth had been joined by many other periodicals aimed at young people aspiring for a New China. Magazines were filled with articles on Western ideas of all sorts, including socialism, anarchism, democracy, liberalism, Darwinism, pragmatism, and science" (Ebrey, p. 422). Historians estimate that several hundred such magazines appeared in 1919 alone.

Then the state followed. Ebrey says the Ministry of Education decided in 1921 that elementary textbooks would be in the vernacular. The order actually came earlier: in January 1920 the ministry told the schools to teach the "national language," the vernacular, in the first two grades, and a second order that spring set 1922 as the year the old classical textbooks would go. The slide uses those dates.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960).

Why this matters: Within five years an idea in a magazine had become the law of the schools.

Q6 story · Visitors from the West · 1919–1922

Dewey, Russell, and Sanger Came to Lecture, and Dewey Found the Students Hungry for New Ideas

The philosopher John Dewey arrived at the end of April 1919 and lectured across China for two years.
Bertrand Russell lectured in 1920–21; Margaret Sanger spoke in Beijing in 1922.
Dewey: no country had students "so unanimously and eagerly interested in what is modern and new."
Hu Shi, Dewey's former student, often translated his lectures.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Chow, The May Fourth Movement (1960), p. 183; Hu Danian, China and Albert Einstein (2005). Image: Shanghai, 1919.

The movement invited the world in. "Among the prominent intellectuals from the West invited to visit China to lecture were Bertrand Russell (in 1920 and 1921), Albert Einstein (in 1922), and Margaret Sanger (in 1922)." Einstein was invited, but his Beijing lectures never took place: after a misunderstanding, he wrote to Cai Yuanpei in December 1922 that he could not come, as Hu Danian has shown. He stopped only briefly in Shanghai, so the slide leaves him out. The longest visit was John Dewey's, from 1919 to 1921 (Ebrey, p. 426).

Dewey landed in Shanghai at the end of April 1919, days before the May Fourth protests. The photograph shows him there with his wife and his Chinese hosts. Standing at the left is Hu Shi, who had studied with him at Columbia.

Here is what Dewey saw: "There seems to be no country in the world where students are so unanimously and eagerly interested in what is modern and new in thought, especially about social and economic matters, nor where the arguments which can be brought in favor of the established order and the status quo have so little weight." This is an outsider's view, and a friendly one; Dewey met mostly the students who came to hear him.

Why this matters: The New Culture leaders looked to Western thinkers for the "new culture" they wanted.

Q6 story · The pushback · 1920

Not Everyone Agreed: Liang Qichao Now Feared China Would Lose Its "National Essence"

Liang Qichao, the great reformer of 1898, toured Europe in 1918–1920, just after the war.
He came home saying the West's faith in science had led to ruin.
Ebrey: some now found Western culture "too materialistic."
They feared China would lose its "national essence" · Ch. 24 L1 · Liang in 1903 ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Joseph R. Levenson, Liang Ch'i-ch'ao and the Mind of Modern China (1953). Image: about 1917.

Not everyone wanted to throw the past away. "Not all intellectuals saw salvation in modern Western culture. Some who for a while had been attracted to things Western came to feel that Western culture was too materialistic. Fear that China was in danger of losing its 'national essence' was raised" (Ebrey, p. 426).

The most famous convert was Liang Qichao. You met him in the first lecture as the young reformer who studied the West and, after his 1903 trip to America, wanted a strong state; the link takes you back. In 1918 he went to Europe just after the First World War and saw a continent in ruins. In his travel essays of 1920 he wrote that Europeans had dreamed science would bring happiness, and the dream had failed.

Ebrey sums up his new view: "Liang Qichao, by now a conservative, saw more to admire in China's humanistic culture than in the West's rationalism and hedonism and worried about the threat to China's national character."

Source beyond Ebrey: Joseph R. Levenson, Liang Ch'i-ch'ao and the Mind of Modern China (1953).

Why this matters: The New Culture Movement had critics, and the debate over what to keep from the past never ended.

Check yourself: Why might the First World War have made some Chinese doubt Western culture?

Q7 story · Shaoxing and Tokyo · 1881–1904

Lu Xun Went to Japan to Study Medicine After Old-Style Doctors Failed to Save His Father

Born Zhou Shuren in 1881 in Shaoxing, he watched his father sicken and die in 1896.
For years he carried clothes to the pawnshop to pay for strange cures.
In 1902 he went to Tokyo on a government scholarship · Ch. 24 L1 · Tokyo students ↗
In 1904 he entered the medical school at Sendai, the only Chinese student there.

Names in This Story

Lu Xun pen name of Zhou Shuren, writer, 1881–1936; pronounced "loo shyun"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922); Jon Eugene von Kowallis, "Lu Xun," in Dictionary of Literary Biography (2007). Image: Tokyo, 1903.

The third leader of this lecture is a writer. "One of the first to write well in the vernacular was Lu Xun (1881–1936). In 1902, Lu had gone to Japan to study medicine after traditional doctors had failed to cure his father of tuberculosis" (Ebrey, p. 422). Lu Xun was a pen name; he was born Zhou Shuren, in Shaoxing, south of Hangzhou.

He told the story himself in 1922. For more than four years, as a boy, he went almost daily to a pawnshop and then to a medicine shop, buying the rare ingredients a famous doctor prescribed, such as "sugar-cane that had been three years exposed to frost." His father died anyway. Later, in a Western-style school in Nanjing, he decided those doctors had been "either unwitting or deliberate charlatans."

In Tokyo he joined thousands of Chinese students, as you saw in the first lecture; the link takes you back. This photograph, taken in 1903, shows him after he cut off his queue, a small act of rebellion against the Qing. In 1904 he moved north to the medical college at Sendai.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922); Jon Eugene von Kowallis, "Lu Xun," in Dictionary of Literary Biography (2007).

Why this matters: Lu Xun began as one of the many young Chinese who hoped Western science would save China.

Q7 story · Sendai, Japan · about 1906

In Class, Lu Xun Later Wrote, a Lantern Slide Showed Chinese Calmly Watching a Chinese Man About to Be Executed

After class, his teacher sometimes showed lantern slides of the Russo-Japanese War.
One slide showed a bound Chinese man, said to be a Russian spy, about to be executed by Japanese soldiers.
Other Chinese stood around him, "strong fellows" who looked "completely apathetic."
In 1906 he left medicine: "The most important thing … was to change their spirit."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922), trans. Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang. Image: AI-generated illustration (2026).

This is the most famous turning point in modern Chinese literature, and we know it only from Lu Xun himself. Ebrey says he gave up medicine "after watching a newsreel of the Russo-Japanese War that showed a group of Chinese watching apathetically as Japanese in Manchuria executed a Chinese accused of spying for the Russians" (Ebrey, p. 422).

In his 1922 preface he says it was a lantern slide, a glass slide projected on a screen, shown in a microbiology class at Sendai to fill the time. The slide showed a Chinese man with his hands bound; the commentary said he was a Russian spy about to be beheaded, and the Chinese around him "had come to enjoy the spectacle." His Japanese classmates clapped. The slide itself has never been found, and historians treat the story as his own later account of a real turning point.

His conclusion: "The people of a weak and backward country, however strong and healthy they may be, can only serve to be made examples of, or to witness such futile spectacles." So "the most important thing … was to change their spirit," and literature was the means. The picture is an AI-generated illustration made for this deck, an interpretation of his account and not a record of the scene.

Why this matters: Lu Xun decided that China's sickness was in its spirit, not its bodies.

Check yourself: In Lu Xun's account, what upset him most: the execution or the watchers?

Q7 story · Beijing · April–May 1918

In May 1918 New Youth Printed Lu Xun's First Story, "Diary of a Madman": Your Primary-Source Reading

For years in Beijing, Zhou Shuren had been a quiet ministry official copying old inscriptions.
A New Youth editor, Qian Xuantong, kept asking him to write.
He wrote the story in April 1918; it was the first time he signed himself "Lu Xun."
It is this chapter's primary-source reading · read it online ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922). Image: Xin qingnian, vol. 4, no. 5, 1918.

This slide sets up your primary-source reading; it does not tell you what happens. "The May 1918 issue of New Youth contained his first vernacular short story, 'Diary of a Madman'" (Ebrey, p. 422). Read the story itself before you read Ebrey's one-sentence summary.

Here is the context. After Japan, Zhou Shuren returned to China, taught, and in 1912 joined the Ministry of Education in Beijing. He spent his evenings copying ancient inscriptions. In his 1922 preface he says a friend from New Youth, the linguist Qian Xuantong, came by and asked why he did not write something. Lu Xun answered with an image: an iron house without windows, with many people asleep inside who will soon suffocate. Should you wake a few, if they cannot escape? His friend replied that if a few awake, "you can't say there is no hope of destroying the iron house."

He agreed, and wrote this story in April 1918. It was the first time he used the name Lu Xun. The title echoes Nikolai Gogol's Russian story of 1835.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922).

Why this matters: This is where modern Chinese fiction is usually said to begin.

Check yourself: In the iron-house image, who are the sleepers, and who is the person who shouts?

Q7 story · Beijing · 1921–1922

In "The True Story of Ah Q," a Village Nobody Called Every Humiliation a Victory

The story ran in a Beijing newspaper supplement from December 1921 to February 1922.
Ah Q is a poor laborer who is beaten, mocked, and cheated.
Each time, he tells himself he has really won: his "spiritual victory."
Within a few years it was translated into Russian, French, English, and Esperanto.

Names in This Story

Ah Q hero of Lu Xun's 1921–22 story; pronounced "ah kyoo"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 422; William A. Lyell, Lu Xun's Vision of Reality (1976). Image: Beijing, 1925.

Lu Xun's longest story made a character into a national byword. "The protagonist of his longest story, 'The True Story of Ah Q,' is a man of low social standing. Always on the lookout for a way to get ahead, he is too cowardly and self-deceiving ever to succeed. No matter how he is humiliated, he claims moral superiority" (Ebrey, p. 422).

Readers called Ah Q's habit his "method of spiritual victory." When the village bullies beat him, he tells himself he has been beaten by his sons, so he is the winner. Many readers saw themselves, and China, in him: a weak country that answered defeat with boasts about its ancient greatness.

The ending takes Ah Q into 1911. Ebrey explains that he hears of the revolution, but the old elite and the new foreign-educated elite "are collaborating to take over the revolution for themselves," and in the end he is executed for a robbery he did not commit. Lu Xun's verdict on 1911 is hidden in that plot: the revolution changed the flags, not the people.

The photograph was taken in 1925 for the Russian translation, a sign of how fast Ah Q became famous abroad.

Source beyond Ebrey: William A. Lyell, Lu Xun's Vision of Reality (1976).

Why this matters: Ah Q gave Chinese readers a name for a habit of self-deception they feared was national.

Check yourself: What is Ah Q's "spiritual victory," and why would Lu Xun see it as dangerous?

Q7 concept · National character

Concept: "National Character" Was the Idea That a People's Habits Explain Its Fate

Concept

National character

The belief, common around 1900, that each nation has a shared set of habits and attitudes, and that these, more than laws or leaders, decide whether it rises or falls.

Western writers like Arthur Smith listed Chinese "characteristics" such as "indifference to accuracy."
Liang Qichao and others turned the idea around: change the people, and you save the nation.
Lu Xun used fiction to show the habits he blamed: passivity, self-deception, cruelty to the weak.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 422–423; Lydia H. Liu, Translingual Practice (1995). Image: Arthur H. Smith, 1894.

Reading Guide Q7 (p. 422) asks about Lu Xun's diagnosis of China's "national character." That phrase needs explaining. National character was the idea that every people shares certain habits and that those habits, more than laws or rulers, explain its fate.

The idea came partly from Western writers. The American missionary Arthur Smith's Chinese Characteristics (1894) had chapters on traits such as "the disregard of time," "indifference to accuracy," and "the absence of sympathy." It was translated into Japanese in 1896, and Lu Xun read it as a student. Historians such as Lydia Liu point out that Chinese reformers took a foreign stereotype and turned it into a program for self-improvement.

Ebrey's verdict on Lu Xun follows from this: "Lu Xun put the blame for China's plight on China's own flaws much more than on the flaws of foreigners" (Ebrey, p. 422). His stories put those flaws on stage, in the crowd at the execution, in Ah Q, and in the diarist's neighbors.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lydia H. Liu, Translingual Practice (1995).

Why this matters: Lu Xun's diagnosis drew on an idea that was partly a foreign stereotype, which is worth questioning.

Check yourself: What are the risks of explaining a country's problems by its "national character"?

Q7 story · Beijing · February 1925

In 1925 Lu Xun Still Blamed China's Own Flaws: The Republic Was "Still the China of Those Earlier Ages"

In February 1925 Lu Xun compared the republic with the last, cruel years of the Ming.
"It seems as if China alone is untouched by the passage of time."
He blamed "clever people" who always adapt and "always come out on top."
Yet he hoped: no one could say "the national character will never change."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 422–423 (Documents: Lu Xun's "Sudden Notions," trans. Yang Xianyi and Gladys Yang). Image: Beixin shuju, 1926.

Ebrey prints part of a 1925 essay, "Sudden Notions," in the Documents box on page 423. Read it there. It shows the same diagnosis as the stories, now in Lu Xun's own voice.

He compares his own time with the end of the Ming dynasty and finds them "amazingly" alike: "It seems as if China alone is untouched by the passage of time. The Chinese Republic today is still the China of those earlier ages" (Ebrey, p. 423). Then he asks the question at the heart of Reading Guide Q7: "Can it be that 'national character' is so difficult to change?"

His target is a type of person: "clever people, who know that even if China collapses they will not suffer, for they can always adapt themselves to circumstances." His example is Chinese writers under the Qing who praised the Manchu army as "our army." And yet he leaves a door open: "Luckily no one can say for certain that the national character will never change."

The cover shows the 1926 book of essays where "Sudden Notions" was collected.

Why this matters: Ten years after Youth Magazine, Lu Xun still thought the deepest problem was inside China.

Around 1900 Bound Feet Still Marked a Respectable Han Chinese Woman

For about a thousand years, mothers had bound their daughters' feet from the age of five or six.
Small feet were a sign of refinement, hard work at home, and a good marriage.

The last part of this lecture asks what the new ideas meant for women. Start with where they stood around 1900. You met foot binding in Chapter 8, and the links take you back to how the Qing tried to end it and how reformers attacked it.

Ebrey puts the change in one sentence: "In a short period of time, women's seclusion and tiny feet went from being a source of pride in Chinese refinement to a source of embarrassment at China's backwardness" (Ebrey, p. 426). Notice the word pride. Dorothy Ko and other historians remind us that, for many women, bound feet were a mark of skill, discipline, and family respectability, not only of suffering.

The woman in this photograph lived in Beijing in the first decade of the century. She dressed carefully for the camera, and her small feet were part of how she wished to be seen. Within her lifetime, the same feet would be called backward.

Q10 story · The reform societies · 1890s–1930

From the 1890s, Men Pledged to Leave Their Daughters' Feet Unbound and Marry Their Sons to Natural-Footed Women

The first anti-foot-binding societies of the 1890s were made up of men.
Members promised to leave daughters' feet natural and find sons natural-footed wives.
Campaigners said bound feet crippled a large part of China and held it back.
After 1930, young girls had their feet bound only in remote areas.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005). Image: C. H. Graves, about 1902.

Notice who started the campaign. "The earliest anti-foot binding societies, founded in the 1890s, were composed of men who would agree both to leave their daughters' feet natural and to marry their sons to women with natural feet" (Ebrey, p. 426). The second promise mattered most, because mothers bound feet so their daughters could marry well. A family that stopped alone took a risk; a pledge among many families removed it.

The argument was about the nation. "Anti-foot binding campaigners depicted the custom as standing in the way of modernization by crippling a large part of the Chinese population." Photographs like this one, set side by side for comparison, were made to persuade.

The change was fast in the cities and slow elsewhere. "After 1930 it was only in remote areas that young girls still had their feet bound." Older women kept their bound feet for life, since it was "difficult and painful to reverse the process once a girl had reached age ten or twelve."

Source beyond Ebrey: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005).

Why this matters: The campaign shows reform working through family pledges, not just laws.

Check yourself: Why did the pledge include a promise about sons' marriages?

Q10 story · Beijing, Tokyo, Shanghai · 1900–1907

Qiu Jin Left Her Husband, Studied in Japan, and Founded the Chinese Women's Journal

After the Boxer War and the foreign occupation of Beijing, she became an ardent nationalist.
In 1904 she left her marriage and went to study in Tokyo, where she learned to make bombs.
She attacked foot binding, arranged marriages, wife beating, and the cult of widow chastity.
Back in Shanghai, she founded the Chinese Women's Journal in early 1907.

Names in This Story

Qiu Jin revolutionary and feminist, 1875–1907; pronounced "chyou jin"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Mary Backus Rankin, "The Emergence of Women at the End of the Ch'ing: The Case of Ch'iu Chin," in Women in Chinese Society (1975). Images: Japan, before 1907; Shanghai, 1907.

Women did not wait for men to free them. "As women gained access to modern education, first in missionary schools but then also in the new government schools and abroad, they began to participate in politics. Some revolutionaries appeared, most famously Qiu Jin" (Ebrey, p. 426).

She came from an official family in Zhejiang and was married at about twenty. Ebrey says she became an ardent nationalist "after witnessing the Boxer Rebellion and the imperialist occupation of Beijing," where her husband later bought an official post. "Unhappy in her marriage, in 1904 she left her husband and went to Japan, enrolling in a girls' vocational school." She spent most of her time on revolutionary politics, "even learning to make bombs."

She also turned her fire on her own society. In speeches and essays she "castigated female infanticide, foot binding, arranged marriages, wife beating, and the cult of widow chastity." She also told women they were partly to blame, because "they were willing to make pleasing men their goal." In 1906 she returned to found the journal shown here. Historian Mary Rankin calls her an early example of women entering public life.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mary Backus Rankin, "The Emergence of Women at the End of the Ch'ing: The Case of Ch'iu Chin," in Women in Chinese Society (1975).

Why this matters: Qiu Jin joined nationalism and feminism in one life.

Check yourself: Why did Qiu Jin blame women, as well as men, for women's oppression?

Q10 story · Shaoxing · July 1907

In July 1907 the Qing Executed Qiu Jin for Her Part in a Failed Uprising, and She Became a Martyr

She headed the Datong school in Shaoxing, which secretly trained revolutionaries.
On 6 July 1907 her fellow plotter Xu Xilin shot the governor of Anhui; his rising failed.
Troops surrounded her school a week later; she was arrested and questioned.
On 15 July she was executed in Shaoxing, aged about 31.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Mary Backus Rankin, Early Chinese Revolutionaries (1971). Image: 1907.

Ebrey's account is one sentence: "In 1907 she died a martyr, executed for her role in an abortive uprising" (Ebrey, p. 426). Here are the facts behind it.

In 1907 Qiu Jin ran the Datong school in Shaoxing, her home city, where students drilled as soldiers. She and a fellow Shaoxing revolutionary, Xu Xilin, planned risings in two provinces. Xu struck first, on 6 July, when he shot the Manchu governor of Anhui at a police-school ceremony in Anqing. The rising failed and Xu was put to death. A week later, government troops surrounded the Datong school and arrested her.

The document shown is the official record of her questioning. She is said to have written only one line of verse: "Autumn wind, autumn rain, fill my heart with sorrow." She was executed in a Shaoxing street at dawn on 15 July. Within months, writers and women's groups were calling her a martyr.

Lu Xun was from Shaoxing too, and he did not forget. Xu Xilin's name appears in "Diary of a Madman." That was the real name the reading slide asked you to watch for.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mary Backus Rankin, Early Chinese Revolutionaries (1971).

Why this matters: Her death made a woman a national hero of the revolution.

Q10 story · The schools · 1907–1923

After the Qing Ordered Girls' Schools in 1907, Enrollment Rose About Twentyfold by 1919

1907: the Qing mandated schools for girls; 11,936 girls were in 391 primary schools.
1919: 215,626 primary pupils were girls, still far fewer than boys.
1922–23: 3,249 girls in middle schools, against 100,136 boys.
Schools let girls meet unrelated people respectably and led some to jobs.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; Joan Judge, The Precious Raft of History: The Past, the West, and the Woman Question in China (2008). Image: Tianjin, 1912.

The quietest change may have been the biggest. "In 1907 the Qing government mandated the opening of schools for girls. That year 11,936 girls were enrolled in 391 girls' primary schools. By 1919 about twenty times as many primary school students were girls: 215,626 in total" (Ebrey, p. 426).

Read the next figure carefully: in middle school there were about thirty boys for every girl. "Female students were still greatly outnumbered by male ones." Most girls who went to school left after a few years.

Even so, Ebrey argues, school gave girls "much more than literacy: they offered a respectable way for girls to interact with unrelated people." Before this, a respectable young woman rarely spoke to anyone outside her family. "After 1920, opportunities for higher education also rapidly expanded, leading to a growing number of women working as teachers, nurses, and civil servants in the larger cities." Normal schools like the one in this photograph trained the women who would teach the next generation of girls.

Source beyond Ebrey: Joan Judge, The Precious Raft of History: The Past, the West, and the Woman Question in China (2008).

Why this matters: Schools gave women a place in public life before the law gave them rights.

Q10 story · Lu Xun on women · 1918–1924

Lu Xun Attacked the Chastity Code, and in "The New Year's Sacrifice" a Poor Widow Ends Up Begging

In 1918 he asked why no one wrote the life of a woman who killed herself after being raped.
Society honored only the woman who died to avoid it: one more sign of a cruel code.
In the 1924 story, a widow forced to remarry is called unlucky when her second husband dies.
She fears being split in two after death to serve two husbands.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 427; Lu Xun, "My Views on Chastity," New Youth (1918). Image: Beixin shuju, 1926.

Lu Xun turned the same diagnosis on the treatment of women. "Young women in middle and high schools read New Youth and other periodicals just as avidly as their brothers" (Ebrey, p. 427), and he wrote for them.

His 1918 essay on chastity made a pointed comparison. A woman who killed herself to avoid being raped "won great glory, but no man of letters would write a biography of a woman who committed suicide after being forcibly raped." The code, he argued, honored women only for dying.

Ebrey also summarizes his story "The New Year's Sacrifice," written in 1924: "a poor widow who was forced by her parents-in-law to remarry was viewed by herself and others as ill omened after her second husband also died. She ended up surviving by begging, worried that she would have to be split in two after death to serve her two husbands." The widow is a character, not a real person, and the people who shun her are ordinary townsfolk. That is his point: the cruelty was in customs everyone shared.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lu Xun, "My Views on Chastity," New Youth (1918).

Why this matters: Lu Xun showed that women's suffering came from beliefs held by women and men alike.

Q10 story · Ibsen in China · 1918–1923

After New Youth Printed A Doll's House in 1918, Young Women Asked Whether They Could Leave Home Like Nora

In June 1918 a special New Youth issue printed Ibsen's 1879 play in Chinese.
At the end, the wife, Nora, walks out on her husband and children to find herself.
The play was soon staged in many cities, and "Nora" became a word for a rebel daughter.
In 1923 Lu Xun asked students: what happens to Nora after she leaves, without money?

Names in This Story

Nora heroine of Ibsen's A Doll's House (1879); in Chinese, Nala

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 427; Lu Xun, "What Happens After Nora Leaves Home?" lecture at Beijing Women's Normal College, 26 December 1923. Images: 1918; 1898.

Foreign writing mattered too. "Foreign literature also had an impact, especially Ibsen's A Doll's House, which first appeared in translation in New Youth and soon was performed on stage many places. Women debated whether, like Ibsen's Nora, they could leave the old system and create their own identities" (Ebrey, p. 427).

The Norwegian play of 1879 ends with Nora leaving her husband, who has treated her like a doll. Hu Shi helped translate it for the June 1918 Ibsen issue shown here and praised Ibsen for teaching individuals to be themselves. For Chinese students, the play was about arranged marriage and the authority of parents.

Lu Xun was more doubtful. On 26 December 1923 he lectured at the Beijing Women's Normal College on "What Happens After Nora Leaves Home?" A woman without money of her own, he argued, had few choices once she walked out. What women needed first was economic rights. That warning matched what many young women found when they tried.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lu Xun, "What Happens After Nora Leaves Home?" lecture at Beijing Women's Normal College, 26 December 1923.

Why this matters: Nora gave Chinese women a model of leaving, and Lu Xun asked the hard question about what came next.

Check yourself: Why did Lu Xun think economic rights mattered more than leaving home?

Q10 story · Law and the parties · 1912–1927

In the 1920s Both Parties Promised Equal Rights and Free Divorce, but Divorce Proved "the Trickiest Issue"

In 1912 women led by Tang Qunying demanded the vote from the provisional senate and lost.
In the 1920s the Nationalists and Communists set up women's departments.
Both called for equal rights and freedom of marriage and divorce.
When wives sought divorce, peasant husbands, who had paid for them, objected.

Names in This Story

Song Qingling widow of Sun Yatsen, 1893–1981; pronounced "sung ching-ling"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 427–428; Christina K. Gilmartin, Engendering the Chinese Revolution (1995). Image: Wuhan, 1927.

Ideas were one thing; law was another. "Efforts to get the vote were generally unsuccessful" (Ebrey, p. 427). In 1912 a group of women led by Tang Qunying, a revolutionary who had studied in Japan, pressed the provisional senate in Nanjing for the vote; it was refused.

"However, in the 1920s, both the Nationalist and Communist parties organized women's departments and adopted resolutions calling for equal rights for women and freedom of marriage and divorce." Christina Gilmartin has shown how women activists inside both parties pushed for these promises.

Putting them into practice was harder. Song Qingling, who worked for women's rights in the revolutionary government at Wuhan, explained the problem in 1927: "If we do not grant the appeals of the women, they lose faith in the union and in the women's freedom we are teaching. But if we grant the divorces, then we have trouble with the peasant's union, since it is very hard for a peasant to get a wife, and he has often paid much for his present unwilling one" (Ebrey, pp. 427–428).

Source beyond Ebrey: Christina K. Gilmartin, Engendering the Chinese Revolution (1995).

Why this matters: Women's rights clashed with the interests of poor men the revolutionaries also needed.

Check yourself: Explain Song Qingling's dilemma in your own words.

In the Villages, Change Came Slowly: in the 1930s, Fewer Than 2 Percent of Rural Women Could Read

The new schools, jobs, and magazines were mostly in the cities.
A large survey of farm households in the 1930s measured who could read.
About 30 percent of the men could, and fewer than 2 percent of the women.
Most Chinese women still lived in a world their grandmothers would have known.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 426; John Lossing Buck, Land Utilization in China (1937).

Keep the scale in mind. Everything so far, the magazines, the schools, Nora, the party women's departments, happened mostly in the large cities. "In the countryside, change came much more slowly. A large-scale survey of rural households in the 1930s found that fewer than 2 percent of the women were literate, compared to 30 percent of the men" (Ebrey, p. 426).

That survey was led by the American agricultural economist John Lossing Buck, who with his Chinese students studied thousands of farm families across China between 1929 and 1933. It was the largest such survey of its time.

The great majority of Chinese lived in the countryside. So when you weigh how far the women's movement had come by 1927, put these two numbers beside the school figures and the stories of Qiu Jin and Song Qingling. The change was real, but it reached a small number of urban women first.

Source beyond Ebrey: John Lossing Buck, Land Utilization in China (1937).

Why this matters: A history told from the cities can make change look faster than it was.

Pierian Spring · Story · Vassar, Chicago, Beijing · 1914–1920

Sophia Chen Refused an Arranged Marriage, Studied at Vassar, and Became Beijing University's First Woman Professor

In her teens she talked her father out of the marriage he had arranged for her.
In 1914, aged 24, she won a Boxer Indemnity scholarship to study in the United States.
At Vassar she wrote "One Day" (1917), one of the first stories in vernacular Chinese.
After a Chicago master's degree, she was offered a Beijing University professorship in 1920.

Names in This Story

Sophia Chen Chen Hengzhe, historian and writer, 1890–1976; pronounced "chuhn heng-juh"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 424 (Biography: Sophia Chen and H. C. Zen); Denise Gimpel, Chen Hengzhe: A Life Between Orthodoxies (2015). Images: 1919; 1920.

Chen Hengzhe, who took the English name Sophia Chen, came from an official family in Jiangsu. She "had experienced more difficulty getting satisfactory schooling in China, either being tutored at home or studying in mediocre schools. In her teens, she convinced her father to withdraw from a marriage arrangement he had made for her so that she could continue her studies" (Ebrey, p. 424).

"In 1914 at age twenty-four, she was selected to study in the United States in the examinations held for Boxer Indemnity Fund scholarships." Those scholarships were paid for by money the United States returned from the Boxer indemnity. "She studied history at Vassar, then went on for a master's degree at the University of Chicago in 1920."

While at Vassar she published a short story in the vernacular, "One Day," in a Chinese students' magazine in 1917, a year before "Diary of a Madman." Literary historians count it among the first modern vernacular stories. "That year she returned to China and became the first woman to be offered a professorship at Peking University," and married H. C. Zen. She later wrote a widely used History of the West.

Source beyond Ebrey: Denise Gimpel, Chen Hengzhe: A Life Between Orthodoxies (2015).

Why this matters: Her life shows the New Culture ideals of free marriage and women's education being lived.

Pierian Spring · Story · Tokyo, Cornell, Shanghai · 1905–1937

H. C. Zen Joined Sun Yatsen's Alliance, Studied Chemistry at Cornell, and Led the Science Society of China

He passed the first stage of the civil service exams just before they were abolished in 1905.
In Tokyo he joined Sun Yatsen's Revolutionary Alliance; in 1912 he was secretary to the president.
Opposing Yuan Shikai, he left to study chemistry at Cornell and Columbia, 1912–1917.
He was president of the Science Society of China, 1914–1923.

Names in This Story

H. C. Zen Ren Hongjun, chemist and science organizer, 1886–1961; pronounced "run hung-jyun"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, p. 424 (Biography: Sophia Chen and H. C. Zen); Zuoyue Wang, "Saving China through Science: The Science Society of China," Osiris 17 (2002); Peter Buck, "Order and Control," Social Studies of Science 5 (1975). Images: undated; 1915.

H. C. Zen was the English name of Ren Hongjun, from an educated family in Sichuan. His life crosses every turning point you have met in this chapter. In 1904 he "graduated from a modern middle school, then took the first stage of civil service examinations. After the exam system was abolished the next year, he left China to study at a technical college in Tokyo, where he joined Sun Yatsen's Revolutionary Alliance" (Ebrey, p. 424).

In 1911 he went home and "at age twenty-five was made secretary to the president." Disagreeing with Yuan Shikai, he resigned and studied chemistry in New York State. There he befriended Hu Shi and courted Sophia Chen.

With other Chinese students at Cornell in 1914 he founded the Science Society (Ebrey says "while still in China," but historians such as Peter Buck and Zuoyue Wang place its founding at Cornell), which "sponsored scientific monographs and translations, lectures, and exhibitions." Its monthly Kexue (Science), first published in 1915, was printed in horizontal lines with Western punctuation. Back in China from 1917, Zen worked at Beijing University, the Commercial Press, and later headed Sichuan University and an Academia Sinica institute.

Why this matters: Zen built "Mr. Science" into real institutions.

Background essay: the historians’ debates behind this lecture ↗

Everything in the story slides comes from Ebrey, Chapter 24 (pp. 422–428, with the boxes on pp. 423 and 424), and each slide's source line gives the page. Where a slide or note adds something from sources beyond Ebrey, the source line or the notes name it, and the books above are where those facts come from.

Three points differ from Ebrey. The magazine began in 1915 as Youth Magazine and took the name New Youth in 1916. The Ministry of Education ordered vernacular textbooks for the first two grades in January 1920, not 1921. And by Lu Xun's own account, what he saw at Sendai was a lantern slide, not a newsreel.

Every picture in this deck is a real historical image except one, the Sendai lecture hall, which is an AI-generated illustration and labeled as such. Four magazine covers come from a 1954 reprint, so they show the original design but not the original paper. The portraits show people as they wished to be seen, and the bound-foot comparison was made to persuade.

Why this matters: Knowing where a fact or a picture comes from is how historians decide how much to trust it.

An Lushan’s Rebellion Changed Tang Government and Society

The rebellion shifted power to regional armies and reshaped taxation, trade, and political life.

The military background

Frontier defense shifted from rotating farmer-soldiers to professional armies.

Professional troops
Recruited long-service troops, 737

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84–85.

The earlier fubing system relied on farmer-soldiers who served in rotation and whose social position remained tied to household registration and land. By Xuanzong’s time, the frontier had moved far from the militia prefectures. The Tibetan Empire and the Khitans posed persistent threats, and the decay of the equal-field system left many militia households unable to supply their own equipment.

The court adopted recruited long-service troops in 737 and abolished the militia muster in 749. These troops were costly, but they could remain on the frontier and respond to threats. Military governors received broad authority to handle crises without waiting for central approval. The system improved immediate defense but concentrated armed power in provincial commands, a vulnerability that became clear during the rebellion.

The military trade-off

The Tang replaced an inadequate army with one that created a new danger.

QuestionFubing militiaProfessional frontier army
Cost to the stateLowHigh
Military effectivenessInadequateExcellent
Where loyalty ranHousehold and landThe commander
Between campaignsDissolvedPersisted
An inadequate army traded for a dangerous one. The court had no third option.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84–85. Dates and institutional context added for historical background.

The contrast here is not between a wise old system and a foolish new one. Under the fubing militia, households furnished soldiers and equipment, so the system cost the state less. Soldiers returned home between campaigns and the force dissolved. The fubing militia had become inadequate for the empire’s frontier needs.

A professional force cost more because the court funded long-service troops and supplies, but it produced experienced forces that remained ready year-round. The army persisted under regional command and created permanent armed communities whose livelihoods depended on commanders.

This structural trade-off frames the rest of the lecture. Individual court decisions could be reasonable while the aggregate result undermined central control.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84–85.

Ask students to treat this as a real policy problem. The question is not whether the professional army proved dangerous. It did. The question is whether the court had a workable alternative once the old militia system no longer met the needs of a distant frontier.

Frontier commands

Tang military governors commanded troops across a wide frontier zone.

  • First jiedushi appointments c. 710–711; ~10 commands by 742
  • Shi = commissioner, envoy — authority lent, expected back
  • The court appointed commissioners; what it got were governors
  • Each grant answered a real problem: act, tax, appoint

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84. Dates and office terminology added for historical background.

Xuanzong established a chain of military provinces along the frontiers, from Manchuria toward Sichuan, to address threats from groups including the Turks, Uighurs, and Tibetans. The first jiedushi appointments came around 710–711, and there were roughly ten commands by 742.

The word shi means commissioner or envoy. The office began as an extraordinary commission from the throne, not as a territorial governorship. No decree converted commissioners into governors. The change accumulated as each practical grant of authority—acting without waiting, collecting revenue, and appointing subordinates—answered a real frontier problem.

Read the map from west to east: Anxi and Beiting guarded the far western approaches; Hexi and Longyou secured the Gansu corridor; Shuofang and Hedong faced the steppe; Fanyang and Pinglu watched the Khitan frontier. Jiannan and Lingnan anchored the south. The crimson northeastern cluster marks the commands that later gave An Lushan his exceptional military base. The boundaries are approximate; the point is the scale of delegated command and the strategic reach of the system.

Movement II

The Rise of An Lushan

How does one frontier general come to hold the empire's best armies?

⏱ ~12:30 — Section II runs to about the 17-minute mark.

Transition line: "Every disaster has a biography at the center of it. Here's his."

Who Was An Lushan?

A Man of the Frontier

  • Half Sogdian, half Turk — a true child of the borderlands
  • A professional soldier from a family of soldiers
  • Made his name fighting the Khitans of Manchuria

A Man of Power

  • Commands the northern Hebei frontier army from 744
  • By the 750s controls the empire's northeastern commands
  • At his back: 100,000+ veteran troops

He is exactly the kind of man the new system was built to promote.

⏱ ~13:00 (~2.5 min).

An Lushan is the frontier system made flesh. His father was Sogdian — that's the Central Asian merchant people whose caravans ran the Silk Road; their cities, Samarkand and Bukhara, sat in what's now Uzbekistan. His mother was a Turk. He grows up in the polyglot world of the northeastern garrisons, reportedly working as a young man as a market broker precisely because he could move among half a dozen frontier languages. He is, in other words, exactly the cosmopolitan Tang type we celebrated two slides ago — just the military version of it.

And he's good. A professional soldier from a family of soldiers, Ebrey says, who makes his reputation in the hard, unglamorous wars against the Khitans of Manchuria. From 744 he commands the frontier army in northern Hebei — the northeast corner of that map — and through the next decade he accumulates more. By the early 750s he holds the northeastern commands together; the standard accounts beyond Ebrey count three jiedushi posts in one man's hands — Pinglu, Fanyang, and Hedong, if you want the names. Call it the largest concentration of veteran soldiers in the empire: over a hundred thousand long-service professionals who have marched, fought, and been paid under one general for ten years.

Pause on the obvious question: why would any court allow this? Part of the answer is that he was useful — the Khitan frontier was genuinely dangerous. Part of it is that he was trusted — the charming, obese court favorite, Yang Guifei's adopted "son," the man who made the emperor laugh. And part of it is court politics: a non-Chinese professional with no aristocratic pedigree and no examination degree looked, to the chancellors, like a man who could never be a rival for their jobs. Every safeguard failed for a reason that made sense at the time.

The Collision at Court

  • 752: chancellor Li Linfu dies — the balance at court breaks
  • Power passes to Yang Guozhong, Yang Guifei's cousin
  • An Lushan and Yang Guozhong quarrel over control of the government
  • Each man convinces himself the other is plotting his destruction
  • December 755: An Lushan marches south from Fanyang — claiming secret orders to remove Yang Guozhong

Ebrey's phrase: the quarrel "led to open warfare."

⏱ ~15:30 (~2 min).

So what finally lights the fuse? Not ideology, not rebellion-from-below — a personnel dispute at the very top.

As long as Li Linfu lived, the pieces balanced: the old chancellor managed the frontier generals and the Yang family alike. He dies in 752, and into the vacuum steps Yang Guozhong — Yang Guifei's cousin, elevated the way the whole Yang clan has been elevated. Ebrey compresses what happens next into one devastating sentence: An Lushan "got into a quarrel with Yang's cousin over control of the government, which led to open warfare."

Unpack that. Yang Guozhong distrusts An Lushan — loudly, repeatedly, telling the emperor the general will rebel. An Lushan, in turn, watches a man he despises accumulate the power to destroy him. By 755 each is certain the other is plotting his ruin — and here's the tragic mechanics of it: once Yang Guozhong has spent years predicting rebellion, An Lushan knows that sooner or later the prediction becomes an arrest warrant. The accusation makes the crime rational.

In December 755, An Lushan moves. He marches his veterans south from Fanyang, near modern Beijing, under a classic fig leaf: he claims he carries secret orders from the emperor to march on the capital and remove the wicked minister Yang Guozhong. Nobody needs to believe it. It just needs to let his soldiers tell themselves they're loyal. Movement three — the storm itself.

Movement III

The Rebellion, 755–763

Eight years that hollowed out an empire

⏱ ~17:30 — Section III runs to about the 27-minute mark. This is the narrative heart; keep the pace up.

Transition line: "From here the story moves fast — faster than anyone at court could believe."

The Storm Breaks, 755–756

  • An's veterans strike south toward Luoyang — the eastern capital falls within weeks
  • Early 756: An Lushan proclaims his own dynasty — the Great Yan
  • The court's hastily recruited troops are no match for veterans
  • Loyal armies hold the Tong Pass, shielding Chang'an —
  • — until the court orders them out to attack: the army is destroyed (summer 756)
  • The road to Chang'an lies open

⏱ ~18:00 (~2.5 min).

The campaign opens with terrifying speed. An's column drives south through Hebei toward Luoyang, the eastern capital, and the government's response exposes everything we said about the hollow center: the court starts raising an army — recruiting, in the moment, against a hundred thousand veterans. Ebrey is blunt: the newly recruited troops were no match. Luoyang falls in a matter of weeks — about a month after the first march. At the lunar new year, early 756, An Lushan drops the fig leaf about rescuing the emperor and proclaims himself emperor of a new dynasty, the Great Yan.

Now, between Luoyang and Chang'an the land itself offers one gift: the Tong Pass — Tongguan — where the road squeezes between mountains and the Yellow River. A defender who sits in that pass cannot be dislodged, and through the first half of 756 the Tang's remaining frontier veterans, under the old general Geshu Han, do exactly that. The rebellion stalls. Time, supply, and loyalist risings in Hebei are all starting to work against An Lushan.

And then the court throws it away. Yang Guozhong — who suspects the generals at the pass almost as much as he fears the rebels — convinces the emperor that the army is stalling treasonously, and the order goes out: leave the pass and attack. Geshu Han reportedly wept when he obeyed. In the summer of 756 the Tang field army marches out of its impregnable position and is annihilated. This detail comes from the fuller chronicles rather than our textbook, but it matters, because it shows the rebellion succeeding not on rebel genius but on court paranoia — the same paranoia that started the war. And now nothing stands between An Lushan and Chang'an.

Flight — and the Mawei Mutiny

  • Xuanzong abandons Chang'an, fleeing west toward Sichuan
  • At the Mawei post-station, his escort mutinies
  • The soldiers kill Yang Guozhong — then refuse to march until Yang Guifei dies too
  • The heir apparent breaks away northwest; proclaimed Emperor Suzong (756)
  • Xuanzong, informed afterward, does not contest it

The flight to Shu (Sichuan), Ming-dynasty handscroll after Qiu Ying. Freer Gallery of Art / Wikimedia Commons

⏱ ~20:30 (~3 min). This is the emotional center of the lecture — slow down and tell it.

What follows is the single most retold scene in Chinese history — painters and poets will return to it for a thousand years, and this Ming-dynasty scroll on the screen is one of those returns: the imperial party winding through the mountains toward Sichuan.

With the pass lost, Xuanzong slips out of Chang'an before dawn with Yang Guifei, Yang Guozhong, the heir apparent, and a column of imperial guards, heading west for the safety of Sichuan — the old land of Shu, walled off by mountains. The flight is chaos: rain, hunger, no provisions, the world's most powerful man begging food from villagers.

At a post-station called Mawei, the guards stop marching. They are hungry, humiliated, and they know exactly whom they blame for all of it. They fall on Yang Guozhong and kill him — the chancellor whose feud began the war, dead at the roadside. And then they surround the emperor's own lodging and deliver the demand Ebrey records: they would not continue until Yang Guifei and her relatives had been killed. Not exile. Killed.

Think about what's being demanded: the emperor's soldiers are requiring him to order the death of the woman at the center of his life. And he does it. Tradition says she was strangled with a silk cord at a Buddhist shrine by the road while the emperor sat apart. The column then marches on, and the old man who rode out of Chang'an as Son of Heaven arrives in Sichuan as something much smaller.

Meanwhile the heir apparent has split off and ridden northwest to the loyal frontier armies, and at Lingwu his officers convince him to take the throne himself as Emperor Suzong. Word reaches Xuanzong in Sichuan that he has, in effect, retired. Ebrey's dry phrase: he "did not contest" it. The golden-age emperor will live out his days a guarded, grieving ex-sovereign — he and his son both die in 762, within weeks of each other, before the war is even over.

Recovery — at a Price

  • Suzong's problem: loyal armies exist, but not enough of them
  • The solution: call in the Uighurs — Turkic allies from the steppe
  • 757: Tang and Uighur forces retake Chang'an and Luoyang
  • The price: the Uighurs loot the capitals and leave only when paid off in silk
  • Afterward: forced silk-for-horses trade at extortionate rates, year after year

The dynasty is saved by foreign cavalry it can never again refuse.

⏱ ~23:30 (~2 min).

How does a dynasty that just lost both capitals claw its way back? Ebrey frames the answer in one word: compromises. Every element of the recovery is bought, and the bills run for decades.

Suzong does have assets. The loyalist generals — Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi are the great names — hold the northwest and keep resistance alive in Hebei. But to retake walled capitals from veteran armies he needs cavalry he doesn't have, and he gets it from the Uighurs — the Turkic power that now dominates the steppe where the old Turk empire used to be. The Uighur khagan sends his horsemen, and in late 757 the combined armies take back Chang'an and then Luoyang.

And then comes the invoice. Ebrey: after the Uighurs took Chang'an from the rebels, "they looted it and would not leave until they were paid off with huge quantities of silk." The liberators sack the city they liberated — Luoyang gets it even worse. And the relationship doesn't end there: to keep the Uighurs from raiding thereafter, the Tang must buy their horses, every year, at prices the Uighurs set — Ebrey's word is extortionate. Silk for horses becomes a permanent tax paid to the steppe.

Mark the reversal for your notes: fifty years earlier, Tang armies dictated terms across Central Asia. Now the Tang pays protection money to stay on its own throne. The empire survives 757 — but it survives as a client of its own allies.

The Rebellion Devours Itself

  • 757: An Lushan — half-blind, violently paranoid — is murdered by his own son, An Qingxu
  • 759: An's general Shi Siming kills An Qingxu and takes over the rebellion
  • 761: Shi Siming is murdered by his own son, Shi Chaoyi
  • 763: Shi Chaoyi, defeated and abandoned, takes his own life — the war ends
  • Four rebel emperors in eight years — each destroyed from within

This is why Chinese sources call it the An–Shi Rebellion.

⏱ ~25:30 (~2 min). Students love this slide — let the pattern land.

Here's the part the textbook compresses and the chronicles relish: the rebel side is eating itself the entire time. Watch the pattern.

An Lushan barely outlives his own triumph. By 757 he is going blind, tormented by ulcers, and so violently paranoid that his own household fears for their lives — and in January 757, barely a year after proclaiming his Great Yan dynasty, he is murdered in his palace in a plot around his own son, An Qingxu, who takes the rebel throne.

An Qingxu is weaker than his father, and in 759 the rebellion's best remaining general — Shi Siming, An Lushan's old comrade from the Hebei frontier — kills him and takes over, even recapturing Luoyang. Shi Siming is a genuinely capable commander; the Tang's recovery stalls against him. And then in 761 history repeats with mechanical cruelty: Shi Siming is murdered by his own son, Shi Chaoyi.

Shi Chaoyi inherits a movement that is now a feud wearing a crown. Tang and Uighur forces grind him back; his commanders begin cutting their own deals with the court; and in early 763, abandoned, he takes his own life. Eight years, four rebel emperors — father killed by son, usurper killed by comrade, comrade killed by son — every single transition a murder inside the family of the movement. That's why the Chinese tradition names it for both houses: the An–Shi Rebellion.

But notice what did not happen: the Tang never decisively crushed this rebellion on the battlefield. So how did it actually end? Next slide — and the answer explains the next hundred and fifty years of Chinese history.

How It Ended: Peace by Pardon

  • No final victory — the court buys the surrender of the remaining rebel commanders
  • Rebel generals are pardoned and appointed military governors of the very regions they held
  • In key provinces the governors now act like warlords:
  • — they pay no taxes to the center
  • — they appoint their own subordinates
  • — they pass their posts to their heirs

The war ends in 763 — but the northeast never truly returns.

⏱ ~27:00 (~2 min).

Write this down carefully, because it's the single most consequential fact of the whole war: the An Lushan Rebellion was not defeated. It was bought.

By 763 both sides are exhausted. The court has no appetite — and honestly no capacity — for reconquering Hebei fortress by fortress. So it makes the deal Ebrey describes: to get the remaining rebel leaders to submit, the Tang "offered pardons and even appointed many as military governors of the regions they held." Read that twice. The men who spent eight years in rebellion end the war holding official Tang titles over the exact territories they rebelled with.

And in those key regions — above all the old rebel heartland of Hebei, the provinces the sources call the three garrisons — the governorship becomes a hereditary warlordship in all but name. Ebrey lists the three features and I want all three in your notes: they pay no taxes to the central government; they appoint their own subordinate officials; and they pass their positions down to their heirs. Each of those is a defining power of a sovereign state. The Tang map still colors Hebei as Tang territory, but the court's writ stops at the border of those commands — for the next century and a half.

One more Ebrey observation while we're here: across the empire, posts that had been held by civil officials are increasingly filled by military men, often non-Chinese or, as she puts it, semi-sinified. The civil, examination-bred empire of movement one is becoming a patchwork of soldiers' domains. Now — movement four. What did all this cost?

Regional power

Provincial governors began to act like hereditary local rulers.

Military

Commanders controlled professional troops and passed authority to heirs.

Administration

Governors appointed their own subordinates; military men displaced civil officials.

Revenue

Some key regions paid no taxes to the central government.

The emperor remained, but the center no longer monopolized military and fiscal power.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

After the rebellion, the Tang court still claimed authority, but military governors controlled important local resources. Some provinces functioned like hereditary kingdoms. The court had exchanged direct control for the more immediate goal of ending civil war.

This distinction matters: the Tang state did not collapse in 763. Rather, central power became more negotiated and uneven across the empire.

Synthesis

The Tang state survived 755 by sharing power, then lost the capacity to reunify China.

Bring the political, economic, and social threads together. The Tang survived the rebellion by relying on allies and accommodating military governors. The compromises weakened central control, while new tax strategies and a retreat from market regulation fostered economic change.

The collapse was structural rather than personal. The court faced a real frontier problem, chose the workable solution of professional regional armies, and then found that those armies concentrated permanent force in commanders whose troops depended on them rather than on the throne.

Tang’s changing frontiers

The rebellion weakened Tang influence across Central and Southeast Asia.

  • The Tang withdrew forces from the western frontier to fight An Lushan.
  • Tibet expanded its influence over Silk Road cities.
  • Nanzhao attacked Tang prefectures in northern Vietnam; Vietnam became independent in the tenth century.

New regional powers

Balhae, Tibet, and Nanzhao consolidated states along Tang borders.

The late Tang no longer aimed to dominate Central Asia as the early Tang had.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

As Tang forces moved west to fight the rebellion, Tibet took advantage of the opening and claimed overlordship over Silk Road cities. Nanzhao pressed Tang positions in the southwest, and Vietnam eventually became independent in the tenth century. Balhae also consolidated power in the northeast.

The chapter describes a shift in ambition: even after neighboring empires later weakened, the Tang no longer sought the same degree of Central Asian dominance as before.

Land and taxation

The Tang abandoned equal-field land allocation and taxed actual holdings.

Before the rebellionEqual-field land assignments and standard household taxes
AfterwardLand could be bought and sold more freely
New tax basisTaxes reflected actual landholding and were paid twice a year
The new system matched taxes to property, but debt pushed land toward the wealthy.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

Because the central government no longer had the local infrastructure to enforce the equal-field system, it eventually abandoned it. Land could again be bought and sold. Taxes were assessed on actual landholding and paid in two installments each year.

Each region received a quota to remit to the center and flexibility in raising it. The shift was practical for a weaker central state, but poor households that fell into debt sold land to wealthier people, helping large estates proliferate.

The salt monopoly

Salt taxes became the central government’s most effective revenue source.

More than ½of central revenue came from salt by 779

The state taxed salt indirectly by controlling production and distribution and charging licensed merchants a surcharge.

A specialized Salt Commission administered the system.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

The late Tang government developed a salt monopoly, returning to a revenue strategy also used under the Han. It controlled salt production and distribution, then collected revenue through surcharges paid by licensed salt merchants.

By 779, salt supplied more than half of central government revenue. The Salt Commission became a powerful agency staffed by officials specializing in finance. The system provided revenue even from places where the central government had little direct authority.

Trade after state retreat

Less government control over land and markets helped commerce expand.

  • Markets opened in more towns; provincial capitals became trade centers.
  • Silver bullion and notes of exchange helped address coin shortages.
  • Southern Jiangnan cities grew as refugees and merchants moved south.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

Alongside new tax policies, the post-rebellion government withdrew from supervising markets and setting prices. The textbook argues that this retreat unintentionally stimulated commerce. Markets appeared in more towns, and provincial capitals became trade centers.

Merchants used silver bullion and notes of exchange to work around shortages of copper coins. The south, especially the Jiangnan region and cities such as Yangzhou, Suzhou, and Hangzhou, prospered as refugees and people on official or business assignments settled there.

The court tries to recentralize

The palace army failed to solve the Tang court’s dependence on military factions.

Regional commanders grow stronger
Local military power limits the court
→
Palace army created
Intended to counter provincial troops
→
Eunuchs gain command
They dominate court affairs in the early ninth century
Attempts to restore central control created new power brokers inside the palace.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 86.

Post-rebellion rulers did not abandon centralization. They created a palace army to counter regional commanders. But the eunuchs placed in charge of this force became powerful in their own right and dominated court affairs in the early ninth century.

Senior officials had to ally with one eunuch faction or another to influence policy. The court thus faced competing centers of armed and political power inside and outside the capital.

Late Tang scholarship

Han Yu called scholars back to the Confucian classics and a stronger state.

Political argument

Han Yu opposed provincial fragmentation and supported stronger central government.

  • He promoted a plain “ancient style” of prose.
  • He traced the Confucian Way through the Duke of Zhou, Confucius, and Mencius.
  • His attack on a Buddha relic offended the emperor.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 90.

After An Lushan’s rebellion weakened institutions that had supported Confucian scholarship, some scholars reconsidered their political and cultural role. Han Yu (768–824) looked directly to the classics and argued that the Confucian Way had been passed through a line of sages from the Duke of Zhou to Confucius and Mencius.

He promoted a clear, concise “ancient style” of prose and supported a stronger central government. His memorial against the Buddha bone brought him into conflict with the emperor. The episode shows that Tang intellectuals debated the relationship among Confucianism, Buddhism, and imperial policy rather than simply choosing one tradition.

Dunhuang

The Dunhuang cave preserved records of Tang life far beyond the court.

After 1000A Buddhist cave temple was sealed as invasion threatened the region.
1900A Daoist monk discovered the hidden collection.
1907–1908The bulk of the scrolls was sold to foreign scholars.

The cache included Buddhist sutras and about 13,500 paper scrolls containing many kinds of texts.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 90.

Thousands of original documents were sealed in a Buddhist cave near Dunhuang, about 700 miles from Chang’an. The cave was sealed soon after 1000, when invasion threatened the region, and was discovered in 1900 by a Daoist monk who found a gap in the plaster.

In 1907 and 1908, he sold most of the roughly 13,500 paper scrolls to Aurel Stein and Paul Pelliot. Most were Buddhist sutras, but the cache also contained records of ordinary and administrative life.

What the documents reveal

Dunhuang records let historians reconstruct ordinary social and economic life.

Family and work

Bills of sale, service contracts, and forms for divorce, adoption, and dividing family property.

Learning and worship

Calendars, primers, Buddhist texts, and charters of lay religious associations.

Local government

Household registers, lists of eminent families, and government documents.

These sources make people, households, and local institutions visible alongside emperors.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 90–91.

The Dunhuang collection contains everyday material: sales and service contracts, calendars, school primers, legal forms, religious society charters, family lists, and government documents. These records allow historians to study household registration, tenancy, literacy, religious practice, and local organization.

The evidence also has limits: the documents come from one region and survive because they were sealed in a particular cave. They are invaluable, but they do not represent every Tang community.

Late Tang instability

After 860, banditry and rebellion further weakened the Tang state.

  • Bandit groups attacked merchants, tax convoys, and walled cities.
  • Huang Chao, a failed examination candidate and salt merchant, led the most successful group.
  • His forces captured Guangzhou in 879 and Chang’an two years later.

Power moved local

After 860, regional strongmen could build armies and claim kingship or imperial titles.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 91.

After 860, state structures no longer maintained order in many areas. Bandit gangs, sometimes as large as small armies, attacked the countryside, merchants, and tax convoys. Huang Chao was a failed examination candidate who became a salt merchant and led the most successful of these groups.

His army captured Guangzhou in 879, killing thousands of foreign merchants, and captured Chang’an two years later. The episode marked the depth of Tang instability and the increasing power of armed local leaders.

The transition to 960

In 960, the Song dynasty began a new bid to reunify China.

South

The Ten Kingdoms remained divided among regional rulers, but their governments promoted trade and taxed commerce.

North

Five Dynasties rose and fell around Kaifeng; Chang’an and Luoyang had been devastated by war.

In 960, the Song dynasty began the next effort to reunify China.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 91.

From 860 to 960, military and political power devolved to the local level. In the south, the Ten Kingdoms never consolidated much beyond one or two modern provinces, but rulers promoted trade and taxed it. The southern economy remained active despite political fragmentation.

In the north, Turkic military rulers controlled a sequence of Five Dynasties based at Kaifeng. Chang’an and Luoyang had been badly damaged, and none of the northern dynasties built a stable government before being displaced. The Song dynasty was founded in 960 and began another process of reunification.

Sogdians

An Iranian-speaking Central Asian people centered on the oasis cities of Samarkand and Bukhara (in modern Uzbekistan). The Sogdians were the great merchant intermediaries of the Silk Road — their language served as its trade lingua franca — and Sogdian communities lived throughout Tang China. An Lushan's father was Sogdian; "An" was the Chinese surname conventionally used by Sogdians from Bukhara.

Khitans

The dominant nomadic people of northern Manchuria in the eighth century, and the chief military threat on the Tang's northeastern frontier — the wars in which An Lushan made his reputation. Remember the name: in Chapter 8 the Khitans return as founders of the Liao Dynasty, one of the great rival empires of the Song era.

Uighurs

A Turkic people who built a steppe empire on the ruins of the old Turk khaganates in the 740s, dominating Mongolia. As Tang allies they supplied the cavalry that retook the capitals in 757 — then looted them, demanded silk payoffs, and locked the Tang into a forced silk-for-horses trade at prices Ebrey calls "extortionate." Their empire broke up in the 840s.

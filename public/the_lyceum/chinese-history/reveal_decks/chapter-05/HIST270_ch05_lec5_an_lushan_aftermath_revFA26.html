An Lushan’s Rebellion Changed Tang Government and Society

The rebellion shifted power to regional armies and reshaped taxation, trade, and political life.

755–763An Lushan’s rebellion and its aftermath
Late 8th centuryNew taxes and growing regional markets
After 860Banditry, revolts, and local power
960Song reunification begins

The argument

The Tang survived the rebellion by compromising with regional military power.

Political change

Military governors gained control of armies, appointments, and local revenue.

Economic change

New taxes and less market regulation helped trade expand while landholding concentrated.

Historical evidence

Dunhuang documents reveal everyday life beyond the imperial court.

The An Lushan Rebellion did not immediately end the Tang. It forced the dynasty to recover through alliances and concessions that weakened central control over the provinces. These political changes reshaped the military and tax systems, while economic life continued to grow in new regional centers.

The lecture follows that sequence, then considers late Tang intellectual life, the Dunhuang evidence, and the fragmentation that preceded Song reunification in 960.

The military background

Frontier defense shifted from rotating farmer-soldiers to professional armies.

Earlier fubing
Farmer-soldiers served in rotation
→
Changing frontier needs
Threats demanded forces ready for extended service
→
Professional troops
Long-service soldiers commanded by frontier governors
The new system made military power more permanent and more regional.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84–85.

The earlier fubing system relied on farmer-soldiers who served in rotation. By Xuanzong’s time, the Tang used professional frontier armies of long-service veterans. These troops were costly, but they could remain on the frontier and respond to threats.

Military governors received broad authority to handle crises without waiting for central approval. The system improved immediate defense but concentrated armed power in provincial commands, a vulnerability that became clear during the rebellion.

Frontier commands

Tang military governors commanded troops across a wide frontier zone.

  • Frontier military provinces stretched from Manchuria toward Sichuan.
  • Governors could act quickly without waiting for the court.
  • Many commanders and soldiers came from outside the Chinese civil elite.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84.

Xuanzong established a chain of military provinces along the frontiers, from Manchuria toward Sichuan, to address threats from groups including the Turks, Uighurs, and Tibetans. Governors, often non-Chinese, received significant authority so they could respond without waiting for the central court.

The schematic is not a map of exact provincial borders. Its purpose is to show the scale of delegated command and the strategic reach of the system.

An Lushan

An Lushan’s military career placed him at the center of frontier power.

A professional soldier

An Lushan commanded a frontier army in northern Hebei beginning in 744 and had experience fighting the Khitans.

  • He came from a family of soldiers.
  • The textbook describes him as half Sogdian and half Turk.
  • He commanded veteran troops rather than a temporary militia.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84.

An Lushan had commanded the frontier army in northern Hebei since 744. The textbook identifies him as half Sogdian and half Turk, from a military family, and notes his experience fighting the Khitans.

His background reflects the multiethnic character of Tang frontier forces. His importance came from his command of a large, experienced army and his access to the political center, not from ethnicity alone.

The rebellion begins · 755

An Lushan’s veterans overwhelmed newly raised imperial troops.

755An Lushan rebels and marches south toward Luoyang.
At courtNewly recruited troops prove no match for experienced veterans.
FlightXuanzong flees west toward Sichuan as the capital is threatened.
The rebellion turned delegated frontier command into a direct challenge to the emperor.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

When An Lushan rebelled in 755, his army moved south toward Luoyang. The court tried to raise a new army, but its troops were inexperienced compared with the frontier veterans. As the capital faced imminent danger, Xuanzong fled toward Sichuan.

The crisis exposed the military imbalance created by the frontier system: a provincial commander could deploy a seasoned force while the court struggled to assemble an effective response.

A dynasty under pressure

The rebellion forced Xuanzong from power and left the Tang dependent on allies.

  • Mutinying troops demanded the deaths of Yang Guifei and her relatives before continuing.
  • The heir apparent raised forces in western Shaanxi and became emperor.
  • The rebellion lasted from 755 to 763.

Recovery required help

The court called on the Uighurs, a Turkic ally, to help retake Chang’an.

In return, the allies demanded payment in silk and later received silk for horses.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

During Xuanzong’s flight, the troops accompanying him mutinied and refused to continue until Yang Guifei and her relatives were killed. Meanwhile the heir apparent raised an army in western Shaanxi and, with support from his followers, took the throne; Xuanzong did not contest the change.

The dynasty depended on Uighur troops to recover Chang’an. Those allies looted the city and demanded large quantities of silk before leaving; afterward the Tang traded silk for horses at costly rates to deter raids. The state’s survival came with significant compromises.

Military governors after 763

The Tang pardoned rebel leaders and gave many of them provincial commands.

Rebel leaders submit
Amnesty and accommodation
→
Receive governorships
They retain control of regions and troops
→
Local power hardens
Posts and command pass to heirs in key provinces

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

To bring the rebellion to an end, Tang rulers pardoned rebel leaders and appointed many as military governors of the regions they held. This brought a measure of peace but institutionalized local military control.

In key regions, governors paid no taxes to the central government, appointed their own subordinates, and passed their offices to heirs. Military men, many non-Chinese or partly assimilated, increasingly filled posts previously held by civil officials.

Regional power

Provincial governors began to act like hereditary local rulers.

Military

Commanders controlled professional troops and passed authority to heirs.

Administration

Governors appointed their own subordinates; military men displaced civil officials.

Revenue

Some key regions paid no taxes to the central government.

The emperor remained, but the center no longer monopolized military and fiscal power.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

After the rebellion, the Tang court still claimed authority, but military governors controlled important local resources. Some provinces functioned like hereditary kingdoms. The court had exchanged direct control for the more immediate goal of ending civil war.

This distinction matters: the Tang state did not collapse in 763. Rather, central power became more negotiated and uneven across the empire.

Tang’s changing frontiers

The rebellion weakened Tang influence across Central and Southeast Asia.

  • The Tang withdrew forces from the western frontier to fight An Lushan.
  • Tibet expanded its influence over Silk Road cities.
  • Nanzhao attacked Tang prefectures in northern Vietnam; Vietnam became independent in the tenth century.

New regional powers

Balhae, Tibet, and Nanzhao consolidated states along Tang borders.

The late Tang no longer aimed to dominate Central Asia as the early Tang had.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

As Tang forces moved west to fight the rebellion, Tibet took advantage of the opening and claimed overlordship over Silk Road cities. Nanzhao pressed Tang positions in the southwest, and Vietnam eventually became independent in the tenth century. Balhae also consolidated power in the northeast.

The chapter describes a shift in ambition: even after neighboring empires later weakened, the Tang no longer sought the same degree of Central Asian dominance as before.

Land and taxation

The Tang abandoned equal-field land allocation and taxed actual holdings.

Before the rebellionEqual-field land assignments and standard household taxes
AfterwardLand could be bought and sold more freely
New tax basisTaxes reflected actual landholding and were paid twice a year
The new system matched taxes to property, but debt pushed land toward the wealthy.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

Because the central government no longer had the local infrastructure to enforce the equal-field system, it eventually abandoned it. Land could again be bought and sold. Taxes were assessed on actual landholding and paid in two installments each year.

Each region received a quota to remit to the center and flexibility in raising it. The shift was practical for a weaker central state, but poor households that fell into debt sold land to wealthier people, helping large estates proliferate.

The salt monopoly

Salt taxes became the central government’s most effective revenue source.

More than ½of central revenue came from salt by 779

The state taxed salt indirectly by controlling production and distribution and charging licensed merchants a surcharge.

A specialized Salt Commission administered the system.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

The late Tang government developed a salt monopoly, returning to a revenue strategy also used under the Han. It controlled salt production and distribution, then collected revenue through surcharges paid by licensed salt merchants.

By 779, salt supplied more than half of central government revenue. The Salt Commission became a powerful agency staffed by officials specializing in finance. The system provided revenue even from places where the central government had little direct authority.

Trade after state retreat

Less government control over land and markets helped commerce expand.

  • Markets opened in more towns; provincial capitals became trade centers.
  • Silver bullion and notes of exchange helped address coin shortages.
  • Southern Jiangnan cities grew as refugees and merchants moved south.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85.

Alongside new tax policies, the post-rebellion government withdrew from supervising markets and setting prices. The textbook argues that this retreat unintentionally stimulated commerce. Markets appeared in more towns, and provincial capitals became trade centers.

Merchants used silver bullion and notes of exchange to work around shortages of copper coins. The south, especially the Jiangnan region and cities such as Yangzhou, Suzhou, and Hangzhou, prospered as refugees and people on official or business assignments settled there.

The court tries to recentralize

The palace army failed to solve the Tang court’s dependence on military factions.

Regional commanders grow stronger
Local military power limits the court
→
Palace army created
Intended to counter provincial troops
→
Eunuchs gain command
They dominate court affairs in the early ninth century
Attempts to restore central control created new power brokers inside the palace.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 86.

Post-rebellion rulers did not abandon centralization. They created a palace army to counter regional commanders. But the eunuchs placed in charge of this force became powerful in their own right and dominated court affairs in the early ninth century.

Senior officials had to ally with one eunuch faction or another to influence policy. The court thus faced competing centers of armed and political power inside and outside the capital.

Late Tang scholarship

Han Yu called scholars back to the Confucian classics and a stronger state.

Political argument

Han Yu opposed provincial fragmentation and supported stronger central government.

  • He promoted a plain “ancient style” of prose.
  • He traced the Confucian Way through the Duke of Zhou, Confucius, and Mencius.
  • His attack on a Buddha relic offended the emperor.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 90.

After An Lushan’s rebellion weakened institutions that had supported Confucian scholarship, some scholars reconsidered their political and cultural role. Han Yu (768–824) looked directly to the classics and argued that the Confucian Way had been passed through a line of sages from the Duke of Zhou to Confucius and Mencius.

He promoted a clear, concise “ancient style” of prose and supported a stronger central government. His memorial against the Buddha bone brought him into conflict with the emperor. The episode shows that Tang intellectuals debated the relationship among Confucianism, Buddhism, and imperial policy rather than simply choosing one tradition.

Dunhuang

The Dunhuang cave preserved records of Tang life far beyond the court.

After 1000A Buddhist cave temple was sealed as invasion threatened the region.
1900A Daoist monk discovered the hidden collection.
1907–1908The bulk of the scrolls was sold to foreign scholars.

The cache included Buddhist sutras and about 13,500 paper scrolls containing many kinds of texts.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 90.

Thousands of original documents were sealed in a Buddhist cave near Dunhuang, about 700 miles from Chang’an. The cave was sealed soon after 1000, when invasion threatened the region, and was discovered in 1900 by a Daoist monk who found a gap in the plaster.

In 1907 and 1908, he sold most of the roughly 13,500 paper scrolls to Aurel Stein and Paul Pelliot. Most were Buddhist sutras, but the cache also contained records of ordinary and administrative life.

What the documents reveal

Dunhuang records let historians reconstruct ordinary social and economic life.

Family and work

Bills of sale, service contracts, and forms for divorce, adoption, and dividing family property.

Learning and worship

Calendars, primers, Buddhist texts, and charters of lay religious associations.

Local government

Household registers, lists of eminent families, and government documents.

These sources make people, households, and local institutions visible alongside emperors.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 90–91.

The Dunhuang collection contains everyday material: sales and service contracts, calendars, school primers, legal forms, religious society charters, family lists, and government documents. These records allow historians to study household registration, tenancy, literacy, religious practice, and local organization.

The evidence also has limits: the documents come from one region and survive because they were sealed in a particular cave. They are invaluable, but they do not represent every Tang community.

Late Tang instability

After 860, banditry and rebellion further weakened the Tang state.

  • Bandit groups attacked merchants, tax convoys, and walled cities.
  • Huang Chao, a failed examination candidate and salt merchant, led the most successful group.
  • His forces captured Guangzhou in 879 and Chang’an two years later.

Power moved local

After 860, regional strongmen could build armies and claim kingship or imperial titles.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 91.

After 860, state structures no longer maintained order in many areas. Bandit gangs, sometimes as large as small armies, attacked the countryside, merchants, and tax convoys. Huang Chao was a failed examination candidate who became a salt merchant and led the most successful of these groups.

His army captured Guangzhou in 879, killing thousands of foreign merchants, and captured Chang’an two years later. The episode marked the depth of Tang instability and the increasing power of armed local leaders.

The transition to 960

In 960, the Song dynasty began a new bid to reunify China.

South

The Ten Kingdoms remained divided among regional rulers, but their governments promoted trade and taxed commerce.

North

Five Dynasties rose and fell around Kaifeng; Chang’an and Luoyang had been devastated by war.

In 960, the Song dynasty began the next effort to reunify China.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 91.

From 860 to 960, military and political power devolved to the local level. In the south, the Ten Kingdoms never consolidated much beyond one or two modern provinces, but rulers promoted trade and taxed it. The southern economy remained active despite political fragmentation.

In the north, Turkic military rulers controlled a sequence of Five Dynasties based at Kaifeng. Chang’an and Luoyang had been badly damaged, and none of the northern dynasties built a stable government before being displaced. The Song dynasty was founded in 960 and began another process of reunification.

Synthesis

The Tang state survived 755 by sharing power, then lost the capacity to reunify China.

Rebellion
Frontier armies challenge the court
→
Concessions
Governors retain troops, taxes, and appointments
→
Fragmentation
Local regimes endure until Song reunification in 960

Meanwhile, markets expanded, fiscal tools changed, and Dunhuang sources made everyday society more visible.

Bring the political, economic, and social threads together. The Tang survived the rebellion by relying on allies and accommodating military governors. The compromises weakened central control, while new tax strategies and a retreat from market regulation fostered economic change.

Late Tang instability grew over time, not in a single collapse. Dunhuang documents reveal the range of local life that continued through these transitions. In 960, the Song began a renewed drive toward reunification.

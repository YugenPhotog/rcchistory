The Storm That Broke the Tang

The An Lushan Rebellion, 755–763

Ebrey, A History of China, Chapter 5

⏱ 0:00 — Opening (speak over this slide, ~1 min).

Good morning. In December of the year 755, a single army began marching south out of what is now the Beijing region, and by the time the fighting stopped eight years later, the most powerful, most cosmopolitan empire on earth had been hollowed out from the inside. The dynasty survived — the Tang limps on for another century and a half — but it was never the same empire again. Historians of China treat the winter of 755 the way historians of Rome treat the crossing of the Rubicon: a before-and-after line running through the middle of an entire civilization's story.

Today we're going to do this the way historians have to do it: the story first, the judgment last. For the first thirty minutes I want you to hold off on asking "what does it mean" and just get the sequence of events into your notes — who, where, in what order. At the very end, we'll step back and ask why scholars call this the hinge of Chinese history.

⏱ ~1:00 — Orientation (~1.5 min).

Here's our map for the day. We pick up where the last lecture left us: the Tang dynasty in the first half of the eighth century — the reign of Emperor Xuanzong — is the high-water mark. Chang'an is the largest city in the world. Poetry, painting, music, trade along the Silk Road: all of it is peaking at exactly this moment. That's movement one — I need you to see what the empire looked like at full height, because you can't measure the fall without knowing the height.

Movement two introduces the man himself: who An Lushan actually was, where he came from, and — this is the question students always ask — why on earth the court handed one frontier general that much military power. Movement three is the rebellion: eight years, two capitals lost, an imperial family torn apart on the road. Movement four is the bill: what the war cost, in numbers and in the shape of the empire itself.

Keep three names straight in your notes — Xuanzong the emperor, Yang Guifei his consort, An Lushan the general. Everything today runs through the triangle of those three people.

Movement I

The Empire at Its Height

What did the Tang look like on the eve of the storm?

⏱ ~2:30 — Section I runs to about the 11-minute mark.

Transition line: "So let's set the stage. The year is roughly 740, and if you could stand anywhere on earth and look around, the best place to be standing would be Chang'an."

Xuanzong's Tang (r. 713–756)

The Longest Reign

  • Took the throne in 712 after decades of palace coups
  • Early reign: an activist emperor — census, tax reform, curbing the monasteries
  • 44 years on the throne — the longest of any Tang emperor

A Brilliant Court

  • Chang'an grows past 2 million people — the world's largest city
  • Poet Li Bai and horse-painter Han Gan serve at court
  • Exams select officials — but family pedigree still counts

Later generations remember this as the Tang golden age.

⏱ ~3:00 (~2.5 min).

Xuanzong does not inherit a calm throne — he seizes one. Remember the half-century of women dominating court politics after Empress Wu: Empress Wei poisons her own husband in 710, and it's a young prince — the future Xuanzong — who storms the palace with a handful of followers, kills Empress Wei and her faction, and puts his own father on the throne. Two years later, in 712, the father abdicates in his favor. So this is a man who begins his career decisive, ruthless, and energetic.

And the first decades of his reign show it. Ebrey calls him an activist: he curbs the power of the Buddhist monasteries that had grown fat under Empress Wu, he orders a new census to shore up the land system, he reorganizes frontier defense. This is the era later writers call the Kaiyuan age — the golden age the poets are still mourning a thousand years later.

Chang'an at this moment passes two million inhabitants — nothing in Europe or the Middle East comes close. The court itself is a work of art: Li Bai, probably the most famous poet in all of Chinese literature, drinks and writes there; Han Gan paints the emperor's horses. Officials increasingly come up through the examination system — though notice Ebrey's caution: pedigree still matters so much that Xuanzong commissions a two-hundred-chapter genealogy just to keep the ranking of the great families up to date. Merit and aristocracy are sharing power, uneasily.

Set the hook: hold this image of the empire at full height. Everything we see for the rest of the hour is the unraveling of this picture.

The Frontier Problem

  • Threats on every border: Turks, Uighurs, Tibetans, Khitans in the northeast
  • The old farmer-militia system is collapsing under its own weight
  • Replaced by costly, long-service professional soldiers
  • Xuanzong builds a ring of military provinces from Manchuria to Sichuan
  • Their governors — the jiedushi (military governors) — may act without waiting for central authorization
  • Many of these governors are non-Chinese frontier professionals

The empire's best armies now sit on its edges — under men the court must simply trust.

⏱ ~5:30 (~2.5 min).

Now, the structural flaw — and I want to be fair to Xuanzong here, because every piece of this was a reasonable solution to a real problem.

Problem one: the frontiers. The Tang is a continental empire with enemies on every side — Turkic peoples and then the Uighurs on the steppe, the rising Tibetan empire in the west, the Khitans up in Manchuria. Frontier war is now constant, distant, and professional.

Problem two: the army he inherited can't fight that kind of war. The early Tang ran on divisional militia — farmer-soldiers who held land allotments under the equal-field system and rotated through service cheaply. But population growth has broken the equal-field math: Ebrey notes that allotment holders were receiving only a fraction of the land they were due while still owing the full household tax, so they fled the registers — which shrank both the tax base and the militia rolls. Part-time farmers can't match full-time steppe cavalry anyway.

So Xuanzong does the professional thing: he replaces militia with long-service career soldiers — far more effective, far more expensive — and he organizes the frontier into a ring of military provinces under governors called jiedushi. Say it with me: jee-eh-doo-shr. Write it down — it's on everything from here to the end of the course. These governors get sweeping authority — Ebrey's phrase is that they could deal with crises "without waiting for central authorization." And many are non-Chinese professionals, men whose whole world is the frontier army.

Each step is rational. Add them together and you get an empire whose striking power lives on the rim, loyal not to institutions but to individual commanders. Now let's look at the map.

The Ten Commands, 745

  • Ten great frontier commands circle the empire
  • Frontier armies now dwarf the forces near the capital
  • Commanders hold their posts for years at a time
  • An Lushan's base: the northeast — facing the Khitans

The center is rich — but militarily hollow.

The ten jiedushi commands, 745. Map: SY, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0

⏱ ~8:00 (~2 min). Walk the map physically — point as you go.

Here's the empire in 745, and I want you to read this map the way a Tang court official should have read it. Start in the northeast corner, up by modern Beijing, and trace the ring: command after command running west along the steppe frontier, down past the Tibetan border, all the way to Sichuan in the southwest. Ten great military commands, each under a jiedushi.

Now the arithmetic that matters. Add up the troop strengths printed on this map and the frontier commands come to roughly half a million professional soldiers. What guards the interior? Palace guards and leftover militia units — a fraction of that, and nothing like the same quality. The best armies in Asia are stationed on the rim of this wheel, and the hub is essentially undefended, because for a century the Tang's wars have all been out there.

And look at the northeast — the commands facing the Khitans. Hold your finger there. By the early 750s, one man holds that corner of the map. His name is An Lushan, and the region he commands — the Hebei frontier — is exactly where our story begins. One more look at the court before we meet him.

The Court Turns Inward

  • After 736, Xuanzong lets chancellor Li Linfu run the government (d. 752)
  • The emperor, aging, turns to Daoism and Tantric Buddhism
  • By 742 he is 57, thirty years on the throne — and in love
  • Yang Guifei: the consort who now fills his world
  • To please her, he lets her friends and relatives fill high offices
  • Among her favorites: a general named An Lushan, spending more and more time at court

⏱ ~10:00 (~2.5 min).

While the frontier militarizes, the center goes soft — and the activist emperor of 712 slowly disappears. The turning point Ebrey flags is 736: Xuanzong hands day-to-day government to his chancellor Li Linfu, an aristocrat proud of his pedigree, who will run the state until his death in 752. The emperor's attention drifts to religion — he writes a commentary on the Laozi, founds a school for Daoist examinations, and is fascinated by the newly arrived Tantric school of Buddhism, with its spells and incantations. In 742 he personally holds the incense burner while a Ceylonese master chants for Tang victory. Some historians, Ebrey notes, blame exactly these enthusiasms for his fading interest in administration. He's also simply old: fifty-seven in 742, three decades on the throne.

And then there is Yang Guifei. She is the great romantic figure of Chinese history — a full-figured beauty, as Ebrey puts it, in an age that admired rounded proportions — and the aging emperor is utterly devoted to her. Poets will spend the next thousand years on the two of them. But notice what the devotion costs: to keep her happy, Xuanzong lets her place friends and relatives throughout the government. Her family — the Yangs — becomes a power bloc at court.

And one of her particular favorites is a fat, charming, Turkic-speaking general from the northeastern frontier who has learned that time spent at court is as valuable as time spent fighting Khitans. Tradition says she even adopted him ceremonially as her son — the court found him hilarious and harmless. His name is An Lushan. Movement two: who is this man?

Movement II

The Rise of An Lushan

How does one frontier general come to hold the empire's best armies?

⏱ ~12:30 — Section II runs to about the 17-minute mark.

Transition line: "Every disaster has a biography at the center of it. Here's his."

Who Was An Lushan?

A Man of the Frontier

  • Half Sogdian, half Turk — a true child of the borderlands
  • A professional soldier from a family of soldiers
  • Made his name fighting the Khitans of Manchuria

A Man of Power

  • Commands the northern Hebei frontier army from 744
  • By the 750s controls the empire's northeastern commands
  • At his back: 100,000+ veteran troops

He is exactly the kind of man the new system was built to promote.

⏱ ~13:00 (~2.5 min).

An Lushan is the frontier system made flesh. His father was Sogdian — that's the Central Asian merchant people whose caravans ran the Silk Road; their cities, Samarkand and Bukhara, sat in what's now Uzbekistan. His mother was a Turk. He grows up in the polyglot world of the northeastern garrisons, reportedly working as a young man as a market broker precisely because he could move among half a dozen frontier languages. He is, in other words, exactly the cosmopolitan Tang type we celebrated two slides ago — just the military version of it.

And he's good. A professional soldier from a family of soldiers, Ebrey says, who makes his reputation in the hard, unglamorous wars against the Khitans of Manchuria. From 744 he commands the frontier army in northern Hebei — the northeast corner of that map — and through the next decade he accumulates more. By the early 750s he holds the northeastern commands together; the standard accounts beyond Ebrey count three jiedushi posts in one man's hands — Pinglu, Fanyang, and Hedong, if you want the names. Call it the largest concentration of veteran soldiers in the empire: over a hundred thousand long-service professionals who have marched, fought, and been paid under one general for ten years.

Pause on the obvious question: why would any court allow this? Part of the answer is that he was useful — the Khitan frontier was genuinely dangerous. Part of it is that he was trusted — the charming, obese court favorite, Yang Guifei's adopted "son," the man who made the emperor laugh. And part of it is court politics: a non-Chinese professional with no aristocratic pedigree and no examination degree looked, to the chancellors, like a man who could never be a rival for their jobs. Every safeguard failed for a reason that made sense at the time.

The Collision at Court

  • 752: chancellor Li Linfu dies — the balance at court breaks
  • Power passes to Yang Guozhong, Yang Guifei's cousin
  • An Lushan and Yang Guozhong quarrel over control of the government
  • Each man convinces himself the other is plotting his destruction
  • December 755: An Lushan marches south from Fanyang — claiming secret orders to remove Yang Guozhong

Ebrey's phrase: the quarrel "led to open warfare."

⏱ ~15:30 (~2 min).

So what finally lights the fuse? Not ideology, not rebellion-from-below — a personnel dispute at the very top.

As long as Li Linfu lived, the pieces balanced: the old chancellor managed the frontier generals and the Yang family alike. He dies in 752, and into the vacuum steps Yang Guozhong — Yang Guifei's cousin, elevated the way the whole Yang clan has been elevated. Ebrey compresses what happens next into one devastating sentence: An Lushan "got into a quarrel with Yang's cousin over control of the government, which led to open warfare."

Unpack that. Yang Guozhong distrusts An Lushan — loudly, repeatedly, telling the emperor the general will rebel. An Lushan, in turn, watches a man he despises accumulate the power to destroy him. By 755 each is certain the other is plotting his ruin — and here's the tragic mechanics of it: once Yang Guozhong has spent years predicting rebellion, An Lushan knows that sooner or later the prediction becomes an arrest warrant. The accusation makes the crime rational.

In December 755, An Lushan moves. He marches his veterans south from Fanyang, near modern Beijing, under a classic fig leaf: he claims he carries secret orders from the emperor to march on the capital and remove the wicked minister Yang Guozhong. Nobody needs to believe it. It just needs to let his soldiers tell themselves they're loyal. Movement three — the storm itself.

Movement III

The Rebellion, 755–763

Eight years that hollowed out an empire

⏱ ~17:30 — Section III runs to about the 27-minute mark. This is the narrative heart; keep the pace up.

Transition line: "From here the story moves fast — faster than anyone at court could believe."

The Storm Breaks, 755–756

  • An's veterans strike south toward Luoyang — the eastern capital falls within weeks
  • Early 756: An Lushan proclaims his own dynasty — the Great Yan
  • The court's hastily recruited troops are no match for veterans
  • Loyal armies hold the Tong Pass, shielding Chang'an —
  • — until the court orders them out to attack: the army is destroyed (summer 756)
  • The road to Chang'an lies open

⏱ ~18:00 (~2.5 min).

The campaign opens with terrifying speed. An's column drives south through Hebei toward Luoyang, the eastern capital, and the government's response exposes everything we said about the hollow center: the court starts raising an army — recruiting, in the moment, against a hundred thousand veterans. Ebrey is blunt: the newly recruited troops were no match. Luoyang falls in a matter of weeks — about a month after the first march. At the lunar new year, early 756, An Lushan drops the fig leaf about rescuing the emperor and proclaims himself emperor of a new dynasty, the Great Yan.

Now, between Luoyang and Chang'an the land itself offers one gift: the Tong Pass — Tongguan — where the road squeezes between mountains and the Yellow River. A defender who sits in that pass cannot be dislodged, and through the first half of 756 the Tang's remaining frontier veterans, under the old general Geshu Han, do exactly that. The rebellion stalls. Time, supply, and loyalist risings in Hebei are all starting to work against An Lushan.

And then the court throws it away. Yang Guozhong — who suspects the generals at the pass almost as much as he fears the rebels — convinces the emperor that the army is stalling treasonously, and the order goes out: leave the pass and attack. Geshu Han reportedly wept when he obeyed. In the summer of 756 the Tang field army marches out of its impregnable position and is annihilated. This detail comes from the fuller chronicles rather than our textbook, but it matters, because it shows the rebellion succeeding not on rebel genius but on court paranoia — the same paranoia that started the war. And now nothing stands between An Lushan and Chang'an.

Flight — and the Mawei Mutiny

  • Xuanzong abandons Chang'an, fleeing west toward Sichuan
  • At the Mawei post-station, his escort mutinies
  • The soldiers kill Yang Guozhong — then refuse to march until Yang Guifei dies too
  • The heir apparent breaks away northwest; proclaimed Emperor Suzong (756)
  • Xuanzong, informed afterward, does not contest it

The flight to Shu (Sichuan), Ming-dynasty handscroll after Qiu Ying. Freer Gallery of Art / Wikimedia Commons

⏱ ~20:30 (~3 min). This is the emotional center of the lecture — slow down and tell it.

What follows is the single most retold scene in Chinese history — painters and poets will return to it for a thousand years, and this Ming-dynasty scroll on the screen is one of those returns: the imperial party winding through the mountains toward Sichuan.

With the pass lost, Xuanzong slips out of Chang'an before dawn with Yang Guifei, Yang Guozhong, the heir apparent, and a column of imperial guards, heading west for the safety of Sichuan — the old land of Shu, walled off by mountains. The flight is chaos: rain, hunger, no provisions, the world's most powerful man begging food from villagers.

At a post-station called Mawei, the guards stop marching. They are hungry, humiliated, and they know exactly whom they blame for all of it. They fall on Yang Guozhong and kill him — the chancellor whose feud began the war, dead at the roadside. And then they surround the emperor's own lodging and deliver the demand Ebrey records: they would not continue until Yang Guifei and her relatives had been killed. Not exile. Killed.

Think about what's being demanded: the emperor's soldiers are requiring him to order the death of the woman at the center of his life. And he does it. Tradition says she was strangled with a silk cord at a Buddhist shrine by the road while the emperor sat apart. The column then marches on, and the old man who rode out of Chang'an as Son of Heaven arrives in Sichuan as something much smaller.

Meanwhile the heir apparent has split off and ridden northwest to the loyal frontier armies, and at Lingwu his officers convince him to take the throne himself as Emperor Suzong. Word reaches Xuanzong in Sichuan that he has, in effect, retired. Ebrey's dry phrase: he "did not contest" it. The golden-age emperor will live out his days a guarded, grieving ex-sovereign — he and his son both die in 762, within weeks of each other, before the war is even over.

Recovery — at a Price

  • Suzong's problem: loyal armies exist, but not enough of them
  • The solution: call in the Uighurs — Turkic allies from the steppe
  • 757: Tang and Uighur forces retake Chang'an and Luoyang
  • The price: the Uighurs loot the capitals and leave only when paid off in silk
  • Afterward: forced silk-for-horses trade at extortionate rates, year after year

The dynasty is saved by foreign cavalry it can never again refuse.

⏱ ~23:30 (~2 min).

How does a dynasty that just lost both capitals claw its way back? Ebrey frames the answer in one word: compromises. Every element of the recovery is bought, and the bills run for decades.

Suzong does have assets. The loyalist generals — Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi are the great names — hold the northwest and keep resistance alive in Hebei. But to retake walled capitals from veteran armies he needs cavalry he doesn't have, and he gets it from the Uighurs — the Turkic power that now dominates the steppe where the old Turk empire used to be. The Uighur khagan sends his horsemen, and in late 757 the combined armies take back Chang'an and then Luoyang.

And then comes the invoice. Ebrey: after the Uighurs took Chang'an from the rebels, "they looted it and would not leave until they were paid off with huge quantities of silk." The liberators sack the city they liberated — Luoyang gets it even worse. And the relationship doesn't end there: to keep the Uighurs from raiding thereafter, the Tang must buy their horses, every year, at prices the Uighurs set — Ebrey's word is extortionate. Silk for horses becomes a permanent tax paid to the steppe.

Mark the reversal for your notes: fifty years earlier, Tang armies dictated terms across Central Asia. Now the Tang pays protection money to stay on its own throne. The empire survives 757 — but it survives as a client of its own allies.

The Rebellion Devours Itself

  • 757: An Lushan — half-blind, violently paranoid — is murdered by his own son, An Qingxu
  • 759: An's general Shi Siming kills An Qingxu and takes over the rebellion
  • 761: Shi Siming is murdered by his own son, Shi Chaoyi
  • 763: Shi Chaoyi, defeated and abandoned, takes his own life — the war ends
  • Four rebel emperors in eight years — each destroyed from within

This is why Chinese sources call it the An–Shi Rebellion.

⏱ ~25:30 (~2 min). Students love this slide — let the pattern land.

Here's the part the textbook compresses and the chronicles relish: the rebel side is eating itself the entire time. Watch the pattern.

An Lushan barely outlives his own triumph. By 757 he is going blind, tormented by ulcers, and so violently paranoid that his own household fears for their lives — and in January 757, barely a year after proclaiming his Great Yan dynasty, he is murdered in his palace in a plot around his own son, An Qingxu, who takes the rebel throne.

An Qingxu is weaker than his father, and in 759 the rebellion's best remaining general — Shi Siming, An Lushan's old comrade from the Hebei frontier — kills him and takes over, even recapturing Luoyang. Shi Siming is a genuinely capable commander; the Tang's recovery stalls against him. And then in 761 history repeats with mechanical cruelty: Shi Siming is murdered by his own son, Shi Chaoyi.

Shi Chaoyi inherits a movement that is now a feud wearing a crown. Tang and Uighur forces grind him back; his commanders begin cutting their own deals with the court; and in early 763, abandoned, he takes his own life. Eight years, four rebel emperors — father killed by son, usurper killed by comrade, comrade killed by son — every single transition a murder inside the family of the movement. That's why the Chinese tradition names it for both houses: the An–Shi Rebellion.

But notice what did not happen: the Tang never decisively crushed this rebellion on the battlefield. So how did it actually end? Next slide — and the answer explains the next hundred and fifty years of Chinese history.

How It Ended: Peace by Pardon

  • No final victory — the court buys the surrender of the remaining rebel commanders
  • Rebel generals are pardoned and appointed military governors of the very regions they held
  • In key provinces the governors now act like warlords:
  • — they pay no taxes to the center
  • — they appoint their own subordinates
  • — they pass their posts to their heirs

The war ends in 763 — but the northeast never truly returns.

⏱ ~27:00 (~2 min).

Write this down carefully, because it's the single most consequential fact of the whole war: the An Lushan Rebellion was not defeated. It was bought.

By 763 both sides are exhausted. The court has no appetite — and honestly no capacity — for reconquering Hebei fortress by fortress. So it makes the deal Ebrey describes: to get the remaining rebel leaders to submit, the Tang "offered pardons and even appointed many as military governors of the regions they held." Read that twice. The men who spent eight years in rebellion end the war holding official Tang titles over the exact territories they rebelled with.

And in those key regions — above all the old rebel heartland of Hebei, the provinces the sources call the three garrisons — the governorship becomes a hereditary warlordship in all but name. Ebrey lists the three features and I want all three in your notes: they pay no taxes to the central government; they appoint their own subordinate officials; and they pass their positions down to their heirs. Each of those is a defining power of a sovereign state. The Tang map still colors Hebei as Tang territory, but the court's writ stops at the border of those commands — for the next century and a half.

One more Ebrey observation while we're here: across the empire, posts that had been held by civil officials are increasingly filled by military men, often non-Chinese or, as she puts it, semi-sinified. The civil, examination-bred empire of movement one is becoming a patchwork of soldiers' domains. Now — movement four. What did all this cost?

Movement IV

Counting the Cost

What the war took — and what it left behind

⏱ ~29:00 — Section IV runs to about the 33-minute mark.

Transition line: "Before the numbers, let's hear from someone who lived through it."

A Witness: Du Fu in Occupied Chang'an

"The state is shattered — mountains and rivers remain;
spring comes to the city, and the grass grows deep."
— Du Fu, "Spring View" (757), written in rebel-held Chang'an
  • Du Fu (712–777): now honored as China's greatest poet
  • Caught in the capital when it fell; his family scattered by famine and war
  • His verse becomes "the voice of the moral man protesting injustice"
  • For historians: the war as ordinary people lived it

⏱ ~29:30 (~2 min). Read the couplet aloud twice — once to deliver it, once slowly.

The chronicles give us armies; Du Fu gives us the war's interior. He's a minor would-be official who keeps failing the examinations — Ebrey's biography box on page 87 tells the story — and the rebellion catches him at the worst of a hard life. In 754 famine forces him to move his family; in the chaos of 755 he moves them again; and in a poem from that year he comes home to the sound of wailing and learns his youngest child has died — and his response, astonishingly, is to turn his grief outward, toward the conscripts and the dispossessed who have it worse. Ebrey quotes that poem on page 87 — read the full passage; it's worth your time. Her line about him is the one on the slide: he creates "the voice of the moral man protesting injustice."

The couplet I've put on screen is my own rendering of the opening of "Spring View," which he writes in 757 while held in occupied Chang'an: the state shattered, the mountains and rivers indifferent, the spring grass growing deep in the streets of a half-emptied capital. Twenty-some characters that have carried the memory of this war for thirteen centuries.

Teaching note for you at the podium: Ebrey's box prints Du Fu's dates as 712–777; most scholarship puts his death in 770 — if a sharp student checks Wikipedia and raises it, that's a nice thirty-second lesson in why we cite sources and check them against each other. Methodological point to make out loud: poetry is evidence. When the state's own record-keeping collapses — hold that thought for the next slide — the poets are sometimes the best census of suffering we have.

The Ledger: What the Registers Say

~53 million
people on the tax registers, 754 — the eve of the rebellion
~17 million
people on the registers, 764 — the year after it ended
  • Caution: this measures the state's ability to count, not deaths alone
  • Millions dead — millions more fled, displaced, or simply off the books
  • Both capitals sacked; the Hebei heartland devastated

⏱ ~31:30 (~2 min). This slide is also a lesson in reading sources — make both points.

These two numbers come from the Tang state's own census registers — they're not in our textbook chapter, but they appear in virtually every scholarly treatment of the war, and they are among the most startling numbers in all of pre-modern history. On the eve of the rebellion, in 754, the registers count roughly fifty-three million people. In 764, the year after the war ends, they count about seventeen million. On paper, two of every three people in the Chinese empire have vanished in a single decade.

Now the historian's discipline — and this is the part I actually care that you remember. You will find websites ranking the An Lushan Rebellion among the deadliest wars in human history by subtracting those two numbers and calling it thirty-six million dead. Don't do that. A tax register measures the state's ability to count people, and that ability is precisely what the war destroyed: whole provinces — Hebei above all — simply stopped reporting; refugees by the millions walked off the registers and south into Jiangnan; the autonomous governors we just met had every incentive never to report their populations again, because registered people are taxable people.

So the honest statement is this: the death toll was immense — plausibly in the millions, with both capitals sacked and the richest provinces in the empire fought over for eight years — but the sixty-eight-percent collapse is, first and foremost, the collapse of the state. And in a way that's the deeper lesson: the number is a photograph of a government that can no longer see its own people. Hold both truths at once: enormous human catastrophe, and a measuring instrument that broke in the middle of measuring it.

An Empire Transformed

Shrinking Reach

  • Tibetans seize the Silk Road cities while Tang armies fight inland — and even raid Chang'an (763)
  • Central Asia is gone; the Tang never again dominates it

A New Way to Govern

  • The equal-field system is abandoned — land can be bought and sold
  • Taxes now fall on actual landholding, twice a year
  • The salt monopoly: over half of central revenue by 779
  • Refugees stream south — Jiangnan's cities boom

A government that once managed everything learns to tax what it cannot control.

⏱ ~33:00 (~2 min). Keep this brisk — it's the bridge to the next lecture.

The war redraws the empire from the outside in. Start with the borders: when the Tang pulled its western garrisons east to fight An Lushan, the Tibetan empire walked into the vacuum and claimed the Silk Road cities for itself — in 763 a Tibetan army even briefly occupies Chang'an itself. Ebrey's summary judgment: even after Tibet and the Uighur empire both collapse in the 840s, the Tang court "no longer had the ambition to dominate Central Asia." The cosmopolitan empire's western window closes.

Inside, the state reinvents itself. The equal-field system — remember, the government allocating land to households — is finally abandoned; the center no longer has the local machinery to run it. People may buy and sell land freely again, which means the rich buy and the indebted poor sell, and great estates proliferate. Taxation flips to match reality: instead of one-tax-fits-all per household, taxes fall on actual landholding, collected in semiannual installments, with each region assigned a quota — this is the famous two-tax reform. And the fiscal genius move: the salt monopoly. The state adds its surcharge to salt sold through licensed merchants, and because everyone everywhere must buy salt, it collects revenue even from provinces where its officials can't set foot. By 779 more than half of central government income is salt money.

And Ebrey adds the twist you wouldn't expect: this forced retreat from managing the economy — giving up land control, market supervision, price-setting — actually stimulates trade. Markets multiply, merchants invent silver bullion and notes of exchange, and the refugee flood south makes the Jiangnan cities — Yangzhou, Suzhou, Hangzhou — the boom towns of the ninth century. Weakness at the center, dynamism in the south. File that pattern away: it's the seed of everything in Chapter 8.

Now the Analysis: Why 755 Is a Hinge

Before 755

  • Expansive, cosmopolitan, aristocratic
  • Center allocates land and commands the frontiers
  • Power radiates outward from Chang'an

After 763

  • Defensive, decentralized, militarized provinces
  • Center taxes trade and salt — and negotiates with its own governors
  • Wealth and people shift south
Yet Ebrey warns us: late Tang is not simply decline — art, poetry, and commerce flourish in the fragments.

⏱ ~35:00 target — closing analysis (~2 min). Only now do we interpret.

You've waited thirty minutes for the "so what," so here it is. Lay the two columns side by side. Before 755: an expansive, cosmopolitan, aristocratic empire — the state allocates the land, the examination-and-pedigree elite staffs the government, the armies push outward, and everything radiates from Chang'an. After 763: a defensive empire of semi-independent military provinces, financed not by counting peasants but by taxing salt and trade, negotiating with the steppe instead of commanding it, with its demographic and economic center of gravity sliding permanently south. That's why historians treat this one event as the hinge not just of the Tang but of the entire imperial story — many scholars see the late Tang as the beginning of a transformation that runs straight into the Song world we'll meet in Chapter 8.

But end on Ebrey's caution, because it's the sophisticated point: do not write "decline" in your notes and close the book. Her exact framing in the chapter opening is that late Tang "should not be viewed simply in terms of dynastic decline because art and culture continued to flourish." The state shrank; Chinese civilization did not. The best poetry, the commercial revolution, the rise of the southern cities — all of it happens in the fragments the rebellion left behind. A weaker government and a more dynamic society, at the same time. If you can hold that paradox, you understand the late Tang better than most textbooks' chapter titles do.

What to Carry Out the Door

  • The rebellion grew from reasonable solutions: professional armies + powerful frontier governors
  • It was triggered by a court feud — An Lushan vs. Yang Guozhong — not a popular rising
  • It ended by pardon, not victory: rebel generals kept their provinces
  • The cost: lost Central Asia, a broken census, autonomous Hebei — and a new tax state built on salt and trade
  • Next: Ch. 8, "China Among Equals" — the world this war created

⏱ ~37:00 — wrap (~1 min, with buffer).

Run the takeaways briskly — they're the skeleton for the quiz. One: the rebellion wasn't born of folly but of fixes — professional armies and empowered frontier governors each solved a real problem and together created an unaccountable concentration of force. Two: the spark was personal — a feud between An Lushan and Yang Guozhong at a court that had stopped paying attention. Three: it ended in a deal, not a triumph, and the deal — rebel generals confirmed as governors of their own provinces — set the terms of Chinese politics for the next century and a half. Four: the bill — Central Asia lost, the census system shattered, Hebei autonomous, and a central state that reinvented itself around salt and commerce.

Closing line: "In 755 the Tang was the empire that commanded the world. In 763 it was the empire that negotiated with it. Next time, in Chapter 8, we meet the China that learned to live — and thrive — among equals."

Divisional Militia (fubing)

The early Tang army system: farmer-soldiers who received land allotments under the equal-field system and rotated through military service part-time. Cheap for the state — but poorly suited to constant, long-distance frontier warfare. As the equal-field land math broke down in the early 700s, the militia broke down with it, and the Tang shifted to expensive full-time professional soldiers.

An esoteric school of Buddhism newly arrived in Tang China from India via Central Asia, making heavy use of magical spells, incantations, and elaborate rituals. Xuanzong was fascinated by it: in 726 he had the Javanese monk Vajrabodhi perform Tantric rites against drought, and in 742 he personally held the incense burner while the Ceylonese master Amoghavajra chanted for Tang military victory.

An Iranian-speaking Central Asian people centered on the oasis cities of Samarkand and Bukhara (in modern Uzbekistan). A Turkic people who built a steppe empire on the ruins of the old Turk khaganates in the 740s, dominating Mongolia. Equal-Field System

The land system inherited from the Northern Dynasties: the state allocated farmland to households in standard allotments and taxed each household at a flat rate. It required an enormous, accurate local registration apparatus. Population growth had strained it before 755; the rebellion destroyed the infrastructure that ran it, and the late Tang abandoned it, legalizing free purchase and sale of land.

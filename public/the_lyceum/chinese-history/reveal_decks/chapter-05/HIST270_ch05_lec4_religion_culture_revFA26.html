Buddhism woven into Tang society

Monasteries taught, lodged, lent, and prayed for the living and the state.

Schools: temple literacy for many boys, below elite Confucian standards but far from nothing.
Welfare: lodging, infirmaries, care for the aged.
Credit and land: tenants, mills, oil presses, loans, pawn services, safekeeping.
State use: Taizong ordered temples at major founding battle sites (629); later prefectures kept official temples for the realm.
Wealth explains popularity and official worry about lost revenue.

Sources: Weinstein; Britannica on temple schools; Cambridge History of China, vol. 3.

Purpose. Establish monasteries as multi-function institutions before any empress or suppression.

Story. By Tang times Buddhism had been in China about five centuries. Monasteries were not only quiet halls for private meditation. They taught literacy and doctrine; lodged travelers and pilgrims; offered prayers for families and for the state; and, in many cases, managed land, tenants, and credit. Temple schools and charitable lending made Buddhism socially useful. Elite and imperial gifts enlarged some establishments. Define monastery wealth carefully for first-years: it meant land, labor obligations, and financial resources—not only gold statues. That mix invited devotion and official suspicion at the same time. A finance official could respect Buddhist teaching and still resent tax-exempt land or unregistered labor tied to a successful house.

Adaptation · family duty meets Buddhist aid for the dead

Monks told Mulian's journey so listeners heard filial piety inside Buddhist rescue.

Story: Mulian, a monk, journeys to the underworld to save his suffering mother.
Medium: oral telling and pictures for audiences who could not read scripture.
Festival: seventh-month Ghost Festival offerings joined Buddhists and non-Buddhists.
Adaptation meant mutual change, not simple replacement of Chinese tradition.

Sources: Stephen F. Teiser, The Ghost Festival in Medieval China.

Purpose. Adaptation story: Mulian makes Buddhist rescue speak Chinese family duty.

Story. Keep it human. Mulian (Maudgalyayana in Sanskrit tradition; Mulian in Chinese telling) is the disciple who learns his mother suffers in a hungry-ghost realm and will not abandon her. Chinese audiences heard filial piety—the duty of children to parents—inside a Buddhist rescue plot. Rituals connected to the Ghost Festival (Yulanpen) let families care for the dead with Buddhist means. This is adaptation, not simple replacement of Chinese values. Buddhism entered family language by amplifying a value students already recognize: you do not walk away from your mother, even after death. Monks performed and explained the story so ordinary listeners could feel continuity rather than foreign rupture.

Why it matters. Shows how Buddhism became Chinese social religion. Adaptation helps explain deep integration before any later crackdown.

Act II · Empress Wu

Wu built a claim no woman had held before from inside Tang institutions.

Court politics · Mount Tai · exams · Great Cloud · Zhou rule

AI interpretation · Wu's rise inside the palace world

Purpose. Pivot to Act II: Wu Zhao's unprecedented claim built from inside Tang institutions.

Story. Shift from social Buddhism to one political career that used many religious and cultural vocabularies. Wu Zhao (often called Empress Wu or Wu Zetian in older English usage) rose from the Tang palace world, became empress beside Gaozong, ruled as empress dowager, and in 690 claimed the throne as emperor of a new Zhou dynasty—the only woman in Chinese history to hold the imperial title in her own name. She did not invent a separate female constitution from nowhere. She worked through court factions, capitals (especially Luoyang), examinations, Confucian ritual symbols, and Buddhist prophecy. That is why she belongs in a religion-and-culture lecture: legitimacy was performed with sacred and classical tools.

Why it matters. Wu is the clearest case tonight of patronage and ritual as tools of legitimacy—and of their limits when coalitions turn.

Purpose. Narrative of Wu's rise (651–690) with documented tools and a caution against hostile cartoon.

Story. Keep roles distinct. Wu entered Gaozong's court as a consort, became empress, and after Gaozong's death ruled as empress dowager before taking the throne in 690. Purpose. Mount Tai feng and shan rites (666) as Confucian ritual theater Wu used beside Gaozong.

Story. In late 665 the court traveled; in the first month of 666 Gaozong performed the feng and shan sacrifices at Mount Tai, among the most solemn Confucian legitimating rites an emperor could stage. Wu participated in a carefully framed public ritual role that used yin-yang complementarity: male and female paired powers in cosmology, translated into ceremonial presence beside the emperor. The mountain became theater of legitimacy. This did not erase gender hierarchy in Tang law or ideology. It showed Wu working within classical symbols to claim public religious-political space no ordinary consort held. Buddhist prophecy and stone · 675 to 690

Wu's supporters read a female monarch out of the Great Cloud Sutra while Longmen stone made power colossal.

Longmen: Wu helped patronize the colossal Vairocana Buddha, completed 675, near Luoyang.
Great Cloud: sutra foretold a female world ruler; commentary (c. 689 to 690) identified her with Wu and linked her to Maitreya.
Spread: after 690, a Great Cloud temple in every prefecture pushed the message.
Also Confucian: Mingtang (688); dynasty named Zhou after the classical model.
Many vocabularies at once: Buddhist prophecy, Confucian ritual hall, mythic and maternal models.

Purpose. Great Cloud Sutra prophecy (690 context) and Longmen Vairocana stone as Buddhist public argument.

Story. Around the time Wu moved to claim the throne, supporters promoted a commentary on the Great Cloud Sutra that could be read as prophesying a female monarch and righteous Buddhist rule. This arrived as a packaged political-religious argument, not a random miracle in the street. A copy survives among Dunhuang manuscripts, which helps historians study how the claim circulated. Meanwhile, monumental Buddhist stone at Longmen near Luoyang—especially the colossal Vairocana completed in 675, with Wu among major patrons—made power visible in rock. Imperial bodies and cosmic Buddhas could share a visual language of scale. Prophecy plus stone told audiences that unprecedented rule had sacred cover. One tradition was still not enough: Wu continued to use Confucian symbols such as the Mingtang (Bright Hall) and the ancient Zhou dynastic name.

Purpose. Close the Wu arc: Zhou dynasty 690–705 ended by coalition politics, not cartoon villainy.

Purpose. Open Act III: Xuanzong as patron-regulator after palace crises.

Story. After palace coups that removed Empress Wei and then the Taiping Princess, Xuanzong (Li Longji) held full power from 713 and reigned until 756. His long High Tang court became famous for wealth, poetry, painting, and music—and for religious policy that both funded and policed sacred institutions. Students should keep two verbs together for this reign: patronize and regulate. Xuanzong is not a simple "pro-Buddhist" or "anti-Buddhist" label. He shows patronage as a governing tool that can lavish gifts and still tighten control. Preview Pear Garden arts and the Li Bai / Du Fu contrast; save 845 for the coda.

Why it matters. Moves the thesis forward: culture and religion still make authority visible, still fail to buy permanent security—as Lecture 5's An Lushan crisis will show harshly.

Purpose. Concrete patronage and regulation examples in one reign.

Ennin's diary turns late Tang religion into weather, roads, meals, and police checks.

Dates: in China 838 to 847, including the Huichang years.
Wutai: pilgrimage logistics, patronage networks, and sacred geography in practice.
Value: an outsider's eye on monastic life and state pressure.
Read Reischauer's companion volume beside the diary translation.

← Back to the lecture: pilgrimage and Chan

Sources: Edwin O. Reischauer, Ennin's Travels in T'ang China; Ennin diary translation.

Purpose. Optional Ennin depth: a Japanese monk's diary as weather, roads, meals, and police checks.

Story. Ennin (円仁) traveled in Tang China in the mid-ninth century; Edwin O. Reischauer's translation and study make the diary usable for students. Unlike a polished court edict, Ennin records rain, road conditions, food, lodging, and official inspections. He turns late Tang religion into an itinerary. That human texture helps explain how pilgrimage networks and state regulation felt on the ground—especially as policies tightened toward the Huichang years. Optional depth for students who want experience rather than policy summary alone.

Monastery wealth

In Tang China, large Buddhist houses often held farmland worked by tenants and dependents, ran mills and oil presses, lent money, took pledges, and stored valuables. That economic role made monasteries local institutions of credit and landholding, which is why emperors both endowed them and later targeted them for revenue.

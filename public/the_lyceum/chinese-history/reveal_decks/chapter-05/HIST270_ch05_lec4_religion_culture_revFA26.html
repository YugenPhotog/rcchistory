Tang Rulers Used Religion and Culture to Shape Imperial Authority

Empress Wu and Xuanzong drew on religious traditions, court patronage, and literary culture.

628–629Taizong honors the war dead through Buddhist rites
690Wu declares herself emperor of a new Zhou dynasty
713–756Xuanzong rules during a celebrated cultural era
845The state suppresses much of the Buddhist monastic establishment

The argument

Religious and cultural patronage strengthened rulers, but never removed political tensions.

Buddhism

Monasteries served religious, social, and economic roles across Tang society.

Empress Wu

She combined court ritual, political skill, and Buddhist claims to legitimize female rule.

Xuanzong’s court

Imperial patronage fostered religion, poetry, painting, and music.

This lecture examines how religion and culture became part of Tang political life. Buddhist institutions were woven into everyday society; Wu used both Confucian ritual and Buddhist legitimation; Xuanzong patronized multiple traditions and presided over a celebrated court.

Patronage was not a simple expression of private belief. Rulers used ceremonies, temples, scholarship, and the arts to express authority, while institutions and court politics created tensions that could change over time.

Buddhism in daily life

Buddhist monasteries served communities as well as worshippers.

  • Monasteries taught children and lodged travelers.
  • They hosted officials and scholars, held land, and stored merchants’ wealth.
  • The state also used Buddhist services to pray for protection.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 79.

During the Tang, monasteries operated as more than places for religious practice. They provided schooling and lodging, hosted social gatherings, owned land, and safeguarded merchants’ money and goods. In this way some monasteries functioned like banks and warehouses.

The state also directed every prefecture to establish a Buddhist temple to recite sutras and pray for the protection of the state. These overlapping roles help explain why Buddhism became deeply embedded and why monasteries could attract both patronage and later scrutiny.

Buddhism adapted to Chinese life

Buddhist stories connected religious teaching with familiar family duties.

The story of Mulian

A son journeys to the netherworld to rescue his suffering mother.

  • Monks told and illustrated the story for audiences who could not read.
  • Popular versions linked Buddhist aid for the dead with filial devotion.
  • The story helped shape observances for hungry ghosts.
Buddhist practice could travel across cultures by speaking to values already important in Chinese society.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 80.

The story of Mulian and his mother was popular in Tang China. Monks used pictures and storytelling to reach audiences who could not read. The story presented a son using Buddhist practice to help a deceased mother, joining Buddhist ideas to filial devotion.

The textbook connects popular elaborations of this story with the ghost festival, when Buddhists and non-Buddhists alike offered food for hungry ghosts. This example shows adaptation and exchange without suggesting that Buddhism simply replaced Chinese traditions.

Chinese pilgrimage sites drew visitors from across East Asia.

A sacred geography developed across East Asia during the Tang. Mount Wutai, associated with the bodhisattva Manjusri, attracted pilgrims from India. The Japanese Buddhist monk Ennin lived in China from 838 to 847 and hoped to visit the mountain.

Religious travel was one part of the same larger world of mobility seen in the previous lecture. These journeys linked local communities to a network that extended well beyond the Tang capital.

A Chinese Buddhist tradition

Chan Buddhism developed distinctive ideas about teaching and enlightenment.

  • Chan, known in Japan as Zen, became popular among the educated elite.
  • It emphasized direct transmission of Buddhist insight from mind to mind.
  • Some Chan teachings stressed sudden enlightenment rather than long study of sutras.

Huineng

Traditions about the Sixth Patriarch used his lack of literacy at the time of enlightenment to show that insight did not depend on textual learning alone.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 81.

Buddhism continued to develop in distinctively Chinese directions. The Chan school gained popularity among the educated elite. It rejected the authority of sutras as the sole path to truth and emphasized the transmission of insight from mind to mind.

The story of Huineng, the Sixth Patriarch, stresses that enlightenment could come through sudden insight even to someone who was not literate. Present this as a tradition’s account of its own authority, rather than as a neutral biography.

The 845 suppression

In 845, fiscal pressure helped drive a major attack on Buddhist institutions.

260,000+monks and nuns ordered back to secular life

More than 4,600 monasteries closed
About 40,000 temples and shrines closed

The state targeted Buddhist institutions whose tax-exempt status aggravated fiscal problems.

The ban was lifted after a few years, but the monastic establishment never fully recovered.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 81.

Opposition to Buddhism resurfaced in the late Tang, in large part because monasteries’ tax-exempt status aggravated state fiscal problems. In 845, an emperor ordered more than 4,600 monasteries and about 40,000 temples and shrines closed, and more than 260,000 Buddhist monks and nuns returned to secular life.

The ban was lifted after a few years, but the monastic establishment never fully recovered. Lay Buddhism and basic beliefs such as karma and reincarnation persisted. Emphasize the institutional target and the fiscal pressure; the measure did not erase Buddhism from Chinese life.

Empress Wu

Wu drew on political skill and religious authority to become emperor.

Her rise was unprecedented, but it took place within Tang court politics.

Wu’s rise at court

Wu moved from a lesser consort to the center of Tang power.

651Wu enters Gaozong’s palace as a lesser consort.
Within a few yearsShe replaces the empress and becomes empress herself.
660sAfter Gaozong’s stroke, she increasingly makes decisions for the court.
690She takes the throne as emperor and declares a new Zhou dynasty.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 82–83.

Wu entered Gaozong’s palace in 651 as a lesser consort. Within a few years, Gaozong demoted the sitting empress and elevated Wu. After he suffered a stroke four years later, Wu began making decisions in his place, initially following the convention of ruling from behind a screen.

In 690, she accepted her son’s abdication, declared herself emperor, and established a new Zhou dynasty. She was the only woman in Chinese history to rule as emperor. Keep distinct her earlier role as empress and her later status as sovereign.

Ritual and sovereignty

Wu used the yin-yang relationship to claim a role in state sacrifices.

At Mount Tai in 665, Wu argued that she and her palace women should perform the sacrifice to Earth while the emperor performed the sacrifice to Heaven.

Ritual gave Wu a public language for female political authority.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 83.

In 665, Gaozong and Wu traveled to Mount Tai for the feng and shan sacrifices to Heaven and Earth, rites that had not been performed since the Western Han. Wu argued that the emperor should perform the yang sacrifice to Heaven at the summit, while she and the palace women should perform the yin sacrifice to Earth at the mountain’s base.

The ceremony did not erase the gendered structure of political life. Wu used the complementary yin-yang framework to claim a formal role within it. The diagram represents her stated rationale, not a reconstruction of the actual ritual site.

A new political base

Examinations helped Wu recruit officials outside the old northwestern aristocracy.

The opportunity

Some men who had felt excluded under the early Tang could seek office through civil service examinations.

  • New officials owed their careers to the examination route and the court.
  • Wu gained supporters while the earlier ruling circle had less control over recruitment.
  • Examinations expanded access without ending elite privilege.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 83.

One source of Wu’s political strength was her use of the civil service examinations to bring new people into government. Many of these officials had felt left out when the early Tang court was dominated by the northwestern military aristocracy.

This practice widened recruitment and built a political constituency, but did not make office equally accessible. It also connected Wu’s rise to institutional changes already underway in Tang civil service.

Buddhist legitimation

Wu’s supporters used a Buddhist prophecy to support female sovereignty.

689year a supporter’s commentary identified the prophesied ruler with Wu

The Great Cloud Sutra foretold the reincarnation of Maitreya as a female monarch who would bring an age free of illness, worry, and disaster.

Wu patronized Buddhism while also performing Confucian state rituals.

The prophecy offered a religious argument for a claim that had no precedent in Chinese imperial history.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 83.

Wu was personally drawn to Buddhism and became a major patron of the Longmen cave temples. Her supporters also used the Great Cloud Sutra, which prophesied that Maitreya Buddha would return as a female monarch and inaugurate an age without illness, worry, or disaster. A supporter’s 689 commentary identified that monarch with Wu.

She did not abandon Confucian political language: she performed state rituals and used their traditions to claim authority. Her legitimacy drew on more than one religious and political vocabulary.

Wu’s Zhou dynasty

Wu ruled as emperor from 690 until senior officials forced her abdication in 705.

  • She named her new dynasty Zhou and circulated the Great Cloud Sutra.
  • She ordered a Great Cloud temple in every prefecture.
  • She continued to govern into her eighties.

Political limits

Wu had to manage succession, opposition from Tang princes, and the loyalty of officials and armies.

In 705, high officials pressured her to abdicate when she was ill.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 83–84.

After taking the throne, Wu circulated the Great Cloud Sutra and ordered each prefecture to establish a Great Cloud temple. The new dynasty connected her rule to Buddhist prophecy, while her participation in Confucian rituals continued.

Her reign also involved opposition and difficult succession choices. She retained power into her seventies and abdicated in 705, when she was around eighty and too ill to get out of bed, after senior officials successfully pressured her. Her rise was extraordinary, but it depended on maintaining coalitions inside a male-dominated imperial system.

Xuanzong and court culture

Xuanzong’s reign joined active government with religious and artistic patronage.

The cultural brilliance of the court rested on institutions and choices.

Xuanzong takes power

Xuanzong began as an activist ruler after decades of court struggles.

710Xuanzong enters the palace and defeats Empress Wei’s faction.
712Ruizong abdicates in Xuanzong’s favor.
713Xuanzong begins his reign and curbs monastery power.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84.

After Empress Wu’s abdication, women continued to shape court politics. Xuanzong rose amid conflict involving Empress Wei and the Taiping Princess. He entered the palace in 710 with a small group of followers and defeated Wei’s faction; in 712, his father Ruizong abdicated in his favor.

Xuanzong began his own reign as an activist ruler, including efforts to curb the power of monasteries. This change illustrates that imperial religious policy shifted across rulers and was not a straight line of ever-increasing Buddhist patronage.

Xuanzong and religion

Xuanzong patronized Daoism and Buddhism while seeking state control over monasteries.

Daoism

Xuanzong honored Laozi, whom the Tang ruling family claimed as an ancestor, and wrote a commentary on the Laozi.

Buddhism

He invited Buddhist clerics to court and supported Tantric rituals, including ceremonies for rain and military victory.

Imperial authority

He also curbed monastery power, showing that patronage and regulation could operate together.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84.

Xuanzong supported more than one tradition. Because the Tang imperial house shared the surname Li, court policy elevated Laozi as an ancestral figure; Xuanzong wrote a commentary on the Laozi and established schooling for Daoist-scripture examinations. He also invited Buddhist clergy to court and used Tantric rites for rain and military victory.

At the same time, he curbed monastery power. Avoid framing this as inconsistent: rulers could support religious traditions and regulate their institutions as part of governing.

The imperial court

Xuanzong’s court became a center for painting, music, and poetry.

  • The painter Han Gan worked at court.
  • Li Bai had a brief, celebrated connection to Xuanzong’s court.
  • Imperial patronage brought artists and writers into the political center.

Patronage shaped culture

Literary and artistic achievement depended on court access, education, and networks of support.

Court culture reflected political life as well as individual talent.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84, 86.

The textbook presents Xuanzong’s court as brilliant and notes that it included the horse painter Han Gan and the poet Li Bai. The emperor appreciated poetry, painting, and music and attracted cultural figures to court.

Connect this patronage to the earlier lecture on Chang’an: cultural life flourished within an urban and social world shaped by travel, elite education, and imperial support. Court access was valuable but selective.

The Tang age of poetry

Poetry was both a major art and a skill of the educated elite.

48,000+poems in the Complete Tang Poems

About 2,200 poets

Educated men were expected to recognize earlier verse and compose polished poems at social occasions.

Poetry was also tested in civil service examinations.

The scale of surviving poetry matters, but the chapter also emphasizes the achievement of individual poets.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 86.

The Complete Tang Poems contains more than 48,000 poems by about 2,200 poets, according to the textbook. Poetry was a practical marker of elite education: men aspiring to recognized membership in the educated elite were expected to know earlier lines and compose at social occasions.

Poetry was also part of the examination system. But the chapter distinguishes the large surviving corpus from the exceptional work of a smaller group of poets who raised the art to new levels.

Du Fu and the world beyond court

Du Fu’s career shows that poetic achievement did not guarantee political success.

Repeated examination failure

Although later celebrated as one of China’s greatest poets, Du Fu repeatedly failed the civil service examinations.

  • He spent much of his adult life traveling through China.
  • His poetry reflected political disorder and human suffering.
  • After the An Lushan Rebellion, he abandoned hopes for office and devoted himself to poetry.
His poetry offers a path from literary culture into the lived consequences of political crisis.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 86–87.

Du Fu wanted to follow his grandfather into government service but repeatedly failed the examinations. He spent much of his adult life wandering, and after the An Lushan Rebellion gave up hopes for an official career. His poetry became a record of political upheaval and its human consequences.

This lecture introduces the cultural achievement; the next lecture will examine the rebellion and its effects in detail. Du Fu connects the literary world of the Tang to the people who experienced its political crises.

Synthesis

Tang rulers used religious and cultural institutions to make authority visible.

Institutions

Monasteries served social and economic roles while rulers funded, used, and regulated them.

Legitimacy

Wu combined Buddhist claims, Confucian ritual, and new political alliances.

Cultural achievement

Xuanzong’s court patronized arts, while poetry connected elite learning with political experience.

Summarize the lecture as a story of institutions and choices. Buddhism became deeply embedded in society; Wu drew on religious and ritual traditions to support an unprecedented claim to sovereignty; Xuanzong both patronized and regulated religious institutions while cultivating court arts and poetry.

These developments did not eliminate political conflict or social limits. The next lecture examines the An Lushan Rebellion and the changes it brought to Tang government and society.

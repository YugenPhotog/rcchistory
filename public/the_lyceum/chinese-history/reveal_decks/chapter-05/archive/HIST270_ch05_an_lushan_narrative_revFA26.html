HIST 270 · CHAPTER 5 · NARRATIVE INSERT

The An Lushan
Rebellion

755–763

Court rivalry became civil war.
Victory took eight years.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, pp. 84–85. Narrative framework added from standard histories.

01

In December 755, a general who had been showered with imperial favors turned his army toward the heart of the Tang empire. Within months, he would claim an imperial throne. Within a year, the emperor who had trusted him would be a refugee. Yet capturing the capitals did not win the war, and murdering the rebel leader did not end it.

This is the story of the An Lushan Rebellion, conventionally dated 755 to 763. The Tang had already concentrated enormous military resources on its frontiers. That is our starting condition. The story now follows the people who used those resources, the decisions that changed the campaign, and the civilians who lived through the results.

SETUP · THE ARMY BEFORE THE MARCH

Power entrusted to one commander

≈160,000

Troops assigned across three frontier commands

  • Pinglu, Fanyang, and Hedong formed An’s power base
  • By 751, An held all three commands
  • Jiedushi: military commissioner with regional armies

The Tang court had built this army.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 84. Military and biographical detail added from standard histories.

02

By 751, An Lushan held three frontier commands: Pinglu, Fanyang, and Hedong. Their combined establishment amounted to roughly one hundred sixty thousand troops. That is a measure of the forces assigned to his commands, not an exact count of the men who would march in rebellion. Accounts of the marching army differ.

The emperor Xuanzong had promoted An Lushan because he needed experienced commanders. A jiedushi was a military commissioner entrusted with regional forces. An Lushan brought military experience, personal connections, and knowledge of the multilingual frontier. His Sogdian and Turkic background did not place him outside Tang service. The rebellion would divide an imperial military world in which men of varied origins served on both sides.

I · RIVALRY · 752–755

A favorite without a protector

An Lushan

  • Xuanzong promoted him as a trusted frontier general
  • Yang Guifei adopted him into the imperial family
  • Sources dispute whether ambition or fear drove rebellion

Protection removed · 752/753

  • Chief minister Li Linfu protected An at court
  • Li’s death left An unprotected
  • Successor Yang Guozhong investigated his rival

Court rivalry made obedience look dangerous.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Political and biographical detail added from standard histories.

03

An Lushan, the frontier general at the center of this rebellion, had cultivated the emperor Xuanzong and the emperor’s favored consort, Yang Guifei. She even adopted him as a son in a court ceremony. This was a connection to the ruling household, not evidence for the later stories of a sexual affair. He was a familiar presence at court, not a stranger suddenly appearing at its gates.

The chief minister Li Linfu had helped protect his position. Li’s death, usually placed in 752 in narrative histories, removed that protection. The traditional year ends in early 753 by the Western calendar. His successor, Yang Guozhong, was Yang Guifei’s cousin and An Lushan’s rival. Accusations of treason, investigations of associates, and demands that the general appear at court made their rivalry increasingly dangerous.

The sources disagree about what An Lushan intended. Some portray a long conspiracy driven by ambition. A different reading emphasizes fear that Guozhong would destroy him once imperial protection failed. These possibilities can overlap. A man can fear arrest and also imagine himself an emperor. Our sources were written with knowledge of the rebellion’s outcome; they cannot give us unfiltered access to his thoughts. What they establish is a confrontation in which remaining obedient began to look dangerous, while An Lushan possessed the means to resist.

WINTER 755–756

The army turns south

  • December 755: An marched from Fanyang
  • Veteran troops overwhelmed hurried local resistance
  • January 756: An crossed the Yellow River
  • The successful crossing opened the road to Luoyang

Reported army100,000 / 150,000

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

04

In December 755, An Lushan raised the banner of rebellion at Fanyang, near present-day Beijing. His announced purpose was to punish Yang Guozhong under a supposedly secret imperial order. That claim offered a way to march against the court while presenting the march as service to the emperor. It also gave officials along the route a frightening problem: who could safely decide that the claim was false?

The army moved south across the North China Plain in winter. The older biography reports about one hundred thousand men; later accounts give one hundred fifty thousand. These are reported strengths, not numbers checked against surviving muster rolls. What matters for the campaign is the concentration of experienced soldiers moving against places with little preparation for a war inside the empire.

The Yellow River was the great obstacle on the route. The rebels crossed near Lingchang in January 756 and turned toward Luoyang. A river crossing exposes an army while only part of it is on the far bank; preventing that crossing would have changed the campaign. Here it succeeded, opening the route into Henan. The map compresses a long march into an arrow, but the advance required men, animals, food, and repeated decisions by local officials to resist, flee, or submit. The speed of collapse reflected those decisions as well as battlefield strength.

FEBRUARY

  • An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor of Yan
  • His rival throne turned rebellion into dynastic war

Capturing Luoyang changed the object of the war.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

05

Luoyang was the Tang’s eastern capital, not merely another town on the road. Feng Changqing tried to defend it with an army assembled in haste. Experienced leadership could not instantly give new recruits the cohesion of soldiers who had campaigned together. The rebels defeated the defense and occupied the city in January 756.

For the people inside, a change of ruler meant uncertainty about property, family safety, and the commands that would arrive next. Officials had to choose whether to serve the conqueror, attempt escape, or risk punishment by refusing. Later Tang restoration would make service to Yan dangerous in a different way. A city could change sides more quickly than the lives inside it could recover.

In February, An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor of the Yan dynasty. That step matters more than any claim to know his original intention. Whatever he had meant when he marched, he now claimed the authority to replace the Tang. But the western capital, Chang’an, still lay beyond a formidable barrier. Taking Luoyang had made him an emperor; it had not made him the only emperor.

ACT II · THE GATE · EARLY 756

The road narrows

  • Rebels had crossed the open plains at speed
  • Tong Pass narrowed the road between river and mountains
  • Fortifications let Tang block a larger attacking army
  • Time favored loyalist attacks on the rebel rear

The pass guarded the road to Chang’an

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

06

The route west led toward a narrow passage between the Yellow River and the mountains. Tong Pass guarded the approach to the valley containing Chang’an. This is why the two capitals did not fall together. An army that could sweep across a plain faced a different problem at a fortified choke point.

The defenders did not have to chase down and annihilate An Lushan’s army. They had to keep it from moving through the pass. Meanwhile, loyalist forces elsewhere could attack the rebels’ communications and threaten the territory behind them. Time, which had favored the rapid rebel march, could begin to favor the Tang.

This diagram simplifies the terrain rather than showing an exact battlefield plan. Its essential relationship is the river to the north, mountains to the south, and limited room for movement between them. The next disaster did not happen because those physical advantages vanished. It happened because the court ordered its army to leave them.

Han

  • More than 100,000 defenders held fortified ground
  • Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi threatened the rebel rear
  • Delay forced the rebels to fight on two fronts

Holding the pass was an active strategy.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

07

Geshu Han took command after the court executed Feng Changqing and Gao Xianzhi, the generals who had withdrawn to this defensible position. He therefore inherited more than an army. He inherited a warning about what the court might do to a commander whose decisions it disliked.

He was an experienced frontier general, but illness had weakened him. The sources describe a stroke before he was recalled to service. Still, the strategic logic of holding Tong Pass was strong. A large force, reported at over one hundred thousand men, occupied a position that restricted how the enemy could approach. The number should not tempt us to imagine an equal number of equally capable veterans. These forces had different origins and levels of readiness.

While Geshu Han held the gate, Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi fought farther north. Their operations threatened the rebel rear in Hebei. If the rebels had to turn back to protect their home territory and supply connections, their pressure on the capital’s approaches might weaken. Waiting behind fortifications was therefore not the same thing as doing nothing.

From Chang’an, however, the months of waiting could look like failure, disobedience, or preparation for another revolt. A successful defense depended on the court trusting its commander long enough for the wider strategy to work. That trust was breaking down.

JULY 756

Ordered out. Drawn in. Broken.

  • Yang Guozhong pressed Xuanzong to order an attack
  • Geshu Han advanced despite his objections
  • Cui Qianyou drew the Tang column into narrow terrain
  • Ambush and panic destroyed the army

Defeat exposed Tong Pass and Chang’an

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

08

Yang Guozhong pressed for an offensive. He distrusted the general commanding the army between the rebels and the capital, while reports suggested that the enemy in front of the pass was vulnerable. Xuanzong ordered Geshu Han to advance. The authority to issue that command belonged to the emperor; blaming the minister alone would erase the ruler’s part in the decision.

Geshu Han objected, but eventually obeyed. His army moved east into the constricted corridor near Lingbao, where the rebel commander Cui Qianyou had prepared his position. The advantage of the pass had been that the rebels had to attack on terms chosen by the defenders. Now the Tang army was moving into terrain where its opponent could choose the encounter.

The traditional account describes a weak-looking enemy screen, an ambush, and a Tang force whose length and crowded position made orderly movement difficult. When the attack failed, men trying to retreat pressed against those behind them. The river and the narrow ground that could protect a stationary defense became dangers for an army in flight. The sources report immense losses, including drownings, but those figures cannot be treated as a precise casualty return.

The army’s collapse exposed Tong Pass. Geshu Han was captured, and the barrier protecting Chang’an was lost. He had not been guaranteed victory by waiting; war offers no such guarantee. But the court had surrendered a strong defensive position for an offensive that failed catastrophically. In a matter of days, the problem in Chang’an changed from how to defeat a rebel to how to escape him.

09

Xuanzong fled Chang’an with members of the court and an armed escort. The escape carried the rebellion into the emperor’s immediate household. At Mawei, west of the capital, the escort mutinied. The soldiers killed Yang Guozhong, whom they blamed for the catastrophe. Then they refused to accept the survival of his cousin, Yang Guifei, the emperor’s favored consort.

Their demand forced Xuanzong to choose between protecting her and obtaining the obedience of the soldiers on whom his own escape depended. He ordered her death. The accounts describe her being strangled. The emperor who had raised favorites to extraordinary power could no longer guarantee the life of the woman closest to him.

This episode later became a famous tragic love story. It also became a convenient explanation for political disaster: a ruler bewitched by beauty, a corrupt family, a faithless general. That story gives suffering a memorable shape, but it does not explain the war. Guifei did not command the army that left Tong Pass. Her killing shows who became expendable when armed men demanded a victim.

Mawei therefore belongs in the narrative as a struggle over immediate power. The escort could stop the emperor’s journey. Xuanzong could still issue an order, but the soldiers had determined which order he must give. The imperial party continued toward Sichuan carrying the consequences of that reversal.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

10

The imperial family did not remain together on the road. Xuanzong continued southwest toward Sichuan. His son and crown prince, Li Heng, went northwest toward the military resources around Lingwu. There, in August 756, he took the throne as Emperor Suzong.

It is tempting to compress this into a neat statement that Xuanzong abdicated in Sichuan and handed power to his son. The order of events matters. Suzong first became emperor without waiting for his father’s authorization. Xuanzong subsequently accepted the change and the position of retired emperor while in Sichuan. The Tang now had a living former ruler and a new ruler directing the struggle to recover the capital.

For the campaign, this meant that the dynasty still possessed a center from which resistance could be organized. Chang’an was occupied, but the imperial family, loyal commanders, and armies outside the rebel zone had not disappeared. An Lushan’s conquest of a capital had driven his enemy away without capturing the whole political and military network that sustained him. The Tang could rebuild an army beyond his immediate reach.

CHANG’AN · SPRING 757

The occupied city, at walking pace

“A kingdom smashed,its hills and rivers still here,”

Du Fu · “The View in Spring” · 757

Stephen Owen translation · 1996

Du Fu walked through rebel-occupied Chang’anWar separated him from his family

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Literary context added from Du Fu and scholarship.

11

In the spring of 757, the poet Du Fu was in rebel-held Chang’an, separated from his family. His poem begins, in Stephen Owen’s translation, with a kingdom smashed and the hills and rivers still present. The political order has broken, yet the landscape continues. Spring does not wait for peace.

A campaign map encourages us to see possession: one side holds a city, then the other side does. Du Fu brings us down to the pace of a person living inside it. Familiar places can become frightening. Ordinary news from home can become precious. The uncertainty of whether relatives are alive belongs to the war as surely as a general’s uncertainty about his enemy.

The poem is not a census or an objective survey of every neighborhood. It is a crafted expression of one person’s experience. That is exactly what it contributes here. A capital can remain a place on a map while the relationships that made it home have been broken. The next campaigns will decide who governs Chang’an. They cannot simply rewind the lives of the people who have endured its occupation.

EARLY 757 · INSIDE THE YAN COURT

The rebel armies outlived their founder.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Biographical detail added from standard histories.

12

Earlier that year, while the Tang court rebuilt beyond Chang’an, violence had reached the heart of the Yan court. An Lushan’s eyesight deteriorated into blindness, and he suffered other serious illnesses. The histories describe an increasingly isolated ruler who abused the people attending him. Those accounts come through hostile sources, so they should not become an excuse to diagnose him across thirteen centuries.

His son An Qingxu feared for his position in the succession. A conspiracy brought together the son and men around the emperor. Early in 757, an attendant carried out the killing. Saying that An Lushan was murdered by his son captures political responsibility, but not the physical act: the son organized or joined the plot; a servant killed him.

An Qingxu became emperor of Yan. A story organized around one great traitor ought to end here. This war did not. The rebel armies still occupied territory. Their officers and soldiers still had reasons to fear defeat, and their local resources did not vanish with the ruler’s death. A new claimant could take over the name and machinery of the rebel regime.

The murder also exposed a danger within the rebellion. A commander could defeat imperial armies and still be vulnerable in his own palace. As the war continued, questions of succession and survival would repeatedly turn fathers, sons, and generals against one another.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

13

The contest for the capitals was not the whole war. Farther east, Suiyang stood on the Bian Canal corridor leading toward the Huai and lower Yangzi regions. The Tang needed the resources of the south to continue fighting. A successful rebel advance in that direction could threaten the places and routes that helped keep the dynasty alive.

Zhang Xun, a civil official turned military leader, joined Xu Yuan in defending Suiyang. Their garrison reportedly numbered about six thousand eight hundred after Zhang arrived, facing a much larger enemy. Precise ratios in later accounts are uncertain, so the important comparison is a small garrison using walls against an army capable of surrounding it. The defenders could survive an assault that would have overwhelmed them in the open.

But walls do not produce food. Every successful day of resistance consumed another day’s provisions. The defenders needed either relief from outside or a change in the wider campaign. A besieging army could trade repeated attacks for time and hunger, while the garrison’s strength diminished even when it won local encounters.

Suiyang’s resistance tied down rebel troops for months. Later writers credited the defenders with saving the southeast. That judgment captures the city’s strategic importance, but it should not turn the map into a single locked door through which every possible invasion had to pass. The defense was one important part of containing the rebellion across a broad region.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

14

By the final phase of the siege, Suiyang’s food had run out. The histories report cannibalism, including the killing of people within the city to feed the defense. These are accounts of extreme violence, not a colorful military anecdote. Their enormous victim totals are uncertain, and we cannot turn them into a reliable count.

Later celebration of Zhang Xun made endurance proof of loyalty. That framing directs attention toward the commander’s sacrifice and away from the people whose lives were consumed by the defense. We can recognize the city’s military importance without repeating that choice of emphasis. The inhabitants were not merely the price written below a victory.

Suiyang fell in the autumn of 757, and Zhang Xun was executed. It was a rebel victory at the city itself, yet the long resistance had absorbed effort and time while the Tang assembled its recovery farther west. Those two truths belong together. A garrison can lose its final battle after contributing to a larger campaign, and the suffering inside its walls remains suffering whatever the eventual outcome.

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Alliance detail added from standard histories.

15

Suzong needed forces capable of taking the offensive. The Uighur khaganate, a steppe power north of the Tang empire, could supply experienced cavalry. The Tang entered an alliance, and Uighur horsemen became an important part of the army assembled to recover the capitals. Uighur is the spelling commonly used for this eighth-century polity; Uyghur is another familiar rendering.

This was an agreement between rulers with their own interests. The Uighur ruler did not send his men simply because the Tang emperor commanded him. The bargain involved silk and promises of plunder, as well as a relationship expressed through diplomatic kinship. In 758, after the first recovery of the capitals, Suzong’s daughter Princess Ningguo married the khagan. That marriage consolidated the alliance; it did not precede the 757 campaign.

For a court trying to survive, such terms could be acceptable. For the residents of a city promised as booty, they could be terrifying. A military ally might help defeat an occupier and then become another source of danger. That tension will be visible when the armies reach Luoyang.

The alliance also reminds us why ethnicity cannot explain which side a soldier chose. The Tang relied on a multiethnic coalition, just as the rebellion had drawn on the mixed military society of the frontier. Political commitments and immediate bargains shaped the opposing armies.

LATE 757

Two capitals recovered

  • Guo Ziyi and the Prince of Guangping led the counteroffensive
  • Tang-Uighur forces recovered Chang’an, then Luoyang
  • Uighur troops plundered Luoyang after its recovery
  • An Qingxu escaped north with a surviving rebel army

Recovery drove Yan forces toward Ye

Source: Textbook, ch. 5, p. 85. Campaign detail added from standard histories.

16

Late in 757, the loyalist counteroffensive finally succeeded. Guo Ziyi and the Prince of Guangping led the Tang forces, joined by their Uighur allies. The campaign required defeating the rebel field army outside Chang’an, not simply arriving at the city and announcing that the rightful dynasty had returned. Infantry had to hold under pressure while cavalry threatened the enemy’s position and retreat.

After Chang’an was recovered, the coalition moved east and regained Luoyang. An Qingxu withdrew toward Ye in southern Hebei. This was a major reversal: the rebels had lost both imperial capitals, while Suzong’s government could reoccupy its central seat of rule. It was reasonable to hope that the war was nearing its end.

But the alliance’s cost now became immediate. Uighur troops plundered Luoyang, and the restoration brought new suffering to the eastern capital. Winning for the Tang did not guarantee protection for every Tang subject. Nor were abuses confined to one ethnic group; armies on both sides extracted resources and harmed civilians.

The retreat to Ye also preserved a rebel army beyond the recovered capitals. A map with Chang’an and Luoyang colored Tang again would look reassuring. The survival of organized forces to the northeast made that reassurance premature. The war had entered a new phase, not reached its conclusion.

17

The easiest version of this story would end with the return to Chang’an. The emperor has come home, the rebel founder is dead, and the eastern capital has been recovered. Yet the rebellion still has years to run.

The reason is visible in what the counteroffensive had not accomplished. It had not destroyed every rebel force or taken every base in Hebei. It had not made all commanders trust one another or made surrender safe for every participant. Armies could survive the loss of a capital, reorganize under another leader, and resume the offensive.

The next part of the story follows that unfinished military problem. It explains why the war conventionally spans eight years even though the most spectacular rebel advances happened near its beginning.

18

An Qingxu’s retreat brought him to Ye, in southern Hebei. Tang armies converged on the city in a campaign intended to finish off the remaining rebel regime. A siege looked like a way to turn the recovery of the capitals into a final victory.

But several armies gathered around a city do not automatically become one army. Command relationships, supplies, and the ability to meet a relieving force all matter. The Tang coalition was vulnerable to those problems. Its opponent outside the siege was Shi Siming, one of An Lushan’s most capable generals.

Shi had briefly submitted to the Tang after the recovery of the capitals. That did not produce lasting trust. He returned to rebellion and intervened against the forces besieging An Qingxu. In 759, the Tang siege collapsed. The attempt to close the war had instead given the rebellion room to survive.

Shi then had An Qingxu killed and took the Yan throne himself. Relief had not been a gift to the young emperor. It had placed the stronger general in a position to remove him. The rebellion now had a leader who combined an imperial claim with an experienced military following. This was not An Lushan’s son continuing exactly where his father had stopped. It was a renewed war under a commander who had already shifted sides and could exploit the failure of the Tang siege.

19

Shi Siming retook Luoyang in 759. The eastern capital’s recovery had lasted only a short time before the war returned. The same city could be liberated, plundered, reoccupied, and forced to accommodate another ruler. For inhabitants, a campaign’s apparent conclusion could prove to be only an interval.

The Tang continued to resist, and Chang’an did not fall to this renewed Yan offensive. The struggle became a prolonged contest in which taking one objective did not remove the enemy’s ability to fight elsewhere. Shi’s military skill helped revive the rebellion, but he could not simply repeat An Lushan’s first rapid advance.

In 761, his own son Shi Chaoyi participated in the conspiracy that killed him and became the last Yan emperor. A second father had been overthrown by a son. That parallel is striking, but it should not be mistaken for a complete explanation. Each killing changed who claimed the throne while leaving armies, territory, and survival calculations in place. The rebellion could outlive another ruler, though the divisions within it were making a final coordinated resistance more difficult.

20

The Tang also changed emperors. Suzong died in 762, and his son succeeded as Emperor Daizong. The dynasty had now fought this rebellion under three rulers: Xuanzong, Suzong, and Daizong. The continuity lay in the surviving claim of the ruling house and the armies still willing to fight for it.

A renewed Tang offensive, again supported by Uighur forces, recovered Luoyang in 762. The eastern capital suffered another destructive sack. Shi Chaoyi retreated northeast, but retreat only helps if the next commander opens the gates, provides supplies, and accepts your authority. Increasingly, rebel commanders chose their own survival over his cause.

Some surrendered with their forces instead of sharing the last emperor’s fate. Early in 763, Shi Chaoyi died by suicide, and the Yan imperial claim collapsed. The Tang had won, but the end was not a single battle in which every rebel soldier was killed or captured. The coalition around Yan broke apart under military pressure and the possibility of accommodation.

The arrangements made with surrendering commanders belong to the larger history of Tang government. For this narrative, the essential point is more immediate: the last rebel emperor ran out of dependable followers and places to go. That is how a rebellion which had survived the deaths of three rulers finally lost its fourth.

21

The scale of suffering is beyond doubt. The size of the death toll is not. The often repeated figures of roughly fifty-three million before the rebellion and seventeen million afterward refer to people in government registers. The comparison here uses 755 and 765, not two complete counts of everyone alive on the first and last days of the war.

Subtracting those figures does not produce thirty-six million deaths. People could disappear from registration because they fled, because records were destroyed, or because officials could no longer count and tax them. Real mortality was substantial, but scholarship rejects that subtraction as a measure of it. Its actual level remains unknown and is generally understood to be far below the simple difference.

This is the place where a compressed textbook statement can mislead. If the textbook’s population decline is read as a count of the dead, the standard historical interpretation requires correction. The distinction does not make the catastrophe less real. It keeps an administrative failure from becoming a falsely precise account of human death.

The narrative begins with a general whose powerful position had been created within Tang service. Court rivalry made that position dangerous. His march against Yang Guozhong became an open bid for the throne after the fall of Luoyang. At Tong Pass, a defensible barrier held until an imperial order sent the army into disaster. Xuanzong fled, Yang Guifei died at Mawei, and Suzong established a new center of resistance.

The war then spread across several stories at once: a rebel ruler murdered inside his palace, a poet separated from his family in Chang’an, a garrison starving at Suiyang, and a court bargaining for the cavalry that could help it return. Recovering the capitals did not destroy the rebellion. Shi Siming revived it, another son seized another throne, and only the last retreat and the surrender of commanders ended the Yan claim.

Eight years is the conventional span; from late 755 to early 763 is a little over seven elapsed years. What made the war long was the survival of armies and resources after dramatic defeats. The Tang survived. The deaths, separations, and destroyed homes could not be undone by its victory.

The An Lushan Rebellion

HIST 270 · 755–763 · Presenter narratives

01 · The An Lushan
Rebellion

HIST 270 · CHAPTER 5 · NARRATIVE INSERT · 1 minutes

In December 755, a general who had been showered with imperial favors turned his army toward the heart of the Tang empire. Within months, he would claim an imperial throne. Within a year, the emperor who had trusted him would be a refugee. Yet capturing the capitals did not win the war, and murdering the rebel leader did not end it.

This is the story of the An Lushan Rebellion, conventionally dated 755 to 763. The Tang had already concentrated enormous military resources on its frontiers. That is our starting condition. The story now follows the people who used those resources, the decisions that changed the campaign, and the civilians who lived through the results.

02 · Power entrusted to one commander

SETUP · THE ARMY BEFORE THE MARCH · 1 minutes

By 751, An Lushan held three frontier commands: Pinglu, Fanyang, and Hedong. Their combined establishment amounted to roughly one hundred sixty thousand troops. That is a measure of the forces assigned to his commands, not an exact count of the men who would march in rebellion. Accounts of the marching army differ.

The emperor Xuanzong had promoted An Lushan because he needed experienced commanders. A jiedushi was a military commissioner entrusted with regional forces. An Lushan brought military experience, personal connections, and knowledge of the multilingual frontier. His Sogdian and Turkic background did not place him outside Tang service. The rebellion would divide an imperial military world in which men of varied origins served on both sides.

03 · A favorite without a protector

I · RIVALRY · 752–755 · 2 minutes

An Lushan, the frontier general at the center of this rebellion, had cultivated the emperor Xuanzong and the emperor’s favored consort, Yang Guifei. She even adopted him as a son in a court ceremony. This was a connection to the ruling household, not evidence for the later stories of a sexual affair. He was a familiar presence at court, not a stranger suddenly appearing at its gates.

The chief minister Li Linfu had helped protect his position. Li’s death, usually placed in 752 in narrative histories, removed that protection. The traditional year ends in early 753 by the Western calendar. His successor, Yang Guozhong, was Yang Guifei’s cousin and An Lushan’s rival. Accusations of treason, investigations of associates, and demands that the general appear at court made their rivalry increasingly dangerous.

The sources disagree about what An Lushan intended. Some portray a long conspiracy driven by ambition. A different reading emphasizes fear that Guozhong would destroy him once imperial protection failed. These possibilities can overlap. A man can fear arrest and also imagine himself an emperor. Our sources were written with knowledge of the rebellion’s outcome; they cannot give us unfiltered access to his thoughts. What they establish is a confrontation in which remaining obedient began to look dangerous, while An Lushan possessed the means to resist.

04 · The army turns south

WINTER 755–756 · 2 minutes

In December 755, An Lushan raised the banner of rebellion at Fanyang, near present-day Beijing. His announced purpose was to punish Yang Guozhong under a supposedly secret imperial order. That claim offered a way to march against the court while presenting the march as service to the emperor. It also gave officials along the route a frightening problem: who could safely decide that the claim was false?

The army moved south across the North China Plain in winter. The older biography reports about one hundred thousand men; later accounts give one hundred fifty thousand. These are reported strengths, not numbers checked against surviving muster rolls. What matters for the campaign is the concentration of experienced soldiers moving against places with little preparation for a war inside the empire.

The Yellow River was the great obstacle on the route. The rebels crossed near Lingchang in January 756 and turned toward Luoyang. A river crossing exposes an army while only part of it is on the far bank; preventing that crossing would have changed the campaign. Here it succeeded, opening the route into Henan. The map compresses a long march into an arrow, but the advance required men, animals, food, and repeated decisions by local officials to resist, flee, or submit. The speed of collapse reflected those decisions as well as battlefield strength.

05 · From minister-hunter to emperor

JANUARY–FEBRUARY 756 · 1.5 minutes

Luoyang was the Tang’s eastern capital, not merely another town on the road. Feng Changqing tried to defend it with an army assembled in haste. Experienced leadership could not instantly give new recruits the cohesion of soldiers who had campaigned together. The rebels defeated the defense and occupied the city in January 756.

For the people inside, a change of ruler meant uncertainty about property, family safety, and the commands that would arrive next. Officials had to choose whether to serve the conqueror, attempt escape, or risk punishment by refusing. Later Tang restoration would make service to Yan dangerous in a different way. A city could change sides more quickly than the lives inside it could recover.

In February, An Lushan proclaimed himself emperor of the Yan dynasty. That step matters more than any claim to know his original intention. Whatever he had meant when he marched, he now claimed the authority to replace the Tang. But the western capital, Chang’an, still lay beyond a formidable barrier. Taking Luoyang had made him an emperor; it had not made him the only emperor.

06 · The road narrows

ACT II · THE GATE · EARLY 756 · 1.5 minutes

The route west led toward a narrow passage between the Yellow River and the mountains. Tong Pass guarded the approach to the valley containing Chang’an. This is why the two capitals did not fall together. An army that could sweep across a plain faced a different problem at a fortified choke point.

The defenders did not have to chase down and annihilate An Lushan’s army. They had to keep it from moving through the pass. Meanwhile, loyalist forces elsewhere could attack the rebels’ communications and threaten the territory behind them. Time, which had favored the rapid rebel march, could begin to favor the Tang.

07 · Geshu Han’s strongest move: wait

TONG PASS · EARLY–MID 756 · 2 minutes

Geshu Han took command after the court executed Feng Changqing and Gao Xianzhi, the generals who had withdrawn to this defensible position. He therefore inherited more than an army. He inherited a warning about what the court might do to a commander whose decisions it disliked.

He was an experienced frontier general, but illness had weakened him. The sources describe a stroke before he was recalled to service. Still, the strategic logic of holding Tong Pass was strong. A large force, reported at over one hundred thousand men, occupied a position that restricted how the enemy could approach. The number should not tempt us to imagine an equal number of equally capable veterans. These forces had different origins and levels of readiness.

While Geshu Han held the gate, Guo Ziyi and Li Guangbi fought farther north. Their operations threatened the rebel rear in Hebei. If the rebels had to turn back to protect their home territory and supply connections, their pressure on the capital’s approaches might weaken. Waiting behind fortifications was therefore not the same thing as doing nothing.

From Chang’an, however, the months of waiting could look like failure, disobedience, or preparation for another revolt. A successful defense depended on the court trusting its commander long enough for the wider strategy to work. That trust was breaking down.

08 · Ordered out. Drawn in. Broken.

JULY 756 · 2.5 minutes

Yang Guozhong pressed for an offensive. He distrusted the general commanding the army between the rebels and the capital, while reports suggested that the enemy in front of the pass was vulnerable. Xuanzong ordered Geshu Han to advance. The authority to issue that command belonged to the emperor; blaming the minister alone would erase the ruler’s part in the decision.

Geshu Han objected, but eventually obeyed. His army moved east into the constricted corridor near Lingbao, where the rebel commander Cui Qianyou had prepared his position. The advantage of the pass had been that the rebels had to attack on terms chosen by the defenders. Now the Tang army was moving into terrain where its opponent could choose the encounter.

The traditional account describes a weak-looking enemy screen, an ambush, and a Tang force whose length and crowded position made orderly movement difficult. When the attack failed, men trying to retreat pressed against those behind them. The river and the narrow ground that could protect a stationary defense became dangers for an army in flight. The sources report immense losses, including drownings, but those figures cannot be treated as a precise casualty return.

The army’s collapse exposed Tong Pass. Geshu Han was captured, and the barrier protecting Chang’an was lost. He had not been guaranteed victory by waiting; war offers no such guarantee. But the court had surrendered a strong defensive position for an offensive that failed catastrophically. In a matter of days, the problem in Chang’an changed from how to defeat a rebel to how to escape him.

09 · At Mawei, the escort decides

JULY 756 · THE FLIGHT FROM CHANG’AN · 2 minutes

Xuanzong fled Chang’an with members of the court and an armed escort. The escape carried the rebellion into the emperor’s immediate household. At Mawei, west of the capital, the escort mutinied. The soldiers killed Yang Guozhong, whom they blamed for the catastrophe. Then they refused to accept the survival of his cousin, Yang Guifei, the emperor’s favored consort.

Their demand forced Xuanzong to choose between protecting her and obtaining the obedience of the soldiers on whom his own escape depended. He ordered her death. The accounts describe her being strangled. The emperor who had raised favorites to extraordinary power could no longer guarantee the life of the woman closest to him.

This episode later became a famous tragic love story. It also became a convenient explanation for political disaster: a ruler bewitched by beauty, a corrupt family, a faithless general. That story gives suffering a memorable shape, but it does not explain the war. Guifei did not command the army that left Tong Pass. Her killing shows who became expendable when armed men demanded a victim.

Mawei therefore belongs in the narrative as a struggle over immediate power. The escort could stop the emperor’s journey. Xuanzong could still issue an order, but the soldiers had determined which order he must give. The imperial party continued toward Sichuan carrying the consequences of that reversal.

10 · A father in flight; a son on the throne

SUMMER–AUTUMN 756 · 1.5 minutes

The imperial family did not remain together on the road. Xuanzong continued southwest toward Sichuan. His son and crown prince, Li Heng, went northwest toward the military resources around Lingwu. There, in August 756, he took the throne as Emperor Suzong.

It is tempting to compress this into a neat statement that Xuanzong abdicated in Sichuan and handed power to his son. The order of events matters. Suzong first became emperor without waiting for his father’s authorization. Xuanzong subsequently accepted the change and the position of retired emperor while in Sichuan. The Tang now had a living former ruler and a new ruler directing the struggle to recover the capital.

For the campaign, this meant that the dynasty still possessed a center from which resistance could be organized. Chang’an was occupied, but the imperial family, loyal commanders, and armies outside the rebel zone had not disappeared. An Lushan’s conquest of a capital had driven his enemy away without capturing the whole political and military network that sustained him. The Tang could rebuild an army beyond his immediate reach.

11 · The occupied city, at walking pace

CHANG’AN · SPRING 757 · 1.5 minutes

In the spring of 757, the poet Du Fu was in rebel-held Chang’an, separated from his family. His poem begins, in Stephen Owen’s translation, with a kingdom smashed and the hills and rivers still present. The political order has broken, yet the landscape continues. Spring does not wait for peace.

A campaign map encourages us to see possession: one side holds a city, then the other side does. Du Fu brings us down to the pace of a person living inside it. Familiar places can become frightening. Ordinary news from home can become precious. The uncertainty of whether relatives are alive belongs to the war as surely as a general’s uncertainty about his enemy.

The poem is not a census or an objective survey of every neighborhood. It is a crafted expression of one person’s experience. That is exactly what it contributes here. A capital can remain a place on a map while the relationships that made it home have been broken. The next campaigns will decide who governs Chang’an. They cannot simply rewind the lives of the people who have endured its occupation.

12 · The founder dies; the war survives

EARLY 757 · INSIDE THE YAN COURT · 2 minutes

Earlier that year, while the Tang court rebuilt beyond Chang’an, violence had reached the heart of the Yan court. An Lushan’s eyesight deteriorated into blindness, and he suffered other serious illnesses. The histories describe an increasingly isolated ruler who abused the people attending him. Those accounts come through hostile sources, so they should not become an excuse to diagnose him across thirteen centuries.

His son An Qingxu feared for his position in the succession. A conspiracy brought together the son and men around the emperor. Early in 757, an attendant carried out the killing. Saying that An Lushan was murdered by his son captures political responsibility, but not the physical act: the son organized or joined the plot; a servant killed him.

An Qingxu became emperor of Yan. A story organized around one great traitor ought to end here. This war did not. The rebel armies still occupied territory. Their officers and soldiers still had reasons to fear defeat, and their local resources did not vanish with the ruler’s death. A new claimant could take over the name and machinery of the rebel regime.

The murder also exposed a danger within the rebellion. A commander could defeat imperial armies and still be vulnerable in his own palace. As the war continued, questions of succession and survival would repeatedly turn fathers, sons, and generals against one another.

13 · Suiyang holds the southeastern road

757 · ANOTHER FRONT · 2 minutes

The contest for the capitals was not the whole war. Farther east, Suiyang stood on the Bian Canal corridor leading toward the Huai and lower Yangzi regions. The Tang needed the resources of the south to continue fighting. A successful rebel advance in that direction could threaten the places and routes that helped keep the dynasty alive.

Zhang Xun, a civil official turned military leader, joined Xu Yuan in defending Suiyang. Their garrison reportedly numbered about six thousand eight hundred after Zhang arrived, facing a much larger enemy. Precise ratios in later accounts are uncertain, so the important comparison is a small garrison using walls against an army capable of surrounding it. The defenders could survive an assault that would have overwhelmed them in the open.

But walls do not produce food. Every successful day of resistance consumed another day’s provisions. The defenders needed either relief from outside or a change in the wider campaign. A besieging army could trade repeated attacks for time and hunger, while the garrison’s strength diminished even when it won local encounters.

Suiyang’s resistance tied down rebel troops for months. Later writers credited the defenders with saving the southeast. That judgment captures the city’s strategic importance, but it should not turn the map into a single locked door through which every possible invasion had to pass. The defense was one important part of containing the rebellion across a broad region.

14 · The price inside the walls

SUIYANG · AUTUMN 757 · 1.5 minutes

By the final phase of the siege, Suiyang’s food had run out. The histories report cannibalism, including the killing of people within the city to feed the defense. These are accounts of extreme violence, not a colorful military anecdote. Their enormous victim totals are uncertain, and we cannot turn them into a reliable count.

Later celebration of Zhang Xun made endurance proof of loyalty. That framing directs attention toward the commander’s sacrifice and away from the people whose lives were consumed by the defense. We can recognize the city’s military importance without repeating that choice of emphasis. The inhabitants were not merely the price written below a victory.

Suiyang fell in the autumn of 757, and Zhang Xun was executed. It was a rebel victory at the city itself, yet the long resistance had absorbed effort and time while the Tang assembled its recovery farther west. Those two truths belong together. A garrison can lose its final battle after contributing to a larger campaign, and the suffering inside its walls remains suffering whatever the eventual outcome.

15 · The Uighur bargain

756–758 · ALLIANCE · 2 minutes

Suzong needed forces capable of taking the offensive. The Uighur khaganate, a steppe power north of the Tang empire, could supply experienced cavalry. The Tang entered an alliance, and Uighur horsemen became an important part of the army assembled to recover the capitals. Uighur is the spelling commonly used for this eighth-century polity; Uyghur is another familiar rendering.

This was an agreement between rulers with their own interests. The Uighur ruler did not send his men simply because the Tang emperor commanded him. The bargain involved silk and promises of plunder, as well as a relationship expressed through diplomatic kinship. In 758, after the first recovery of the capitals, Suzong’s daughter Princess Ningguo married the khagan. That marriage consolidated the alliance; it did not precede the 757 campaign.

For a court trying to survive, such terms could be acceptable. For the residents of a city promised as booty, they could be terrifying. A military ally might help defeat an occupier and then become another source of danger. That tension will be visible when the armies reach Luoyang.

The alliance also reminds us why ethnicity cannot explain which side a soldier chose. The Tang relied on a multiethnic coalition, just as the rebellion had drawn on the mixed military society of the frontier. Political commitments and immediate bargains shaped the opposing armies.

16 · Two capitals recovered

LATE 757 · 2 minutes

Late in 757, the loyalist counteroffensive finally succeeded. Guo Ziyi and the Prince of Guangping led the Tang forces, joined by their Uighur allies. The campaign required defeating the rebel field army outside Chang’an, not simply arriving at the city and announcing that the rightful dynasty had returned. Infantry had to hold under pressure while cavalry threatened the enemy’s position and retreat.

After Chang’an was recovered, the coalition moved east and regained Luoyang. An Qingxu withdrew toward Ye in southern Hebei. This was a major reversal: the rebels had lost both imperial capitals, while Suzong’s government could reoccupy its central seat of rule. It was reasonable to hope that the war was nearing its end.

But the alliance’s cost now became immediate. Uighur troops plundered Luoyang, and the restoration brought new suffering to the eastern capital. Winning for the Tang did not guarantee protection for every Tang subject. Nor were abuses confined to one ethnic group; armies on both sides extracted resources and harmed civilians.

The retreat to Ye also preserved a rebel army beyond the recovered capitals. A map with Chang’an and Luoyang colored Tang again would look reassuring. The survival of organized forces to the northeast made that reassurance premature. The war had entered a new phase, not reached its conclusion.

17 · The capitals were not the whole war

III · LONG WAR · 757–763 · 1 minutes

The easiest version of this story would end with the return to Chang’an. The emperor has come home, the rebel founder is dead, and the eastern capital has been recovered. Yet the rebellion still has years to run.

The reason is visible in what the counteroffensive had not accomplished. It had not destroyed every rebel force or taken every base in Hebei. It had not made all commanders trust one another or made surrender safe for every participant. Armies could survive the loss of a capital, reorganize under another leader, and resume the offensive.

The next part of the story follows that unfinished military problem. It explains why the war conventionally spans eight years even though the most spectacular rebel advances happened near its beginning.

18 · A siege that failed to end Yan

YE · 758–759 · 2 minutes

An Qingxu’s retreat brought him to Ye, in southern Hebei. Tang armies converged on the city in a campaign intended to finish off the remaining rebel regime. A siege looked like a way to turn the recovery of the capitals into a final victory.

But several armies gathered around a city do not automatically become one army. Command relationships, supplies, and the ability to meet a relieving force all matter. The Tang coalition was vulnerable to those problems. Its opponent outside the siege was Shi Siming, one of An Lushan’s most capable generals.

Shi had briefly submitted to the Tang after the recovery of the capitals. That did not produce lasting trust. He returned to rebellion and intervened against the forces besieging An Qingxu. In 759, the Tang siege collapsed. The attempt to close the war had instead given the rebellion room to survive.

Shi then had An Qingxu killed and took the Yan throne himself. Relief had not been a gift to the young emperor. It had placed the stronger general in a position to remove him. The rebellion now had a leader who combined an imperial claim with an experienced military following. This was not An Lushan’s son continuing exactly where his father had stopped. It was a renewed war under a commander who had already shifted sides and could exploit the failure of the Tang siege.

19 · Shi Siming turns the war back

759–761 · 1.5 minutes

Shi Siming retook Luoyang in 759. The eastern capital’s recovery had lasted only a short time before the war returned. The same city could be liberated, plundered, reoccupied, and forced to accommodate another ruler. For inhabitants, a campaign’s apparent conclusion could prove to be only an interval.

The Tang continued to resist, and Chang’an did not fall to this renewed Yan offensive. The struggle became a prolonged contest in which taking one objective did not remove the enemy’s ability to fight elsewhere. Shi’s military skill helped revive the rebellion, but he could not simply repeat An Lushan’s first rapid advance.

In 761, his own son Shi Chaoyi participated in the conspiracy that killed him and became the last Yan emperor. A second father had been overthrown by a son. That parallel is striking, but it should not be mistaken for a complete explanation. Each killing changed who claimed the throne while leaving armies, territory, and survival calculations in place. The rebellion could outlive another ruler, though the divisions within it were making a final coordinated resistance more difficult.

20 · The last retreat

762–763 · 2 minutes

The Tang also changed emperors. Suzong died in 762, and his son succeeded as Emperor Daizong. The dynasty had now fought this rebellion under three rulers: Xuanzong, Suzong, and Daizong. The continuity lay in the surviving claim of the ruling house and the armies still willing to fight for it.

A renewed Tang offensive, again supported by Uighur forces, recovered Luoyang in 762. The eastern capital suffered another destructive sack. Shi Chaoyi retreated northeast, but retreat only helps if the next commander opens the gates, provides supplies, and accepts your authority. Increasingly, rebel commanders chose their own survival over his cause.

Some surrendered with their forces instead of sharing the last emperor’s fate. Early in 763, Shi Chaoyi died by suicide, and the Yan imperial claim collapsed. The Tang had won, but the end was not a single battle in which every rebel soldier was killed or captured. The coalition around Yan broke apart under military pressure and the possibility of accommodation.

The arrangements made with surrendering commanders belong to the larger history of Tang government. For this narrative, the essential point is more immediate: the last rebel emperor ran out of dependable followers and places to go. That is how a rebellion which had survived the deaths of three rulers finally lost its fourth.

21 · Missing from the registers

REGISTERS · 755–765 · 1.5 minutes

The scale of suffering is beyond doubt. The size of the death toll is not. The often repeated figures of roughly fifty-three million before the rebellion and seventeen million afterward refer to people in government registers. The comparison here uses 755 and 765, not two complete counts of everyone alive on the first and last days of the war.

Subtracting those figures does not produce thirty-six million deaths. People could disappear from registration because they fled, because records were destroyed, or because officials could no longer count and tax them. Real mortality was substantial, but scholarship rejects that subtraction as a measure of it. Its actual level remains unknown and is generally understood to be far below the simple difference.

This is the place where a compressed textbook statement can mislead. If the textbook’s population decline is read as a count of the dead, the standard historical interpretation requires correction. The distinction does not make the catastrophe less real. It keeps an administrative failure from becoming a falsely precise account of human death.

22 · An army’s march; an empire’s ordeal

THE WAR · 755–763 · 1.5 minutes

The narrative begins with a general whose powerful position had been created within Tang service. Court rivalry made that position dangerous. His march against Yang Guozhong became an open bid for the throne after the fall of Luoyang. At Tong Pass, a defensible barrier held until an imperial order sent the army into disaster. Xuanzong fled, Yang Guifei died at Mawei, and Suzong established a new center of resistance.

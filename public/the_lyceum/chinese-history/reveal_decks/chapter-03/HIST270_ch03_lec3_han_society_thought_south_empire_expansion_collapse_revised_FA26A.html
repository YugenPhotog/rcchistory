📖 Lecture Overview

Chapter: The Founding of the Bureaucratic Empire: Qin-Han China (256 BCE–200 CE) Review Questions Covered: Q7, Q8, Q9, Q10, Q12 Textbook: Ebrey, A History of China , Ch. 3, pp. 45–54 IRM: Ebrey IRM3e Ch. 03 | Note: Q11 (Silk Road) covered in the independent-study deck

Review Questions Covered

Q7 - How did cosmological theories and religious beliefs evolve during the Han Dynasty to explain the relationship between the emperor, humanity, and the cosmos?
Q8 - What methodological and literary innovations did Sima Qian introduce in his Records of the Grand Historian , and what were his primary motivations for completing this massive work?
Q9 - Describe the evolution of the social hierarchy during the Han Dynasty. How did wealth, landownership, and political access define the new elite classes after the eradication of the Zhou nobility?
Q10 - Discuss the structure of the Chinese family during the Han period. How did foundational texts like the Classic of Filial Piety and Ban Zhao's Admonitions for Women shape social and gender roles?
Q12 - How did Chinese migration and military expansion impact the southern region of Nam Viet (modern Vietnam), and how did the indigenous populations respond to Chinese overlordship?

Where We’re Going Today

  • How did Han people explain Heaven, humanity, and the emperor?
  • Why did Sima Qian change how Chinese history was written?
  • How did status, family, and farming shape Han society?
  • What happened when Han institutions and migrants moved south?
  • Why was the Later Han system becoming harder to control?
Larger question: What kind of society grew inside the Han imperial system, and why did that system become vulnerable?

Roadmap

Move from ideas, to history-writing, to farmers and elites, to family life, and finally to southern expansion and resistance.

Part 1

Many Ways to Explain the World

Han intellectual life was a synthesis, not a single orthodoxy.

Many Ways to Explain the World

Court and politics

  • Confucian classics
  • Imperial rituals

Cosmology

  • Yin and yang
  • Five Phases

Popular religion

  • Spirits and omens
  • Immortality traditions
Han intellectual life was a mixture of traditions and practices.

Intellectual Diversity

“Han = Confucian” is insufficient. Huang-Lao combined Daoist ideas, statecraft, cosmology, and imperial rule. Emperor Wu supported Confucian scholars while also pursuing rituals and immortality traditions.

Han Religion Was Not Simply Confucian

  • Huang-Lao linked traditions surrounding Laozi and the Yellow Emperor.
  • Emperor Wu pursued rituals, immortality, omens, and supernatural powers.
  • Court ritualists connected cosmic forces to imperial government.
  • Popular religion included spirits, divination, omens, and local practices.
Confucian learning became important to government without replacing the wider religious world of Han China.

Teaching Point

Students may assume that because the Han government increasingly supported Confucian learning, Han religion became simply “Confucian.” Correct that impression.

Huang-Lao traditions associated Laozi and the Yellow Emperor with political and religious ideas. Emperor Wu supported Confucian scholars while also showing intense interest in rituals, omens, immortality, supernatural powers, and cosmic forces.

The larger point is that Han intellectual and religious culture was plural. Confucian learning became increasingly important within government without eliminating Daoist traditions, popular religion, divination, spirits, or other cosmological beliefs.

This slide prepares students for the next discussion of yin-yang, the Five Phases, and the relationship between cosmic order and imperial government.

Heaven, Earth, Humanity, and the Emperor

  • Yin and yang described complementary forces
  • The Five Phases described cycles of change
  • Nature, government, and behavior could reflect one another
  • Some thinkers read disasters as warnings about imperial government
The emperor had a special responsibility to keep government in harmony with Heaven and Earth.

Cosmology could explain political order.

Q7

Dong Zhongshu helped combine Confucian moral concerns with broader cosmological thinking. Present this as the view of particular thinkers, not universal Han belief.

Not Everyone Believed It

  • Wang Chong (27–ca. 100 CE) criticized popular explanations
  • Questioned ghosts, immortality, omens, and lucky days
  • Asked whether disasters could be blamed on choosing an inauspicious moment
  • Used ordinary evidence and logical criticism against superstition
Han thinkers disagreed about how the universe worked.
Book culture was changing too: By the end of the Han, improved paper made written texts easier to produce, store, and handle than books made from bamboo or wooden strips.

Evidence of Debate

Wang Chong shows that Han intellectual culture included skepticism and disagreement, not only official belief.

The textbook places the development of paper within its discussion of Han intellectual and literary culture. Various plant fibers were experimented with during the Han, and by the end of the dynasty paper with a useful writing surface was being produced. Paper made written works much less cumbersome than texts written on bamboo or wooden strips.

Important qualification: do not imply that Sima Qian wrote the Records of the Grand Historian on mature paper technology. The callout is about the broader development of Han book culture, especially toward the end of the dynasty.

Part 2

Investigating the Past

How did Han historians turn memory into evidence?

Sima Tan, Sima Qian, and a History of China

  • Sima Tan began a comprehensive history; Sima Qian continued it
  • The Records of the Grand Historian used annals, tables, treatises, houses, and biographies
  • It included officials, generals, thinkers, merchants, rebels, and foreign peoples
  • Sima Qian investigated success, failure, rise, fall, and change across time
History became an investigation of why people, states, and dynasties rise and fall.

Q8

The family project matters: Sima Tan began it, and Sima Qian expanded it. Do not reduce the work to moral judgment alone.

How Sima Qian Investigated the Past

  • Compared documents, archives, artifacts, and competing accounts
  • Visited historical sites and questioned people
  • Examined causes, not only dates
  • Defended a surrendered general, endured castration, and survived to finish the work
Evidence, breadth, and comparison made historical change explainable.

Research, comparison, and historical judgment.

Qualification

His personal experience sharpened the stakes of judgment, but do not portray him as detached from his age. The textbook emphasizes sources, travel, multiple perspectives, and causation.

Midpoint: From Ideas to Everyday Life

How does Sima Qian show that Han intellectual life was not simply obedience to imperial authority?

Next: farmers, families, and local elites.

Transition

Use one synthesis moment here instead of repeating a pause after every review question.

Part 3

People, Families, and Status

What did imperial stability mean for ordinary Han society?

Most Han People Were Farmers

  • Villages ranged from dozens to hundreds of households
  • Households owed about one month of labor service each year
  • Men faced military obligations during much of the Han
  • Low taxes, famine relief, and migration assistance aimed to preserve productive households
The state depended on independent farmers - but debt and land loss threatened that independence.

Farmers at the Center

The Han state needed farmers to remain productive and taxable. Relief policies supported recovery, but did not eliminate vulnerability.

Peace, Productivity, and Population

  • Improved plows, irrigation, and grain-processing tools supported cultivation
  • Population growth depended on agriculture as well as political stability
  • 59 million in the census of 2 CE indicates the scale of the registered population
  • A census is evidence of administrative reach, not a perfect count of everyone
Agriculture made imperial scale possible.

Scale and Limits

Keep the census figure qualified as registered population. One or two technologies are enough to show why agriculture mattered.

Hierarchy Changed - But It Did Not Disappear

  • The old Zhou aristocracy disappeared, but family background remained powerful
  • Land, wealth, education, office, marriage, and political access overlapped
  • Merchants and manufacturers could become very wealthy
  • Debt, purchase, privilege, intimidation, and family ties contributed to land concentration
Powerful local families were precursors to later scholar-landowning elites.

Q9

Do not say birth no longer mattered. Connect farmer debt to elite formation: debt → land sale → tenancy or dependence → larger estates → stronger local magnates. Do not imply every large landowner used coercion.

How Did Hierarchy Operate Inside the Household?

  • The senior male usually headed the family; a widow could lead when sons were minors
  • Family members carried legal responsibilities toward one another
  • Property was generally divided among sons; daughters usually received dowries
  • Marriages were arranged by family heads; brides usually joined the husband’s family
Filial piety linked household duties to wider social order.

Q10

Use concrete mechanisms - authority, inheritance, marriage, law, and gender. Avoid reducing the family to one abstract label.

Filial Responsibility and Social Order

filial responsibility at home → responsible behavior in society → political order

  • The Classic of Filial Piety presented family conduct as moral training
  • Respect, care, and duty were supposed to extend beyond the household
  • Elite texts prescribed ideals; actual families did not always follow them

Prescriptive Text

The Classic of Filial Piety is evidence for ideals, not proof that every household practiced them identically.

The Ban Family: History, Education, and Power

  • Ban Biao began a history of the Western Han
  • Ban Gu continued it; Ban Zhao helped complete it
  • Ban Zhao taught at court and advised Empress Deng
  • She advocated girls’ education while defending hierarchical gender roles in Admonitions for Women
Ban Zhao’s life connects family, education, historical writing, and political access.

Historical Complexity

Do not present Ban Zhao as a modern feminist or simply as a teacher of submission. Her significance lies in the tension between education and hierarchy.

Part 4

Empire Moves South

Expansion spread institutions, but local societies shaped what Han rule became.

Empire Moved South in Two Ways

  • Armies and officials imposed conquest and administration
  • Migrants and merchants moved along river valleys
  • Counties, roads, schools, and waterways extended Han institutions
  • Local societies adopted, adapted, negotiated, and resisted
Expansion was demographic and cultural as well as military.

Q12

Avoid a one-way Sinicization story. Local societies adopted, adapted, negotiated, and resisted Han institutions.

Han Expansion Reached Several Different Borderlands

  • Emperor Wu established Han commanderies in northern Korea.
  • Han armies conquered Dian in Yunnan in 109 BCE.
  • Han rule expanded into Nam Viet and northern Vietnam.
  • Millions of migrants also moved south into the Yangzi region and beyond.
Han expansion combined conquest, migration, administration, settlement, and cultural exchange.

Teaching Point

Use this as a geographic overview before zooming in on Nam Viet and the Trưng Sisters.

The textbook presents Han expansion as a multi-directional process involving northern Korea, Central Asia, Dian in modern Yunnan, southern China, and northern Vietnam. Expansion occurred through armies, migration, settlement, administration, roads, schools, trade, and cultural exchange.

Emphasize that these regions did not all experience Han expansion in exactly the same way.

The estimate that millions of migrants moved from the North China Plain toward the Yangzi Valley and farther south helps students understand that expansion was not only military conquest. Population movement gradually changed the geographic center and cultural boundaries of the Chinese world.

Do not expand Korea or Dian into separate case studies. Nam Viet remains the detailed southern example.

Nam Viet / Nanyue

  • After Qin collapsed, Zhao Tuo / Triệu Đà established an independent kingdom
  • Nam Viet included areas of modern southern China and northern Vietnam
  • Zhao Tuo incorporated local warriors and encouraged intermarriage
  • Emperor Wu’s forces conquered Nam Viet in 111 BCE
Do not project a modern Vietnamese nation-state backward.

Historical Framing

Use both names and geographic language such as “the region of modern northern Vietnam.” Existing political communities mattered.

Resistance: The Trưng Sisters, 39 CE

  • Local aristocratic widows organized regional chiefs and armed followers
  • They seized Chinese strongholds and established an independent local regime
  • Han armies reconquered the region several years later
  • The revolt became important in later Vietnamese historical memory
Cultural exchange did not mean political acceptance.

Q12 Conclusion

Avoid projecting a modern national identity backward. Explain both the immediate Han context and the later importance of the sisters in Vietnamese memory.

What Han Expansion Changed

  • Chinese-style institutions, language, schools, roads, and waterways became influential
  • Migration created new farming communities and mixed local elites
  • Local societies continued to shape how Han institutions operated
  • Adoption, adaptation, negotiation, and resistance all formed part of the process
Han expansion spread institutions without producing a single uniform culture.

Synthesis

Connect southern expansion to the opening thesis: political incorporation and cultural exchange were related but not identical.

Part 5

The Han System Under Strain

Chapter 3 explains why the dynasty became vulnerable before Chapter 4 explains how it broke apart.

Section Purpose

This section should not teach the Way of Great Peace, Dong Zhuo, Cao Cao, or the Three Kingdoms. Its job is to prepare students to understand why the Han government was vulnerable before the crisis of 184.

Child Emperors Made Regency Politics Dangerous

  • Han emperors inherited the throne
  • Some heirs became emperor while still children
  • A child emperor required adults to govern for him
  • Empress dowagers and their relatives could gain enormous influence
A succession system designed for continuity could create a struggle over who actually controlled the emperor.

Teaching Point

Start with the institution, not the person. Students need to understand why child emperors created regency politics before Wang Mang makes sense. The basic question is: if the emperor is a child, who actually rules?

Wang Mang Rose Through the Imperial Family

  • Wang Mang belonged to the powerful family of Empress Wang
  • He served as regent for two young emperors
  • His position gave him extraordinary authority at court
  • In 9 CE, he replaced the Han with his Xin Dynasty
Wang Mang began as a regent within the Han system before becoming emperor himself.

Wang Mang's Rise

Make the path clear: Empress Wang's family had influence because of succession politics. Wang Mang did not begin as an outside rebel. He rose from inside the Han court and then replaced the dynasty he was supposed to protect.

Wang Mang Tried to Restore an Ideal Social Order

  • Wang Mang was a learned Confucian scholar
  • He limited large private landholdings
  • He outlawed slavery and revived public granaries
  • He expanded government control of parts of the economy
Wang Mang believed classical ideals could be used to repair social and political problems.

Avoid Caricature

Do not teach Wang Mang simply as a villain or fool. The textbook presents him as a serious Confucian reformer whose ambitious program created disruption and opposition. Students should see both the idealism and the instability.

Economic Disruption and Disaster Destroyed the Xin Dynasty

  • Currency and economic policies produced disruption
  • The Yellow River changed course and caused catastrophic flooding
  • Millions of farmers were displaced and rebellion spread
  • A Han imperial clansman restored the dynasty
9-23 CE
Xin Dynasty
25 CE
Han restored at Luoyang

Why the Xin Fell

Separate three causes for students: policy disruption, environmental disaster, and rebellion. The Han survived this crisis, but the episode exposed how vulnerable the imperial succession and court system could become.

Restoring the Han Did Not Fix the Succession Problem

  • The throne still passed by hereditary succession
  • Young emperors could still require regents
  • Empress families could still dominate a regency
  • Control of the emperor remained a path to political power
The dynasty returned, but the political structure that had helped Wang Mang rise remained intact.

Transition

This slide is the bridge from Wang Mang to the Later Han. It tells students that restoration did not remove the underlying institutional vulnerability. The next problem is who gained power around weak or young emperors.

Palace Eunuchs Had Something Other Officials Did Not: Access

  • Eunuchs served inside the imperial palace
  • They had unusually close access to emperors
  • Confucian officials generally looked down on them
  • Emperors could view them as trustworthy palace servants
Eunuch influence began with proximity to the emperor.

Define Before Judging

Students may know the word eunuch only vaguely. Do not begin with corruption. First explain their palace role and why they could become influential. The key concept is access to the ruler.

Eunuchs Became a Political Power Inside the Palace

  • Eunuchs gradually built networks of influence
  • In 124 CE, eunuchs helped place a child emperor on the throne
  • In 159 CE, an emperor used eunuchs against a consort-family faction
  • Their victory increased their influence over court politics
A group valued because it supposedly lacked an outside power base developed a power base inside the palace.

Concrete Chronology

Use 124 and 159 as anchors, not as trivia. They show an escalation from palace service to kingmaking and factional power.

Officials Challenged Eunuch Control of the Court

  • Confucian officials believed advising rulers was part of their duty
  • Many regarded eunuch influence as harmful to government
  • Officials protested eunuch power in 166 and 169 CE
  • The eunuchs retaliated against their opponents
A palace rivalry had become a conflict between the court and members of the educated political elite.

Connect to Confucian Officeholding

This is where earlier material on Confucian officials matters. These men did not see criticism as disloyalty. They saw admonition as part of responsible service.

The Purges Pushed Educated Men Out of Government

  • Critics were imprisoned and barred from office
  • More than one hundred reportedly died in prison
  • Persecution continued from 166 to 184
  • Excluded shi still retained prestige and political connections
The government was alienating educated men whose cooperation had helped the Han bureaucracy function.

Why This Matters

The point is not only that eunuchs punished opponents. The larger problem is that respected educated elites could become politically influential outside government service. That weakens the bond between the court and the governing class.

Powerful Families Were Becoming Stronger in Local Society

  • Debt could force farmers to sell land or become dependent
  • Wealthy families accumulated property and retainers
  • Education and recommendation helped elite families enter office
  • Local influence could rest on family networks as well as government position
Political influence was not confined to the imperial court.

Connect Backward

This slide deliberately connects to the earlier hierarchy slide. Do not claim large estates alone caused collapse. The narrower point is that society contained influential local families whose authority did not depend entirely on the emperor.

Maintaining the Han Empire Required Permanent Military Resources

  • Northern defenses required thousands of soldiers
  • Soldier-farmers were settled along the frontiers
  • Supplying distant garrisons was expensive
  • Frontier warfare encouraged a more professional military system
The empire's size created military obligations that did not disappear when conquest ended.

Military Burden

Chapter 3 says northern defenses required about ten thousand men and another fifty to sixty thousand soldier-farmers. Keep those numbers in notes unless you want to emphasize scale. The slide teaches the concept: empire required permanent military resources.

Han Frontier Armies Changed During the Eastern Han

  • Universal military service ended in 31 CE
  • Frontier armies increasingly relied on professional soldiers
  • Forces included mercenaries, convicts, and surrendered Xiongnu
  • Large Southern Xiongnu communities lived within Han territory
By the Later Han, the military system looked very different from the mass conscript armies of earlier centuries.

Prepare Chapter 4 Carefully

Do not present the Xiongnu or other frontier soldiers as a built-in threat. They served the Han state. Their presence becomes more important after central authority begins to break down.

By the Late Second Century, the Han Had Several Vulnerabilities

  • Court: regencies encouraged struggles among palace factions
  • Officials: eunuch purges alienated educated elites
  • Local society: powerful families gained wealth and influence
  • Military: maintaining the empire required specialized forces
None of these problems alone destroyed the Han. Together, they made the dynasty increasingly difficult for the central court to control.

Synthesis

This is a Chapter 3 synthesis, not the Chapter 4 collapse narrative. Emphasize vulnerability, not inevitability. The dynasty is still standing, but its political system is under strain.

What Did Han Empire Create?

A political empire also became a social and cultural world.

  • Thinkers linked government to the cosmos, but disagreed about what those links meant
  • Sima Qian turned the past into evidence for understanding change
  • Most people remained farmers while land and wealth created inequalities
  • Family hierarchy shaped authority, inheritance, and gender roles
  • Migration and conquest carried Han institutions south while local peoples resisted and adapted

Han unity was powerful, but it was never complete or uncontested.

Final Synthesis

Imperial institutions affected ordinary society, but shared institutions did not produce total uniformity. Han created a durable and diverse social world.

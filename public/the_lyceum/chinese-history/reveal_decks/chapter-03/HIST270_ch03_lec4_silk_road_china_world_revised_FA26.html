Part 1 · Q11 story · Emperor Wu’s frontier war · r. 141–87 BCE

Emperor Wu’s Armies Could Not Pin Down the Xiongnu

  • Emperor Wu sent armies of 100,000 to 300,000 into Xiongnu lands
  • These costly campaigns were of limited value: the nomads simply decamped
  • Troops could not carry enough food to stay long
  • The Yuezhi, driven west by the Xiongnu, might be allies

A war on the steppe gave Emperor Wu a reason to look west for help.

Review: Emperor Wu’s offensive in Lecture 2 →

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, pp. 43, 52.

This slide gives you the problem behind the whole lecture. Emperor Wu, who took the throne as a teenager in 141 BCE and reigned until 87 BCE, chose war against the Xiongnu (p. 43).

The war went badly. He sent several armies of one hundred thousand to three hundred thousand troops deep into Xiongnu territory, but the Xiongnu were a moving target: fighting nomads was not like attacking walled cities. If they did not want to fight, they simply decamped, and Han troops could not carry enough food to stay long (p. 43). What the armies could do was hold the land the Xiongnu left, with forts, officials, and settlers.

So Emperor Wu looked for help. The Yuezhi (yweh-jir) had moved west after a defeat by the Xiongnu several decades earlier, and he hoped they would return to fight the Xiongnu for him (p. 52). You studied his frontier policy in Lecture 2; open the green link to review it.

Q11 concept · Envoy

Concept: An Envoy Speaks for a Ruler Abroad

Envoy

An official a ruler sends to another people or state to carry his messages, make agreements, and bring back news.

A Han envoy, imagined as Zhang Qian (Zhāng Qiān), at the chalkboard. Course cartoon, AI-generated in the style of Professor Austin’s chalkboard cartoon; not a portrait or historical source.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

You need one word for the next slide: envoy. An envoy is an official a ruler sends abroad to speak for him: to carry messages, make agreements, and report back what he learns.

Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian as an envoy to look for the Yuezhi (p. 52). Notice that an envoy’s job has two halves. One is the official mission, here an alliance. The other is information: an envoy sees roads, cities, markets, and rulers that no one at home has seen. In Zhang Qian’s case the first half failed and the second half changed history.

Envoys carried a staff as a sign of the emperor’s authority, like the tasseled staff in the cartoon.

Yuezhi

Yuezhi (yweh-jir): a people who moved west after a defeat by the Xiongnu several decades before Zhang Qian’s mission; by his time they lived in Bactria. Emperor Wu hoped they would return and fight the Xiongnu for him (p. 52).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This slide gives you the journey. Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian (jahng chyen) as an envoy to look for the Yuezhi, hoping they would come back east to fight the Xiongnu for him (p. 52).

He set out in 138 BCE. The road west ran past the Xiongnu, and they captured him and held him for about ten years before he escaped and went on (p. 52). He reached Ferghana and found the Yuezhi, who by then had settled in Bactria; he also gathered reports of Parthia and India, lands he heard about but did not visit himself. Your textbook groups these as the lands he reached: Bactria, Parthia, and Ferghana (p. 52). Find them on the map later in this lecture. The Yuezhi had no interest in returning to help the Han (p. 52), and Zhang Qian made it home about 126 BCE, some 13 years after he left.

Notice the gap between the plan and the result. A military alliance was the whole point of the trip, and it never happened. Keep that failure in mind; the next slide shows why the trip still mattered.

Part 1 · Q11 story · What Zhang Qian learned · second mission about 119–115 BCE

Zhang Qian’s Reports Showed the Han a Wider World

  • His second mission (about 119–115 BCE) to the Wusun also failed
  • His reports described the countries of Central Asia firsthand
  • Silk was already trading with countries farther out, such as Rome
  • Other civilizations had cities and writing too

The alliance failed. The information changed how the Han saw the world.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This slide gives you the payoff of a failed mission. About 119 BCE, Emperor Wu sent Zhang Qian west a second time, to the Wusun (woo-swun), another people of the steppe, who proved just as unwilling to move east and fight; he was back in China by 115 BCE, and his deputies carried Han missions on to Ferghana, Bactria, Parthia, and India (p. 52).

Yet his travels were not in vain. From his reports of these two trips, the Chinese learned firsthand of the countries of Central Asia and heard about the trade in silk with other countries farther out, such as Rome (p. 52). Put more strongly: it was during the Han that the Chinese first learned that theirs was not the only civilization with cities and writing, and that these distant civilizations had been obtaining silk from China through merchants who traveled across Eurasia (p. 52).

Notice what that means. Chinese silk was already moving west before any Han envoy arrived. Zhang Qian did not start the trade; he discovered it.

Q11 concept · Silk Road

Concept: The Silk Road Was a Network of Trade Routes

Silk Road

The overland trade routes across Central Asia that linked Han China, step by step, to lands as far west as Rome.

Professor Austin at the chalkboard. Course cartoon, AI-generated in the style of Professor Austin’s chalkboard cartoon; not a portrait or historical source.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This concept slide defines the lecture’s main term. The Silk Road means the overland trade routes across Central Asia that linked China to lands as far west as Rome (p. 52). The name is modern: a German geographer, Ferdinand von Richthofen, coined it in 1877.

Notice three things about it. First, it is plural: routes, not one road. On the map later in this part you will see a northern and a southern route around the Taklamakan Desert. Second, it runs across Central Asia, through oasis city-states that lived by trade (p. 52). Third, it is named for its most famous product. Silk was the Chinese product most in demand outside China (p. 53), but caravans also carried gold, horses, glass, and new foods (p. 52).

Part 2 · Q11 story · Han armies in Central Asia · 104 and 102 BCE

Han Armies Subdued Ferghana and Won Control of the Routes

  • In 104 BCE the general Li Guangli led an army toward Ferghana; it failed
  • In 102 BCE a second army forced Ferghana to submit and hand over its horses
  • Overlordship gave China control of the trade routes across Central Asia
  • Oasis city-states traded more conveniently under Han garrisons

Military expansion opened the way for trade.

Review: overlordship in Lecture 3 →

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This slide gives you the moment the Silk Road came under Han control. In 104 and 102 BCE, the Han general Li Guangli (lee gwahng-lee) led Chinese armies west over the mountains toward Ferghana (p. 52). The first campaign broke down in the desert; the second, in 102 BCE, besieged Ferghana’s capital until its nobles submitted and handed over the prized Ferghana horses Emperor Wu wanted for his cavalry.

Recognition of Chinese overlordship followed. It gave China control over the trade routes across Central Asia, commonly called the Silk Road (p. 52). Lecture 3 defined overlordship as rule by an outside power that claims authority over a people, who keep some of their own leaders and ways. Open the green link if you need a review.

Notice why the oasis city-states went along. They did not resist the Chinese presence because they could carry out the trade on which they depended more conveniently with Chinese garrisons to protect them (p. 52). Han soldiers made the caravan trade safer, and the cities profited.

Part 2 · Q11 map · The Silk Road, about 100 BCE

The Silk Road Ran Around Deserts and Over Mountains

  • Start at Chang’an, the Han capital
  • Follow the northern or southern route around the Taklamakan Desert
  • Cross the Pamir and Tian Shan ranges to Ferghana, Bactria, and Parthia
  • The Xiongnu steppe lies just north of the routes

Click the map to enlarge it.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52 and Map 3.1; Natural Earth.

This slide gives you the geography of the Silk Road. Use it to locate every place in this lecture; click the map to see it full size.

Start at Chang’an, the Han capital, in the east. Moving west, the routes pass through the Western Regions, the oasis city-states that accepted Han overlordship (p. 52). Notice that there are two routes, one along the northern edge of the Taklamakan Desert and one along the southern edge. Niya, where the silk arm cover on a later slide was found, lies on the southern arm (p. 53). Farther west, the routes cross the Pamir Mountains, and the neighboring ranges that Han armies crossed in 104 and 102 BCE, to reach Ferghana, Bactria, and Parthia, the lands Zhang Qian visited (p. 52). From there, merchants carried silk on to Rome (p. 52).

Notice too how close the routes run to the Xiongnu steppe. Controlling them was part of the Han contest with the Xiongnu.

Part 2 · Q11 evidence · Who carried the trade

Foreign Merchants Passed Silk West Toward Rome

  • Much of the trade was in the hands of Sogdian, Parthian, and Indian merchants
  • Goods changed hands in stages; silk eventually reached Rome
  • Buyers wanted skeins of silk thread and finished silk cloth
  • Cloth was woven in Chinese or Syrian workshops

The Han guarded the routes; foreign merchants ran most of the trade.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This slide answers a question you may not have asked: who actually carried the silk? Not, for the most part, Han officials. Much of the trade was in the hands of Sogdian, Parthian, and Indian merchants who carried silk and other goods by caravan toward Rome (p. 52). Few, if any, made the whole trip: silk usually moved in stages, sold from one merchant to the next at oasis and city markets, which is why historians call it relay trade. Your textbook puts it more simply, saying caravans carried silk “all the way to Rome” (p. 52).

Notice what they sold. There was a market both for skeins of silk thread and for silk cloth woven in Chinese or Syrian workshops (p. 52). So some silk left China as raw thread and was woven into cloth far to the west.

Put the pieces together. The Han government supplied armies, garrisons, and overlordship; the oasis cities supplied markets and supplies; foreign merchants supplied the caravans. The Silk Road was a joint enterprise of many peoples, with China at its eastern end.

This slide gives you the other direction of the trade: what came east. Your textbook says caravans returning to China carried gold, horses, and occasionally handicrafts of West Asian origin, such as glass beads and cups (p. 52).

The longest-lasting change came from food. Through the trade along the Silk Road, the Chinese learned of new foodstuffs, including walnuts, pomegranates, sesame, and coriander, all of which came to be grown in China (p. 52). Gold was spent and glass cups broke, but these plants became part of Chinese farming and cooking.

Part 2 · Q11 evidence · Material culture: silk

Silk Was the Chinese Product Most in Demand Abroad

  • The silkworm was domesticated in China by the Shang period
  • Filaments were twisted together into yarns for weaving
  • Weavers made flat weaves, gauzes, and patterned weaves
  • Multicolored patterns required a draw loom

Centuries of skill stood behind every bolt of silk.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, pp. 48, 53.

This slide shows you why the Silk Road was named for silk: it was the Chinese product most in demand outside China (p. 53).

The skill behind it was old. The silkworm had been domesticated in China by the Shang period, and the excellence of Chinese silk technology in Zhou times is well documented through excavations of tombs (p. 53). Silk is very strong and amazingly fine; a single silkworm can spin a filament about 1,000 meters long, finer than a human hair, and several filaments have to be twisted together to make yarn (p. 53). Besides flat weaves and light gauzes, Chinese weavers made patterned weaves, including multicolored ones that required a draw loom (p. 53).

The image follows a Han tomb painting in your textbook that shows three women reeling, twisting, and weaving silk under a tile roof (p. 48). Many Han tombs had scenes of daily life like this on their walls.

This slide gives you a real object from the Silk Road, the one pictured in your textbook’s Material Culture feature. Many fragments of Han textiles survived in the arid climate of Chinese Central Asia; this piece came from tomb 8 at Niya, along the southern arm of the Silk Road (p. 53). Excavated in 1995, it is usually dated to the late Han or the decades just after, about the 2nd to 3rd century CE.

Look closely. The weave is exceptionally fine, with 220 warp threads per centimeter, and the five-color design shows clouds, birds, a single-horned beast, and a tiger along with Chinese characters (p. 53). The five colors, blue, green, red, yellow, and white, are the colors of the five planets (p. 53).

Now read the inscription, an astrological omen that your textbook links to a Han emperor’s command to a general bringing order to the northwest frontier: “The Five Planets appear in the east. This is very auspicious for China. The barbarians will be defeated.” (p. 53) It joins three threads of this chapter: fine silk, frontier war, and the cosmology of the heavens you studied in Lecture 3.

This slide shows you that the Silk Road had to be won more than once. Han overlordship in the Western Regions had been lost since Wang Mang’s time, the usurper you met in Lecture 3 (p. 48).

The hero of this story is the general Ban Chao (bahn chow, 32–102 CE). After distinguishing himself as a junior officer in campaigns against the Xiongnu, he was sent in 73 CE to the Western Regions to see about the possibility of restoring Chinese overlordship there. He spent most of the next three decades in Central Asia. Through patient diplomacy and a show of force, he reestablished Chinese control over the oasis cities of Central Asia, and in 91 CE he was appointed protector general of the area (p. 48; your textbook gives 92, but the Han histories date the appointment to 91). He retired to the capital, Luoyang, in 102 and died there a month later.

Notice the method: diplomacy first, force in reserve. Ban Chao was the twin brother of the historian Ban Gu and brother of Ban Zhao, the family you studied in Lecture 3.

Part 3 · Q11 analysis · What the Silk Road changed

The Silk Road Changed What China Grew, Sold, and Spent

New crops

Walnuts, pomegranates, sesame, and coriander came to be grown in China (p. 52).

Silk abroad

Silk, most in demand abroad, went west as thread and cloth (pp. 52–53).

New costs

Armies, garrisons, and lavish gifts to foreign envoys cost the Han dearly (pp. 43, 55).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, pp. 43, 52–53, 55.

This slide pulls Parts 2 and 3 together into one answer about China’s economy. Read the three cards as three kinds of change.

New crops. Through the trade along the Silk Road, the Chinese learned of walnuts, pomegranates, sesame, and coriander, all of which came to be grown in China (p. 52). This is the most lasting change.

Silk abroad. Silk was the Chinese product most in demand outside China (p. 53), sold as skeins of thread and as woven cloth (p. 52). In return, caravans brought gold, horses, and glass (p. 52).

New costs. To pay for his military campaigns, Emperor Wu took over the minting of coins, sold offices and titles, and increased taxes on private businesses (p. 43). Diplomacy cost money too: gifts to foreign envoys may have reached 10 percent of state revenue, though the system reduced the cost of defense (p. 55).

China Was Never Completely Isolated from Eurasia

  • Wheat and the chariot arrived from west Asia in Shang times
  • Animal art spread across the steppe in late Zhou times
  • Xiongnu bronzes link to the animal art of other steppe nomads
  • Yet China had less contact with other early centers than they had with each other

The Silk Road widened contacts that already existed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, pp. 43, 56; The Metropolitan Museum of Art.

This story gives you the long background to the Silk Road. East Asian civilization was never completely isolated from the rest of Eurasia (p. 56).

Its evidence reaches far back. Wheat and the chariot arrived in China from west Asia in Shang times, and animal art spread across the steppe in late Zhou times (p. 56). The bronze plaque in the image is a real Xiongnu object of this kind. Your textbook shows a similar Xiongnu belt plaque and notes its connections to the animal art of other nomadic groups on the Eurasian steppe (p. 43).

Notice the balance, though. Ancient China had less contact with the other early centers of civilization, Mesopotamia, India, Egypt, and Greece, than they had with each other (p. 56). The Silk Road did not create contact from nothing; it widened and regularized contacts that had been thin.

The Two-Humped Bactrian Camel Carried the Trade

  • The Silk Road trade was largely carried by the two-humped Bactrian camel
  • A heavy coat of hair let it withstand bitter winter cold
  • Each camel could carry about 500 pounds of cargo
  • Caravans of camels crossed deserts and mountain passes

No camels, no caravans: the animal made the trade possible.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 3, p. 52.

This story gives you the animal that made the Silk Road work. The trade was largely carried by the two-humped Bactrian camel (p. 52).

Two details explain why. With a heavy coat of hair to withstand the bitter cold of winter, it could survive the high passes and desert nights; and each camel could carry about 500 pounds of cargo (p. 52). Look back at the map: the routes crossed the Taklamakan Desert and the Pamir Mountains, ground that no cart could easily cross.

Notice how this connects to the merchants. Sogdian, Parthian, and Indian traders moved silk west by caravan (p. 52), and caravans meant camels. The name Bactrian points west too: Bactria was where Zhang Qian found the Yuezhi (p. 52).

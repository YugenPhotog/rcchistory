The Barrel of a Gun

Shanghai 1927, the Communist Party, and Mao's Turn to the Peasants

1919–1930 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome to the first lecture of Chapter 25. It covers 1919 to 1930: how the Chinese Communist Party was born, how its alliance with the Nationalists ended in blood in 1927, and how one party organizer, Mao Zedong, drew a lesson from that disaster (Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 439–442).

The lecture opens in Shanghai in April 1927, where the last lecture of Chapter 24 stopped, then goes back to 1919 to see how the party got there.

Check yourself: As you go, ask who gave the Chinese Communists their orders, and what happened when those orders failed.

Q1 story · Shanghai · 12 April 1927

On 12 April 1927, Gangsters and Soldiers Attacked the Unions That Had Just Handed Shanghai to Chiang

Before dawn, armed gangsters in workers' armbands attacked the union pickets.
Chiang Kaishek's soldiers then disarmed the unions "to keep order."
The next day troops fired on a protest march; the killing went on for weeks.
Hundreds died in the first days and thousands in the weeks after.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Elizabeth J. Perry, Shanghai on Strike (1993); Harold R. Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution (1938, rev. 1961). Image: Shanghai, April 1927.

Picture Shanghai in the early hours of 12 April 1927. Three weeks earlier the city's workers had risen, seized the Chinese districts and welcomed Chiang Kaishek's army. Now, before dawn, armed men wearing workers' armbands attacked the headquarters of the union pickets. They were not workers. They belonged to the Green Gang, Shanghai's great criminal society, and they had been armed for the job.

Ebrey tells it in two sentences: "The next day Green Gang members and soldiers loyal to Chiang attacked union headquarters. Soon soldiers were mowing down civilians with machine guns" (p. 440). When workers marched to protest on 13 April, troops fired into the crowd on Baoshan Road.

How many died? Ebrey gives "an estimated five thousand." No reliable count exists. Historians of Shanghai labor, such as Elizabeth Perry, describe hundreds killed in the first days and thousands more arrested, shot or missing in the weeks of terror that followed.

The photograph shows soldiers leading away suspects.

Source beyond Ebrey: Elizabeth J. Perry, Shanghai on Strike (1993); Harold R. Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution (1938, rev. 1961).

Why this matters: In one morning the alliance of Nationalists and Communists became a war between them.

Check yourself: Why would Chiang use gangsters before he used his own soldiers?

Q1 story · Recap · Guangzhou to Wuhan · 1924–1927

Recap: Since 1924 the Communists Had Worked Inside Sun Yatsen's Nationalist Party

The Comintern backed Sun Yatsen's party with money, arms, and advisers · Ch. 24 L4 · Comintern ↗
Communists joined that party as individuals: the First United Front · Ch. 24 L4 · United Front ↗
Its army marched north in 1926 and reached the Yangzi · Ch. 24 L4 · Northern Expedition ↗
By March 1927 the party's left sat at Wuhan, and Chiang stood apart · Ch. 24 L4 · Wuhan and Shanghai ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 24, pp. 428–429; Ch. 25, p. 440. Image: Wuhan, March 1927.

You have met this alliance already. In the last lecture of Chapter 24 you saw Sun Yatsen take help from the Comintern, the Moscow organization that spread revolution abroad. You saw the small Communist Party join his Nationalist Party "as individuals," and the army trained at Whampoa march north in 1926. The links take you back to those slides.

Ebrey sums up the alliance this way: it "was expedient for both at the time because they could concentrate on their common foe, the warlords. However, it covered over deep differences" (p. 440).

By early 1927 the differences were out in the open. The party's left wing and its Communist allies had moved the government to Wuhan. The photograph shows its Central Committee meeting there on 10 March 1927, trying to cut back Chiang's power. Chiang, with the army, stayed downriver and moved on Shanghai.

To see why the Communists were in this alliance at all, the lecture now goes back to 1919.

Why this matters: The collapse in 1927 makes sense only if you remember how odd the alliance was.

Q1 story · Beijing · May 1919

In 1919 Li Dazhao, the Beijing University Librarian, Explained Marx to the Readers of New Youth

The Russian Revolution of 1917 made Marxism look scientific, Western, and successful.
Li's essay explained class struggle and capitalist exploitation.
China had few factory workers, but Li argued the whole nation was exploited by imperialism.
He was the university's librarian and an editor of New Youth · Ch. 24 L3 · Beijing University ↗

Names in This Story

Li Dazhao librarian and co-founder of the Communist Party, 1889–1927; pronounced "lee dah-jow"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 439–441; Maurice Meisner, Li Ta-chao and the Origins of Chinese Marxism (1967). Image: about 1920.

After 1917, Ebrey writes, "Chinese intellectuals began to take an interest in Marxism-Leninism, which seemed to provide a blueprint for a world of abundance without exploitation. Communism appeared scientific, Western, anti-imperialist, and successful: it had just proved itself capable of bringing revolution to a backward country" (pp. 439–440).

The man who explained it first was Li Dazhao, Beijing University's librarian. For the May 1919 issue of New Youth, the magazine you met in Chapter 24, he wrote an introduction to Marxist theory. Marx said the revolution would be led by factory workers, the proletariat, and China had few. Li's answer was bold: the nation as a whole was the exploited class, exploited by the imperialist powers.

Li had studied law for six years at a school in Tianjin and then at Waseda University in Tokyo; Ebrey's "six years in Japan" merges the two. Maurice Meisner's biography traces how his ideas made Marxism fit China.

Source beyond Ebrey: Maurice Meisner, Li Ta-chao and the Origins of Chinese Marxism (1967).

Why this matters: Li made Marxism a theory of national liberation, not just of factory workers.

Check yourself: How did Li get around the problem that China had few factory workers?

Q1 story · Beijing, Shanghai, and France · 1919–1920

Study Groups Formed in Beijing and Shanghai, and Students in France Met Strikes and Marxism

In 1920 Li Dazhao started a Marxist study group at Beijing University.
Chen Duxiu, who had left Beijing, started another in Shanghai · Ch. 24 L3 · Chen Duxiu ↗
Thousands of young Chinese, men and women, went to France to work and study.
In French factories they met strikes and Marxism firsthand.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Marilyn A. Levine, The Found Generation: Chinese Communists in Europe during the Twenties (1993). Image: Shanghai, 1920.

In 1920, Ebrey writes, "Li organized a Marxist study group at Beijing University. At the same time, Chen Duxiu organized one in Shanghai, where he had gone after resigning his university post in Beijing" (p. 440). Chen was the founder of New Youth; the link takes you back to him.

These groups were small, a few dozen young people reading pamphlets and arguing. A second stream of Marxism came from France. "Another source of knowledge of European Marxism were the thousands of Chinese students, male and female, who had gone to France in 1919 and 1920 to participate in work-study programs. Most worked in factories, where they were introduced to both strikes and Marxism-Leninism" (p. 440).

The photograph shows a farewell in Shanghai for members of a Hunan students' society about to sail. Marilyn Levine has traced this "found generation": among the work-study students were Zhou Enlai and Deng Xiaoping, future leaders of the party.

Source beyond Ebrey: Marilyn A. Levine, The Found Generation: Chinese Communists in Europe during the Twenties (1993).

Why this matters: The party's first members were young readers and students, not workers.

Check yourself: Where did young Chinese learn about Marxism, and from whom?

Q1 concept · Democratic centralism

Concept: Democratic Centralism Meant Debate Inside the Cell, Then Every Member Obeyed

Concept

Democratic centralism

The Leninist rule of party discipline: local cells elect delegates to higher levels, up to the national congress and its committees. Members may debate a question, but once a decision is made every member must carry it out.

In 1920 Comintern agents came to turn the study groups into party cells.
Delegates flowed up; decisions flowed down.
Ebrey: this was discipline "beyond anything in the prior repertoire" of Chinese organizations.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440. Image: Beijing, 1920.

The Comintern noticed the study groups quickly. "In 1920, soon after the Comintern learned of the existence of Marxist study groups in China, agents were sent to help turn the groups into party cells. This entailed teaching 'democratic centralism,' the secret to party discipline" (p. 440). That second sentence is the one the Reading Guide asks you to find for Question 1.

Here is how it worked. "Each local cell elected delegates to higher levels, up to the national party congress, with its central executive committee and the latter's standing committee. Delegates flowed up, and decisions flowed down. Decisions could be debated within a cell, but once decisions were reached, all were obligated to obey them."

Think of the difference from a club or a magazine circle, where members leave if they disagree. In a Leninist party, disagreement ends when the vote is taken. The photograph shows the first of these agents, Grigori Voitinsky, with Li Dazhao in 1920.

Why this matters: This discipline let a tiny party survive terror, but it also meant orders from Moscow were obeyed.

Check yourself: In your own words, what were members allowed to do before a decision, and what after?

Q1 story · Shanghai · July 1921

In July 1921 Thirteen Delegates Founded a Secret, Disciplined Communist Party

Thirteen Chinese delegates met in Shanghai, with two Comintern advisers.
They made the party "secret, exclusive, centralized," and put class struggle first.
Chen Duxiu, absent in Guangzhou, was chosen secretary general.
Their first task: organize labor unions and recruit workers.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Tony Saich, The Origins of the First United Front in China: The Role of Sneevliet (Alias Maring) (1991). Image: Shanghai, 2021.

In July 1921 the study groups became a party. "Following Comintern advice, thirteen delegates met in July 1921 to form the Chinese Communist Party as a secret, exclusive, centralized party. The party broke with the anarchists and guild socialists and asserted the primacy of class struggle. Chen Duxiu was chosen as secretary general" (p. 440).

They met in a house in the French Concession, where Chinese police could not easily reach them; the photograph shows it today. Two Comintern men attended, the Dutchman Henk Sneevliet, known as Maring, and a Russian, Nikolsky. When a stranger looked in on the last session, the delegates fled and finished the meeting on a hired boat on a lake at Jiaxing. Tony Saich's study of Maring tells the story. The delegates spoke for only about fifty members.

"The party agreed to put priority on organizing labor unions and recruiting workers into the party." In Shanghai it ran a Russian language school and a Socialist Youth Corps.

Source beyond Ebrey: Tony Saich, The Origins of the First United Front in China: The Role of Sneevliet (Alias Maring) (1991).

Why this matters: The party began tiny, but it began with Lenin's rules.

Check yourself: Why would a new party want to be secret and exclusive?

Q1 story · Hangzhou and Moscow · 1922–1923

Moscow Ordered the Alliance with the Nationalists Over Many Members' Objections

In August 1922 Maring pressed party leaders to join Sun's party.
Many objected: Sun's party was full of landlords, officers, and merchants.
Comintern discipline won, and the party accepted the alliance.
Many Nationalist officers were "appalled by talk of class warfare."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Saich, The Origins of the First United Front (1991). Image: Comintern Second Congress, 1920.

Now democratic centralism was tested. "At the insistence of the Comintern and against the advice of many of the Chinese members, the decision was made in 1922 to ally with the Nationalists" (p. 440).

The man who pressed it was Maring, shown here beside Lenin in 1920. At a party meeting at Hangzhou's West Lake in August 1922, Chen Duxiu and others objected: Sun Yatsen's party was a party of landlords, officers and merchants, and joining it would blur the class struggle. Maring answered with the Comintern's authority, and the leaders gave way. Saich's study shows the argument. The formal alliance came in 1923 and 1924, as you saw in the last chapter.

Ebrey sees the problem clearly. "The Nationalist military included many staunch anticommunists who were appalled by talk of class warfare." The alliance gave the Communists a way to reach workers and peasants, but it put them inside an army they did not control.

Source beyond Ebrey: Saich, The Origins of the First United Front (1991).

Why this matters: This was the Comintern's biggest decision for China, and the Chinese members did not make it.

Check yourself: Why did Chinese Communists accept a policy many of them disagreed with?

Q1 and Q2 story · Guangdong and Hunan · 1922–1926

Along the March North, Farmers' Associations Grew to More Than a Million Members

The United Front let Communists organize workers and peasants openly.
Peng Pai built the first big farmers' associations in Haifeng, Guangdong.
Organizers went ahead of the army in 1926, village by village.
By the end of 1926 the associations had more than a million members.

Names in This Story

Peng Pai Communist peasant organizer, 1896–1929; pronounced "pung pie"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 440, 442; Fernando Galbiati, P'eng P'ai and the Hai-Lu-Feng Soviet (1985). Image: 1920s.

Why did the Communists stay in an alliance with men who hated class war? Ebrey answers: "One reason the communists remained in the United Front was that it gave them the opportunity to organize both workers and peasants. Along the route of the Northern Expedition, farmers' associations were established, with membership exceeding 1 million people by the end of 1926" (p. 440).

The pioneer was Peng Pai, the son of a landlord family in Haifeng, on the Guangdong coast. In 1922, Fernando Galbiati's biography tells, he began talking to tenant farmers in the fields, and within a year his association had tens of thousands of members who refused to pay high rents. The Nationalist Party then made peasant organizing official and trained organizers in Guangzhou.

When the army marched north in 1926, these organizers went ahead of it into Hunan and Hubei. Villagers formed associations, cut rents and in places attacked landlords. Many Nationalist officers came from landlord families.

Source beyond Ebrey: Fernando Galbiati, P'eng P'ai and the Hai-Lu-Feng Soviet (1985).

Why this matters: The peasant movement was the Communists' biggest gain, and the officers' biggest fear.

Check yourself: Why would the farmers' associations frighten many Nationalist officers?

Q1 story · Shanghai · 21–27 March 1927

On 21 March 1927, Hundreds of Thousands of Workers Struck and Seized Shanghai Before Chiang Arrived

The Communist-led General Labor Union called a general strike as the army neared.
Ebrey counts more than six hundred thousand strikers; armed pickets took the Chinese districts.
Flush with victory, workers demanded the foreign concessions back.
Foreign powers had already landed troops to defend the concessions.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Perry, Shanghai on Strike (1993). Image: Shanghai, 27 March 1927.

Now return to Shanghai. "On March 21, as the Nationalist army neared Shanghai, the Communist-led General Labor Union called for a general strike. More than six hundred thousand workers responded and seized the city" (p. 440). Other counts run higher, to about eight hundred thousand; every count puts it in the hundreds of thousands.

Armed union pickets fought the warlord garrison in the Chinese districts for two days and won. Zhou Enlai, back from France, helped lead them. When Chiang's troops entered, the city was already in workers' hands, and a provisional city government met, shown in the photograph. In the front row stands the union leader Wang Shouhua.

Then the workers went further. "Flush with victory, they began demanding the return of the foreign concessions." Britain, the United States and other powers had already landed thousands of troops behind barbed wire to defend the International Settlement. To Shanghai's bankers and merchants, armed workers looked like revolution at their door.

Source beyond Ebrey: Perry, Shanghai on Strike (1993).

Why this matters: The rising showed the Communists' strength and made Chiang decide to destroy it.

Check yourself: Why would the workers' victory alarm both Chiang and the foreign powers?

Q1 story · Shanghai · 11 April 1927

On 11 April the Union Leader Was Invited to the Green Gang Boss's House and Murdered

The Green Gang ran Shanghai's opium trade, gambling, and protection rackets.
Its boss, Du Yuesheng, invited the union leader Wang Shouhua to dinner.
Wang was killed that night, and his body was buried outside the city.
Bankers and Chiang's officers had already made their deal with the gang.

Names in This Story

Du Yuesheng Green Gang boss, 1888–1951, "Big-Eared Du"; pronounced "doo yweh-shung"

Wang Shouhua Communist union leader, 1901–1927

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Brian G. Martin, The Shanghai Green Gang: Politics and Organized Crime, 1919–1937 (1996). Images: 1924; 1940s.

Ebrey tells this scene in one sentence: "On April 11, the head of the union was invited to the home of the leader of the mafia-like Green Gang, where he was murdered" (p. 440).

The historian Brian Martin fills in the names. The union leader was Wang Shouhua, a young Communist. The gang boss was Du Yuesheng, "Big-Eared Du," who controlled the opium trade, gambling houses and protection rackets of the French Concession. Du had friends among the French police, the bankers and Chiang's officers. In early April, with money from Shanghai businessmen, the gang formed a fake workers' association and armed its men.

On the night of 11 April, Wang went to Du's house for dinner. He was seized and killed, and his body was buried outside the city. The next morning, as you saw at the start of this lecture, the gang and the soldiers struck the pickets.

The Communists had not seen it coming. Their orders from Moscow were to keep the alliance with Chiang.

Source beyond Ebrey: Brian G. Martin, The Shanghai Green Gang: Politics and Organized Crime, 1919–1937 (1996).

Why this matters: Chiang won Shanghai with the help of its underworld, and that alliance lasted.

Check yourself: Why might Chiang have let the gang strike first?

Q1 story · Shanghai, Wuhan, and Beyond · April–July 1927

The Killing Spread; by July the Soviet Advisers Were Gone and the Alliance Was Over

The terror spread to Guangzhou, Nanjing, and other cities.
In Beijing a warlord hanged Li Dazhao on 28 April.
Wuhan's left-wing Nationalists turned on the Communists in July.
By July the Soviet advisers had left, and the United Front was over.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution; Meisner, Li Ta-chao (1967). Image: Wuhan, 1927.

Shanghai was only the beginning. "The terror quickly spread to other cities. The labor union base of the Communist Party was destroyed" (p. 440). In Guangzhou and other cities the army and police rounded up Communists and union men.

In the north, where Chiang did not rule, the warlord Zhang Zuolin raided the Soviet embassy in Beijing and seized Li Dazhao, who had sheltered there. On 28 April 1927 Li was hanged. Meisner's biography tells his end.

"Although the party tried to continue working with the left wing of the Nationalist Party in Wuhan, Chiang's show of force carried the day." The leaders in the photograph held out for a few months. Then, in the summer, the Wuhan Nationalists learned that Moscow had told the Communists to build their own army, and they too expelled the Communists. "By July 1927 the Soviet advisers had withdrawn from the Nationalist army, and the United Front was over."

Source beyond Ebrey: Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution; Meisner, Li Ta-chao (1967).

Why this matters: Both wings of the Nationalist Party, right and left, ended up against the Communists.

Check yourself: Why did the Wuhan left also break with the Communists?

Q1 story · Across China · 1927–1930

Party Membership Fell from 60,000 to Under 10,000, and Bobbed Hair Could Get a Young Woman Shot

Uprisings that autumn in cities and villages failed.
From 1927 to 1930, troops hunted Communist organizers everywhere.
In some places short hair alone was "evidence" that a young woman was a Communist.
Membership fell from about 60,000 to under 10,000 within a year.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 440–441; Christina K. Gilmartin, Engendering the Chinese Revolution (1995). Image: Wuhan, 1928.

"That fall, the Communist Party tried to organize uprisings in both cities and the countryside, but none met with much success" (p. 440). Then the hunt began. "From 1927 through 1930, the hunt was on for Communist organizers all over the country; in some areas, the only evidence that troops needed in order to conclude that a young woman was a communist was bobbed hair."

Why hair? In the 1920s cutting off a woman's long hair was a sign of the new woman, the student who read New Youth and joined marches. Christina Gilmartin's study of women in the early party shows how many young women activists bobbed their hair, and how soldiers in 1927 treated it as a badge of radicalism. The young women in this photograph, students at Wuhan in 1928, wore the style that could have cost them their lives a year earlier.

The numbers tell the rest. "Party membership, which had reached about sixty thousand in April 1927, plummeted to fewer than ten thousand within the year" (pp. 440–441).

Source beyond Ebrey: Christina K. Gilmartin, Engendering the Chinese Revolution (1995).

Why this matters: In 1927 the party nearly died, and it lost its city base.

Check yourself: What does the bobbed-hair test tell you about how the terror worked?

Q2 story · Shaoshan, Hunan · 1893–1910

Mao Zedong Was Born in 1893 in a Hunan Village and Worked on His Father's Farm

Mao grew up in Shaoshan, a village south of Changsha, Hunan's capital.
From age six he worked on his father's 3-acre farm.
He studied six years at the village school, then farmed full time.
Through the 1920s he was "just one of hundreds" of party organizers.

Names in This Story

Mao Zedong 1893–1976; older spelling Mao Tse-tung; pronounced "mow dzuh-dong"

Course assignment · Mao Stream & Discuss

Your Mao Stream & Discuss assignment is due Thursday, 10 December. Keep notes on Mao from this lecture on; you will need them.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 441. Image: Changsha, 1919.

Now meet the man this lecture is named for. Ebrey is careful: "Through the 1920s, Mao Zedong was just one of hundreds of Communist Party organizers. He ended up playing such an important role in twentieth-century Chinese history that it is useful to begin with his early experiences" (p. 441). The Reading Guide asks you to find the next sentence for Question 2.

"Mao was born in 1893 in a farming village" south of Changsha, the capital of Hunan province. The village was Shaoshan. The photograph shows him at twenty-five, in 1919, the year after he left school. "He began helping out on his father's 3-acre farm when he was six. At age eight, in 1901, he entered the local primary school, where he studied for six years." Then he worked full time on the farm, from thirteen to sixteen.

His father was a hard-working farmer who also traded grain and lent money, prosperous by village standards. So Mao knew farm work and village life from inside.

At fourteen he was betrothed to a neighbor's daughter of eighteen. She died in 1910, and Mao left the farm to study.

Why this matters: Few Communist leaders grew up working in the fields, and Mao did.

Q2 story · Changsha · 1911–1918

In Changsha He Cut Off His Queue, Joined the 1911 Army, and Read Rousseau and Darwin in the Library

In 1911 he went to middle school in Changsha and cut off his queue.
After the Wuchang rising he served six months in the republican army · Ch. 24 L1 · 1911 ↗
For a year he read world history and Western thinkers in the public library.
In 1913 he entered a teachers' school, which became the First Normal School.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 441; Stuart Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 1 (1992). Images: 1910s; Changsha, 2014.

In 1911, at seventeen, Mao went to Changsha "to enter a middle school. Not only was Changsha a large city, but also the new provincial assembly was then meeting, and all sorts of newspapers were in circulation. Mao joined student demonstrations against the Qing government and cut off his queue" (p. 441). When revolutionary soldiers rose at nearby Wuchang, the rising you saw in Chapter 24, "Mao, wanting to be a part of the action, joined the republican army," and quit after six months of garrison duty.

Then came a year in the library, reading "world history and Chinese translations of works by such Western writers as Rousseau, Montesquieu, J. S. Mill, Adam Smith, and Charles Darwin."

In 1913 he entered the Hunan Fourth Normal School, a teachers' college. It merged into the First Normal School the next year, and he graduated from the First Normal in 1918, as Stuart Schram's edition of his early writings shows. There his teacher Yang Changji shaped him most, and Mao published an article on physical education in New Youth in 1917.

Source beyond Ebrey: Stuart Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 1 (1992).

Why this matters: Mao was largely self-taught, a reader of Western ideas before he was a Marxist.

Check yourself: Which experiences in Changsha drew Mao into politics?

Q2 story · Beijing and Changsha · 1918–1921

In Beijing He Worked as a Library Clerk Under Li Dazhao, Then Went Home to Organize

His teacher Yang Changji got him a job as a clerk in the Beijing University library.
His boss was Li Dazhao; Mao missed May Fourth while back in Hunan.
In Changsha he ran a magazine and organized a student strike.
He lived with Yang's daughter Yang Kaihui and was a Hunan delegate in 1921.

Names in This Story

Yang Changji Mao's teacher, 1871–1920

Yang Kaihui Yang's daughter and Mao's wife, 1901–1930; pronounced "yahng ky-hway"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 441. Images: Changsha, 1919.

When Yang Changji took a post at Beijing University, Mao followed him. Yang "helped him get a job as a clerk in the library, which made him a subordinate of Li Dazhao," the librarian you met earlier in this lecture (p. 441). Mao, a provincial with a Hunan accent, shelved newspapers and listened to famous professors.

He did not stay. His mother fell ill, and "Mao thus missed the excitement at Beijing University during the May Fourth incident." Back in Changsha he taught, "started his own magazine," formed the Hunan United Students Association and organized "a strike of thirteen thousand middle school students against the local warlord." The photograph shows the study club he had helped found.

In early 1920 both his father and Professor Yang died. Yang's daughter, Yang Kaihui, returned to Hunan, "and by the end of 1920 she and Mao were living together." Only in 1920, partly through letters from friends in France, did Mao take up Marxism. In July 1921 he was "one of the two delegates from Hunan" to the founding congress.

Why this matters: Mao became a Communist late, through his own organizing in Hunan.

Check yourself: Who connected Mao to Beijing University and to Marxism?

Q2 story · Hunan, Guangzhou, and Wuhan · 1923–1927

In February 1927 Mao Reported That Hunan's Peasants Had Risen Against the Landlords

In 1925 Mao went back to his home village to work with peasants.
In 1926 he ran the Nationalists' Peasant Training Institute in Guangzhou.
In February 1927 he reported that peasants had "seized power from landlords."
"A revolution is not a dinner party." (Mao, 1927)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 441–442; Mao Zedong, "Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan" (1927), in Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 2 (1994). Image: Wuchang, 2017.

In the early 1920s Mao organized unions and strikes in Hunan, as the party ordered. In 1923 he joined the Nationalist Party, and in December he sent a gloomy report: Hunan's peasant groups had been crushed (p. 441).

Then he changed direction. In 1925 "he did the opposite, returning to his home village to work with peasants out of the reach of city authorities." In 1926 he became "the director of the Peasant Training Institute," and his trainees "organized peasants in advance of the army" (p. 442).

In January 1927 he toured five Hunan counties, and in February he "submitted a highly positive report to the Communist Party on the revolution among the peasants in Hunan who had seized power from landlords and felt the joy of righting ancient wrongs." The report's most famous lines defend the violence: "A revolution is not a dinner party." The peasants, Mao predicted, would rise "like a mighty storm" and sweep away anyone in their way. Many party leaders, following Moscow, thought he exaggerated.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mao Zedong, "Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan" (1927), in Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 2 (1994).

Why this matters: This report is the first sign that Mao saw the peasants, not the workers, as the main force.

Check yourself: What did Mao see in Hunan that convinced him the peasants could make a revolution?

Q2 story · Hunan and Wuhan · April–September 1927

After the Terror, Mao Concluded That "Political Power Grows Out of the Barrel of a Gun"

In April 1927 the terror crushed Hunan's peasant movement.
In August Mao told party leaders that power "grows out of the barrel of a gun."
In September he led the Autumn Harvest Uprising in Hunan, and it failed.
His conclusion: ideas need an army behind them.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 3 (1995). Image: Wuhan, 1927.

"In April 1927, when Chiang Kaishek unleashed the terror in Shanghai, Mao was in Hunan. Following party instructions, he tried to ignite peasant insurrection, but found that the terror had crushed the movement that only recently had looked so promising to him" (p. 442).

Landlords and soldiers struck back in Hunan as hard as Chiang had in Shanghai. The peasant associations had numbers but no guns.

Ebrey writes that Mao "now wrote a report that emphasized the need to back political ideas with military force, contending, in his oft-quoted phrase, that 'political power is obtained from the barrel of the gun.'" The words come from his speech to an emergency party meeting at Hankou on 7 August 1927, recorded in Schram's edition of his writings. In English the phrase is usually given as "political power grows out of the barrel of a gun."

In September Mao led a rising of peasants, miners and mutinous soldiers in eastern Hunan, the Autumn Harvest Uprising. It failed within days. With the few hundred men left, he headed for the mountains.

Source beyond Ebrey: Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 3 (1995).

Why this matters: From now on Mao's revolution would have its own army.

Q2 story · Jinggangshan, Hunan–Jiangxi border · 1927–1929

With His Last Followers He Took to the Jinggang Mountains in October 1927

His base was a mountain lair once used by secret-society bandits.
He redistributed all the land of the rich.
His troops lacked arms, clothes, and medicine.
Zhu De's army joined him in 1928; He Zizhen became his partner.

Names in This Story

Zhu De Red Army commander, 1886–1976; pronounced "joo duh"

He Zizhen Mao's third wife, 1909–1984; pronounced "huh dzih-jun"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442. Images: 2025; Yan'an, 1937.

"In October 1927 he led his remaining peasant followers into a mountain lair used by secret society members on the border between Hunan and Jiangxi, called Jinggangshan" (p. 442). The first photograph shows those mountains: steep, forested and easy to defend.

Mao was cut off. He "lost contact with Yang Kaihui, who had just given birth to their third son. He also was out of touch with the party hierarchy." He drew in other Communists, among them "nineteen-year-old He Zizhen," from a local landlord family. "She and Mao, then thirty-four years old, became lovers and had a child in 1929." In 1928 the army of Zhu De, a former officer who had joined the Communists, arrived, and the force became the Red Army.

Here Mao tried revolution on the ground. "He pushed through an extreme form of land reform, redistributing all the land of the rich and requiring all the physically able to work. His troops suffered, however, with little in the way of arms or ammunition, clothes, or medicine." The second photograph shows the few survivors ten years on.

Why this matters: Jinggangshan was Mao's first base: an army, a territory and land reform together.

Q2 story · Jiangxi–Fujian border · 1929–1931

In 1929 He Moved to the Jiangxi–Fujian Border and Built the Jiangxi Soviet

In January 1929 Mao left for a better-supplied border region.
There he built what came to be called the Jiangxi Soviet.
Party leaders in Shanghai condemned his rural revolution and his use of force.
Mao fell ill and never obeyed their order to come to Shanghai.

Names in This Story

soviet a government by elected councils, from the Russian word for "council"

Jiangxi pronounced "jyahng-shee"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Stephen C. Averill, Revolution in the Highlands: China's Jinggangshan Base Area (2006). Image: Ruijin, 1931.

"In January 1929, Mao decided to look for a better-supplied base area that would be less vulnerable to Nationalist attacks. His choice was a border region between Jiangxi and Fujian, where he set up what came to be called the Jiangxi Soviet" (p. 442).

A soviet is a government by councils, the Russian word the Communists borrowed. In November 1931 the base became the capital of a Chinese Soviet Republic at Ruijin, with Mao as its chairman; the photograph shows party leaders there that month. Stephen Averill's history of the base areas shows how they grew from local networks of teachers, students and village leaders.

Mao's way was not the party's way. "The party leadership, which could reach him there, quickly condemned him for his views on rural revolution and the role of military force." The leaders, hiding in Shanghai and following Moscow, still believed the revolution would come from city workers. "Mao fell ill and managed to avoid responding to the party's order that he go to Shanghai."

Source beyond Ebrey: Stephen C. Averill, Revolution in the Highlands: China's Jinggangshan Base Area (2006).

Why this matters: Mao built his ideas in the field, often against his own party.

Q2 concept · Rural classes

Concept: Mao Sorted Villagers into Landlords and Rich, Middle, and Poor Peasants

Concept

Rural class categories

Mao's labels for villagers by how they got their living: landlords lived off rents; rich peasants farmed but also rented out land or lent money; middle peasants worked their own land without borrowing or hiring; poor peasants rented land or owned too little to live on; below them came hired hands, porters, and boatmen.

The labels decided who would lose land and who would gain it.
Mao found that most villagers were poor peasants or lower.
So land reform would give land to many more people than it took from.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442.

To answer Question 2 you need these categories, because Mao built his strategy on them. In his study of one county, Ebrey explains, Mao "classified the population into landlords (those who lived off the rents of their lands, subdivided into large, medium, and small landlords), rich peasants (those who rented out some land or made loans but worked the rest themselves), middle peasants (those who worked their own land without borrowing or hiring help), poor peasants (tenants and owners of plots too small to support them), and others, including hired hands, loafers, and those who did such manual labor as boatmen and porters" (p. 442).

Notice what decides the label: not how rich a family looked, but how it got its living, from rent, from its own work, or from working for others.

Then comes the political arithmetic. "The vast majority of the population fell into the category of poor peasant or lower. When land was redistributed, many more would receive land than would lose it." A party that gave land to the many could win their loyalty.

Why this matters: These labels later decided the fate of millions of families in the People's Republic.

Check yourself: What is the difference between a rich peasant and a middle peasant?

Q2 story · Xunwu County, Jiangxi · 1929–1930

In Xunwu County Mao Counted Land, Shops, and Schools, and Found Most People Were Poor

Mao interviewed merchants, clerks, and farmers in one county for weeks.
He recorded who owned land, who rented, and who lent money.
He counted literacy, the post, shops, and even prostitutes.
His aim: learn how a party could be built on a peasant base.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Mao Zedong, Report from Xunwu, trans. Roger R. Thompson (1990). Image: Xunwu, 2018.

"In 1929–1930, Mao did, however, conduct an exhaustive study of rural life in one county in the Jiangxi Soviet, Xunwu County, to learn more about how a party could be built on a peasant base" (p. 442). Xunwu is pronounced "shyun-woo."

The historian Roger Thompson, who translated the survey into English, shows how Mao worked. In May 1930 he sat for days with a small group of local men, a former clerk, a shopkeeper, a poor scholar and farmers, and asked them about everything: the market town's shops, what they sold and who owned them, rents, interest on loans, and who had land.

Ebrey notes the range: "Mao also recorded literacy rates, postal service, shops and services, and even the number of prostitutes." This was the classification you just learned, put to work on real people. Mao's rule was blunt: no investigation, no right to speak.

The result confirmed his strategy: most families were poor peasants or below, and they had most to gain from land reform.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mao Zedong, Report from Xunwu, trans. Roger R. Thompson (1990).

Why this matters: Mao built peasant revolution on what he found in the villages, not on theory alone.

Check yourself: Why would a revolutionary need to count shops and prostitutes?

Q2 story · Changsha · July–November 1930

In 1930 the Red Army's Attacks on Changsha Failed, and the Nationalists Shot Mao's Wife

Party leaders still wanted cities, and ordered the Red Army to take Changsha.
The Red Army held the city briefly in July 1930; a second attack in September failed.
In October the Nationalists arrested Yang Kaihui; on 14 November they shot her.
Friends took the three boys to Shanghai; Mao did not see the two survivors until 1946.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 3 (1995); Philip Short, Mao: A Life (1999). Image: 1920s.

"The Communist Party leadership was still trying to ignite urban uprisings" (p. 442). In the summer of 1930 the leaders in Shanghai ordered the Red Army to seize cities. Ebrey dates the assault on Changsha to October; the record, in Schram's edition and Philip Short's biography, shows Peng Dehuai's troops held Changsha for about ten days at the end of July, and a second attack, which Mao joined, failed in September.

The cost fell on Mao's family. "Not only did the attack fail, but also the Nationalists arrested Yang Kaihui and had her shot." She was arrested near Changsha on 24 October and shot on 14 November 1930, aged twenty-nine. She had refused to denounce Mao. "The three young boys were sent by friends to Shanghai. The youngest died, and Mao did not see the other two until 1946." The photograph shows her with the two older sons.

For Mao the failure confirmed his view: attacking cities wasted the Red Army.

Source beyond Ebrey: Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 3 (1995); Philip Short, Mao: A Life (1999).

Why this matters: The party's city strategy failed again, and Mao paid a personal price.

Pierian Spring · Story · Guangzhou · 11–13 December 1927

In December 1927 a Communist Rising Held Guangzhou for Three Days

Under Comintern orders, the party planned a city rising in Guangzhou.
On 11 December worker guards and cadets seized much of the city and proclaimed a soviet.
Nationalist troops retook it within three days.
Ebrey counts three thousand to four thousand worker revolutionaries executed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution (rev. 1961); C. Martin Wilbur, The Nationalist Revolution in China, 1923–1928 (1984). Image: Guangzhou, 2019.

Ebrey mentions this event in one sentence: "A failed uprising in Guangzhou led to the execution of three thousand to four thousand worker revolutionaries" (p. 440). Here is the story behind it.

By late 1927 Stalin needed a victory in China. His rivals in Moscow, led by Trotsky, said his China policy had failed. So the Comintern's agents in China pressed the party to rise in Guangzhou, where Communist influence among workers and cadets was strong. Harold Isaacs and Martin Wilbur both tell the story.

Before dawn on 11 December, worker guards and cadets of a training regiment seized police stations and government offices and proclaimed the Canton Commune, a city soviet. Few of the city's people joined them. Nationalist troops marched back in, and by 13 December the rising was crushed. Then came the reprisals. Soldiers shot men on the street for the red neckerchiefs the rebels had worn. The memorial in the photograph was built after 1949.

Source beyond Ebrey: Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution (rev. 1961); C. Martin Wilbur, The Nationalist Revolution in China, 1923–1928 (1984).

Why this matters: The rising showed the cost of following Moscow's city strategy after 1927.

Check yourself: Why did the party attempt a city rising after the disasters of that spring?

Pierian Spring · Story · Wuhan, Shanghai, and Nanjing · 1927–1932

Moscow Blamed Chen Duxiu, and the Party Expelled Its Founder

In August 1927 the party removed Chen as its leader.
Moscow blamed him for policies Moscow itself had ordered.
In 1929 the party expelled him for siding with Trotsky.
In 1932 the Nationalists arrested him; he spent five years in prison.

Names in This Story

Chen Duxiu founder of New Youth and first leader of the party, 1879–1942; pronounced "chun doo-shyoh"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 440; Lee Feigon, Chen Duxiu: Founder of the Chinese Communist Party (1983). Images: 1927; Nanjing, 1932.

Ebrey is brief: "On orders of the Comintern, Chen Duxiu was blamed for these disasters and expelled from the Communist Party" (p. 440). You met Chen in Chapter 24 as the founder of New Youth.

The irony is sharp. As secretary general, Chen had doubted the alliance from the start, and he had obeyed Moscow's orders to keep it. When it collapsed, Stalin could not admit his own policy had failed. At an emergency meeting on 7 August 1927, the same meeting where Mao spoke of the barrel of a gun, Chen was removed as leader. Lee Feigon's biography tells his story.

Out of power, Chen read Trotsky's attacks on Stalin's China policy and agreed with them. In November 1929 the party expelled him. He led a small Trotskyist group in Shanghai until the Nationalists arrested him in 1932; the second photograph shows him then. He was released in 1937 and died in 1942.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lee Feigon, Chen Duxiu: Founder of the Chinese Communist Party (1983).

Why this matters: The party's founder became the scapegoat for the Comintern's mistakes.

HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 25 · Lecture 3

Eight Thousand Arrived

The Long March, Japan's Invasion, and the War of 1937

1931–1938 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

This third lecture of Chapter 25 covers 1931 to 1938: the Communists' escape from Jiangxi, which became the Long March, and Japan's advance from Manchuria to full war in 1937 (Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 445–450).

First, why the Communists left Jiangxi, and how a disastrous retreat made Mao the party's leader. Second, how Japanese aggression pushed Chiang Kaishek, against his will, into a second alliance with the Communists, and what the war of 1937 did to Shanghai and Nanjing.

Check yourself: As you go, ask who gained and who lost from each disaster in this story.

Q6 story · Yudu, Jiangxi · October 1934

In October 1934 About 86,000 Communists Broke Out of the Encirclement in Jiangxi

In mid-October 1934, columns crossed by night the Yudu River heading west.
About 86,000 soldiers, cadres, and porters went.
They carried printing presses, files, and the mint's machinery.
No one told them where they were going; the leaders did not know.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Harrison E. Salisbury, The Long March: The Untold Story (1985). Image: Yudu, 2014.

In the third week of October 1934, long columns of men crossed the Yudu River in southern Jiangxi on pontoon bridges, heading west. They moved after dark so that Nationalist planes would not see them. The memorial in the photograph now marks the crossing.

Ebrey: "In October about eighty-six thousand Communist soldiers, cadres, porters, and followers broke out of the encirclement, the start of the much mythologized year-long Long March in search of a new place to set up a base" (p. 445).

It did not look like a heroic march at first. Harrison Salisbury, who interviewed survivors in the 1980s, describes porters struggling under printing presses, X-ray machines, party files and the equipment of the Soviet's mint. The column stretched for many kilometers and moved slowly. Most marchers did not know where they were going. Neither, in truth, did their leaders: the plan was only to break out and join another Communist base to the west.

Source beyond Ebrey: Harrison E. Salisbury, The Long March: The Untold Story (1985).

Why this matters: The Long March began as a retreat from defeat, not as a plan for victory.

Check yourself: Why would an army in retreat carry printing presses and a mint?

Q6 story · Jiangxi · 1933–1934

A Million Nationalist Troops and a Ring of Blockhouses Closed In on the Soviet

Chiang's fifth campaign began in the autumn of 1933 · Ch. 25 L2 · Encirclement ↗
Ebrey counts a million-man force around the Soviet.
Blockhouses and roads tightened the ring and cut off salt.
The Red Army fought pitched battles to hold ground, and lost them.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; William Wei, Counterrevolution in China (1985). Image: 1934.

You saw the start of this campaign in the last lecture. Here is how it ended.

Ebrey: "In the fall of 1934, with the German-planned fifth 'extermination campaign' of the Nationalists encircling them with a million-man force, the Communist Party leadership, without consulting Mao, decided to give up the Jiangxi Soviet" (p. 445). The photograph shows provincial troops assembling for the campaign in 1934.

The fifth campaign worked because Chiang changed his method. As William Wei shows, his armies no longer chased the Red Army into the hills. They built blockhouses and roads and advanced slowly, a ring that grew tighter each month. Salt and cloth stopped reaching the Soviet. Worse, the Red Army's new commanders chose to meet the Nationalists head on, defending fixed lines instead of retreating and striking from ambush as Mao had done. In April 1934 they lost thousands of men defending the town of Guangchang.

By the autumn the Soviet was shrinking, and the leaders decided to leave.

Source beyond Ebrey: William Wei, Counterrevolution in China (1985).

Why this matters: This answers the first half of Question 6: the party marched because it was losing its base.

Check yourself: Why were fixed battle lines a mistake against a larger army?

Q6 story · Ruijin, Jiangxi · 1933–1934

The Party's Leaders Had Moved from Shanghai to Jiangxi in 1933, and Mao Was Pushed Aside

Hunted in Shanghai, the party's leaders moved to the Jiangxi Soviet in January 1933.
Bo Gu, aged twenty-six and trained in Moscow, led the party.
Otto Braun, a German sent by the Comintern, ran the army.
Mao kept his title as chairman of the Soviet but lost his say over the army.

Names in This Story

Bo Gu Qin Bangxian, party leader 1932–1935, 1907–1946; pronounced "baw goo"

Otto Braun Comintern military adviser, called Li De, 1900–1974

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Stuart R. Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 4 (1997); Otto Braun, A Comintern Agent in China, 1932–1939 (1982). Images: 1930s.

Ebrey writes that "in 1932 the Central Committee of the Communist Party gave up trying to foment urban insurrection and joined Mao in the Jiangxi Soviet" (p. 445). Party records give a slightly later date: with the police closing in, the leaders left Shanghai in secret in the winter of 1932 to 1933, and Bo Gu reached the Soviet in late January 1933.

The new arrivals were young men trained in Moscow. Bo Gu, in the first photograph, was twenty-six. With him came, a few months later, Otto Braun, in the second, a German Communist sent by the Comintern as military adviser; the Chinese called him Li De.

"Mao was the chairman of the soviet, but after their arrival, he was on the sidelines, his recommendations often overruled." The newcomers called his guerrilla tactics timid and replaced them with the positional warfare you saw on the last slide. Mao spent much of 1934 ill and out of favor. "Without consulting Mao," Ebrey adds, they decided to abandon the Soviet.

Source beyond Ebrey: Stuart R. Schram, Mao's Road to Power, vol. 4 (1997); Otto Braun, A Comintern Agent in China, 1932–1939 (1982).

Why this matters: Mao's exclusion is the starting point for his rise: when the new strategy failed, his old one looked right.

Check yourself: Why did the Moscow-trained leaders distrust Mao?

Q6 story · Jiangxi · October 1934

Most Wives and Children Stayed Behind; Only About Thirty-Five Women Went

Ebrey counts only thirty-five women on the march.
About 15,000 troops stayed in Jiangxi with the sick and wounded.
Mao's wife He Zizhen, pregnant, was allowed to go.
They left their two-year-old son behind; Mao never found him.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Helen Praeger Young, Choosing Revolution: Chinese Women Soldiers on the Long March (2001). Image: Yan'an, 1937.

Ebrey: "Most wives and children had to be left behind (only thirty-five women joined the march). To protect them and the thousand or so sick or wounded soldiers left behind, about fifteen thousand troops remained in Jiangxi" (p. 445). Counts of the women vary a little; Helen Praeger Young, who interviewed women veterans, writes of about thirty in the main column, and many more in other Red armies.

Mao's own family shows the cost. "Mao's wife, He Zizhen, although pregnant, was allowed to come, but they had to leave their two-year-old child behind with Mao's younger brother. When Mao's brother, like many of those left behind, was killed in 1935, Mao lost track of the child." On the march He Zizhen gave birth and had to leave the baby with a local family; she was later badly wounded in an air raid. The photograph shows her with Mao in Yan'an in 1937, when she was worn out by the march.

The men and women left in Jiangxi were hunted down. Many were killed; others hid in the hills for years.

Source beyond Ebrey: Helen Praeger Young, Choosing Revolution: Chinese Women Soldiers on the Long March (2001).

Why this matters: The march's heroism had a price, paid largely by those who could not go.

Check yourself: Why would the leaders take so few women?

Q6 story · Zunyi, Guizhou · 15–17 January 1935

At Zunyi in January 1935, the Leaders Blamed Braun and Bo Gu, and Mao Rose to the Top

The marchers had lost more than half their strength crossing the Xiang River.
At Zunyi about twenty party and army leaders met for three days.
They blamed Braun and Bo Gu for fighting from fixed positions.
Mao joined the top leadership and gained a key role in military affairs.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 446; Benjamin Yang, "The Zunyi Conference as One Step in Mao's Rise to Power," China Quarterly 106 (1986). Image: Zunyi, 2018.

By December 1934 the march was a disaster. Crossing the Xiang River in Guangxi, under Nationalist attack, the column lost tens of thousands of men; by most accounts fewer than half of those who left Jiangxi were still in the ranks. In January 1935 the Red Army took the town of Zunyi in Guizhou and stopped to rest.

There, in the house in the photograph, the leaders met. Ebrey: "When the marchers reached Zunyi in Guizhou province in early 1935, they paused to hold an enlarged meeting of the Politburo and assess their strategy." She counts "seventeen veteran party leaders," including Mao, Otto Braun, and Deng Xiaoping "to take notes"; party records list about twenty, with Braun and his interpreter attending without a vote. "Blame was placed on Braun and others who had urged positional warfare." "Mao was named to the Standing Committee of the Politburo and given new responsibility for military affairs" (p. 446).

Benjamin Yang shows that Zunyi was one step, not the whole climb: Mao won full command only over the following months, as his tactics kept the army alive.

Source beyond Ebrey: Benjamin Yang, "The Zunyi Conference as One Step in Mao's Rise to Power," China Quarterly 106 (1986).

Why this matters: This is the second half of Question 6: the march's failures gave Mao his chance.

Check yourself: Why would a defeat help Mao, who had not planned the strategy?

Q6 story · Guizhou to Shaanxi · 1935

Rivers, Snow Mountains, and Grasslands: A Year of Retreat

The marchers zigzagged through Guizhou, then turned north toward Sichuan.
In May 1935 they crossed the Dadu River on the chain bridge at Luding.
They climbed snow-covered passes and crossed swampy grasslands.
They covered thousands of kilometers; the party later said 25,000 li.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 445–446; Salisbury, The Long March (1985); Ed Jocelyn and Andrew McEwen, The Long March (2006). Images: map; museum, 2011.

Follow the red line on the map. From Zunyi the marchers zigzagged through Guizhou to shake off pursuit, then crossed the Jinsha River and turned north. Ebrey: "Month after month the Red Army kept retreating, often just a step or two ahead of the pursuing Nationalist troops. Casualties were enormous. The farther west they went, the more rugged the terrain; as they skirted Tibet, they also had to deal with bitter cold" (p. 445).

The most famous episode came in May 1935 at Luding, where the army crossed the Dadu River on an old chain bridge. Party histories tell of volunteers swinging across the chains under fire; historians who retraced the route think the fighting was smaller than the legend. The second picture shows one of the bridge's chains. Then came the snow passes and the marshy grasslands, where many died of cold and hunger.

How far? Ebrey says "almost 10,000 kilometers"; the party's figure is 25,000 li, about 12,500 kilometers. Two British walkers, Ed Jocelyn and Andrew McEwen, retraced the main route in 2002–2003 and measured about 6,000. All agree it was thousands of kilometers, mostly on foot.

Source beyond Ebrey: Salisbury, The Long March (1985); Ed Jocelyn and Andrew McEwen, The Long March (2006).

Why this matters: The hardships became the raw material of the legend.

Check yourself: Why do distances in such stories tend to grow?

Q6 story · Northern Shaanxi · October 1935

Perhaps 8,000 of the Original Marchers Reached Shaanxi in October 1935

In October 1935 Mao's column reached a small Communist base in northern Shaanxi.
Ebrey counts about 8,000 of the original marchers.
With recruits and other Red armies the total came to nearly 20,000.
Nanjing saw a victory; the marchers saw proof of their destiny.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 446; Salisbury, The Long March (1985). Image: Yan'an, 1937.

In October 1935, a year after leaving Jiangxi, Mao's column reached a small Communist base in the dry loess hills of northern Shaanxi. The photograph shows four of the leaders who made it, Mao, Zhu De, Zhou Enlai and Bo Gu, two years later in Yan'an.

Ebrey: "By the time they found an area in Shaanxi where they could establish a new base, they had marched almost 10,000 kilometers. Only about eight thousand of those who began the march made it the whole way, though some new recruits and communists from other base areas had joined en route, to bring the total to nearly twenty thousand" (pp. 445–446). Other counts of the survivors differ a little; all agree that about nine in ten of those who left Jiangxi did not arrive.

Then Ebrey makes the key point. "To the Nationalists in Nanjing, the Long March must have seemed a huge victory." The Communists had been driven into one of the poorest corners of China. "Those who made the Long March, however, saw it as a victory. That they had overcome such daunting odds reinforced their belief that they were men of destiny with a near-sacred mission to remake China" (p. 446).

Source beyond Ebrey: Salisbury, The Long March (1985).

Why this matters: Defeat and victory depended on who was telling the story.

Check yourself: Why could both sides call the Long March a victory?

Q6 concept · Founding myth

Concept: A Founding Myth Turns a Disaster into Proof of Destiny

Concept

Founding myth

A story a nation or movement tells about its origins, true in its outline but shaped and polished in the retelling, so that suffering becomes proof that the group was chosen to win and its leaders deserve to rule.

The Long March became the founding myth of the People's Republic.
It turned a near-destruction into a heroic trial.
It made Mao the leader who had saved the revolution.
Veterans of the march held power in China for fifty years.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 445–446; Jocelyn and McEwen, The Long March (2006).

You need this concept to answer the second half of Question 6: how the Long March strengthened Mao.

Every nation has founding stories. Americans tell of Valley Forge, where Washington's army froze and starved in the winter of 1777 to 1778 and came out stronger. Such stories are not lies; the suffering was real. But they are shaped in the retelling. Losses become sacrifices, lucky escapes become proof of good leadership, and the survivors become heroes with a right to lead.

Ebrey calls the Long March "much mythologized" (p. 445), and she explains the effect: the survivors came to believe "they were men of destiny with a near-sacred mission to remake China" (p. 446). Mao told the story himself, in 1935: the march, he said, was a manifesto, a propaganda force and a seeding-machine for revolution. After 1949, paintings, songs and textbooks retold it, with Mao at the center and Zunyi as the turning point. Long March veterans, Deng Xiaoping among them, ruled China into the 1990s.

Source beyond Ebrey: Jocelyn and McEwen, The Long March (2006).

Why this matters: The myth, as much as the march, gave Mao his authority.

Check yourself: What makes a founding myth different from a plain lie?

Q7 recap · Japan and China · 1895–1928

Recap: Japan Took Taiwan in 1895, Won South Manchuria in 1905, and Pressed Its Demands in 1915

1895: after defeating China, Japan took Taiwan · Ch. 18 L4 · Shimonoseki ↗
1905: after defeating Russia, Japan gained a dominant position in southern Manchuria · Ch. 24 L2 · Borders ↗
1915: the Twenty-One Demands won Japan broad economic privileges · Ch. 24 L2 · Twenty-One Demands ↗
1928: Japanese officers assassinated Zhang Zuolin, the warlord of Manchuria.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 448.

Ebrey begins the Japanese story with a summary you already know: "From the time of the May Fourth protests in 1919, Chinese patriots saw Japan as the gravest threat to China's sovereignty. In 1895, Japan had won Taiwan. In 1905, after an impressive victory over Russia, it gained a dominant position in southern Manchuria. In 1915, by applying pressure on Yuan Shikai, Japan had secured a broad range of economic privileges" (p. 448). Each link on this slide goes back to the lecture where you learned it.

Now one new piece. Japan's army in Manchuria, the Kwantung Army, guarded the South Manchurian Railway. Its officers, Ebrey writes, were "full of militarists who kept pushing Japanese civil authorities to let the army occupy the entire area." In June 1928 some of them blew up the train of Zhang Zuolin, the warlord of Manchuria, "hoping for a crisis that would allow Japan to extend its power base." Instead his son Zhang Xueliang, whom you met in the last lecture, joined the Nationalists.

Why this matters: By 1930 Japan's army in Manchuria was ready to act on its own.

Q7 story · Shenyang (Mukden), Manchuria · 18 September 1931

In September 1931 Japanese Officers Bombed Their Own Railway and Seized Manchuria

On 18 September 1931 Japanese officers set off a bomb on their own railway.
They blamed Chinese troops and occupied Shenyang "in defense."
Within months the Kwantung Army held all of Manchuria.
The League of Nations found China in the right but imposed no sanctions.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Louise Young, Japan's Total Empire (1998). Image: Shenyang, 1931.

On the night of 18 September 1931, a small bomb went off beside the South Manchurian Railway just north of Shenyang, which foreigners called Mukden. The damage was so slight that a train passed over the spot minutes later. The photograph shows Japanese investigators at the scene.

Ebrey: "Japanese soldiers set a bomb on the Southern Manchurian Railroad to give themselves an excuse to occupy Shenyang 'in defense.' China did not attempt to resist militarily but did appeal to the League of Nations, which recognized China as being in the right but imposed no real sanctions on Japan" (p. 450). The plotters were Kwantung Army staff officers acting without orders from Tokyo; the government at home, as Louise Young shows, followed where the army led, cheered on by the Japanese press.

Zhang Xueliang, under orders from Chiang to avoid war, withdrew his troops south of the Great Wall. A League commission led by Lord Lytton reported in 1932 that Japan's action was not self-defense. Japan's answer, in 1933, was to leave the League.

Source beyond Ebrey: Louise Young, Japan's Total Empire (1998).

Why this matters: Mukden began fourteen years of war between Japan and China, and showed that the League would not stop it.

Check yourself: Why did Chiang choose to appeal to the League rather than fight?

Q7 story · Changchun, Manchuria · 1932–1945

Japan Made the Last Qing Emperor the Figurehead of "Manchukuo"

In 1932 Japan set up a new state in Manchuria, "Manchukuo."
Its head was Puyi, who had abdicated as a child in 1912 · Ch. 24 L1 · Abdication ↗
In 1934 Japan made him emperor, but Japanese officers ruled.
In January 1932 Japan also attacked Shanghai, and withdrew after four months.

Names in This Story

Puyi the last Qing emperor, reign name Xuantong (1908–1912); emperor of Manchukuo as Kangde (1934–1945); 1906–1967; pronounced "poo-yee"

Course film · The Last Emperor

Bernardo Bertolucci's film (1987) follows Puyi from the Forbidden City to Manchukuo and prison. As you watch, ask how the film shows his years as Japan's puppet, and what it leaves out.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Rana Mitter, The Manchurian Myth (2000). Image: about 1934.

"In Manchuria they set up a puppet regime, making the last Qing emperor the nominal head of Manchukuo ('Manchu land')" (p. 450). A puppet regime is a government that looks independent but is controlled by a foreign power. The photograph shows Puyi, whom you saw abdicate as a six-year-old in 1912, now in the uniform of the Kangde emperor.

Puyi had lived in Tianjin's Japanese concession since being expelled from the Forbidden City in 1924. In 1932 the Japanese made him chief executive of their new state, with its capital at Changchun, and in 1934 emperor. He hoped to restore his dynasty; Japanese officers made every decision. Rana Mitter shows how Manchukuo's officials tried to persuade the people that it was a real, modern Manchu state.

Meanwhile, Ebrey adds, "in January 1932, Japan attacked Shanghai to retaliate against anti-Japanese protests." The bombing of civilian districts "was widely condemned," and Japan withdrew after four months.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rana Mitter, The Manchurian Myth (2000).

Why this matters: Manchukuo showed that Japan meant to keep what it took, and to rule through Chinese and Manchu faces.

Check yourself: Why would Japan want a Qing emperor as the face of its new state?

Q7 story · Beiping and Nanjing · 1931–1936

Students Demanded War, but Chiang Said China Must Unite Before It Could Fight

Anger at Japan brought boycotts and "national salvation" leagues.
Chiang thought Japan's firepower too strong to fight yet.
His motto: first internal pacification, then external resistance.
On 9 December 1935 thousands of students marched in Beiping for war.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; John Israel, Student Nationalism in China, 1927–1937 (1966); Parks M. Coble, Facing Japan (1991). Image: Beiping, 1935.

"Anger at Japanese aggression heightened Chinese nationalism and led to the formation of national salvation leagues and boycotts of Japanese goods" (p. 450). Merchants refused to sell Japanese goods; students formed leagues and demanded war.

Chiang refused. "Chiang, like most other military men of the day, did not see any point in putting up a fight when Japanese firepower was so clearly superior. Chiang was convinced that all Chinese would have to be united under one leader before China could hope to thwart Japan." His slogan was "first internal pacification, then external resistance": destroy the Communists, then fight Japan. Parks Coble shows how this policy cost him public support, as Japan pressed into north China in 1933 and 1935.

On 9 December 1935, students in Beiping, the photograph on this slide, marched through the snow against Japan's plans for an autonomous north China. Police turned fire hoses on them. John Israel's history of student nationalism shows how this December Ninth Movement spread to other cities, and how many of its leaders later joined the Communists.

Source beyond Ebrey: John Israel, Student Nationalism in China, 1927–1937 (1966); Parks M. Coble, Facing Japan (1991).

Why this matters: Chiang's refusal to fight Japan was becoming his greatest political weakness.

Check yourself: Was Chiang's "unity first" policy cowardly or realistic? Defend your view.

Q7 story · Xi'an, Shaanxi · 12 December 1936

In December 1936 Chiang's Own Generals Kidnapped Him at Xi'an

Chiang ordered the Manchurian troops at Xi'an to attack the Communists.
Driven from their homes by Japan, they wanted to fight Japan instead.
On 12 December 1936 their commanders seized Chiang.
They demanded he end the civil war and unite with the Communists against Japan.

Names in This Story

Zhang Xueliang the "Young Marshal," son of Zhang Zuolin, 1901–2001; pronounced "jahng shweh-lyahng"

Yang Hucheng commander of the Shaanxi army at Xi'an, 1893–1949; pronounced "yahng hoo-chung"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Rana Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009). Image: October 1936.

Ebrey: "In 1936 troops that had been driven out of Manchuria by the Japanese were ordered by Chiang to blockade the Communists" in the northwest. "When Chiang came to Xian, they kidnapped him and refused to release him until he agreed to form a united front with the Communists against Japan. These troops did not want to be fighting other Chinese when the Japanese had occupied their home towns" (p. 450). (Ebrey places the Communists at Yan'an; in December 1936 their headquarters was still at Bao'an, and they moved into Yan'an the next month.)

The photograph shows the three men two months earlier. Zhang Xueliang had lost Manchuria in 1931; his Northeastern Army had then been sent to fight the Communists and had lost badly. Yang Hucheng commanded the local Shaanxi army. Both had been talking secretly with the Communists.

Chiang flew to Xi'an in December to push them to attack. Before dawn on 12 December, Zhang's guards stormed his lodgings at the Huaqing hot springs outside the city. Chiang fled up the hillside in his nightclothes and was found hiding among the rocks.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rana Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009).

Why this matters: His own generals forced the choice Chiang had refused to make.

Q7 story · Xi'an to Nanjing · December 1936

Stalin Told the Communists to Keep Chiang Alive, and the Civil War Paused

The Communists did not plan the kidnapping.
Stalin urged them to keep Chiang alive as China's leader against Japan.
Zhou Enlai joined the talks, and on 25 December Chiang was released.
Zhang Xueliang went with him, and spent decades under arrest.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009). Image: December 1936.

What would the Communists do with their greatest enemy in their allies' hands? Some leaders wanted Chiang tried and shot. Ebrey: "The Communists played no part in the kidnapping but joined the negotiations when Stalin urged them to keep Chiang alive and create a nationwide united front against Japan" (p. 450). Stalin feared Japan on his own eastern border; only Chiang, he judged, could hold China together against it.

Zhou Enlai flew to Xi'an. Soong Meiling, Chiang's wife, flew in too, with her brother T. V. Soong. Chiang never signed anything, but on Christmas Day 1936 he was released. The photograph shows him and Soong Meiling being welcomed home. Jay Taylor's biography argues that Chiang had already been moving toward a deal with the Communists; the kidnapping forced the pace.

Zhang Xueliang, in a gesture of loyalty, flew back with Chiang. He was tried and then kept under house arrest, in China and later on Taiwan, for more than fifty years. Yang Hucheng was imprisoned and killed in 1949.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009).

Why this matters: Xi'an ended the anti-Communist campaigns and saved the Communists at their weakest moment.

Check yourself: Why would Stalin protect the man who had massacred Communists in 1927?

Q7 concept · Second United Front

Concept: The Second United Front Joined Two Enemies Against Japan, 1937–1941

Concept

Second United Front

The alliance of the Nationalists and the Communists against Japan, agreed after the Xi'an Incident and announced in September 1937. The Red Army became, in name, part of the national army, but each party kept its own troops, territory, and aims.

The First United Front (1923–1927) ended in the Shanghai massacre.
The Second joined the same two parties against Japan.
The Communists kept their own army and their own base area.
By 1941 the alliance had collapsed in fighting, though not formally ended.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 450–451; Lyman Van Slyke, "The Chinese Communist Movement during the Sino-Japanese War," Cambridge History of China 13 (1986).

You need this concept to answer Question 7.

You met the First United Front in the first lecture: the Nationalists and Communists working together, with Soviet help, from 1923 until Chiang turned on the Communists in Shanghai in 1927. The Second United Front joined the same two enemies, this time against Japan.

It was a loose alliance. In September 1937, after war had begun, the two parties announced it publicly. The main Red Army was renamed the Eighth Route Army and the troops left in the south became the New Fourth Army, both in name part of the national army. But the Communists kept their own commanders, their own base at Yan'an, and their own plans for China's future. Lyman Van Slyke shows how both sides used the alliance to strengthen themselves. In January 1941 Nationalist troops attacked the New Fourth Army, and the Front was dead in all but name.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lyman Van Slyke, "The Chinese Communist Movement during the Sino-Japanese War," Cambridge History of China 13 (1986).

Why this matters: The Second United Front gave the Communists eight years to grow.

Check yourself: How did the Second United Front differ from the First?

Q7 story · Wanping, near Beiping · 7 July 1937

In July 1937 a Clash at the Marco Polo Bridge Became Full War

On 7 July 1937 Japanese troops on night exercises traded shots with Chinese troops.
Japan demanded to search the walled town of Wanping; the Chinese refused.
This time Chiang decided to fight.
Japan sent more divisions, and by August the war had spread to Shanghai.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013). Image: 1937.

The Marco Polo Bridge, called Lugou Bridge in Chinese, crosses a river about fifteen kilometers southwest of Beiping. Marco Polo had admired it in the thirteenth century. The photograph is a Japanese reconnaissance picture: the bridge, and across the river the walled town of Wanping, held by Chinese troops.

On the night of 7 July 1937, Japanese troops stationed nearby under a treaty right were on night exercises. Shots were fired; one Japanese soldier was reported missing, though he soon turned up. The Japanese demanded to search Wanping, the Chinese refused, and fighting began. Ebrey writes that "the Japanese staged another incident as an excuse for taking more territory." Rana Mitter and many other historians now think this clash, unlike Mukden, was not planned; what made it a war was the choice of both governments to escalate.

Ebrey explains Chiang's choice: "Chiang was probably hoping to inflict a quick defeat to convince Japan that the Nanjing government was a power to be reckoned with so Japan would negotiate with him" (p. 450). Instead Japan poured in troops, and Beiping and Tianjin fell by the end of July.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013).

Why this matters: For China, the Second World War began here, in July 1937.

Check yourself: Why did Chiang fight in 1937 when he had refused in 1931?

Q8 story · Shanghai · August–November 1937

Chiang's Best Troops Held Shanghai for Three Months and Took 250,000 Casualties

In August 1937 Chiang opened a second front at Shanghai.
He sent in the German-trained divisions he had spent a decade building.
For three months they held out under shelling and bombing.
Ebrey counts 250,000 Chinese killed or wounded, against 40,000 Japanese.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Peter Harmsen, Shanghai 1937 (2013). Image: Shanghai, 1937.

In August 1937 Chiang chose to fight where the whole world could see: Shanghai, with its foreign concessions and foreign press. He hoped, Peter Harmsen argues, that the Western powers would step in.

Ebrey: "He used his best troops to hold off the Japanese at Shanghai for three months. He asked for an all-out stand, and his troops courageously persisted despite heavy shelling and bombing, absorbing 250,000 killed or wounded (compared to 40,000 Japanese casualties)" (p. 450). Other estimates differ, but all show a terrible imbalance. The men in the photograph wear gas masks; their German-style helmets show that they belonged to Chiang's elite divisions, trained by the German advisers you met in the last lecture.

No foreign power intervened. In November the Japanese landed behind the Chinese lines at Hangzhou Bay, and the defense collapsed. "When Shanghai fell, the Nationalist troops streamed toward the Nationalist capital, Nanjing" (p. 450). Most of the army Chiang had spent a decade building was gone.

Source beyond Ebrey: Peter Harmsen, Shanghai 1937 (2013).

Why this matters: Shanghai showed that China would fight, and cost Chiang the army he needed later.

Check yourself: Was the stand at Shanghai worth what it cost? Defend your view.

Nanjing · December 1937–January 1938

In December 1937 Japanese Troops Took Nanjing and Killed Civilians and Prisoners for Seven Weeks

Japanese troops entered Nanjing on 13 December 1937.
For seven weeks they killed prisoners and civilians and raped women.
A few foreigners ran a Safety Zone that sheltered some 200,000 people.
The death toll is still disputed; most historians say tens of thousands to over 200,000.

Documentary · The Rape of Nanking

Video coming soon. The documentary will be added here.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Joshua A. Fogel, ed., The Nanjing Massacre in History and Historiography (2000); Takashi Yoshida, The Making of the "Rape of Nanking" (2006). Image: Nanjing, 13 December 1937.

This slide is about one of the worst atrocities of the war, and it shows no pictures of the violence. Ebrey: after "the Japanese easily took Nanjing in December 1937, they went on a rampage, massacring somewhere between 40,000 and 300,000 civilians and fugitive soldiers, raping perhaps 20,000 women, and laying the city waste. The seven weeks of mayhem was widely reported in the foreign press, where it was labeled the Rape of Nanking" (p. 450).

Why such a range? Records were lost, soldiers and civilians were mixed together, and the numbers became political. The postwar Tokyo tribunal judged that more than 200,000 were killed; the Chinese government's figure is 300,000; some Japanese writers have denied the massacre entirely. Joshua Fogel's and Takashi Yoshida's books trace this argument.

The photograph shows members of the International Committee, the twenty-odd Westerners who stayed. John Rabe, in the center, was a German businessman and a Nazi Party member; Minnie Vautrin, an American, sheltered thousands of women at Ginling College. Their Safety Zone, a few square kilometers in the city center, sheltered some 200,000 or more people.

Source beyond Ebrey: Joshua A. Fogel, ed., The Nanjing Massacre in History and Historiography (2000); Takashi Yoshida, The Making of the "Rape of Nanking" (2006).

Why this matters: Nanjing still shapes how Chinese and Japanese remember the war.

Check yourself: Why do historians still disagree about the number of victims?

Q8 story · Huayuankou, Henan · June 1938

In 1938 Chiang's Engineers Broke the Yellow River Dikes to Stop the Japanese

In June 1938 Chinese troops broke the Yellow River dikes to block the Japanese advance.
The flood covered more than 4,000 villages.
Hundreds of thousands died, and about 2 million lost their homes.
It delayed the Japanese for only about three months.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 450; Diana Lary, "Drowned Earth," War in History 8 (2001); Micah S. Muscolino, The Ecology of War in China (2015). Image: Henan, June 1938.

"Civilian casualties were also inflicted by the Nationalist government. When the Chinese had to retreat from Kaifeng, Chiang ordered his engineers to blow up the dikes on the Yellow River, creating a gigantic flood that engulfed more than four thousand villages" and "left 2 million homeless. It delayed the Japanese for only three months" (p. 450). The break was made at Huayuankou, near Zhengzhou, on 9 June 1938.

Ebrey says the flood "drowned some three hundred thousand people." Later research puts the toll higher. Diana Lary and Micah Muscolino, counting deaths from the famine and disease that followed, give at least 400,000 to 500,000, and a postwar Chinese estimate was higher still. The river did not return to its old course until 1947.

Look at the caption. The photograph was released by the Japanese, who blamed the flood on the Chinese and filmed their own soldiers "rescuing" the victims. The Nationalist government at first blamed Japanese bombing. Both sides used the disaster as propaganda.

Source beyond Ebrey: Diana Lary, "Drowned Earth," War in History 8 (2001); Micah S. Muscolino, The Ecology of War in China (2015).

Why this matters: The war killed Chinese by their own government's choices as well as by Japan's.

Check yourself: Can a government ever justify flooding its own people to stop an invader?

Pierian Spring · Bao'an, northern Shaanxi · July–October 1936

In 1936 an American Reporter, Edgar Snow, Spent Four Months with the Red Army

In the summer of 1936 Snow slipped through the blockade to the Red capital at Bao'an.
He interviewed Mao for many nights, and Mao told him his life story.
His book Red Star Over China (1937) made the Long March famous.
Readers met the Communists as patriots and reformers.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 447; Edgar Snow, Red Star Over China (1937); John Maxwell Hamilton, Edgar Snow: A Biography (1988). Images: 1936; 1938.

Until 1936 almost no outsider had seen the Red Army since it left Jiangxi. Then Edgar Snow, an American journalist from Missouri who had worked in China since 1928, got through the Nationalist blockade with help from contacts in the party. He reached the Red capital at Bao'an in July and stayed until October.

Ebrey describes what he saw: "When the American journalist Edgar Snow visited Yan'an in 1936, the survivors of the Long March appeared to him to be an earthy group of committed patriots and egalitarian social reformers, full of optimism and purpose" (p. 447). (In 1936 the Red capital was Bao'an; Yan'an came in January 1937.) Mao, in long night interviews, gave Snow the fullest account of his own life he ever told anyone. The first photograph shows Snow with Xu Teli, Mao's old teacher, and Huang Hua, his interpreter, later China's foreign minister.

Red Star Over China came out in London in 1937 and was read around the world, and in Chinese translation. John Maxwell Hamilton's biography shows how much of the Long March story the world first learned from it, as the party told it.

Source beyond Ebrey: Edgar Snow, Red Star Over China (1937); John Maxwell Hamilton, Edgar Snow: A Biography (1988).

Why this matters: Snow's book carried the founding myth far beyond China.

Check yourself: What are the strengths and dangers of a story told by one reporter, guided by his hosts?

Pierian Spring · Yan'an, northern Shaanxi · 1937–1947

In January 1937 the Party Moved into Yan'an, a Town of Cave Houses

Yan'an was a market town in the dry loess hills of northern Shaanxi.
Many people lived in caves dug into the soft yellow cliffs.
The party's leaders lived and worked there from 1937 to 1947.
The hilltop pagoda became the symbol of the revolution.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 446–447; Mark Selden, China in Revolution: The Yenan Way Revisited (1995). Image: Yan'an, 2012.

Ebrey: "From 1936 to 1946 the Communist Party made its base at Yan'an, a market town in central Shaanxi where homes were often built by cutting caves into the loess soil cliffs" (pp. 446–447). Party records give the exact dates: the Central Committee moved into Yan'an on 13 January 1937 and left it on 18 March 1947, when Nationalist troops attacked.

Loess is fine, wind-blown yellow soil that can be dug easily and stands firm, so cave houses, cool in summer and warm in winter, had been common there for centuries. Mao and the other leaders lived in such caves. "They lived in caves, ate simple food, and showed no disdain for the peasants whom they were mobilizing to fight against the Japanese" (p. 447).

The pagoda in the photograph, on the hill above the town, appears on Communist posters, badges and stamps. Mark Selden's classic study, The Yenan Way, argued that Yan'an's reforms won the peasants over; in his later edition he also faced its darker side, which you will meet in the next lecture.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mark Selden, China in Revolution: The Yenan Way Revisited (1995).

Why this matters: Yan'an became the second great setting of the founding myth.

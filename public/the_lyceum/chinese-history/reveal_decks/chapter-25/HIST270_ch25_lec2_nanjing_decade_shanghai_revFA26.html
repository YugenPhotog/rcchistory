HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 25 · Lecture 2

Paris of the East

Chiang Kaishek's Nanjing Decade and the City of Shanghai

1928–1937 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome to the second lecture of Chapter 25. It covers the ten years from 1928 to 1937, which historians call the Nanjing Decade, after the capital of Chiang Kaishek's government (Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 442–445).

It has three parts. First, what Chiang's government built and where it fell short. Second, how Chiang fought the Communists, and which foreign models he borrowed. Third, Shanghai, which foreigners called the Paris of the East, and the women who worked in its mills.

Check yourself: As you go, keep two lists: what changed for city people, and what changed for villagers.

Q3 story · Nanjing · 1 June 1929

In 1929 Sun Yatsen's Coffin Was Carried Up a Mountain to a Vast New Tomb Outside Nanjing

Sun had died in Beijing in 1925; his body waited four years for a tomb.
On 1 June 1929 it was carried south and up 392 granite steps on Purple Mountain.
Chiang walked beside the coffin as Sun's heir.
A modern state was claiming Sun's legacy, in its new capital.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Charles D. Musgrove, China's Contested Capital: Architecture, Ritual, and Response in Nanjing (2013). Image: Nanjing, 1929.

On 1 June 1929, a coffin was carried up a mountainside outside Nanjing, past hundreds of officials and thousands of onlookers. Inside lay Sun Yatsen, the father of the Republic, who had died of cancer in Beijing in 1925.

Sun had asked to be buried on Purple Mountain, near the tomb of the first Ming emperor. His party held a design competition, and a young architect, Lü Yanzhi, won it with a blue-roofed hall at the top of a long stairway. The historian Charles Musgrove shows how carefully the Nationalists staged the burial: a special train from Beijing, a procession through the new avenues of Nanjing, and Chiang Kaishek at the coffin's side as Sun's chosen heir.

The photograph shows the coffin climbing the steps toward the memorial hall.

Ebrey sets the scene for this lecture: after 1928 the Nationalists ruled from Nanjing, and "Western observers were more optimistic about the prospects for China than they had been for decades" (p. 442).

Source beyond Ebrey: Charles D. Musgrove, China's Contested Capital: Architecture, Ritual, and Response in Nanjing (2013).

Why this matters: Chiang's government wanted to look like the rightful heir of Sun's revolution, and a capital is a stage.

Check yourself: Why would a new government spend so much on a funeral?

Q3 story · Nanjing and Manchuria · 1928

In 1928 Three Key Warlords Joined Chiang, Manchuria Raised His Flag, and Nanjing Became the Capital

In 1928 three key warlords pledged loyalty to the Nationalists.
In December Zhang Xueliang of Manchuria raised the Nationalist flag.
Nanjing became the capital, as it had been for the Taiping and for Sun in 1912 · Ch. 18 L2 · Heavenly Capital ↗
The powers quickly recognized the new government.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Jonathan D. Spence, The Search for Modern China (3rd ed., 2013). Image: about 1930.

"The decision of the Nationalist Party to purge itself of communists did not delay the military unification of the country, and in 1928 the Nationalists gained the allegiance of three key warlords to reunite the country" (p. 442). The northern march ended when the Nationalist armies entered Beijing in June 1928. At the end of that year Zhang Xueliang, the young warlord of Manchuria, raised the Nationalist flag. The photograph shows him with T. V. Soong, Chiang's finance minister.

Unity was partly on paper. The great warlords kept their own armies and provinces, and in 1930 several of them fought a bloody war against Chiang before they were beaten.

Ebrey says Nanjing had not been "used as a capital since the early Ming Dynasty." In fact you have met it twice as a capital since then: as the Taiping Heavenly Capital from 1853 to 1864, and as Sun Yatsen's provisional capital in 1912. Beijing was renamed Beiping, "northern peace," because it was no longer a capital.

Source beyond Ebrey: Jonathan D. Spence, The Search for Modern China (3rd ed., 2013).

Why this matters: For the first time since 1916, one government claimed all of China and the world accepted it.

Check yourself: Why was this unity weaker than it looked?

Q3 story · Nanjing · 1928–1933

Men Trained Abroad Ran the Ministries, and the Powers Began to Give Back Their Privileges

Men with degrees from Harvard, Columbia, and Berlin ran key ministries.
China won back control of its tariffs in 1928–1930.
It took back the Maritime Customs, the Salt Administration, and the Post Office.
Foreign concessions shrank, but extraterritoriality survived for the great powers · Ch. 18 L1 · Extraterritoriality ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; William C. Kirby, "The Internationalization of China," China Quarterly 150 (1997). Image: 1930s.

Meet T. V. Soong, the man in the photograph. He was the brother of Sun Yatsen's widow and of Chiang's new wife, and he held degrees from Harvard and Columbia. As finance minister from 1928 he set up a central bank and balanced budgets as best he could while Chiang spent heavily on armies.

Soong was typical. Ebrey writes that "men who had studied in Western countries were appointed to many key government posts" (p. 442). Engineers, economists and diplomats trained abroad filled the new ministries.

They won real gains. "Tariff autonomy was recovered, as well as control over the Maritime Customs, Salt Administration, and Post Office." China could set its own import duties for the first time since the Opium War, and customs money now paid for the government. Ebrey adds that foreign concessions were reduced from thirty-three to thirteen. But extraterritoriality "was eliminated for some minor countries" only; Britain and the United States gave it up only in 1943. The historian William Kirby calls this diplomacy one of the regime's real successes.

Source beyond Ebrey: William C. Kirby, "The Internationalization of China," China Quarterly 150 (1997).

Why this matters: These were the first steps in undoing the unequal treaties you studied in Chapter 18.

Check yourself: Which of these gains would matter most to the government's budget, and why?

Q3 and Q11 story · Nanjing · 1929–1931

A Land Law Capped Rents, and a New Civil Code Made Marriage Monogamous and Let Wives Sue for Divorce

The Land Law of 1930 capped rents at 37.5 percent of the main crop.
The Civil Code of 1929–1931 recognized only one wife.
Wives could sue for divorce, and daughters could inherit like sons.
Few landlords obeyed the rent cap, and many men kept concubines.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Kathryn Bernhardt, Women and Property in China, 960–1949 (1999); Lisa Tran, Concubines in Court (2015). Image: Wuhan, 1928.

The guide asks you to copy this sentence (p. 442): "Men who had studied in Western countries were appointed to many key government posts, and progressive policies were adopted, such as a new land law limiting rents and a new marriage law outlawing concubinage and allowing women to initiate divorce."

Here is what those laws said. The Land Law of 1930 limited rent to 37.5 percent of the main harvest, down from the half or more that many tenants paid. The new Civil Code, issued in books from 1929 to 1931, defined marriage as one man and one woman, let a wife sue for divorce, and, as Kathryn Bernhardt shows, let daughters inherit family property equally with sons.

Ebrey says the law outlawed concubinage. The historian Lisa Tran's study of court cases shows something more cautious: the code simply did not recognize concubines as wives, and a wife could use her husband's concubine as grounds for divorce. Taking a concubine was not made a crime. The photograph shows the kind of educated young women who used these laws.

Source beyond Ebrey: Kathryn Bernhardt, Women and Property in China, 960–1949 (1999); Lisa Tran, Concubines in Court (2015).

Why this matters: On paper, these were some of the most progressive laws in the world for women.

Check yourself: Who could actually use these laws, and who could not?

Q3 story · Nanjing and the cities · 1930s

Electricity, Engineers, and Doctors: The Cities Modernized

Electricity, running water, and paved avenues reached the big cities.
A professional class of scientists, engineers, architects, and doctors grew.
Many had studied abroad, and they ran new universities and institutes.
Factories, banks, and railways grew fast until 1937.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; William C. Kirby, "Engineering China," in Becoming Chinese, ed. Wen-hsin Yeh (2000). Image: Nanjing, 1930s.

Walk through Nanjing in 1935 and you would see the new capital taking shape: a wide avenue named for Sun Yatsen, government ministries in a mix of Chinese and Western styles, and the great hall of Central University, the photograph on this slide, founded in 1928.

Ebrey stresses that the regime's patriotism was real. "Chiang was a patriot, however, and wanted a strong and modern China. Much progress was made in economic modernization. Life in the major cities took on a more modern look. Conveniences like electricity were gradually changing how all major cities functioned" (p. 444).

Who did this work? "A professional class, composed of scientists, engineers, architects, economists, physicians, and others with technical expertise, often acquired through study abroad, was gaining influence." The historian William Kirby shows how engineers drew up national plans for railways, dams and power stations; some were built, many stayed on paper. Industrial output grew by several percent a year in the early 1930s.

Source beyond Ebrey: William C. Kirby, "Engineering China," in Becoming Chinese, ed. Wen-hsin Yeh (2000).

Why this matters: This is the strongest case for Chiang's government: it built the beginnings of a modern state.

Check yourself: Where in China would you have seen these changes, and where would you not?

Q3 story · The countryside · 1930s

But Most Peasants Lived No Better Than They Had Under the Qing

About 80 percent of Chinese were farmers.
Most had seen no rise in their standard of living since Qing times.
Population passed 500 million by 1930, squeezing the land.
Railways and cheap goods had not yet helped the village economy.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Lloyd E. Eastman, Family, Fields, and Ancestors (1988). Image: 1935.

Now leave the capital and walk out to a village. The woman in the photograph, carrying her baskets on a shoulder pole in 1935, lived much as her grandmother had.

The guide asks you to copy this sentence (p. 444): "The Nationalists' modernizing programs unfortunately failed to bring improvements to the countryside." Ebrey goes on: "Most peasants had seen no improvement in their standard of living since Qing times."

Why not? First, numbers: "Continued population growth to over 500 million by 1930 relentlessly increased the pressure on available land." Each generation divided the family plots again. Second, the benefits of the modern economy, "cheaper transportation by railroads and cheaper manufactured consumer goods," had not yet reached most villages. Third, the government's money came from the cities and went to the army; it never carried out its own rent law. The historian Lloyd Eastman shows how heavy local taxes and debt pressed on farm families.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lloyd E. Eastman, Family, Fields, and Ancestors (1988).

Why this matters: The village was where most Chinese lived, and where the Communists would find their army.

Check yourself: Why did population growth cancel out so much progress?

Q3 story · Ding County, Hebei · 1926–1937

Reformers Tried to Rebuild the Villages County by County, but Their Gains Rarely Spread or Lasted

James Yen, a Yale graduate, moved his Mass Education Movement to Ding County in 1926.
Teams taught reading with a primer of about a thousand characters.
They added clinics, better seed, credit co-operatives, and village theater.
Such projects reached a few counties and ended when Japan invaded in 1937.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Charles W. Hayford, To the People: James Yen and Village China (1990). Image: Washington, 1928.

The man in the photograph, James Yen, had taught Chinese laborers in France to read during the First World War. He came home convinced that illiteracy was China's deepest problem. In 1926 he moved his Mass Education Movement to Ding County, south of Beijing, and his college-trained staff went to live in the villages.

Ebrey describes this kind of work: "The government and private philanthropic organizations sponsored rural reconstruction projects that tried to raise the level of rural education, create facilities for credit, encourage modern enterprises, and form peasant associations, but gains were usually limited to small areas and short periods" (p. 444).

In Ding County, as Charles Hayford's biography of Yen shows, teams taught reading with a primer of about a thousand common characters, trained village health workers, improved pigs and seed, and set up co-operatives. Other reformers, such as the philosopher Liang Shuming in Shandong, ran their own model counties. China had nearly two thousand counties. When Japan invaded in 1937, these experiments ended.

Source beyond Ebrey: Charles W. Hayford, To the People: James Yen and Village China (1990).

Why this matters: Reformers knew what the villages needed; they lacked the reach and the time.

Check yourself: Why did rural reconstruction stay so small?

Q3 story · Exports and the world economy · 1929–1935

Silk and Tea Lost Their Markets, Then the World Depression Hit

Silk and tea were China's great exports.
Japanese silk and Indian and Ceylon tea took their markets.
After 1929 the world Depression cut demand for all exports.
Silk farmers in the Yangzi delta lost their cash income.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Lillian M. Li, China's Silk Trade (1981). Image: 1915.

For centuries, farm families in the Yangzi delta had earned their cash by raising silkworms and reeling the silk at home, like the couple in this lantern slide from 1915. That income paid rent and taxes.

Then the markets slipped away. Ebrey: "China's exports were struggling, silk and tea having lost ground to Japanese and Indian competition, then all exports facing decreased demand due to the worldwide depression of the 1930s" (p. 444). Japanese filatures, using standard cocoons and machines, made the even thread that American stocking mills wanted. Planters in India and Ceylon sold cheaper tea in Britain.

The Depression made it worse. When American and European buyers stopped buying, the price of raw silk collapsed. The historian Lillian Li shows that by the early 1930s many delta families had torn up their mulberry trees. Then in 1934 an American law to buy silver pulled silver out of China, and prices fell further, until the government issued a new paper currency in 1935.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lillian M. Li, China's Silk Trade (1981).

Why this matters: Even good policy in Nanjing could not protect villagers from the world market.

Check yourself: How could a decision in Washington hurt a silk farmer near Shanghai?

Q4 story · Ningbo, Tokyo, Whampoa, Shanghai · 1887–1927

Chiang Kaishek: Trained in Japan, Head of Whampoa, Married to Soong Meiling

Born in 1887 near Ningbo, Chiang studied military science in Japan.
In 1924 he took charge of the Whampoa Academy · Ch. 24 L4 · Whampoa ↗
He was a skilled politician who balanced rival cliques.
In 1927 he married Soong Meiling, the American-educated sister of Sun's widow.

Names in This Story

Chiang Kaishek Jiang Jieshi in pinyin; also called Chiang Chung-cheng (Jiang Zhongzheng); 1887–1975; pronounced "jyahng jyeh-shir"

Soong Meiling Madame Chiang, 1898–2003; pronounced "soong may-ling"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 443–444; Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009). Image: Shanghai, 1927.

In December 1927, eight months after the Shanghai purge, Chiang married in style. The bride in this wedding portrait, Soong Meiling, had studied at Wellesley College in Massachusetts and spoke perfect American English. Her sister was Sun Yatsen's widow, and her brother was T. V. Soong. The marriage tied Chiang to Sun's family and to Shanghai's bankers.

Ebrey sketches the groom. "From a landlord–merchant family near Ningbo, Chiang had aspired to take the civil service examinations, but when they were abolished, he went to Japan to study military science, joining the precursor of the Nationalist Party while there" (p. 443). Heading the Whampoa Academy from 1924 "allowed him to form strong personal ties to young officers in the party's army." You met Whampoa in Chapter 24.

In power, Chiang "proved able to balance different cliques and build personal ties to key power holders" (pp. 443–444). Jay Taylor's biography, based on Chiang's diaries, shows a disciplined, moralistic, often angry man who trusted soldiers more than politicians.

Source beyond Ebrey: Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009).

Why this matters: Chiang's training in Japan and at Whampoa shaped how he would fight the Communists: as an army officer.

Q4 recap · Political tutelage

Recap: Political Tutelage Meant One Party Ruled Until the People Were "Ready"

Concept

Political tutelage

Sun Yatsen's middle stage of the revolution: after military unification, the Nationalist Party would govern alone and train the people in self-government before constitutional democracy began · Ch. 24 L1 · Ch. 24 L1 concept ↗

Chiang declared the period of tutelage in 1928.
So there were no national elections.
The press was censored, and suspected Communists were arrested and often executed.
Tutelage lasted, on paper, until 1947.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974).

You met this idea in the first lecture of Chapter 24, as part of Sun Yatsen's plan. Sun said the revolution had three stages: military rule to unify the country, then political tutelage, when the Nationalist Party would govern alone and teach the people self-government, and finally constitutional democracy.

In 1928 Chiang announced that the military stage was over and tutelage had begun. Ebrey is blunt: "Chiang was not a political progressive. He made no attempt at elective democracy, as this was to be a period of 'political tutelage.' The press was heavily censored, and dissenters and suspected communists were arrested and often executed" (p. 444).

So tutelage gave Chiang a legal reason to rule without elections, and the police and courts to silence critics. The historian Lloyd Eastman counts the cost: writers jailed, newspapers closed, and thousands of suspected Communists shot. A constitution was promised for 1936, delayed by war, and adopted only in 1947.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974).

Why this matters: Tutelage was the first of Chiang's weapons against communism: no rival parties allowed.

Check yourself: How could Sun's idea of tutelage be used to justify one-party rule?

Q4 story · Jiangxi · 1930–1934

Chiang Sent Five Campaigns Against the Jiangxi Soviet, and the Fifth Built Blockhouses Around It

From late 1930 Chiang launched campaigns to destroy the Jiangxi Soviet · Ch. 25 L1 · Jiangxi Soviet ↗
The first four failed against the Red Army's guerrilla tactics.
The fifth, from late 1933, ringed the base with blockhouses and roads.
Chiang said Japan was a disease of the skin; communism, of the heart.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 444–445; William Wei, Counterrevolution in China (1985). Image: Jiangxi, 1934.

You saw Mao set up the Jiangxi Soviet in the last lecture. Chiang meant to destroy it. Starting in December 1930 he sent army after army; Ebrey calls them "extermination campaigns" (p. 445).

The first four failed. The Red Army let the Nationalists march deep into the hills, then struck isolated columns and vanished. The historian William Wei shows how Chiang learned from these defeats. In the fifth campaign, from the autumn of 1933, he did not chase the Red Army. Instead his troops built roads and thousands of small stone blockhouses, and advanced a few kilometers at a time, tightening a ring and cutting off salt and supplies. Ebrey calls this campaign "German-planned"; German advisers helped, and so did Chiang's own officers. The photograph shows Nationalist infantry in forward trenches in 1934.

Why fight Chinese when Japan held Manchuria? Chiang's answer, from a famous speech, was that the Japanese were a disease of the skin, but the Communists were a disease of the heart.

Source beyond Ebrey: William Wei, Counterrevolution in China (1985).

Why this matters: Military force was Chiang's main answer to communism, and in 1934 it nearly worked.

Check yourself: Why did blockhouses succeed where the earlier campaigns failed?

Q4 story · Germany and Nanjing · 1928–1938

German Advisers Trained His Best Divisions, and Chiang Asked, "Can Fascism Save China?"

From 1928 retired German officers advised Chiang's army.
They trained an elite corps and bought German arms for it.
China sent Germany tungsten and other metals in return.
Chiang admired how Germany had rebuilt itself, and asked whether fascism could save China.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 443–444; Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974), p. 40; William C. Kirby, Germany and Republican China (1984). Image: 1930s.

In the photograph Chiang stands with General Alexander von Falkenhausen, the last chief of his German military advisers, looking over new German weapons.

Ebrey explains why Chiang chose Germany: "To modernize his army, Chiang turned to Germany, attracted by the success the Nazis were having in revitalizing and militarizing Germany" (pp. 443–444). "German advisers helped Chiang train an elite corps, plan campaigns against the communist base in Jiangxi, and import German arms" (p. 444).

The historian William Kirby shows that the tie began before Hitler, in 1928, and that it was a trade: German weapons and factories for Chinese tungsten, a metal Germany needed for armor-piercing steel. Hans von Seeckt, the founder of Germany's postwar army, came in 1933–1935, and Falkenhausen stayed until Hitler recalled him in 1938 to please Japan.

Chiang also took ideas. In 1935, Lloyd Eastman found, he told followers: "Can fascism save China? We answer: … Fascism is now what China most needs" (cited in Ebrey, p. 444).

Source beyond Ebrey: Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974), p. 40; William C. Kirby, Germany and Republican China (1984).

Why this matters: Chiang's foreign model was not American democracy but German discipline.

Check yourself: What did Chiang admire in Germany, and what did Germany want from China?

Q4 concept · Fascism

Concept: Fascism Promised National Rebirth Through One Leader, Military Discipline, and No Opposition

Concept

Fascism

A political movement of the 1920s and 1930s, first in Mussolini's Italy and Hitler's Germany: an all-powerful nation led by one leader, a single party, a society organized like an army, and the crushing of liberals, socialists, and Communists.

Fascists hated communism and distrusted liberal democracy.
They promised to end national weakness through unity and obedience.
Chiang borrowed the discipline and the leader cult, not the racial ideas.
Historians debate whether his regime was truly fascist.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974); Frederic Wakeman Jr., "A Revisionist View of the Nanjing Decade," China Quarterly 150 (1997).

You need this concept before the next two slides, because Chiang's answer to communism borrowed from it.

Fascism began in Italy, where Benito Mussolini took power in 1922, and reached its most extreme form in Hitler's Germany after 1933. Fascists said the nation was in danger of decay. Their cure was a single leader, one party, a society drilled like an army, and the destruction of rivals, above all Communists. To many in the early 1930s, during the Depression, Italy and Germany looked like countries that had pulled themselves together.

That is what attracted Chiang. Ebrey notes the appeal of Nazi Germany's "success" in "revitalizing and militarizing" itself (p. 444). Chiang took the call for discipline and obedience to a leader, and joined it to Confucian ethics. He did not take Nazi racial theory.

The historian Lloyd Eastman called the Blue Shirts, the subject of the next slide, a Chinese fascist movement. Frederic Wakeman later argued that their ideas were closer to "Confucian fascism," a blend of old moral order and new mass discipline.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lloyd E. Eastman, The Abortive Revolution (1974); Frederic Wakeman Jr., "A Revisionist View of the Nanjing Decade," China Quarterly 150 (1997).

Why this matters: Without this concept, Chiang's question "Can fascism save China?" makes no sense.

Check yourself: What part of fascism appealed to Chiang, and what part did he leave out?

Q4 story · Nanjing · 1932–1938

The Blue Shirts Trained Young Officers in Obedience and Ran a Secret Police

Young Whampoa graduates founded the Blue Shirts in 1932.
They swore loyalty to Chiang and attacked individualism and other New Culture ideas.
Chiang gave them political training in the army and in schools.
Their secret service, under Dai Li, spied on and killed dissenters.

Names in This Story

Dai Li head of Chiang's secret police, 1897–1946; pronounced "die lee"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Frederic Wakeman Jr., Spymaster: Dai Li and the Chinese Secret Service (2003). Image: 1940s.

The man in the photograph, Dai Li, was the most feared man in Nationalist China. A graduate of the Whampoa Academy, he ran the secret service of a society of young officers known as the Blue Shirts.

Ebrey explains: "Young officers became members of the Blue Shirts, an organization devoted to the nation and against such New Culture ideas as individualism. Chiang entrusted political training in the army and schools to the Blue Shirts, who also took on secret service work" (p. 444). The name copied the colored shirts of the fascist movements in Italy and Germany. You will remember that the New Culture writers of Chapter 24 prized the individual; the Blue Shirts called that selfish.

Frederic Wakeman's biography of Dai Li shows what secret service work meant: informers in universities, kidnappings, and assassinations. In 1933 agents shot Yang Quan, a leader of a civil rights league in Shanghai, and in 1934 they killed Shi Liangcai, the publisher of Shanghai's biggest newspaper, who had criticized Chiang.

Source beyond Ebrey: Frederic Wakeman Jr., Spymaster: Dai Li and the Chinese Secret Service (2003).

Why this matters: Chiang fought communism with spies and terror as well as soldiers.

Check yourself: Why would the Blue Shirts attack individualism?

Q4 story · Nanchang and the cities · from February 1934

In 1934 the New Life Movement Told Citizens to Button Their Collars, Stand Straight, and Never Spit

Chiang launched the New Life Movement in Nanchang in February 1934.
It joined Confucian virtues to rules for clean, orderly daily life.
Its goal was to "militarize the life of the people of the entire nation."
Soong Meiling led its women's work.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Jonathan D. Spence, The Search for Modern China (3rd ed., 2013); Arif Dirlik, "The Ideological Foundations of the New Life Movement," Journal of Asian Studies 34 (1975). Image: 1940.

In February 1934, in Nanchang, the city where he was directing the war on the Jiangxi Soviet, Chiang launched a campaign of a new kind. Posters and pamphlets told citizens how to live: button your collar, walk on the left, stand up straight, do not smoke, spit or urinate in the street, eat in silence.

The guide asks you to copy this sentence (p. 444): "To combat the intellectual appeal of the communists and build support for his government, Chiang in 1934 launched an ideological indoctrination program, the New Life Movement." Its goal, Chiang said, was to "militarize the life of the people of the entire nation" and give them "a capacity to endure hardship and especially a habit and instinct for unified behavior" (quoted from Spence).

The movement joined four Confucian virtues, propriety, righteousness, integrity and a sense of shame, to the drill-ground discipline you saw in the fascist models. Arif Dirlik argues it was meant to give the regime a moral ideology to answer Marxism. Soong Meiling led its women's work; the photograph shows her at an anniversary banquet in 1940. Many city people mocked its rules.

Why this matters: Chiang tried to win minds as well as battles, but with rules rather than land or reform.

Check yourself: Why would rules about spitting be a weapon against communism?

Q5 story · Shanghai · 1930s

By the 1930s Shanghai Had Over 3 Million People and Half of China's Modern Industry

China's most populous city since 1910, Shanghai had over 3 million people by the 1930s.
It had China's largest port and half its modern industry.
Three governments ran it: the International Settlement, the French Concession, and the Chinese city · Ch. 18 L3 · Concessions ↗
Chinese entrepreneurs prospered, especially those who worked with foreigners.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 444; Marie-Claire Bergère, Shanghai: China's Gateway to Modernity (2009). Image: 1930.

Look at the photograph: the Bund, Shanghai's riverfront, in 1930, lined with banks, hotels and trading houses, the river full of ships. This is the city foreigners called the Paris of the East.

Ebrey: "During the Nanjing Decade, Shanghai emerged as one of the major cities of the world. Since 1910 it had been China's most populous city, and by the 1930s it had about 4 million residents" (p. 444). Census figures for the three parts of the city added up to roughly 3.5 million in the mid-1930s, so "over 3 million" is the safe figure. "It attracted Chinese entrepreneurs, especially those willing to collaborate with foreigners. It had China's largest port and was the commercial center of China. In the 1920s and 1930s it had half of China's modern industry."

The city was really three cities, as you saw in Chapter 18: the International Settlement run by a foreign-elected council, the French Concession, and the Chinese districts under Nanjing's mayor. Marie-Claire Bergère shows how this split let business, crime and politics escape any one government.

Source beyond Ebrey: Marie-Claire Bergère, Shanghai: China's Gateway to Modernity (2009).

Why this matters: Shanghai was where China met the modern world, and the gap between rich and poor was widest.

Check yourself: Why did having three governments make Shanghai both freer and more dangerous?

Q5 story · Shanghai · 1920s–1941

Victor Sassoon Built Skyscrapers, and White Russians and Jewish Refugees Found a Haven

Tens of thousands of foreigners lived in Shanghai, most of them British and Japanese.
Victor Sassoon reportedly owned 1,900 buildings, including the Cathay Hotel.
Russians fleeing the 1917 revolution came by the thousands.
From 1938 some 18,000 Jews fleeing the Nazis came, since no visa was needed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Marcia Reynders Ristaino, Port of Last Resort (2001). Images: 1930s; Shanghai, 1938.

The guide asks you to copy this sentence (p. 445): "Shanghai attracted more foreigners than any of the other treaty ports, a high of more than thirty-six thousand." Counts vary with what is counted, but tens of thousands is certain. "The British and Japanese were especially numerous because they owned the most foreign companies."

The richest were the Sassoons, a Jewish trading family from Baghdad and Bombay. Ebrey writes that Victor Sassoon turned to real estate, "in the 1930s reportedly owning nineteen hundred buildings in Shanghai, including those that are now the Peace and Cypress hotels." The first photograph shows his Cathay Hotel, opened in 1929, with its green pyramid roof.

"Because the international districts admitted anyone, no matter what their passport or visa status, Shanghai became a magnet for international refugees." Russians fled the revolution of 1917; many found only menial jobs. Then came Jews from Germany and Austria; the second photograph shows refugees landing in December 1938. Marcia Ristaino counts about 18,000 by 1941, helped by the Sassoons, Hardoons and Kadoories.

Source beyond Ebrey: Marcia Reynders Ristaino, Port of Last Resort (2001).

Why this matters: Shanghai's open door made it a world city, and a refuge for the desperate.

Check yourself: Why could a refugee without a visa land in Shanghai but not in New York?

Q5 story · The Public Garden, Shanghai · 1890–1928

The Bund's Public Garden Kept Most Chinese Out Until 1928, and a Famous Legend Grew From It

The Public Garden on the Bund was built for foreigners.
Its rules barred dogs and, separately, Chinese except servants with foreigners.
Chinese protested for decades; the garden opened to them on 1 June 1928.
Later memory joined the rules into one sign: "No Dogs or Chinese."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Robert A. Bickers and Jeffrey N. Wasserstrom, "Shanghai's 'Dogs and Chinese Not Admitted' Sign: Legend, History and Contemporary Symbol," China Quarterly 142 (1995). Image: 1928.

The postcard shows a small green park at the north end of the Bund, where Suzhou Creek meets the Huangpu River. For decades it was the most bitter symbol of foreign arrogance in China.

Ebrey writes that the bund "was made into a park with signs posted that read 'No dogs' and 'No Chinese'" (p. 445). The historians Robert Bickers and Jeffrey Wasserstrom traced the real story. The park's regulations, posted at the gate, were a numbered list. One rule said dogs and bicycles were not admitted. Another said the garden was reserved for foreigners; Chinese could enter only as servants accompanying foreigners. A notice reading "Dogs and Chinese Not Admitted" in one line has never been found.

But the insult was real. Chinese residents, who paid most of the Settlement's taxes, protested from the 1880s. The council finally opened the park to Chinese on 1 June 1928, after the Nationalists came to power. In memory, the two rules merged into one sign, and the legend became a powerful patriotic symbol, retold in films and textbooks.

Source beyond Ebrey: Robert A. Bickers and Jeffrey N. Wasserstrom, "Shanghai's 'Dogs and Chinese Not Admitted' Sign: Legend, History and Contemporary Symbol," China Quarterly 142 (1995).

Why this matters: Shanghai's modern spaces were not open to everyone, and Chinese never forgot it.

Check yourself: Why did the legend matter more than the exact wording of the rules?

Q5 and Q11 story · Shanghai · 1930s

More Than 170,000 Women Worked in Shanghai's Factories, Many Bound to Labor Contractors

In 1930 more than 170,000 women worked in Shanghai's industries, half in cotton mills.
Most were young, unmarried, and illiterate, recruited in the villages.
A contractor paid their parents a small advance and kept their wages.
About 50,000 women worked as prostitutes, many also in debt.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Emily Honig, Sisters and Strangers: Women in the Shanghai Cotton Mills, 1919–1949 (1986); Gail Hershatter, Dangerous Pleasures (1997). Image: 1929.

Behind the Bund was another Shanghai. The postcard shows a district of straw huts on the edge of the city in 1929. Here, and in crowded dormitories near the mills, lived the young women who spun and wove Shanghai's cotton.

Ebrey: "In 1930 more than 170,000 women worked in industry, about half in cotton mills. The typical prostitute or mill hand was a young, unmarried, illiterate woman recruited in the countryside by a labor contractor. The contractor would supply a small advance payment, often to the girl's parents, and would make arrangements in the city for employment, housing, and food. The women were often kept in conditions of debt servitude" (p. 445).

Emily Honig's study of the cotton mills shows what that meant: twelve-hour shifts, contractors who took most of the wages, and girls who could not leave until the contract ran out. Some joined unions and struck; others pinned their hopes on marriage. Ebrey adds that about fifty thousand women worked as prostitutes; Gail Hershatter's history of Shanghai prostitution shows that many were also in debt to brothel keepers.

Source beyond Ebrey: Emily Honig, Sisters and Strangers: Women in the Shanghai Cotton Mills, 1919–1949 (1986); Gail Hershatter, Dangerous Pleasures (1997).

Why this matters: Shanghai's modern industry ran on the labor of poor young women from the villages.

Check yourself: How did the contract system keep a mill girl from leaving?

Q5 story · Shanghai · 1920s–1930s

From Mill Girl to Film Star, Shanghai Women Wore the Qipao

The qipao was a one-piece dress with a high collar and a side opening.
It was more fitted than anything worn in the nineteenth century.
Factory girls, office workers, and the rich all wore it, in cotton or silk.
Shanghai's film studios and magazines made it the look of the modern woman.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 445, 448; Antonia Finnane, Changing Clothes in China (2008). Images: Shanghai, 1930s.

Look at the two women on this slide. The first, in a hand-coloured studio photograph of the 1930s, holds a parasol; the second is Ruan Lingyu, the most famous film actress of Shanghai's silent cinema. Both wear the qipao.

Ebrey describes it in her Material Culture box: "The qipao is a one-piece dress characterized by an upright ('mandarin') collar, an opening from the neck to under the right arm, and a fairly narrow cut, often with a slit." It "was much more form-fitting than anything worn in the nineteenth century, but reflected traditional styles in its collar, its slanted opening, and sometimes its fastenings" (p. 448). It was Chinese and modern at once.

On p. 445 she adds that women "from factory girls and prostitutes to office workers and the wealthy, commonly wore" it. Antonia Finnane's history of Chinese dress shows how magazines, calendar posters and films spread the style. Ruan Lingyu's life shows the cost of fame: hounded by tabloid gossip about her private life, she died in 1935, aged twenty-four.

Source beyond Ebrey: Antonia Finnane, Changing Clothes in China (2008).

Why this matters: The same dress crossed class lines, while the women wearing it lived in very different worlds.

Check yourself: How could one style of dress be both Chinese and modern?

Q5 story · Shanghai · 1927–1936

Writers and Radicals Worked from the Concessions, Out of Reach of the Chinese Police

As Nationalist censorship tightened, writers moved to Shanghai's foreign districts.
There they were usually safe from the Chinese police.
Lu Xun lived there from 1927 to his death in 1936 · Ch. 24 L3 · Lu Xun ↗
In 1931 the Nationalists shot five young writers of his League.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 445; Leo Ou-fan Lee, Shanghai Modern (1999); Wang-chi Wong, Politics and Literature in Shanghai (1991). Image: Shanghai, 1930.

You met Lu Xun, the man in the photograph, in Chapter 24 as the author of "Diary of a Madman." In 1927 he moved to Shanghai and lived there until he died in 1936.

Ebrey explains why. "Shanghai also attracted Chinese intellectuals, especially as Nationalist censorship became more severe. If they worked from the International District or French Concession, they were usually safe from the Chinese police. Dissidents, radicals, and revolutionaries chose Shanghai for much the same reasons" (p. 445). The Communist Party's own Central Committee hid there until 1933.

Safety had limits. In 1930 Lu Xun helped found the League of Left-Wing Writers. In January 1931 the Settlement police arrested a group of young Communist writers and handed them to the Nationalists, who shot five of them at Longhua in February. Lu Xun wrote a famous lament for them. Leo Lee's history of Shanghai culture shows how the city's presses, cafés and film studios kept this world of letters alive.

Source beyond Ebrey: Leo Ou-fan Lee, Shanghai Modern (1999); Wang-chi Wong, Politics and Literature in Shanghai (1991).

Why this matters: Foreign rule, the symbol of China's weakness, also sheltered China's critics.

Check yourself: Why was the International Settlement safer than Nanjing for a writer who criticized Chiang?

Pierian Spring · Recap · Women and the law · 1912–1931

Recap: By 1927 Both Parties Promised Women Equal Rights, and in 1930 the Civil Code Wrote Some Into Law

New Culture writers attacked arranged marriage and praised the free choice of a partner · Ch. 24 L3 · Divorce ↗
Girls' schools spread in the cities, but most village women stayed illiterate · Ch. 24 L3 · Girls' schools ↗ · Ch. 24 L3 · Rural women ↗
Both the Nationalists and the Communists promised women equal rights in the 1920s.
The Civil Code of 1929–1931 allowed divorce by consent and daughters' inheritance · New laws ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 442; Kathryn Bernhardt, Women and Property in China, 960–1949 (1999).

Review Question 11 asks how legal reforms and social realities shaped women's lives. Start with what you already know.

In Chapter 24 you saw New Culture writers attack arranged marriage and urge young people to choose their own partners. Girls' schools spread in the cities, and a few women went to university, like the Wuhan students on the slide about the new laws. But in the villages most women still could not read, and marriages were still arranged.

In the 1920s both parties promised women equality. Then the Nationalist government wrote some of that into law. Ebrey mentions "a new marriage law outlawing concubinage and allowing women to initiate divorce" (p. 442). As you saw earlier in this lecture, the Civil Code of 1929–1931 defined marriage as monogamous and allowed divorce by mutual consent or by a court on grounds such as cruelty or desertion. Kathryn Bernhardt shows that it also gave daughters the same right to inherit as sons.

Source beyond Ebrey: Kathryn Bernhardt, Women and Property in China, 960–1949 (1999).

Why this matters: To judge the law's effect, you first need to know what it promised.

Pierian Spring · Story · Hong Kong · about 1913–1930

Yuetsim Was Kidnapped at Three and Sold as a Servant Girl to a Hong Kong Family

Born about 1910, Yuetsim was kidnapped at about three and sold.
The Yeo family of Hong Kong bought her as a "slave girl," a mui tsai.
She fetched and carried for the master's concubine from the age of four or five.
She never learned to read and never heard of the campaigns to free girls like her.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 443; Maria Jaschok, Concubines and Bondservants (1988). Image: Hong Kong, 1920s.

Ebrey's Biography box tells the life of one woman, based on interviews by the historian Maria Jaschok. "Yuetsim, born around 1910, knew nothing about her natal family. All she knew was that she had been kidnapped when she was about three years old and sold, through intermediaries, as a 'slave girl'" (p. 443). In Cantonese such girls were called mui tsai, "little sisters," a polite name for bonded servants.

The family that bought her shows how many kinds of women lived in one household. Mr. Yeo, a government clerk, was the son of a concubine. His wife, who had bound feet, "rarely left her room." Because she had no children, he bought a former prostitute as a concubine, who bore four. Yuetsim was bought to help the concubine with the housework and the children. "Naturally, she never learned to read or write."

"Hong Kong newspapers were filled with agitation against the custom of selling girls into bondage. Yuetsim, however, never heard anything of the movement." The photograph shows the Hong Kong waterfront in the 1920s, the city where she grew up.

Source beyond Ebrey: Maria Jaschok, Concubines and Bondservants (1988).

Why this matters: Laws and campaigns meant little to a girl who could not read and did not know they existed.

Check yourself: How did Yuetsim's position differ from that of the concubine she served?

Pierian Spring · Story · Hong Kong · 1923–1978

Laws Changed in 1923 and 1929, but Yuetsim's Life Changed Little

Hong Kong laws of 1923 and 1929 limited the sale of girls and made owners register and pay them.
When Mr. Yeo had to act, his concubine simply threw Yuetsim out.
After years in a shelter, she went back to the Yeos as a maid.
His children forbade him to marry her; she nursed him into old age.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 443; Maria Jaschok, Concubines and Bondservants (1988), pp. 69–77; Susan Pedersen, "The Maternalist Moment in British Colonial Policy," Past & Present 171 (2001).

Notice first that these were not Nationalist laws. Hong Kong was a British colony, and its government acted after a campaign in Hong Kong and in Britain; the historian Susan Pedersen tells that story. Ebrey: "In December 1929 a further strengthening of the laws against child slavery required owners of slave girls to register them with the government, pay them wages, and free them at age eighteen" (p. 443).

The law did reach Yuetsim, but not as its authors hoped. Because Mr. Yeo worked for the government, he had to act. "He might have married her off, as many masters did, but his concubine was so angry at losing Yuetsim's services that she simply ordered Yuetsim out of the house." In 1930 an official found her a place in a home for women and girls. Years later, with nowhere else to go, she returned to the Yeos as a maid.

When the wife and the concubine died, Mr. Yeo wanted to marry her. His children refused: their own mother had been a prostitute, but "marriage to a former slave girl would disgrace the family." She stayed anyway, and "was still living with him when she told her story in 1978."

Why this matters: A law could free a servant girl on paper and still leave her with nowhere to go.

Check yourself: What stopped the 1929 law from changing Yuetsim's life more?

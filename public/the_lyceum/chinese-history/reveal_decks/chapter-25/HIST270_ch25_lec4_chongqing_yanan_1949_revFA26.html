HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 25 · Lecture 4

Two Capitals

Chongqing and Yan'an in Wartime, Mao's Rectification, and the Civil War of 1946–1949

1938–1949 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

This last lecture of Chapter 25 covers 1938 to 1949: the war years in the two capitals, Chiang Kaishek's Chongqing and Mao Zedong's Yan'an, and the civil war that followed (Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 450–454).

First, how the war weakened one government and strengthened the other. Second, how Mao made his thought the party's creed. Third, why the Nationalists, with more troops and better weapons, lost the civil war. The photograph shows the two leaders toasting in September 1945.

Check yourself: As you go, keep a list of what each side gained and lost from the war.

Q8 story · Chongqing, Sichuan · 1938–1943

Japanese Bombers Hit Chongqing Again and Again from 1938 to 1943

The Nationalist government moved to Chongqing, deep in Sichuan, in 1938.
Japanese planes bombed the city for five years, mostly in the clear summer months.
In May 1939 two days of raids killed thousands.
People lived in air-raid tunnels dug into the city's rock cliffs.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 450–451; Rana Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Lee McIsaac, "The City as Nation," in Remaking the Chinese City, ed. Joseph Esherick (2000). Image: Chongqing, 1941.

Ebrey: "The Nationalists' capital was moved inland first to Wuhan, then to Chongqing, deep in Sichuan. Free China, as it was called in the Western press, started with the odds heavily against it. The capital, Chongqing, suffered repeated air raids" (pp. 450–451). Free China meant the parts of China the Nationalists still held, mostly the poor southwest.

Chongqing was a crowded river port on a rocky hill where two rivers meet. In winter thick fog hid it from bombers; in summer it was an easy target. The raids began in 1938 and went on until 1943. On 3 and 4 May 1939, firebombs burned the old city center and killed thousands; Rana Mitter calls it one of the first great terror-bombing campaigns against a city. People dug shelters into the rock; in June 1941 about a thousand people, by some counts many more, suffocated in a tunnel during a long raid.

The photograph shows a street burning after a raid in 1941. Lee McIsaac shows how the government made the bombed city a symbol of national endurance.

Source beyond Ebrey: Rana Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Lee McIsaac, "The City as Nation," in Remaking the Chinese City, ed. Joseph Esherick (2000).

Why this matters: The bombing made Chongqing a symbol of resistance, but it wore down the city and the government.

Check yourself: Why would Japan bomb civilians in a city it could not reach by land?

Q8 story · Free China · 1939–1945

Prices Rose Up to 10 Percent a Month, and Officials' Pay Became Worthless

The government lost the coastal cities that had paid most of its taxes.
It printed money to pay for the war.
Prices rose by as much as 10 percent a month.
Officials and teachers, paid in paper money, turned to corruption or went hungry.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Lloyd E. Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984). Image: 1940s.

War costs money, and the Nationalists had lost most of theirs. The customs duties and taxes of Shanghai and the other coastal cities, which had paid for the government in the Nanjing Decade, were now in Japanese hands. So the government printed money.

Ebrey: Chongqing "faced not only shortages of almost everything but runaway inflation as high as 10 percent a month, leading to widespread corruption as government workers' salaries fell to a pittance" (p. 451). Ten percent a month means prices roughly triple each year. Anyone paid a fixed salary in paper money, such as officials, officers, professors and teachers, saw their pay melt away. Many officials took bribes or traded on the side; others, like professors in Kunming, sold their books and clothes.

The photograph shows porters carrying banknotes in sacks; by the late 1940s it took bundles of notes to buy a meal. Lloyd Eastman's Seeds of Destruction argues that wartime inflation did more than anything else to rot the Nationalist government from within.

Source beyond Ebrey: Lloyd E. Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984).

Why this matters: Inflation turned loyal officials into corrupt or bitter ones.

Q8 story · Free China · 1939–1945

Press Gangs Took Peasants for the Army, and Many Died Before They Reached It

Chiang's trained army had been destroyed at Shanghai and Wuhan.
From 1939 most of China's 5 million soldiers were peasant conscripts.
Press gangs seized able-bodied men from villages.
Ebrey writes that as many as a third died on the way to their units.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984). Image: Yunnan, 1944.

Ebrey: "The army was in worse shape. The army Chiang had spent a decade training had been destroyed. From 1939 on, the bulk of China's 5 million soldiers were ill-trained peasant conscripts. Press gangs would enter villages and seize the able-bodied. As many as a third of the conscripts died on the forced marches to their bases because they were not given enough to eat or medical care. Desertion, not surprisingly, was a huge problem" (p. 451).

The law allowed exemptions for only sons and students, and the rich could buy their sons out, so the burden fell on the poorest families. Recruits were often roped together to stop them running away. Lloyd Eastman and Rana Mitter both use the reports of the government's own inspectors, who were shocked at what they found. Exact numbers are uncertain; the picture is not.

The photograph, taken by the U.S. Army in 1944, shows Chinese soldiers carrying supplies in Yunnan. Every load had to be carried by men or dropped from the air.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984).

Why this matters: Conscription turned many villagers against the Nationalist government.

Check yourself: How would a family that lost a son to a press gang feel about the government?

Q8 story · Free China · 1937–1942

Factories Moved Inland, but Bombs and Lost Supply Routes Starved Them

In 1937–1938 hundreds of factories were carried inland by boat and on men's backs.
Japanese bombing hit them again in Sichuan.
Soviet aid dwindled after 1939.
In 1942 Japan took Burma and cut the Burma Road, China's last land route.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; William C. Kirby, "The Chinese War Economy," in China's Bitter Victory, ed. James C. Hsiung and Steven I. Levine (1992). Image: 1940.

This slide holds the sentence the reading guide asks you to copy. Ebrey: "Another serious disability for Free China was the lack of an industrial base inland. Chinese engineers made heroic efforts to build a new industrial base, but constant Japanese bombing, the end of Soviet aid in 1939, and the closing of the route through Burma in 1942 frustrated their efforts" (p. 451).

Almost all of China's modern industry had been in the coastal cities. In 1937 and 1938 engineers took apart hundreds of factories in Shanghai and Wuhan and moved them up the Yangzi by boat and on porters' backs, an epic that William Kirby describes. But they were too few to supply an army of millions.

Supplies from abroad had to come overland. The Soviet Union sent planes and weapons in 1937–1939, but its aid dwindled after 1939 and ended when it signed a neutrality pact with Japan in 1941. The Burma Road, the truck route over the mountains shown in the photograph, carried American and British goods from Burma until Japan conquered Burma in 1942.

Source beyond Ebrey: William C. Kirby, "The Chinese War Economy," in China's Bitter Victory, ed. James C. Hsiung and Steven I. Levine (1992).

Why this matters: Without industry or supply routes, Free China depended on what its allies could send.

Check yourself: Why did losing the coast cost China so much more than land?

Q8 story · India to Yunnan · 1942–1945

American Planes Flew Supplies over the Himalayas from India

After 1942 China's only supply route was by air.
American planes flew from Assam, in India, over the mountains to Kunming.
Pilots called the route "the Hump"; hundreds of planes crashed.
American aid built some modern divisions, but not an army to drive Japan out.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Barbara Tuchman, Stilwell and the American Experience in China (1971). Image: 1944–1945.

Ebrey: "From 1942 on, American advisers and American aid flown over the mountains from Burma enabled Chiang to build a number of modern divisions, but not an army able to drive the Japanese out of China" (p. 451). One correction: by 1942 Burma was in Japanese hands, so the planes flew from airfields in Assam, in northeast India, over the eastern Himalayas to Kunming in Yunnan.

The crews called the route "the Hump." The planes, like the C-46 in the photograph, flew heavily loaded over mountains higher than they were built to cross, in storms and ice, often at night. Hundreds of aircraft were lost. Yet from 1942 to 1945 they carried some 650,000 tons of supplies.

The American general Joseph Stilwell, sent to command Chiang's forces in Burma, quarreled bitterly with Chiang over how to use them. Barbara Tuchman's biography of Stilwell shaped American views of Chiang for decades; Rana Mitter offers a fairer view of Chiang's choices.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Barbara Tuchman, Stilwell and the American Experience in China (1971).

Why this matters: Free China survived on a thin line through the air.

Check yourself: Why was air supply never enough to build a large modern army?

Q8 story · Washington and Cairo · 1943

Soong Meiling Won Over Congress, and Roosevelt Made China One of the Big Four

After Pearl Harbor, the United States became China's main ally.
In 1943 Soong Meiling addressed the U.S. Congress and toured America.
Britain and America gave up their extraterritorial rights in China in 1943.
At Cairo in November 1943 Chiang met Roosevelt and Churchill as an equal.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Hannah Pakula, The Last Empress: Madame Chiang Kai-shek (2009). Image: Cairo, 1943.

Ebrey: "After Britain proved unable to defend Hong Kong, Singapore, or Burma from Japanese invasions in 1941–1942, it lost its standing in Chinese eyes as the preeminent Western power. Its place was taken by the United States." Chiang's American-educated wife, Soong Meiling, "was popular with the American press and lobbied effectively for China" (p. 451). In February 1943 she spoke to both houses of Congress, the second woman ever to do so, and drew huge crowds across the country.

In January 1943 the United States and Britain gave up extraterritoriality in China, ending a privilege that dated from the Opium War. Roosevelt, Ebrey writes, "wished to see China become the dominant power in East Asia after the defeat of Japan." He brought Chiang to Cairo in November 1943, shown in the photograph. Ebrey also mentions Yalta, but Chiang was not there: at Yalta, in February 1945, Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin made a secret deal on Manchuria without him. Churchill, she notes, called making China one of the Big Four "an absolute farce." Still, China became a permanent member of the UN Security Council.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013); Hannah Pakula, The Last Empress: Madame Chiang Kai-shek (2009).

Why this matters: Abroad, Chiang's China had never stood higher; at home it was growing weaker.

Check yourself: Why did Roosevelt want China treated as a great power?

Q8 story · Southern Anhui · January 1941

In January 1941 Nationalist Troops Attacked the Communist New Fourth Army

At first the two parties cooperated against Japan.
Both used the war to expand their own territory.
In January 1941 Nationalist troops ambushed the New Fourth Army headquarters.
After that the Nationalists blockaded the Communist base area.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Gregor Benton, New Fourth Army (1999). Image: 1941.

Ebrey: "During the first few years of the war, there was some genuine cooperation between the Communists and Nationalists. This largely ended, however, when the Communist divisions of the New Fourth Army were attacked by the Nationalists in January 1941 on grounds that they had not complied rapidly enough with an order to retreat north of the Yangzi. From this point on, the Nationalists imposed an economic blockade on the Communist base area" (p. 451).

The New Fourth Army was made of the Communist troops who had stayed behind in the south in 1934. Both parties had used the alliance to grow; the Communists spread fastest behind Japanese lines in the north and in the lower Yangzi. In southern Anhui, Nationalist forces surrounded the army's headquarters column. Thousands were killed or captured; its commander Ye Ting was imprisoned, and his deputy Xiang Ying was killed. Gregor Benton's history shows that both sides shared the blame for the clashes that led up to it.

The Communists rebuilt the army under Chen Yi, shown in the photograph.

Source beyond Ebrey: Gregor Benton, New Fourth Army (1999).

Why this matters: After 1941 the two sides were rivals again, and both saved strength for the war to come.

Check yourself: Why did the united front survive in name after 1941?

Q8 story · Yan'an, Shaanxi · 1937–1945

100,000 People Walked to Yan'an, and the Party Grew from 40,000 to Over a Million

About 100,000 people made their way to Yan'an during the war.
About half were students, teachers, and writers.
Party schools such as Kangda trained them in caves like these.
Party membership grew from about 40,000 in 1937 to 1.2 million by 1945.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Mark Selden, China in Revolution: The Yenan Way Revisited (1995); Tony Saich, From Rebel to Ruler (2021). Image: Yan'an, 1938.

Meanwhile Yan'an was growing. Ebrey: "Some one hundred thousand people made their way to Yan'an during the war, about half of them students, teachers, and writers" (p. 451). They came because the Communists seemed to be the ones fighting Japan, and because Yan'an promised equality and purpose. Many walked for weeks, past Nationalist checkpoints.

They studied at new party schools. The photograph shows the cave dormitories of the Anti-Japanese Military and Political University, known as Kangda, which trained officers and cadres, the party's full-time organizers.

Ebrey: "Party members swelled from forty thousand in 1937 to about eight hundred thousand in 1940" (p. 451). Growth went on after that. By the Seventh Congress in 1945 the party counted about 1.2 million members, and by the end of 1949 some 4.5 million, according to the party's own figures, which Tony Saich's history uses. In eight years the party had grown about thirty times over.

Source beyond Ebrey: Mark Selden, China in Revolution: The Yenan Way Revisited (1995); Tony Saich, From Rebel to Ruler (2021).

Why this matters: The war turned a remnant of survivors into a mass party.

Check yourself: Why would educated young people choose a poor cave town over Chongqing?

Q8 story · North China base areas · 1937–1945

Communist Troops Helped Bring In the Harvest, and Taxes Fell on the Rich

Behind Japanese lines, peasants were ready to fight.
Communist troops did not steal crops and helped with the harvest.
Graduated taxes and rent cuts pushed big landlords to sell land.
Landlords were invited to help, and class struggle was played down.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Chalmers Johnson, Peasant Nationalism and Communist Power (1962); Chen Yung-fa, Making Revolution (1986). Image: early 1940s.

"The fight against Japan helped the Communists build a base of popular support. In areas of north China where the Japanese armies had penetrated, peasants were ready to join forces against the Japanese." The Communists, Ebrey explains, "gave peasants what they wanted: an army of friendly troops who not only did not steal their crops but also helped them bring in the harvest and implemented popular economic reforms" (p. 451).

"Class struggle was not emphasized during the war against Japan, nor was there much confiscation of land." Instead, graduated taxes made large holdings unprofitable, and landlords "were more than welcome to help." When the Nationalist blockade cut off supplies after 1941, the army grew its own food; the photograph shows soldiers farming the valley of Nanniwan near Yan'an.

Why did peasants follow? Chalmers Johnson argued that Japanese brutality, the "kill all, burn all, loot all" policy, drove them to the Communists as defenders of the nation. Chen Yung-fa showed how much also depended on patient organizing, village by village.

Source beyond Ebrey: Chalmers Johnson, Peasant Nationalism and Communist Power (1962); Chen Yung-fa, Making Revolution (1986).

Why this matters: The Communists won the villages that the Nationalists had never reached.

Check yourself: Was patriotism or economics more important in winning the peasants?

Q8 story · Yan'an and the base areas · 1937–1945

"Under the Nationalists, Too Many Taxes; Under the Communists, Too Many Meetings"

Party workers organized peasants into militias, unions, and women's groups.
They held meeting after meeting to explain the party's goals.
Propaganda stirred patriotism and promised a fairer future.
Villagers in Hebei joked about the meetings.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 451; Edward Friedman, Paul G. Pickowicz, and Mark Selden, Chinese Village, Socialist State (1991). Image: Yan'an, 1944.

Ebrey: "Party propagandists did their best to stoke patriotic passions, glorify the Soviet Union, and convey the message that the Communist Party could build a better, more egalitarian future. They called so many meetings that rural folk in Hebei quipped, 'Under the Nationalists, too many taxes; under the Communists, too many meetings'" (p. 451). The joke comes from a study of one Hebei village by Edward Friedman, Paul Pickowicz and Mark Selden.

Meetings were how the party reached people. Cadres organized peasants into militias, peasant associations, women's associations and literacy classes, and each held meetings to explain the war, the taxes, and the party's plans. Villagers learned to speak up, and also learned that the party was watching.

The photograph, from the U.S. National Archives, shows Mao speaking to a crowd in Yan'an in December 1944.

Source beyond Ebrey: Edward Friedman, Paul G. Pickowicz, and Mark Selden, Chinese Village, Socialist State (1991).

Why this matters: The Communists governed by organizing people; the Nationalists mostly taxed and drafted them.

Check yourself: What does the joke tell you about how villagers saw each government?

Q9 concept · Mao Zedong Thought

Concept: Mao Made Peasants, Not Factory Workers, the Heart of the Revolution

Concept

Mao Zedong Thought

Mao's version of Marxism-Leninism, "adapted to Chinese conditions." It made peasants the main force of the revolution, told cadres to learn from the masses before leading them (the "mass line"), and held that people with the right will could transform themselves and their world.

Marx and Lenin expected revolution from city workers, not peasants.
Mao made the peasants the vanguard of China's revolution.
The mass line: go among the masses, learn from them, then lead them.
Will and ideas, not only economics, could change history.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 451–452; Stuart R. Schram, The Thought of Mao Tse-tung (1989).

You need this concept for Question 9: rectification was the campaign to make everyone in the party think this way.

Ebrey: "Neither Marx nor Lenin had seen much revolutionary potential in peasants, viewing them as petty capitalist in mentality; in Russia, the party had seized power in an urban setting. Because the communists in China failed in the cities, Mao reinterpreted Marxist theory in such a way that the peasants could be seen as the vanguard of the revolution" (pp. 451–452). You saw the roots of this in Mao's Hunan report in the first lecture.

Two more ideas went with it. The first was the mass line: "party cadres had to go among and learn from the peasant masses before they could become their teachers." The second was a faith in will. "Marx was a materialist," Ebrey explains; ideas only reflected the economy. "Mao's vision of revolution, by contrast, was voluntaristic: it emphasized the potential for people, once mobilized, to transform both themselves and the world through the power of their wills" (p. 452). Stuart Schram's classic study calls this the "Sinification of Marxism," Mao's own phrase from 1938.

Source beyond Ebrey: Stuart R. Schram, The Thought of Mao Tse-tung (1989).

Why this matters: This creed guided the party for the rest of Mao's life.

Check yourself: Why would a faith in will appeal to a weak party in a poor country?

Q9 story · Yan'an · 1937–1938

Mao Outmaneuvered the Moscow-Trained Leaders, and the Cult of Mao Began

In November 1937 Wang Ming flew in from Moscow with Stalin's backing.
Mao studied Marxist theory and had his secretary present him as a thinker.
In 1938 the party leaders confirmed Mao as their head.
His portrait and his collected writings began to appear.

Names in This Story

Wang Ming Chen Shaoyu, Moscow-trained party leader, 1904–1974; pronounced "wahng ming"

Kang Sheng head of party security in Yan'an, 1898–1975; pronounced "kahng shung"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 447–448, 452; Gao Hua, How the Red Sun Rose (2018); Raymond F. Wylie, The Emergence of Maoism (1980). Images: 1937; 1940s.

Mao's standing after the Long March was high, "but he still had rivals." Ebrey: "A group of communists who had gone to Russia for training arrived in late 1935 and provoked debate on the errors that had cost the lives of so many party members" (p. 447). The most dangerous of them, Wang Ming, in the first photograph, actually arrived in November 1937, flown in from Moscow with Stalin's backing and with Kang Sheng, in the second.

Mao fought back with study. "Mao realized that he would have to improve his grasp of Communist dialectic and began systematic study. His new secretary, Chen Boda, who had studied in Moscow, began writing of Mao as a theorist" (p. 447). By late 1938 the party's leaders had accepted Mao as their head, and Wang Ming was pushed aside. Ebrey: "He also started to allow or encourage the beginnings of the cult of Mao: in 1937, a portrait of him appeared in the revolutionary newspaper and a collection of his writings was printed" (p. 448).

Ebrey sums it up: Mao Zedong Thought "did not win in a free competition of ideas," but in a power struggle in which Mao was "a master tactician, able to eliminate his rivals one after the other" (p. 452). Gao Hua's history traces each step.

Why this matters: By 1940 Mao led the party; rectification would make his ideas its only creed.

Check yourself: Why did Stalin's favorite lose to Mao?

Q9 story · Yan'an · 1942–1944

In 1942 Every Cadre Studied Mao's Texts, Criticized Himself, and Confessed

In 1942 Mao launched the first rectification campaign.
Cadres studied Mao's texts in small groups and criticized themselves.
Targets were humiliated at mass struggle sessions.
The writer Wang Shiwei, who criticized party privilege, was jailed and later executed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 446–447, 452; Gao Hua, How the Red Sun Rose (2018); David E. Apter and Tony Saich, Revolutionary Discourse in Mao's Republic (1994). Image: Yan'an, May 1942.

This slide holds the sentence the reading guide asks you to copy. Ebrey: "To reform the thinking of both old cadres who had deviated from the correct line and new recruits from bourgeois families, in 1942, Mao launched the first of many rectification campaigns." "Rectification" (zhengfeng) means correcting one's style of work. "Cadres had to study documents Mao selected in small groups, analyze their own shortcomings in Maoist terms, listen to criticism of themselves at mass struggle sessions, and confess their errors" (p. 452). A struggle session was a meeting where a crowd denounced one person until he confessed.

Ebrey names two targets: Wang Ming and the writer Wang Shiwei. In 1942 Wang Shiwei wrote an essay, "Wild Lilies," complaining that leaders enjoyed special food and privileges while ordinary comrades went without. He was denounced, imprisoned, and in 1947 executed. In May 1942, at the forum in the photograph, Mao told writers and artists that their work must serve the masses and the party.

In 1943 Kang Sheng turned rectification into a hunt for spies; thousands were falsely accused. "Those who balked were punished; some even died" (p. 452). Gao Hua's history documents the fear.

Source beyond Ebrey: Gao Hua, How the Red Sun Rose (2018); David E. Apter and Tony Saich, Revolutionary Discourse in Mao's Republic (1994).

Why this matters: Rectification made loyalty to Mao's ideas a test of every member's thoughts.

Check yourself: Why is a confession more powerful than an order?

Q9 story · Yan'an · April–June 1945

In 1945 the Party Made "Mao Zedong Thought" Its Guide

The Seventh Party Congress met in Yan'an in the spring of 1945.
It was the first full congress since 1928.
The new party constitution named Mao Zedong Thought as its guide.
Many members truly believed; others learned to watch what they said.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 452; Saich, From Rebel to Ruler (2021). Image: Yan'an, 1945.

Ebrey judges the results of rectification fairly: "Many of those invited to overcome their errors truly developed a new collective consciousness that greatly increased their usefulness to the party. Others simply learned to be more circumspect when they talked" (p. 452).

Then came the reward. "The Seventh Party Congress, the first since the 1920s, was held at Yan'an in the spring of 1945. The preamble of the new constitution recognized Mao's new role as sage of the party: 'The Chinese Communist Party takes Mao Zedong's thought—the thought that unites Marxist-Leninist theory and the practices of the Chinese revolution—as the guide for all its work, and opposes all dogmatic or empiricist deviations'" (p. 452). The last congress had met in Moscow in 1928. A resolution passed just before the congress rewrote the party's history, blaming Wang Ming and the other Moscow-trained leaders for its defeats and crediting Mao with its successes.

The photograph shows Mao giving his closing speech, the parable of "The Foolish Old Man Who Removed the Mountains," on determination.

Source beyond Ebrey: Saich, From Rebel to Ruler (2021).

Why this matters: Mao's thought was now party law, a power he would use after 1949.

Check yourself: Why rewrite the party's history at the moment of Mao's victory?

Q10 story · Nanjing · 9 September 1945

Japan Surrendered in 1945 with Two Million Troops Still in China

Japan surrendered in August 1945, after the atomic bombs and the Soviet attack.
More than a million Japanese troops were in China, and nearly a million more in Manchuria.
Both Chinese parties raced to take their weapons and territory.
American planes and ships moved Nationalist troops to the eastern cities.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 452–453; Odd Arne Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003). Image: Nanjing, 1945.

Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945, after the atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet declaration of war. On 9 September its commander in China, General Okamura Yasuji, signed the surrender in Nanjing, in the ceremony shown in the photograph.

Ebrey: "When Japan surrendered in August 1945, there were more than 1 million Japanese troops in China proper and nearly another 1 million in Manchuria, as well as about 1.75 million Japanese civilians. Disarming and repatriating them took months, as the Nationalists, the Communists, the Americans, the Russians, and even some warlords jockeyed for position. The United States airlifted 110,000 Nationalist troops to key coastal cities like Shanghai and Guangzhou, and 53,000 U.S. Marines were sent to help secure Beijing and Tianjin" (pp. 452–453).

Whoever accepted a Japanese unit's surrender got its weapons and the land it held. The Nationalists' armies were far away in the southwest; the Communists' were already behind Japanese lines in the north. Odd Arne Westad's history of the civil war begins with this race.

Source beyond Ebrey: Odd Arne Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003).

Why this matters: The end of one war began the race for the next.

Check yourself: Why did the United States help move Nationalist troops?

Q10 story · Manchuria · August 1945–1946

Soviet Troops in Manchuria Let Japanese Arms Reach the Communists

On 9 August 1945 Soviet armies invaded Manchuria, as Stalin had promised at Yalta.
They saw that Japanese weapons reached the Communists.
The Communists rushed about 100,000 troops into Manchuria.
The Soviets also carried off much of Manchuria's industrial machinery.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Steven I. Levine, Anvil of Victory (1987). Image: Harbin, 1945.

"The Russians had entered Manchuria in early August in fulfillment of their secret promise to the United States and Britain to join the eastern front three months after victory in Europe. They saw to it that large stores of Japanese weapons got into the hands of the Red Army—some 740,000 rifles, 18,000 machine guns, and 4,000 artillery pieces—giving them about as much Japanese equipment as the Nationalists got" (p. 453).

Manchuria was China's most industrial region, built up by Japan in the Manchukuo years. The Soviet invasion began on 9 August 1945, and within days the Japanese army there collapsed. The photograph shows Soviet troops entering Harbin. Stalin also signed a treaty with Chiang, so he played both sides: he let Communist troops in, then handed some cities to the Nationalists, and shipped factories home as war booty.

The Communists moved fast. Steven Levine's study shows how about 100,000 troops and thousands of cadres marched into Manchuria in the autumn of 1945, under Lin Biao, and built the army that would win the civil war there.

Source beyond Ebrey: Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Steven I. Levine, Anvil of Victory (1987).

Why this matters: Manchuria became the Communists' base for victory.

Check yourself: Why would Stalin help both Chinese sides at once?

Q10 story · Chongqing and Yan'an · 1945–1947

Mao Flew to Chongqing in 1945, and Marshall Tried for a Year to Make Peace

In August 1945 the U.S. ambassador brought Mao to Chongqing for six weeks of talks.
They agreed to avoid civil war, but fighting went on.
In December 1945 Truman sent General George Marshall to make peace.
A ceasefire in 1946 broke down, and Marshall went home in January 1947.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013). Images: 1946.

Ebrey: "From August 1945 until January 1947, the United States made efforts to avert civil war by trying to convince Chiang to establish a government in which opposition parties could participate. The American ambassador brought Mao and Chiang together for several weeks of meetings in Chongqing, but the agreements reached on cooperation led nowhere. Full-scale civil war ensued" (p. 453).

The ambassador, Patrick Hurley, flew Mao to Chongqing in August 1945; the toast on the title slide comes from those talks. On 10 October the two sides signed an agreement to avoid civil war, but their troops were already fighting in the north.

In December President Truman sent General George Marshall, the wartime army chief. Marshall arranged a ceasefire in January 1946 and sat on a "Committee of Three" with Zhang Zhizhong for the Nationalists and Zhou Enlai for the Communists, shown in the second photograph. Neither side trusted the other, and each thought it could win. Marshall left in January 1947, blaming extremists on both sides.

Source beyond Ebrey: Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Mitter, Forgotten Ally (2013).

Why this matters: American mediation failed because neither party wanted to share power.

Q10 story · North China and Manchuria · 1946–1948

Full War Broke Out in 1946, and the PLA Surrounded the Cities from the Countryside

In June 1946 Nationalist armies attacked the Communist areas; full war began.
At first they won; in March 1947 they even took Yan'an.
The People's Liberation Army (PLA) avoided defending territory and cut off the cities.
Nationalist soldiers defected with their weapons, and the PLA kept growing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 453–454; Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Suzanne Pepper, Civil War in China (1978). Image: Beiping, 1949.

In 1946 the Nationalists had several times as many troops as the Communists, and American weapons. In late June 1946 their armies attacked the Communist areas in central China, and full civil war began. Ebrey's summary dates the war from 1947; most historians, like Odd Arne Westad, date it from the summer of 1946. In March 1947 Nationalist troops even captured Yan'an; Mao and the party leaders slipped away into the hills.

Ebrey: "The civil war itself lasted only about two years. The Red Army (now called the People's Liberation Army, or PLA) began to isolate the cities, starting in Manchuria and working south. It lost battles but built support through moderate land reform. When Nationalist soldiers defected, they took their equipment with them, and the PLA incorporated them into its armies" (p. 453). Land reform was not always moderate: in 1946–1947 many villages saw landlords beaten and killed before the party restrained the violence.

"Within a year the Nationalist forces in Manchuria were routed, and the PLA was moving into China proper." In January 1949 the PLA entered Beiping, shown in the photograph.

Source beyond Ebrey: Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003); Suzanne Pepper, Civil War in China (1978).

Why this matters: The PLA won by isolating cities, not by holding them.

Check yourself: Why did capturing Yan'an not help the Nationalists?

Q10 story · Near Xuzhou · November 1948–January 1949

Near Xuzhou in 1948, Chiang Lost Nearly Half a Million Men

In November 1948 the decisive battle began near the railway center of Xuzhou.
About 600,000 Nationalist troops faced an equal number of Communists.
Chiang had air support, but his armies were surrounded and destroyed.
He lost almost half a million men, and the road to the Yangzi lay open.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003). Image: 1948.

Ebrey: "In 1948 a two-month battle near the railway center of Xuzhou pitted six hundred thousand of Chiang's troops against an equal number of Communist ones. Although Chiang had air support, his army was smashed, and he lost almost a half-million men. Thus, although the Nationalists had started with much more in the way of modern armaments and several times the number of troops, they fared poorly on the battlefield" (p. 453). The Communists call it the Huaihai campaign, after the Huai River and the Longhai railway.

It lasted from November 1948 to January 1949. The Communists mobilized hundreds of thousands of peasant porters to carry food and ammunition by wheelbarrow and shoulder pole. Nationalist armies, badly coordinated from Nanjing, were surrounded one by one; whole divisions surrendered or changed sides. The photograph shows Nationalist soldiers at the front.

Westad shows that Chiang's generals made mistakes, but also that his armies had lost the will to fight.

Source beyond Ebrey: Westad, Decisive Encounters (2003).

Why this matters: After Huaihai the Nationalists could not defend the Yangzi or their capital.

Check yourself: Why might peasants carry supplies for one army and not the other?

Q10 story · Nationalist China · 1946–1949

By 1948 Prices Were 3 Million Times Their 1937 Level

Prices in July 1948 were 3 million times those of July 1937.
In August 1948 a new "gold yuan" currency collapsed within months.
People turned to barter; a tenth of the population were refugees.
The middle class that had supported Chiang was ruined.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Pepper, Civil War in China (1978); Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984). Image: late 1940s.

This slide holds the sentence the reading guide asks you to copy. Ebrey: "The unpopularity of the Nationalists had many roots. Prices in July 1948 were 3 million times higher than they had been in July 1937, and inflation did not let up then. People had to resort to barter, and a tenth of the population became refugees" (p. 453).

The government kept printing money to pay for the war. In August 1948 it replaced the old currency with a new "gold yuan" and ordered people to hand in their gold, silver and foreign money. Chiang's son, Chiang Ching-kuo, enforced price controls in Shanghai and arrested profiteers. Within three months the controls had failed and the gold yuan was worthless. Families who had obeyed lost their savings.

The photograph shows a stack of banknotes with a face value of one billion dollars, labeled as worth a few thousand American dollars. Suzanne Pepper shows how inflation turned teachers, students and shopkeepers, the city people who had once backed the Nationalists, against them.

Source beyond Ebrey: Pepper, Civil War in China (1978); Eastman, Seeds of Destruction (1984).

Why this matters: Inflation destroyed the Nationalists' support in the cities.

Check yourself: Why did the gold yuan reform make people angrier than inflation alone?

Q10 story · Kunming and Nationalist cities · 1945–1948

Soldiers Looted, Students Were Beaten, and Liberal Critics Were Shot

Nationalist officers and soldiers were seen as taking whatever they could.
Police and soldiers broke up student protests by force.
In July 1946 secret police agents shot two liberal critics in Kunming.
Many educated Chinese gave up on the government.

Names in This Story

Wen Yiduo poet and professor, 1899–1946; pronounced "wun yee-dwaw"

Li Gongpu democratic activist, 1902–1946; pronounced "lee gong-poo"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Pepper, Civil War in China (1978); Edmund S. K. Fung, In Search of Chinese Democracy (2000). Image: 1940s.

"Nationalist army officers and soldiers were widely seen as seizing whatever they could for themselves rather than working for the common good. Student protests were often put down by violence. When liberals demanded that Chiang widen participation in his government, he had his secret police assassinate them" (p. 453).

When the Nationalists returned to the coastal cities in 1945, many officials seized Japanese property for themselves, and people mocked the "liberation" as a "takeover." In December 1945 soldiers attacked a student protest against civil war in Kunming and killed four. In July 1946, in Kunming, Nationalist secret police agents shot the democratic activist Li Gongpu and then Wen Yiduo, in the photograph, a famous poet and professor who had spoken at Li's memorial. Chiang denied ordering the killings, and the government punished some of the agents involved, but few believed him. Historians still debate how high the orders went.

Edmund Fung shows how liberals and the small democratic parties, who had hoped for a third way between the two armies, turned toward the Communists.

Source beyond Ebrey: Pepper, Civil War in China (1978); Edmund S. K. Fung, In Search of Chinese Democracy (2000).

Why this matters: Repression cost the Nationalists the support of the educated.

Q10 story · Taiwan · 1949

In December 1949 Chiang Flew to Taiwan, and the Nationalists Ruled Only the Island

In January 1949 Chiang resigned as president but kept control of the army and the gold reserves.
The PLA crossed the Yangzi in April 1949 and took Nanjing and Shanghai.
More than a million soldiers and civilians followed the government to Taiwan.
On 10 December 1949 Chiang flew from Chengdu to Taiwan; he never returned.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 453; Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009); Dominic Meng-Hsuan Yang, The Great Exodus from China (2021). Image: 1949.

Ebrey: "In early 1949, Chiang Kaishek and much of his army and government retreated to Taiwan and reestablished their government there" (p. 453). The retreat in fact took the whole year. In January 1949 Chiang resigned the presidency to his vice president, Li Zongren, who tried to negotiate, but Chiang kept control of the army and quietly shipped the government's gold reserves to Taiwan.

In April the PLA crossed the Yangzi; Nanjing fell that month and Shanghai in May. The Nationalist government moved to Guangzhou, then Chongqing, then Chengdu. On 10 December 1949 Chiang flew from Chengdu to Taiwan, and the government re-established itself in Taipei. The photograph shows soldiers boarding a ship for the island.

Taiwan had been a Japanese colony from 1895 to 1945. In 1947 Nationalist troops had already crushed a Taiwanese uprising, killing thousands. Now more than a million mainlanders arrived, and Chiang ruled the island under martial law until his death in 1975. Dominic Yang's book follows the refugees.

Source beyond Ebrey: Jay Taylor, The Generalissimo (2009); Dominic Meng-Hsuan Yang, The Great Exodus from China (2021).

Why this matters: Two Chinese governments, each claiming all of China, faced each other across the Taiwan Strait.

Looking back · 1927 and 1949

China in 1949 Was Not the China of 1927

Japan's long push into China was over; Japan had been defeated.
The Nationalists had lost the mainland and withdrawn to Taiwan.
The Communist Party had broken free of Moscow and tied itself to the peasants.
Mao led a huge party skilled at land reform, mass organizing, and controlling intellectuals.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 454.

Think back to the first lecture of this chapter. In April 1927 the Communist Party was a small urban party taking orders from Moscow, and its members were being shot in the streets of Shanghai. Chiang Kaishek was about to rule China from Nanjing. Japan was preparing to expand.

Ebrey asks: "What changed between 1927 and the end of 1949? How different was China?" Her answer: "More than a century of struggle against a Japan intent on imperialist expansion was over: Japan had been thoroughly defeated and had turned against war. The Nationalist Party had been defeated by the Communist Party and had withdrawn from the mainland. The Communist Party itself had changed dramatically. It had broken free from Comintern control and tied itself intimately to the peasantry. Mao had risen to the top position in the party and established his version of Marxism as the correct ideology. The party had grown enormously and acquired extensive experience in redistributing land, mobilizing peasants, and keeping a tight rein on intellectuals" (p. 454).

Why this matters: Each of those experiences shaped how the party would rule China after 1949.

Pierian Spring · Yan'an · 1937–1940

An Actress from Shanghai, Jiang Qing, Came to Yan'an and Married Mao

Jiang Qing had been a film and stage actress in Shanghai.
She reached Yan'an in 1937, after Japan invaded.
He Zizhen had gone to the Soviet Union for treatment; Mao married Jiang Qing in 1938.
Thirty years later she would lead the Cultural Revolution.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, p. 448; Ross Terrill, Madame Mao (rev. ed. 1999). Image: Yan'an, about 1940.

Ebrey: "It was during this period that Mao took up with Jiang Qing. He Zizhen and Mao's surviving children had gone to the Soviet Union for safety and medical treatment. Jiang Qing, twenty-four years old, had worked as an actress in Shanghai and made her way to Yan'an after the Japanese invasion. Some of the other Communist leaders resented her liaison with Mao, having liked and admired He Zizhen. Mao and Jiang Qing had a daughter in 1940, the last of his four surviving children (six were lost or died)" (p. 448).

Jiang Qing had acted in Shanghai films and plays, including Ibsen's A Doll's House, the play about Nora you met in Chapter 24. In Yan'an she studied at the Lu Xun Academy of Arts, where Mao saw her. They married in 1938. According to later accounts, party leaders agreed only on condition that she stay out of politics; whether or not that is true, she largely stayed in the background until the 1960s. The photograph shows her with Mao in Yan'an.

In the 1960s she emerged as a leader of the Cultural Revolution. Ross Terrill's biography traces the whole life.

Source beyond Ebrey: Ross Terrill, Madame Mao (rev. ed. 1999).

Why this matters: Personal choices made in Yan'an shaped politics for decades.

Check yourself: Why might other leaders have resented the marriage?

Pierian Spring · Yan'an · 1944–1947

In 1944 American Officers Flew to Yan'an and Liked What They Saw

In 1944 journalists and then American officers were allowed into Yan'an.
The U.S. Army Observation Group, nicknamed the Dixie Mission, arrived in July.
Diplomats such as John S. Service praised the Communists' honesty and energy.
Their reports later cost them their careers in the anti-Communist 1950s.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 25, pp. 447, 451; Carolle J. Carter, Mission to Yenan (1997); John S. Service, Lost Chance in China, ed. Joseph W. Esherick (1974). Images: Yan'an, 1944.

Ebrey notes that "during the war, too, outside observers were impressed with the commitment to group goals of the Yan'an forces" (p. 447). In 1944, under American pressure, Chiang allowed a party of journalists, in the second photograph, and then a U.S. Army team to visit Yan'an. The Americans wanted intelligence on Japan and to judge whether the Communists could help fight it.

The U.S. Army Observation Group arrived in July 1944, under Colonel David Barrett, shown with Mao in the first photograph. Because it went into "rebel" territory, it was nicknamed the Dixie Mission. The diplomat John S. Service reported that Yan'an was honest, energetic and popular, in contrast to Chongqing. Carolle Carter's history of the mission shows how well the Communists managed their guests.

After 1949, Americans asked "who lost China?" Service and other China experts were accused of sympathy for communism and driven from government. His reports are collected in Lost Chance in China.

Source beyond Ebrey: Carolle J. Carter, Mission to Yenan (1997); John S. Service, Lost Chance in China, ed. Joseph W. Esherick (1974).

Why this matters: Yan'an's image abroad was part of its strength, and the party worked hard to shape it.

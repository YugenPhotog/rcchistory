Recap · Beijing · 1949

Where We Left Off: The Communists Won the Civil War and Inherited a Wrecked Country

On 1 October 1949 Mao proclaimed the People’s Republic from the Gate of Heavenly Peace. Ch. 25 L4 · next ↗
Forty years of war had left inflation, torn-up railroads, blown bridges, and millions of refugees.
Chiang Kaishek had moved his government and much of his army to Taiwan and still claimed to rule China. Ch. 25 L4 · taiwan ↗
Mao’s party had run villages for twenty years, but it had never run a modern city or a national economy.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 473; Ch. 25, pp. 453–454.

Chapter 25 ended with a victory. In 1949 the Communist armies took Beijing, Nanjing, and Shanghai, and on 1 October Mao announced the new state from the rostrum of the old palace gate. The links on this slide take you back to that moment and to Chiang’s retreat to Taiwan.

Now look at what the winners inherited. Ebrey lists the damage: “the economy was in shambles. Inflation was rampant. Railroad tracks had been torn up and bridges destroyed. Harbors were clogged with sunken ships” (p. 473). Millions of people had been driven from their homes. Many of the clerks and police who kept cities running had served the Japanese or the Nationalists, so the new rulers did not trust them.

The party knew how to organize peasants and fight a guerrilla war. Running Shanghai, with its banks, factories, and newspapers, was a new job.

Why this matters: Every choice in this lecture was made by leaders who had won a war and now had to govern.

Q1 concept · Beijing · September 1949

Concept: Mao Promised a “People’s Democratic Dictatorship”

Concept

People’s democratic dictatorship

Rule in the name of “the people,” meaning workers, peasants, and friendly businessmen, with no rights for those the party called enemies, such as landlords.

“The people” included workers, poor and rich peasants, and the national bourgeoisie.
Landlords and “certain classes of capitalists” were left out.
People’s congresses represented the people, but they met only now and then.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 473.

What do you call a state that promises democracy and dictatorship at once? Mao gave his answer in 1949: a “people’s democratic dictatorship.”

The Soviet Union called itself a dictatorship of the proletariat, meaning factory workers. China, a country of farmers, widened the circle. Ebrey explains that “the people” included “workers, both poor and rich peasants, and the national bourgeoisie,” Chinese businessmen who had not sided with foreign powers. Landlords and some capitalists were outside it (p. 473). Democracy was promised to the people; dictatorship was for their enemies. Who counted as an enemy was up to the party.

The photograph is real. In September 1949 a conference of the Communists and several small allied parties met in Beijing, chose Mao as chairman of the new government, and adopted the five-star flag.

Reading Guide Q1 (p. 473): this slide is your starting evidence for how the new state was designed. Keep reading to see where power really sat.

Why this matters: A government that decides who “the people” are can move a person from friend to enemy overnight.

Check yourself: Which two groups were left outside “the people”?

Names to know

Mao Zedong chairman of the party, the army commission, and the state

Liu Shaoqi (lyou shau-chee) ran economic policy

Zhou Enlai (joe un-ly) premier; ran foreign policy

Zhu De, Chen Yun army founder; economic planner

Deng Xiaoping (duhng shyow-ping) joined the top circle in 1956

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 473; Frederick C. Teiwes, “The Establishment and Consolidation of the New Regime,” in The Politics of China, ed. Roderick MacFarquhar (3rd ed., 2011).

On paper China had a government with ministries and congresses. Ebrey is blunt: “Real power, however, lay with the Communist Party” (p. 473).

Follow the chart from the bottom. Every village, factory, school, and army unit had a party branch, more than a million by the late 1950s. Each sent delegates up to county and province committees, then to the Central Committee and the Politburo, the party’s small ruling council. At the very top sat a handful of men. Deng Xiaoping joined them in 1956, when the party formally created the Politburo’s Standing Committee. The army, the People’s Liberation Army (PLA), answered to the party’s Military Affairs Commission, not to the government.

Mao stood above everyone. In 1953, at sixty, he chaired the party, the army commission, and the state, and was “treated almost as though he was an emperor” (p. 473). Zhou Enlai ran foreign policy and Liu Shaoqi ran the economy.

The chart is built for this deck from Ebrey’s description.

Why this matters: Because the party controlled the army and every workplace, no rival could organize against it.

Check yourself: To whom did the army answer? Why would that matter in a crisis?

Q1 story · Beijing and Shanghai · 1949–1950

Soldiers Cleared the Streets, and the State Tamed Inflation Within a Year

Peasant soldiers stopped looting and rounded up beggars, prostitutes, addicts, and petty criminals.
On one night in November 1949, police and cadres closed all 224 brothels in Beijing.
New street committees policed dress, hairstyles, and other signs of “decadence.”
By taking over the banks, the state brought inflation under control within a year.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 473; Gail Hershatter, Dangerous Pleasures (1997).

Picture a big city in 1949: prices doubling, gangs and beggars on the streets, opium dens and brothels open. Then the soldiers arrived.

Ebrey writes that the peasant soldiers of the Red Army “put an end to looting and rounded up beggars, prostitutes, opium addicts, and petty criminals” (p. 473). On the night of 21 November 1949, about 2,400 police and cadres closed every brothel in Beijing, 224 in all; Gail Hershatter describes how Shanghai did the same more slowly. Street committees, neighborhood offices of local residents, were told to clean up “flashy clothes, provocative hairstyles, and other signs of decadence.”

The money problem was harder. By taking over the banks and key industries such as the railroads and foreign trade, the government “brought inflation under control within a year” (p. 473). After a decade of runaway prices under the Nationalists, that won the party real support.

The photograph is real: a propaganda truck entering Beijing in February 1949.

Why this matters: Order and stable prices made many city people grateful to the new state, and they gave the party a reach into every street.

Check yourself: What did the government take over to stop inflation?

Q1 story · Moscow · December 1949–February 1950

Mao Spent Nine Weeks in Moscow and Came Home with Loans and Experts

Mao’s first trip abroad lasted nine weeks.
The Sino-Soviet treaty of February 1950 brought loans and technical help.
Over 20,000 Chinese trainees went to the Soviet Union; about 10,000 Soviet technicians came to China.
China copied Soviet ministries, schools, and plans.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 473; Odd Arne Westad, Restless Empire (2012).

In December 1949, two months after founding the new state, Mao took a train to Moscow. It was his first trip abroad, and he stayed nine weeks (p. 473).

Stalin kept him waiting. Mao had come for an alliance and for money, and the Soviet leader made him sit through weeks of ceremonies, including Stalin’s seventieth birthday, which this real photograph shows. In February 1950 the two sides signed a treaty of friendship and alliance. The Soviet Union lent China money and promised help.

Ebrey gives the results: “more than twenty thousand Chinese trainees went to the Soviet Union, and some ten thousand Russian technicians came to China to help set up 156 Soviet-designed heavy industrial plants” (p. 473). China copied Soviet ways of running ministries, universities, and the economy. To pay for it, farmers were taxed heavily, “again on the Soviet model.” Odd Arne Westad shows how much the young state leaned on Moscow in these years.

Why this matters: The Soviet model shaped how China built its economy, and the alliance would sour within ten years.

Check yourself: Name two kinds of help China got from the Soviet Union.

Q1 story · Shanghai · 1951–1952

The Five-Antis Campaign Turned Employees Against Their Bosses

City people were urged to accuse merchants of five crimes, from bribery to stealing state secrets.
In April 1952 alone, 70,000 Shanghai businessmen were investigated and criticized.
Many paid fines with shares of their firms, which became joint state–private ventures.
Former owners often stayed on as state-paid managers, but they had lost face.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 473–474.

The party needed businessmen to keep factories running. It also wanted to break their independence. The answer was a campaign.

In 1951–1952 the Five-Antis campaign mobilized city residents to accuse merchants and manufacturers of five crimes: “bribery, tax evasion, theft of state assets, cheating in labor or materials, and stealing state economic secrets” (p. 473). In April 1952 alone, 70,000 Shanghai businessmen were investigated. Ebrey notes that the targets “often felt betrayed when their family members and friends joined in attacking them” (p. 474).

Confession came with a price. Many paid by handing shares of their enterprises to the government, turning them into joint government–private ventures. The old owners often stayed as managers on a state salary, but their employees had watched them humiliated. The photograph is real: a mass meeting of Shanghai shop employees for the campaign in March 1952.

Why this matters: Without seizing most firms outright, the party took control of private business by 1952.

Check yourself: How did businessmen usually pay for their “crimes”? Why might employees join the attacks?

Q1 story · Changchun · 1953–1957

The First Five-Year Plan Built Heavy Industry the Soviet Way

The plan of 1953–1957 aimed to quadruple steel and double power and cement.
Cotton cloth was to rise by less than half, grain by less than a fifth.
Soviet-designed plants, like this truck factory, were built with Soviet help.
Schools and clinics spread fast in the cities, and everyone was given work and some housing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 473–474.

How does a poor farming country become an industrial power fast? The Soviet answer was a five-year plan, and China adopted one.

The First Five-Year Plan ran from 1953 to 1957. Ebrey gives the targets: “Output of steel was to be quadrupled, power and cement doubled. Consumer goods, however, were to be increased by much smaller increments—cotton piece goods by less than half, grain by less than a fifth” (p. 473). The plan put heavy industry first and people’s daily needs second, and farmers paid for it through taxes.

The photograph is real. In July 1956 the First Automobile Works in Changchun, one of the Soviet-designed plants, turned out China’s first trucks, and the workers celebrated.

There were gains in daily life too. During the 1950s, Ebrey writes, “rapid progress was made in cutting illiteracy and raising life expectancy,” and employment and housing “of some sort” were provided for everyone (p. 474).

Reading Guide Q1 (p. 473): the takeover of banks, the campaigns, and the plan are your evidence for how the economy was transformed.

Why this matters: The plan built factories fast, but it squeezed the countryside to pay for them.

Check yourself: Which grew faster under the plan, steel or cloth? Why?

Q3 story · The Yalu River · October 1950

Chinese “Volunteers” Crossed the Yalu at Night in October 1950

Stalin approved North Korea’s invasion of the South in June 1950.
In October, U.S. troops under the UN flag crossed the 38th parallel and headed for the Yalu, China’s border.
Peng Dehuai’s “volunteers” crossed secretly, with no lights or radios, and in late November drove the Americans south of Seoul.
Over 2.5 million Chinese troops served, with all of China’s tanks and most of its artillery and aircraft.

Names to know

Kim Il-sung leader of North Korea

Peng Dehuai (puhng duh-hwy) commander of the Chinese “volunteers” in Korea

Joseph Stalin Soviet leader, who approved the North’s invasion

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 475; Chen Jian, China’s Road to the Korean War (1994).

Mao did not want this war. Ebrey writes that “China’s development plans hinged on respite from war” (p. 475). But Stalin approved North Korea’s plan to invade the South, and in June 1950 the invasion came.

The United States sent troops under the flag of the United Nations. In October they pushed north across the 38th parallel, the line dividing Korea, and headed toward the Yalu River, Korea’s border with China. For Beijing, an American army on the border of Manchuria, China’s industrial heartland, was a threat. Korea had been China’s buffer before, as in 1894. Ch. 18 L4 · korea-1894 ↗

Chinese troops, called “volunteers” so that China was not officially at war, crossed the Yalu secretly, “using no lights or radios” (p. 475). In late November they surprised the Americans and drove them back south of Seoul. Among the dead that month was Mao’s eldest son, Mao Anying. Chen Jian’s history of the decision shows how hard Mao pushed a reluctant Politburo into the war.

The photograph is real: Peng Dehuai with Kim Il-sung in 1951.

Why this matters: China fought the world’s strongest power to a draw.

Check yourself: Why did China call its soldiers “volunteers”?

Q3 story · Panmunjom · 1951–1953

The War Stalled, and the Truce Talks Deadlocked over Prisoners

By mid-1951 the front stalled near the 38th parallel, and peace talks began.
The talks dragged on for two years, mostly over prisoners of war.
China wanted every prisoner sent home, but over 14,000 of about 21,000 Chinese prisoners refused to go back.
An armistice was signed in July 1953; most of those prisoners went to Taiwan.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 475–476; David Cheng Chang, The Hijacked War (2020).

After the great advance of the winter of 1950–51, the war turned into trench fighting near the 38th parallel, where it had started. Peace talks opened in 1951 and dragged on until 1953, “largely because China wanted all prisoners repatriated,” Ebrey writes (p. 476). Repatriated means sent back to their own country.

Why would prisoners refuse to go home? Many Chinese soldiers captured in Korea had served in Nationalist armies before 1949 and had been absorbed into the PLA. In the camps, pro-Communist and anti-Communist prisoners fought each other. David Cheng Chang’s history of these men counts about 21,000 Chinese prisoners; more than 14,000 refused to return to the People’s Republic and went to Taiwan instead.

An armistice, a halt to fighting rather than a peace treaty, was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953. The photograph is real: the two delegations signing at separate tables. Korea is still divided at that line today.

Why this matters: The prisoners who chose Taiwan were an embarrassment to a government that claimed to speak for all Chinese.

Check yourself: Why did the truce talks take two years?

Q3 story · Beijing and the Taiwan Strait · 1950–1954

The War Made China “Stand Up,” but It Also Made America China’s Enemy

At home the war gave the party legitimacy: China had beaten back the “imperialists.”
China’s official count was about 360,000 casualties; other estimates run far higher.
The U.S. Seventh Fleet patrolled the Taiwan Strait, and aid flowed to Chiang Kaishek.
A U.S.-led UN embargo cut off trade, so self-reliance became a revolutionary virtue.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 476.

“This war gave the Communist Party legitimacy in China,” Ebrey writes: “China had ‘stood up’ and beaten back the imperialists” (p. 476). Legitimacy means the people’s sense that a government has the right to rule. For a century, foreign armies had beaten China. Now a Chinese army had pushed the Americans back.

The costs were huge. China officially counted about 360,000 casualties, killed and wounded, and other estimates run far higher. And the war “eliminated many chances for gradual reconciliation, internal and external” (p. 476). The United States now saw China as its enemy. It sent the Seventh Fleet to patrol the waters between China and Taiwan and increased aid to Chiang Kaishek, so taking Taiwan was off the table. With Taiwan holding China’s seat on the UN Security Council, the United States pushed through a UN embargo on trade with China. China, cut off, made self-reliance a chief virtue. When the United States helped the French in Vietnam, China supplied Ho Chi Minh.

The photograph is real: crowds greeting soldiers home from Korea.

Reading Guide Q3 (p. 476): sort these facts into domestic and international consequences.

Why this matters: The war froze China and the United States into enmity until 1972.

Check yourself: Name one domestic and one international consequence of the war.

Q3 story · Beijing and Guangdong · 1951–1952

The War Turned Suspicion Inward, Against Missionaries and “Counterrevolutionaries”

China expelled most remaining Western missionaries and businessmen as possible spies.
A 1951 campaign targeted “counterrevolutionaries,” mostly people who had served the Nationalists.
The party’s own figures later counted about 712,000 executed, with more sent to labor camps.
It also disarmed the people: over 500,000 rifles were collected in Guangdong alone.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 476; Yang Kuisong, “Reconsidering the Campaign to Suppress the Counterrevolutionaries,” China Quarterly 193 (2008).

War with America made enemies at home look more dangerous. Ebrey writes that China “became more afraid of spies and enemy agents and expelled most of the remaining Western missionaries and businessmen” (p. 476). Christian colleges were closed or merged into state universities; Yenching, in the real photograph, became part of Peking University in 1952.

A “worse fate awaited those who had served in the Nationalist government or army.” A campaign of 1951 against “counterrevolutionaries” sent many to their deaths and many more to harsh labor reform camps (p. 476). How many? When Ebrey wrote, outsiders could only guess. Since then the historian Yang Kuisong has used the party’s own reports: they counted about 712,000 executions in 1950–1953, and he argues the true number was higher. The campaign also disarmed the population; “more than five hundred thousand rifles were collected in Guangdong alone.”

Why this matters: The war gave the party a reason, and the means, to remove anyone it saw as a threat.

Check yourself: Who were the main targets of the 1951 campaign? Why would a war abroad make a government harsher at home?

Segment 3 of 3 · 1950–1956

The Village Remade

In six years the party took land from landlords, gave it to the poor, and then pooled it all in collectives.
This segment teaches the history behind Review Question 4, and it ends with a short self-check and then the question itself.

Segment 3 moves from the cities to the villages, where most Chinese lived. Ebrey writes that “the lives of hundreds of millions of China’s farmers were radically altered in the 1950s” (p. 476).

You will see four changes. First, land reform: work teams took land from landlords and handed it to poor families. Second, collectivization in stages: farmers were pushed into teams, then cooperatives, then large collectives. Third, the state took over the grain trade. Fourth, a new group of rural cadres became the village elite (Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 476–477).

The photograph is real: a mutual-aid team in Shanxi working a field together in 1951. Its leader, Li Shunda, was held up as a national model.

Review Question 4 asks how collectivization changed village life and social relationships. Watch for who rose, who fell, and who now decided things.

Why this matters: This was the largest change in the Chinese countryside in centuries.

Check yourself: Before you start, guess: would farmers welcome or resist giving up their own land?

Q4 story · North and south China · 1950–1952

Work Teams Gave Every Villager a Class Label and Gave the Poor the Land

A team of cadres and students moved into the village and made friends with the poor.
It set up a peasant association, collected grievances, and organized “struggles” against the most hated.
Then it sorted everyone into five classes: landlord, rich, middle, and poor peasant, and hired hand. Ch. 25 L1 · concept-classes ↗
Land taken from landlords and rich peasants went to poor families.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 476; Brian DeMare, Land Wars (2019).

The party had done this before, in its base areas in the 1930s and 1940s; the link takes you back to the class labels of the Jiangxi Soviet. After 1949 land reform came to every village.

Ebrey describes the method. The party sent “a small team of cadres and students to a village to cultivate relations with the poor, organize a peasant association, identify potential leaders from among the poorest peasants, compile lists of grievances, and organize struggles against those most resented” (p. 476). A “struggle” meant a mass meeting where villagers denounced a target to his face. Finally the team classified every household into one of five classes.

That sounds scientific, but it was not. Ebrey asks how to classify an old widow who rented out her land because she could not farm it, or a family that had just bought land with factory wages (p. 476). Labels could be used “to help friends and get back at enemies.” Brian DeMare’s Land Wars shows how work teams staged these meetings like plays.

The photograph is real: villagers hearing the Land Reform Law of 1950.

Why this matters: Land reform gave the poor land and made them owe the party.

Q4 story · The village · 1950–1970s

Landlords Lost Their Land and Often Their Lives, and the Labels Passed to Their Children

Violence flared, especially when villagers tried to force “landlords” to reveal buried gold.
Historians estimate that one to two million people died in land reform, many of them executed.
Poor peasants gained land, and the party gained their support.
The labels became almost hereditary: a landlord’s grandchildren were still kept from leadership, and poor peasants’ families got preference.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 476; Frederick C. Teiwes, “The Establishment and Consolidation of the New Regime,” in The Politics of China, ed. Roderick MacFarquhar (3rd ed., 2011).

Land reform was not only a transfer of land. It was also a reckoning, and it was violent.

Ebrey writes that “violence flared, especially when villagers tried to get those labeled landlords or rich peasants to reveal where they had buried their gold.” Landlords and rich peasants lost their land and faced “punishment for past offenses; a not insignificant number were executed” (p. 476). No exact count exists. Frederick Teiwes and other historians put the deaths at between one and two million.

The labels lasted far longer than the meetings. Ebrey calls the result “a caste-like system in the countryside”: a caste is a rank you are born into and cannot leave. “The descendants of those labeled landlords were excluded from leadership positions, while the descendants of former poor and lower-middle peasants gained preference” (p. 476). A child born in 1955 to a “landlord” family carried that label into school, the army, and marriage.

This slide has no picture, by choice: the surviving photographs of land reform meetings show people being beaten or killed.

Why this matters: Village relationships were rebuilt around class labels that lasted for decades.

Q4 story · Hebei and Shanxi · 1950–1956

Mutual-Aid Teams Became Cooperatives, and Cooperatives Became Collectives

Redistribution won support but did not grow more food.
1950–53Mutual-aid teams share labor and tools
1950–53Cooperatives pool land; pay for land and labor
1954–56Collectives pay for labor only

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 476; Edward Friedman, Paul G. Pickowicz, and Mark Selden, Chinese Village, Socialist State (1991).

Land reform made millions of tiny family farms. Ebrey notes that redistribution “gained peasant support but did not improve productivity” (p. 476). The party’s answer was to pool the land, step by step.

Follow the timeline. First came mutual-aid teams, a few households sharing labor, tools, and animals. Then cooperatives: families pooled their land, tools, and animals, and were paid partly for what they put in and partly for their labor. In the south these spread from 1950 to 1953. Then, from 1954 to 1956, came “higher-level collectives” that “did away with compensation for anything other than labor” (p. 476). Your land and your ox no longer earned you anything. Most collectives were whole villages, and “economic inequality within villages was all but eliminated.”

The photograph is real: members of the cooperative led by Geng Changsuo in Wugong village, Hebei, in 1952. Edward Friedman and his co-authors followed that village for decades in Chinese Village, Socialist State.

Why this matters: In six years, farmers went from owning land to working for a collective.

If the state controls the grain, it controls the country. In 1953 the Chinese state “took control of the grain market,” Ebrey writes (p. 476).

Follow the chart down. The government first took a tax of 5 to 10 percent of each collective’s harvest. It let the unit keep “a meager subsistence ration per person,” just enough to live on. Then it bought a share of the “surplus” at prices it set itself, which Ebrey calls “a hidden form of taxation” (p. 476). The low prices helped feed the cities and pay for the Five-Year Plan.

Markets died with the free trade in grain. Trade between regions was “redefined as criminal speculation,” and “rural markets ceased to function” (p. 477). Poor villages had survived on sidelines, small businesses such as oil presses, paper mills, and rope factories. Those were lost. Carpenters and craftsmen who used to travel “became chained to the land.”

Q4 story · Xigou village, Shanxi · 1950s

Rural Cadres Became the New Village Elite

Team and village leaders now decided who worked where and how much grain each family got.
Some were literate middle peasants who knew farming; others were toughs who had denounced landlords with zeal.
A good leader had to hand over a big surplus without losing his team’s trust.
A talented farmer could rise through the party far beyond his village.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 477; Gail Hershatter, The Gender of Memory (2011).

Who ran the village now? Not the landlords and not the old gentry. Ebrey writes: “Rural cadres became the new elite in the countryside” (p. 477).

A cadre was a party or collective official. How you lived “depended on the personal qualities of the lowest level of party functionaries.” In some villages literate middle peasants who knew farming rose to lead. In others, “toughs from the poorest families rose because of their zeal in denouncing landlord exploitation” (p. 477). A team leader had to deliver grain to the state without taking so much that his team turned against him, and he spent his days settling quarrels.

It was a new ladder. Serving as a cadre offered “possibilities for social mobility well beyond anything that had existed in imperial China” (p. 477). Women climbed it too. The real photograph shows Shen Jilan of Xigou village, who won equal pay for women’s field work and later sat in the national congress. Collectivization took away land and markets. It also gave things back, and many villagers remembered both.

Ebrey lists the gains: “Schools were opened in rural areas, and children everywhere enrolled for at least a few years, cutting the illiteracy rate dramatically” (p. 477). Basic health care reached the countryside through clinics. Ebrey also mentions “barefoot doctors,” peasants with a few months of training who gave vaccinations and antibiotics. That program came later, from the late 1960s, as Xiaoping Fang’s history shows, but it grew out of the collective system built in the 1950s.

The collective also became a safety net. It “took on responsibility for the welfare of widows and orphans who had no one to care for them” (p. 477), a job that families and lineages had done before.

PS · Q2 story · Every city · 1950s

The Work Unit Gave You a Job, a Home, and Permission to Marry

Your danwei was your workplace; for students, the school; for the retired, the neighborhood.
It assigned housing, handed out ration coupons for grain and cloth, and ran clinics.
It organized the mass campaigns and ran birth control programs.
You even needed its permission to marry or divorce.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 474; Xiaobo Lü and Elizabeth J. Perry, eds., Danwei (1997).

How did the party reach into every city household? Through the place where you worked. Ebrey writes that “the Communist Party developed an effective means of social and ideological control through the danwei (work unit)” (p. 474). That sentence is the Reading Guide anchor for Review Question 2.

Most people’s danwei was their place of work; for students it was their school; for the retired or jobless, their neighborhood. Each danwei “assigned housing, supplied ration coupons (for grain, other foodstuffs, cloth, and anything else in short supply), managed birth control programs, and organized mass campaigns” (p. 474). Workers in a state factory usually kept their jobs for life, and they lived, ate, and saw the doctor inside the unit’s walls. The volume edited by Xiaobo Lü and Elizabeth Perry describes it as a small society.

All of that was security, and all of it was leverage. A worker who angered the unit’s party secretary could lose a flat, a promotion, or permission to marry.

The second tool for reshaping society was a law about the family. In 1950, Ebrey writes, “the Marriage Reform Law granted young people the right to choose their marriage partners, wives the right to initiate divorce, and wives and daughters rights to property” (p. 474).

None of this was new on paper. You met the New Culture writers who attacked arranged marriage, and the Nationalist Civil Code of 1930; the links take you back to both. The difference was enforcement. Other changes weakened the old family too: less family property, children in schools and the Youth League, and women in public posts as models.

PS · Biography · Manchuria, Seoul, Beijing · 1917–1980s

Jin Shuyu: A Korean Guerrilla Who Became a Beijing Telephone Minder

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 478; Zhang Xinxin and Sang Ye, Chinese Lives (1987).

Ebrey tells one ordinary life to show how politics and ethnicity worked in the new China (p. 478).

Jin Shuyu was born in 1917 to an ethnic Korean family in southern Manchuria. When Japan took Manchuria in the early 1930s, she ran away into the hills with classmates to join an anti-Japanese guerrilla band. When the band scattered, she slipped into Korea with forged papers, taught middle school in Seoul, and married a Han Chinese restaurant owner eleven years older than she was. In 1953 she took the children north across the 38th parallel and home to China. Her husband stayed. They met again only in 1983, in Japan.

In 1958 her street committee gave her the neighborhood telephone to mind: callers paid her a fee, and she fetched people when calls came in. In the Cultural Revolution she was accused of a bad class background until old comrades vouched for her. She had small privileges as a minority, yet she told her interviewers in the 1980s, “I’m no more Korean than you are. I became Han Chinese long ago.”

PS · Q5 story · All of China · 1950s

Scholars Sorted Over Four Hundred Groups into Fifty-Five Official Nationalities

The party borrowed Stalin’s four tests for a nationality: shared language, territory, economic life, and culture.

The Qing had ruled an empire of many peoples. The People’s Republic wanted to keep all of it. Ebrey explains that the Soviet model of a multinational state gave China a way “to justify reasserting dominion over Tibet and Xinjiang,” both independent after 1911 (p. 477).

First, the state had to decide who its peoples were. Some cases were clear. The Hui, Chinese-speaking Muslims, and the Zhuang of Guangxi, long sinified, were matters of degree.

PS · Q5 story · Tibet · 1950–1959

Tibet Kept Its Dalai Lama Until a Revolt in 1959 Sent Him into Exile

Ebrey calls Tibet “a special case” (p. 478). It held until 1959. Tibetans from neighboring provinces, fleeing collectivization, streamed in; in March massive protests broke out in Lhasa, “the army opened fire,” and the Dalai Lama fled to India (p. 479). Tsering Shakya’s history tells the story from the Tibetan side.

Cadre

A cadre (ganbu) is a party or government official, especially one who carries out policy among ordinary people: a team leader, a clerk, a work-team member.

×

Campaign

A campaign (yundong, “movement”) was a burst of mass action the party organized against a target: meetings, posters, accusations, and confessions, usually for a few months, then a new campaign.

×

Counterrevolutionary

A counterrevolutionary was anyone the party said was working against the revolution: former Nationalist officials and officers, secret-society leaders, “bandits,” and people accused of spying.

×

Mutual-aid team

A mutual-aid team was a group of a few neighboring households that swapped labor, tools, and draft animals at busy seasons while each family kept its own land and harvest.

×

Danwei

A danwei (dahn-way) means “work unit”: the factory, office, school, or neighborhood organization through which the state housed, fed, paid, and watched each city resident.

To Rebel Is Justified

The Cultural Revolution and the End of Mao, 1962–1976

Mao turns on his party · Order under the army · 1976

Background essay ↗

This lecture covers the last fourteen years of Mao Zedong’s life, 1962 to 1976, and the movement he called the Cultural Revolution (Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 484–488).

It has three segments. First, Mao turned China’s students against his own party. Second, the army restored order, and Mao’s chosen heir died fleeing. Third, the year 1976 brought death, disaster, and the arrest of Mao’s closest allies. The photograph is real: Red Guards waiting to see Mao in 1966.

Recap · China · 1956–1962

Where We Left Off: A Famine, a Split, and a Fear of “Revisionists”

The Great Leap Forward ended in a famine that killed about 30 million people. Ch. 27 L2 · famine ↗
Peng Dehuai warned Mao at Lushan and was purged; after that, few dared to argue with Mao. Ch. 27 L2 · lushan-1959 ↗
China broke with Moscow, and Mao called the Soviet leaders revisionists. Ch. 27 L2 · revisionists ↗
Liu Shaoqi, the head of state, and Deng Xiaoping led the recovery after the famine.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 482–484.

Lecture 2 ended in 1962 with China exhausted. The Great Leap Forward had ended in famine, and the links on this slide take you back to it, to the purge of Peng Dehuai at Lushan, and to the split with Moscow.

Two lessons from those years drive this lecture. The first is fear. Ebrey notes that senior leaders “had not forgotten the fate of Peng Dehuai, and honest debate of party policy was no longer attempted in front of Mao” (p. 484). The second is a word: revisionism. Mao had accused Khrushchev of betraying Marxism and steering the Soviet Union back toward capitalism.

In the early 1960s Mao began to ask whether the same thing could happen in China, and he started to look at his own colleagues with that question in mind.

Why this matters: A ruler whom no one dares to contradict can turn suspicion into policy.

Check yourself: What did “revisionism” mean when Mao used it about the Soviet Union?

Segment 1 of 3 · 1962–1967

Mao Turned on His Party

Mao called on China’s students to attack the party he had built.
This segment teaches the history behind Review Question 10, and it ends with a short self-check and then the question itself.

Segment 1 tells how Mao launched the Cultural Revolution and how China’s students became his Red Guards (Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484).

You will see six steps. Mao felt pushed aside after the famine. In 1965 and 1966 a play about a Ming official became his first target. A new small group took over the campaign. Students formed Red Guards and Mao received them in Tiananmen Square. The little red book put Mao’s words in every hand. Then the Red Guards attacked “old” things and people, and Mao’s second-in-command, Liu Shaoqi, fell.

The photograph is real. Look at the five men on the gate in September 1966. Within a year, the second man from the left, Liu Shaoqi, was a prisoner.

Q10 story · Beijing · 1959–1965

After the Famine, Liu Shaoqi Ran the State and Mao Felt Pushed Aside

In 1959 Liu Shaoqi replaced Mao as head of state, and with Deng Xiaoping he revived the economy.
Mao stepped back from daily decisions and lived apart, surrounded by guards.
He feared that revisionism was taking over the party, as he believed it had in Moscow.
When Liu and Deng softened his Socialist Education campaign, Mao decided they were the revisionists.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484.

Start with the two men in the photograph. After the Leap, Ebrey writes, “Mao, by then nearly seventy years old, withdrew from active decision making. Liu Shaoqi replaced Mao as head of state in 1959 and, along with Chen Yun, Zhou Enlai, Deng Xiaoping, and other organization men, set about reviving the economy” (p. 484). They allowed private plots and rural markets again, and the country recovered.

Mao grew isolated. “By the early 1960s, Mao was afraid that revisionism was destroying the party—that capitalist methods and ideas were contaminating Marxism.” In 1962 he started the Socialist Education campaign to make rural cadres focus again on class struggle. When Liu and Deng “rewrote the directives to deemphasize class struggle, Mao concluded that the revisionists were taking over” (p. 484).

His answer was a “Great Revolution to Create a Proletarian Culture,” the Cultural Revolution, which Ebrey says “came close to destroying the party he had led for three decades.”

Why this matters: The Cultural Revolution began as Mao’s struggle against his own colleagues.

Check yourself: What did Liu and Deng do that made Mao call them revisionists?

Q10 story · Beijing and Shanghai · 1965–1966

A Play About an Honest Ming Official Became the First Target

In 1961 the historian Wu Han wrote a play about Hai Rui, the Ming official who told the emperor the truth. Ch. 14 L1 · hai-rui ↗
In November 1965 a Shanghai critic charged that the play attacked Mao for dismissing Peng Dehuai.
In the spring of 1966 the mayor of Beijing, Peng Zhen, who had protected Wu Han, was purged.
Radical students at Beijing University then put up a poster against their university’s leaders.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484; Roderick MacFarquhar and Michael Schoenhals, Mao’s Last Revolution (2006).

The Cultural Revolution began with a history play. You met Hai Rui in the Ming lectures: the official who told the Jiajing emperor that he was failing the country, and was jailed for it. The link takes you back to him.

In 1961 Wu Han, a historian of the Ming and deputy mayor of Beijing, wrote a play called Hai Rui Dismissed from Office. In November 1965 a Shanghai writer, Yao Wenyuan, attacked it in a newspaper. The charge was that the honest official stood for Peng Dehuai, whom Mao had dismissed at Lushan, and the emperor for Mao himself. Ebrey writes that the Cultural Revolution “began in the spring of 1966 with a denunciation of the mayor of Beijing for allowing the staging of a play that could be construed as critical of Mao” (p. 484).

The mayor fell, and the campaign moved to the universities. Roderick MacFarquhar and Michael Schoenhals tell this story in detail. Both pictures are real.

Why this matters: Mao used a fight about the past to strike at people in the present.

Check yourself: Why would a play about a Ming official be read as an attack on Mao?

Q10 story · Beijing · May–August 1966

Mao’s Cultural Revolution Small Group Took Over the Campaign

In May 1966 a new Cultural Revolution Small Group was formed, answering to Mao alone.
Mao’s former secretary Chen Boda headed it, with Kang Sheng as adviser and Mao’s wife, Jiang Qing, as a deputy.
When Liu Shaoqi sent work teams to calm the universities, Mao called it suppression of the students.
In August a meeting of the Central Committee demoted Liu, and Lin Biao became Mao’s second-in-command.

Names to know

Chen Boda head of the Small Group

Kang Sheng its adviser, security chief

Jiang Qing Mao’s wife, its deputy head

Lin Biao defense minister, Mao’s new heir

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484; MacFarquhar and Schoenhals, Mao’s Last Revolution (2006).

To run the campaign, Mao set up a new body outside the normal party chain. Ebrey writes that “Mao’s wife, Jiang Qing, formed a Cultural Revolution Small Group to look into ways to revolutionize culture,” and that she “was widely seen as a stand-in for Mao” (p. 484). You met Jiang Qing in Yan’an. Ch. 25 L4 · ps-jiang-qing ↗ Its formal head was Chen Boda, Mao’s former secretary; Kang Sheng, the party’s security chief, was its adviser; and Jiang Qing, a deputy head, soon became its most powerful voice.

Then the students moved. “Radical students at Beijing University were agitating against party officials who ‘took the capitalist road.’ When Liu Shaoqi tried to control what was going on at Beijing University, Mao intervened, had him demoted by a rump session of the Central Committee, and sanctioned the organization of students into Red Guards” (p. 484). A rump session is a meeting where many members are absent or silenced.

The photograph is real: Jiang Qing, Zhou Enlai, and Kang Sheng greet Red Guards in December 1966.

Why this matters: A small group loyal to Mao now stood above the party itself.

Check yourself: How did Mao turn Liu’s attempt to calm the universities against him?

Q10 story · Every city · 1966

Students Formed Red Guards, and Mao Received Them in Tiananmen Square

In June 1966 schools and universities closed, and students gave all their time to the Red Guards.
Millions rode trains for free to spread the movement or to see Mao in Beijing.
At eight huge rallies Mao, in army uniform, reviewed them; his slogan “to rebel is justified” was everywhere.
Earlier that August he had written his own poster calling on them to “bombard the headquarters.”

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484.

Here are the Red Guards. Ebrey writes that “young people who had grown up in New China responded enthusiastically to calls to help Mao oust revisionists. In June 1966 high schools and universities throughout the country were closed as students devoted all their time to Red Guard activities” (p. 484).

“Millions rode free on railroads to carry the message to the countryside or to make the pilgrimage to Beijing, where they might catch a glimpse of Mao, their ‘Great Helmsman,’ at the massive Red Guard rallies held in Tiananmen Square.” At these rallies “Mao appeared in military uniform” (p. 484). He said little from the rostrum; his endorsement of the old slogan “to rebel is justified” reached the students through letters, newspapers, and loudspeakers. Between August and November 1966 he received about twelve million young people at eight rallies.

The headquarters he wanted bombarded was the party’s own leadership. He wrote the slogan on a poster of his own on 5 August 1966. The photograph is real: Mao reviewing Red Guards from an open car.

Why this matters: For millions of teenagers, rebellion against authority was now a duty.

Check yourself: Why do you think Mao wore military uniform at the rallies?

Q10 story · Every home · 1964–1969

Lin Biao Put Mao’s Words in Every Pocket

Lin Biao, the defense minister, had compiled Quotations from Chairman Mao to teach his soldiers.
Red Guards waved the “little red book” at rallies; hundreds of millions of copies were printed.
Mao’s picture hung in every home, bus, and train, and his sayings blared from loudspeakers.
In 1969 the party wrote Lin Biao into its constitution as Mao’s successor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484.

The little red book was everywhere. At the rallies, Ebrey writes, “the Red Guards in response waved their little red books, Quotations from Chairman Mao, compiled a few years earlier by Lin Biao to indoctrinate soldiers” (p. 484). Lin Biao was the defense minister and a hero of the civil war. Short quotations made Mao’s ideas easy to memorize and repeat, and easy to use as weapons in an argument.

“The cult of Mao became more and more dominant, with his pictures displayed in every household, bus, and train, and even in pedicabs, and his sayings broadcast by loudspeaker at every intersection. From early 1967 on, the People’s Daily regularly printed a boxed statement by Mao on its front page” (p. 484).

Remember Lin Biao. For his loyalty, the party’s Ninth Congress named him Mao’s successor in 1969. Segment 2 tells what happened to him. Both photographs are real.

Why this matters: The cult of Mao turned loyalty to one man into the test of every citizen.

Check yourself: Who compiled the little red book, and what reward did he receive?

Q10 story · Cities large and small · 1966

Red Guards Smashed the “Four Olds” and Humiliated Their Teachers

Red Guards set out to destroy the “four olds”: old customs, habits, culture, and thinking.
They renamed streets and shops, burned books, and broke temple statues and art.
They raided homes of people with “bad” class labels or foreign ties.
At struggle sessions teachers, writers, and officials were humiliated and beaten; many killed themselves.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 484.

Here is the sentence for Review Question 10. “In cities large and small, Red Guards roamed the streets in their battle against things foreign or old” (p. 484). They invaded “the homes of those with bad class backgrounds, ‘bourgeois tendencies,’ or connections to foreigners. Under the slogan of ‘destroy the four old things’ (old customs, habits, culture, and thinking), they ransacked homes, libraries, and museums to find books and artwork to set on fire.”

Old grudges came out. “The tensions and antagonisms that had been suppressed by nearly two decades of tight social control broke into the open as Red Guards found opportunities to get back at people.” At denunciation meetings, “cadres, teachers, and writers were forced to stand with their heads down and their arms raised behind them in the ‘airplane’ position.” Ebrey adds: “Many victims took their own lives; others died of beatings and mistreatment” (p. 484).

The photographs are real and show only signs coming down. Remember the people behind them.

Why this matters: Students were told to destroy their own history and to turn on the people who taught them.

Check yourself: Name the four olds. Why would teachers be among the first targets?

Q10 story · Beijing and Kaifeng · 1966–1969

Liu Shaoqi Died a Prisoner, and Deng Xiaoping Was Sent to a Factory

Liu, still head of state, was labeled China’s “chief capitalist roader.”
In 1967 rebels besieged the leaders’ compound; Liu was dragged before meetings, beaten, and held.
He died in prison in 1969. His wife, Wang Guangmei, was jailed from 1967 to 1978.
Deng Xiaoping fared better: after struggle sessions he was sent to work in a factory in Jiangxi.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 484–486; MacFarquhar and Schoenhals, Mao’s Last Revolution (2006).

The Red Guards’ biggest target was the head of state. “Liu Shaoqi, the head of state but now labeled the ‘chief capitalist roader,’ became a victim of the Red Guards” (p. 484). In the summer of 1967 crowds besieged the compound where the leaders lived, and Liu was struggled against and beaten. “Liu died alone two years later from the abuse he received.” He died in Kaifeng in November 1969, under a false name.

His family suffered too. Ebrey writes that four other family members died of beatings or mistreatment. His wife, Wang Guangmei, who had led a model Socialist Education work team, was held in prison from 1967 until the end of 1978, mostly in solitary confinement.

“Deng Xiaoping, another target of Mao, fared better; he was sent off to labor in a factory in Jiangxi after being humiliated at struggle sessions” (p. 486). Remember Deng: he will be back. The photographs are real.

Why this matters: If the head of state could be destroyed, no one in China was safe.

Check yourself: What happened to Liu and to Deng, and why might Deng’s fate matter later?

Order Under the Army

When the rebels fought each other, Mao called in the army, and then he lost his chosen heir.

Q11 story · Shanghai, Wuhan, Beijing · 1967

Rebels Fought Each Other for Power, and Mao Sent in the Army

By the end of 1966 rebel workers joined the students and “seized power” from party leaders.
Rival groups challenged every takeover as a “sham,” and fighting spread.
In Wuhan in July 1967 local forces seized two envoys from Beijing, and the radicals called on rebels to arm themselves.
Factions fought with guns; rebels burned the British mission in Beijing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 486–487.

The movement spread to the factories. “By the end of 1966 workers were also being mobilized,” Ebrey writes, and Mao “instructed students and workers to seize power from such revisionist party leaders. Confusing power struggles ensued. As soon as one group gained the upper hand, another would challenge its takeover as a ‘sham power seizure’” (p. 486).

“As armed conflict spread, Mao turned to the People’s Liberation Army to restore order.” The army usually backed the more conservative groups. In Wuhan in July 1967 a conservative faction “kidnapped two of the radical leaders from Beijing,” and the Cultural Revolution Small Group “responded by calling on the Red Guards to arm themselves and seize military power from the ‘capitalist roaders’ in the army.” So “began the most violent stage” (p. 486). Rebels took over the Foreign Ministry and burned the British diplomatic compound (p. 487).

The photograph is real: rebels in Shanxi province rally to take over its government in 1967.

Why this matters: Mao had unleashed forces that even he could not control.

Check yourself: Why did the army and the radical leaders end up on opposite sides?

Q11 story · Villages across China · 1968–1980

Mao Disbanded the Red Guards and Sent 17 Million Young People to the Countryside

By the summer of 1968 Mao had to calm the movement to prevent a civil war.
In July he disbanded the Red Guards and sent them to work in the countryside.
High school graduates followed: about 17 million sent-down youth in all.
The official reason was to learn from the peasants; it also saved the cities jobs and housing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 487.

Here is the sentence for Review Question 11. “By the summer of 1968, Mao had no choice but to moderate the Cultural Revolution in order to prevent full-scale civil war. In July he disbanded the Red Guards and sent them off to work in the countryside” (p. 487).

They were the first of many. “High school graduates were sent into the countryside, as the Red Guards had been before them, some 17 million altogether.” The campaign was called “up to the mountains and down to the villages.” Ebrey explains both sides of it: “Although the stated reason for sending them to the countryside was to let them learn from the peasants and give the peasants the advantage of their education, this transfer also saved the government the trouble and expense of putting the graduates on the payroll of urban enterprises or finding them housing when they married” (p. 487).

Many spent ten years in villages far from home. The photograph is real; it was taken in 1964, before the great wave of 1968.

Why this matters: A whole generation lost its schooling and its youth in the villages.

Check yourself: Give the official reason for sending youth to the countryside, and one unstated reason.

Q11 story · Every province · 1967–1971

Revolutionary Committees, Run by the Army, Took the Place of the Party

Revolutionary committees joined rebels, loyal cadres, and army officers.
In most places the army quickly became the dominant force.
The worst killing came now, as the committees hunted “class enemies” in 1968–1971.
Some 3 million party and government officials had lost their jobs.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 486–487; Andrew G. Walder and Yang Su, “The Cultural Revolution in the Countryside,” China Quarterly 173 (2003).

What replaced the party? “Revolutionary committees were set up to take the place of the old party structure. Each committee had representatives from the mass organizations, from revolutionary cadres, and from the army; in most places the army quickly became the dominant force” (p. 487).

Ebrey counts the human cost of the first phase: “some 3 million party and government officials were removed from their jobs, and as many as half a million people were killed or committed suicide” (p. 487). Later research shows that the killing did not stop with the Red Guards. Andrew Walder and Yang Su studied hundreds of county histories and found that most deaths in the countryside came after the army took charge, in campaigns of 1968 to 1971 to “cleanse the class ranks.” They estimate between 750,000 and 1.5 million deaths in rural areas alone.

The photograph is real: a rally for the new Beijing committee in 1967.

Why this matters: Order came back under soldiers, and so did terror under new names.

Q11 story · Stages and screens · 1966–1976

Eight Model Operas Became Almost the Only Culture Allowed

Foreign music, art, and books, except Marxist works, disappeared from the stores.
In their place came eight model works sponsored by Jiang Qing.
The official line was that it was better to be “red” than expert.
Doctors, scientists, and teachers were driven out of their fields or sent to labor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 487.

With order came control. “Culture remained tightly controlled. Foreign music, art, literature, and books (other than works on Marxism, Leninism, and Stalinism) disappeared from stores. Revolutionary works were offered in their place, such as the eight model revolutionary operas Jiang Qing had sponsored” (p. 487).

The model works were modern stories of heroes fighting landlords, Japanese invaders, or Nationalists. They included operas such as Taking Tiger Mountain by Strategy and The Legend of the Red Lantern, and ballets such as The Red Detachment of Women, in the photograph. For years these were almost the only shows on stage, film, and radio.

Work changed too. “The official line was that it was better to be red than expert, and professionals were hounded out of many fields” (p. 487). Red meant politically loyal; expert meant trained. The photograph is real, taken by a White House photographer in 1972.

Why this matters: For a decade one woman decided what a country of 800 million could watch.

Check yourself: What did “better to be red than expert” mean for a doctor or a scientist?

Q11 story · Beijing and Mongolia · 1969–1972

Mao’s Chosen Heir, Lin Biao, Died Fleeing China in 1971

Lin Biao, compiler of the little red book, was Mao’s heir and was always shown at his side.
The official story: fearing Mao had turned on him, Lin plotted to kill Mao.
When his daughter exposed the plot, he fled toward the Soviet Union; the plane crashed in Mongolia.
The news was kept from the public for about a year.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 487.

Now the shock. Remember from Segment 1 that Lin Biao compiled the little red book and in 1969 was written into the party constitution as Mao’s successor. “To the public, Lin Biao was Mao’s most devoted disciple, regularly photographed standing next to him. Yet according to the official account, Lin became afraid that Mao had turned against him and decided to assassinate him. When Lin’s daughter exposed his plot, Lin decided to flee to the Soviet Union. His plane, however, ran out of fuel and crashed over Mongolia” (p. 487). It was 13 September 1971; everyone on board died.

“Whatever the truth of this bizarre story, news of his plot was kept out of the press for a year, the leadership apparently unsure how to tell the people that Lin Biao turned out to be another Liu Shaoqi, a secret traitor who had managed to reach the second highest position in the political hierarchy” (p. 487).

Both photographs are real. After 1971, Ebrey notes, the army’s power declined.

Why this matters: If Mao’s closest disciple was a traitor, people began to ask what else they had been told.

Check yourself: Why would Lin Biao’s fall shock people more than Liu Shaoqi’s?

Segment 3 of 3 · 1972–1976

1976

A visit from an American president, two deaths, an earthquake, and an arrest ended the Mao era.

Q12 story · Beijing · 1972–1975

Nixon Came to Beijing, and Deng Xiaoping Came Back

As Mao’s health declined, the radicals around Jiang Qing faced the moderates around Zhou Enlai.
Fearing the Soviet Union more than America, China welcomed President Nixon in February 1972.
In 1973 many purged leaders, including Deng Xiaoping, were brought back to high posts.
Zhou was dying of cancer, and Deng took over much of his work.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 487.

After Lin Biao’s death, Ebrey writes, “Mao’s health was in decline, and he played less and less of a role in day-to-day management. The leading contenders for power were the more radical faction led by Jiang Qing and the more moderate faction led by Zhou Enlai” (p. 487).

Foreign policy changed first. You saw in Lecture 2 how China broke with Moscow. Ch. 27 L2 · revisionists ↗ In 1969 Chinese and Soviet troops clashed on their border. Now China “softened its antagonistic stance toward the outside world and in 1972 welcomed U.S. President Richard Nixon to visit and pursue improving relations” (p. 487). The enemy of China’s enemy had become a useful friend.

At home, “in 1973 many disgraced leaders, including Deng Xiaoping, were reinstated to important posts” (p. 487). Deng, back from his factory in Jiangxi, soon ran the government for the dying Zhou. The photograph is real.

Why this matters: The visit began the opening to the West that later reshaped China.

Q12 story · Tiananmen Square · January–April 1976

China Mourned Zhou Enlai, and the Radicals Crushed the Mourning

Zhou Enlai died of cancer in January 1976.
On 4 April, the day for honoring the dead, about 2 million people brought wreaths for him to Tiananmen Square.
The radicals called it a counterrevolutionary incident and sent in the militia the next day.
Deng was blamed and stripped of his posts; Mao made Hua Guofeng premier and his successor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 487–488.

The first death came in January. “Zhou Enlai died in January after a long struggle with cancer” (p. 487). Many Chinese saw him as the leader who had protected people during the worst years. The radicals did not allow much public mourning. “In March 1976 a newspaper controlled by the radicals implied that Zhou Enlai was a capitalist roader” (p. 488).

The people answered. “On April 4, the traditional day for honoring the dead, an estimated 2 million people flocked to Tiananmen Square to lay wreaths in honor of Zhou. The radicals saw this as an act of opposition to themselves, had it labeled a counterrevolutionary incident, and called the militia out” (p. 488). The square was cleared by force on 5 April.

Deng Xiaoping was blamed and stripped of his posts. Mao chose a little-known loyalist, Hua Guofeng, as premier and first vice-chairman of the party. That made Hua his successor.

Why this matters: For the first time since 1949, a huge crowd defied the radicals in public.

Q12 story · Tangshan and Beijing · July 1976

An Earthquake Leveled the City of Tangshan in July

Early on 28 July 1976 an earthquake destroyed the industrial city of Tangshan, east of Beijing.
It killed hundreds of thousands of people; the official toll was about 242,000.
The government refused foreign help and relied on soldiers and local people.
Many Chinese still believed disasters warned that a dynasty’s time was ending.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 487.

The second blow came from the earth. “In July, north China was rocked by a huge earthquake that killed hundreds of thousands” (p. 487). It struck before four in the morning on 28 July 1976, when most people were asleep, and flattened the coal and steel city of Tangshan, about 150 kilometers east of Beijing. The government’s official count was about 242,000 dead. Beijing refused offers of foreign aid, and soldiers dug survivors out of the ruins.

Ebrey opens her account of 1976 with a hint about how people read these events: “In 1976 those who still believed in portents from heaven would have sensed that something bad was going to happen” (p. 487). You have seen this idea many times in Chinese history. Floods, famines, and earthquakes were read as signs that Heaven was withdrawing its mandate from a failing ruler.

Both photographs are real and show only buildings: the ruins of Tangshan, and a cracked house in Beijing.

Why this matters: Many people read the disaster as a sign that the Mao era was ending.

Q12 story · Beijing · September 1976

Mao Zedong Died on 9 September 1976

Mao died just after midnight on 9 September 1976, aged eighty-two.
China held a month of national mourning; a million people gathered in Tiananmen Square.
As long as he lived, no one openly challenged him; now the struggle to succeed him came into the open.
His body was preserved and placed in a memorial hall in the square.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 487–488.

The second death was the one everyone had feared. “In September Mao Zedong died” (p. 487). He had been ill for years, and in his last months he could barely speak. He had led the party since the Long March, more than forty years.

Ebrey explains why his death changed everything: “As long as Mao was alive, no one would openly challenge him, but as his health failed, those near the top tried to position themselves for the inevitable” (p. 487). The main struggle, “it seems with hindsight,” was between the radicals, “Jiang Qing and her allies, later labeled the Gang of Four,” and the pragmatists (p. 488).

Hua Guofeng now held the top posts. He had a note in Mao’s hand from April that read, “With you in charge, I am at ease.” But Jiang Qing had her own claim to Mao’s legacy. Across the country, work units stopped to mourn him in silence.

Why this matters: Mao’s death opened the first real contest for power in the People’s Republic.

Q12 story · Beijing · 6 October 1976

Hua Guofeng Arrested the Gang of Four a Month After Mao’s Death

On 6 October 1976 Hua Guofeng, with Marshal Ye Jianying and Mao’s guard chief Wang Dongxing, struck first.
Jiang Qing and her three allies, the Gang of Four, were arrested.
Crowds celebrated in the streets; the four were blamed for the Cultural Revolution.
Deng Xiaoping returned in 1977 and soon pushed Hua aside.

Names to know

Hua Guofeng Mao’s successor, 1976

Ye Jianying army marshal

Jiang Qing Mao’s widow

Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, Wang Hongwen her allies in the Gang of Four

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 488; MacFarquhar and Schoenhals, Mao’s Last Revolution (2006).

Here is the sentence for Review Question 12. “After a month of national mourning for Mao, Jiang Qing and the rest of the Gang of Four were arrested” (p. 488).

Who made the arrests? Ebrey writes that “the pragmatists won out in the end” (p. 488). The man who acted was Mao’s successor, Hua Guofeng. On the night of 6 October 1976 he called the radical leaders to a meeting in the leaders’ compound, and guards led by Wang Dongxing seized them, with the backing of the army’s senior marshal, Ye Jianying. Deng Xiaoping was still out of office. He returned to his posts in July 1977, and within a few years he had replaced Hua as China’s real leader. MacFarquhar and Schoenhals tell the story of that night.

The four were tried in 1980–1981, and Jiang Qing was sentenced to death, later reduced to life in prison. Both photographs are real.

Why this matters: The arrest ended the Cultural Revolution and opened the way for Deng’s reforms.

Summary · All of China · 1949 and 1976

China in 1976 Was Healthier and Better Schooled, but Far Less Free

No foreign power dominated China, and it presented itself as a model to poorer nations.
School enrollment more than doubled; life expectancy reached 67 for men and 69 for women.
Jobs and housing were provided, and inflation had been banished.
But there was no free press, peasants could not leave their villages, and people learned to distrust each other.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 488–489.

Step back and compare 1976 with 1949. Ebrey asks the question directly: “How different was China in 1976 compared to 1949? Although the Cultural Revolution had brought enormous strain and confusion, China was by many measures better off” (p. 489).

“It was not dominated by any other countries and held itself up as a model to developing nations. The proportion of the population in school more than doubled between 1950 and 1978. Life expectancy reached sixty-seven years for men and sixty-nine years for women, due in large part to better survival of infants and more accessible health care.” Unemployment and inflation were gone (p. 489).

“But life was also much more regimented and controlled. There was no longer anything resembling a free press.” Peasants could not leave their villages, graduates had little choice of jobs, and after repeated campaigns “people had learned to distrust each other.” Her verdict: “Material security, in other words, had been secured at a high cost” (p. 489).

Red Guards Wrote: “Long Live the Revolutionary Rebel Spirit”

Red Guards at the middle school attached to Tsinghua University wrote this poster in 1966.
The party journal Red Flag printed it in 1966 as a model for others.
It calls opponents “revisionists” and boasts of being arrogant, crude, and one-sided.

Your textbook prints a document from the first months of the Cultural Revolution. Ebrey explains that Red Guards “used ‘big character posters’ to declare their political values and revolutionary zeal.” This one was “selected by the journal Red Flag in November 1966 as exemplary.” It “was written by a group of Red Guards at a high school in Beijing” (p. 485).

PS · Judging Mao · Beijing · 1981

In 1981 the Party Judged That Mao’s Merits Were Primary and His Errors Secondary

In June 1981 the party issued its official verdict on Mao.
It praised his leadership in the revolution and his ideas, “Mao Zedong Thought.”
It blamed him for the errors of his later years, above all the Cultural Revolution.
Since then, critics have called him a monster; others warn that this excuses everyone else.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 488; Communist Party of China, Resolution on Certain Questions in the History of Our Party (1981).

How should China remember Mao? The party gave its answer in a long resolution in June 1981, under Deng Xiaoping. Ebrey writes that “when the party rendered its judgment of Mao, it still gave him high marks for his military leadership and his intellectual contributions to Marxist theory,” but assigned him much of the blame for what went wrong in his later years (p. 488). The resolution called the Cultural Revolution a disaster for which Mao bore the chief responsibility, and still concluded that his merits were primary and his errors secondary. The verdict is often summed up as “seventy percent right, thirty percent wrong.”

The debate did not end there. “Since then, Mao’s standing has further eroded.” Some critics portray him as a megalomaniac “totally indifferent to others’ suffering.” But “some Chinese intellectuals worry that making Mao a monster relieves everyone else of responsibility and undermines the argument that structural changes are needed to prevent comparable tragedies from recurring” (p. 488).

The photograph is real: Mao’s portrait on the Tiananmen gate in 1972.

Why this matters: How a country judges its founder shapes what it allows itself to change.

Check yourself: Why might blaming Mao alone be convenient for the people who worked with him?

PS · Judging Mao · Ming and People’s Republic · 1368 and 1949

Historians Have Compared Mao with Zhu Yuanzhang, Founder of the Ming

Both grew up in farming families and won power after long years of war. Ch. 14 L1 · purge-1376 ↗
Both brooked no opposition, executed perceived enemies, and suspected treason others could not see.
But Zhu’s system shaped the Ming for nearly three centuries.
Within a few years of Mao’s death, much of what he built was undone.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 488.

You met Zhu Yuanzhang, the Hongwu emperor, at the start of the Ming lectures: the orphaned peasant who became emperor and then purged tens of thousands of his own officials. The link takes you back to his purges. Ebrey ends her judgment of Mao by comparing the two founders.

“Mao Zedong has often been compared to Zhu Yuanzhang, the founder of the Ming Dynasty. Both grew up in farming households, though Mao never experienced the desperate poverty of Zhu’s childhood. Both were formed by the many years of warfare that preceded gaining military supremacy. Both brooked no opposition and had few scruples when it came to executing perceived opponents. Both tended toward the paranoid, suspecting traitorous intentions others did not perceive” (p. 488).

Then comes the difference. “Zhu Yuanzhang cast a shadow over the rest of the Ming Dynasty. As will be seen in the next chapter, within a short period after Mao’s death, much of what he had instituted was undone” (p. 488). The portrait is a real Ming court painting.

Why this matters: Comparing founders across six centuries tests what is new in modern China, and what is old.

Revisionism

Revisionism, in Mao’s use, meant “revising” Marxism away from class struggle and toward capitalist methods such as profits, bonuses, and experts in charge. Mao used it as a charge against the Soviet leaders, and then against his own colleagues.

Revisionism

Revisionism, in Mao’s use, meant steering socialism back toward capitalist methods and away from class struggle. Calling someone a revisionist made them an enemy of the revolution.

×

Red Guards

Red Guards were groups of students, mostly from high schools and universities, who pledged to defend Mao and attack his enemies, from 1966 to 1968.

×

Four Olds

The Four Olds were old customs, old habits, old culture, and old thinking. Red Guards treated anything traditional, religious, or foreign as one of them.

×

Struggle session

A struggle session was a public meeting at which a person accused of political crimes was forced to confess while a crowd shouted accusations, often with violence.

×

Capitalist roader

A capitalist roader was a party official accused of taking China down “the capitalist road.” The label could cost a person his job, his freedom, or his life.

×

Sent-down youth

Sent-down youth were city students and graduates sent to live and work in villages or on state farms, from 1968 until the late 1970s. Most could not return home for years.

×

Revolutionary committee

A revolutionary committee was the body that ran a province, city, factory, or school after 1967, made up of army officers, “revolutionary” cadres, and rebel leaders.

×

Model works

The model works (model operas) were eight revolutionary operas and ballets approved by Jiang Qing. From 1967 to 1976 they were nearly the only performances allowed.

×

Gang of Four

The Gang of Four was the name given after their arrest to Jiang Qing and her Shanghai allies Zhang Chunqiao, Yao Wenyuan, and Wang Hongwen, the leading radicals of the Cultural Revolution.

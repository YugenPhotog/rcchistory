The First Five-Year Plan had built steel mills and truck factories on the Soviet model. Ch. 27 L1 · five-year-plan ↗

Lecture 1 ended in 1956 with a party that could be proud. It had won a war in Korea, taken over business, redistributed the land, and then pooled it in collectives. The links on this slide take you back to the plan, the collectives, and the deal with Moscow.

Ebrey sums up the mood in 1957: “The standard of living was improving, support for the government was strong, and people were optimistic about the future” (p. 480). Yet Mao was not satisfied. Growth seemed too slow and too dependent on experts and money. People’s thinking had not changed as fast as he hoped.

This lecture follows what Mao did about that. He tried to change minds first, then the economy, and in both he refused to listen to bad news.

Segment 1 of 3 · 1956–1957

A Hundred Flowers

Mao invited intellectuals to criticize the party, and then punished the critics.
This segment teaches the history behind Review Question 6, and it ends with a short self-check and then the question itself.

Segment 1 is about China’s educated people, whom Ebrey calls intellectuals, and the party’s relationship with them from 1949 to 1957.

You will see four steps. The party first “reformed” intellectuals in the early 1950s. Then, in 1956, news from Moscow led Mao to invite criticism. In the spring of 1957 students and professors spoke up, some boldly. In June the party struck back with the Anti-Rightist Campaign (Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 479–480).

The photograph is real: Mao at a meeting with Shanghai’s leading scientists, teachers, and writers in July 1957, after the crackdown had begun. Many in that room were afraid.

Q6 concept · Universities and offices · 1950–1955

Concept: Intellectuals Had Already Been “Reformed” Once

Concept

Thought reform

Study meetings in which educated people confessed their old “bourgeois” ideas and thanked the party and Chairman Mao for correcting them.

Most educated people welcomed the new government, and the state kept them in their jobs.
Many had gone to Yan’an and learned that their job was to serve the party. Ch. 25 L4 · rectification ↗
In the early 1950s teachers, editors, and scientists had to confess and be “reeducated.”
There were no more private colleges or independent presses: the state was the only employer.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 479–480.

Before the Hundred Flowers, there was thought reform. Ebrey notes that most of China’s small educated elite were “ready and eager to serve the new government,” glad it had driven out the imperialists, ended inflation, and cleaned the streets. Thousands studying abroad hurried home (p. 479).

Mao, however, “distrusted intellectuals,” and since Yan’an he had been finding ways to put them under the party; the link takes you back to the rectification campaign there. In the early 1950s the people who staffed schools, universities, publishers, and research institutes were “reeducated.” Thought reform meant confessing “their subservience to capitalists and imperialists” and their “gratitude to Chairman Mao for having helped them realize these errors” (p. 480).

For some it felt like a conversion; for others it was devastating. And independence had lost its base: “There were no more rents or dividends, no more independent presses or private colleges” (p. 480).

Why this matters: By 1956 intellectuals had already learned that criticism could be dangerous.

Check yourself: What did a person have to confess in thought reform? Why might they hesitate to criticize the party later?

Q6 story · Moscow and Beijing · 1956

Khrushchev Denounced Stalin, and Mao Invited a Hundred Flowers to Bloom

In February 1956 Khrushchev’s “secret speech” told the Soviet party of Stalin’s crimes.
In the fall, Hungarians rose against their communist government, and Soviet tanks crushed them.
Mao asked intellectuals to help find party members who had become bullies or lost touch.
“Let a hundred flowers bloom” in culture, and “a hundred schools of thought contend” in science.

Names to know

Nikita Khrushchev Soviet leader after Stalin, 1953–1964

Deng Xiaoping party general secretary from 1956

Peng Dehuai defense minister, hero of Korea

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 480; Roderick MacFarquhar, The Origins of the Cultural Revolution, vol. 1 (1974).

Why would Mao invite criticism? The trigger came from Moscow. In February 1956 the new Soviet leader, Nikita Khrushchev, gave a “secret speech” to his party congress listing Stalin’s crimes. De-Stalinization, the attack on Stalin’s memory and methods, shook every communist party. If Stalin could be so wrong, so could any party. In the fall Hungarians rose against their own communist rulers, until Soviet tanks crushed them.

Ebrey writes that “in response to de-Stalinization in the Soviet Union, in 1956 Mao called on intellectuals to help him identify problems within the party, such as party members who had lost touch with the people or behaved like tyrants” (p. 480). His slogan came from classical China: “Let a hundred flowers bloom” in culture and “a hundred schools of thought contend” in science. As long as criticism was not “antagonistic” or “counterrevolutionary,” he said, it would strengthen the party. Roderick MacFarquhar’s history argues that Mao hoped open criticism would prevent a Hungary in China.

The photograph is a real official portrait of Khrushchev.

Why this matters: Events in Moscow kept shaping choices in Beijing.

Check yourself: What did Mao say criticism would do for the party? What limit did he set?

Q6 story · Beijing campuses · May 1957

In the Spring of 1957 Students and Professors Spoke Up

Scientists and engineers spoke first: they wanted party members to stop meddling in their work.
Mao praised the critics, and others lost their fear.
By May students were putting up wall posters, some with fierce charges.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 480; Gregor Benton and Alan Hunter, eds., Wild Lily, Prairie Fire (1995).

At first few dared. Then the criticism grew. Ebrey writes that “the first to come forward with criticisms were scientists and engineers who wanted party members to interfere less with their work” (p. 480). To encourage more, “Mao praised those who spoke up,” and soon “critics lost their inhibitions.”

The center of the storm was the universities. “By May 1957 college students were putting up wall posters, sometimes with highly inflammatory charges” (p. 480). A wall poster was a large handwritten sheet pasted on a public wall for everyone to read. At Peking University a whole stretch of wall became known as the “Democracy Wall.”

Read the poster quoted on the slide, from Qinghua University in Beijing. It attacked Mao by name and accused him of turning families against their own members. Ebrey takes it from a collection of Chinese protest writings edited by Gregor Benton and Alan Hunter.

Some critics, Ebrey notes elsewhere, had simply exposed abuses, such as secret shops where officials bought goods ordinary people could not.

The picture is an AI-generated interpretation made for this deck, not a historical source. Use it to imagine the scene, not as evidence.

Why this matters: For a few weeks Chinese people said in public what many had been thinking.

Check yourself: Who spoke up first, and what did they ask for?

Q6 story · Every work unit · 1957–1979

In June 1957 Deng Xiaoping Turned the Campaign Against the Critics

Every unit was pressed to name 5 percent of its staff as rightists.
Officially about 550,000 people were labeled; historians think the real number was much higher.
Many lost their influence at work, and hundreds of thousands were sent to labor in the countryside.
Most had to wait until 1979 to have the label removed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 480; Yen-lin Chung, “The Witch-Hunting Vanguard,” China Quarterly 206 (2011).

Then the flowers were cut down. “Did Mao plan this campaign to ferret out dissidents? Or was he shocked by the outpouring of criticism?” Ebrey asks. Either way, “in June 1957 the party announced a campaign against rightists orchestrated by the newly appointed secretary general of the party, Deng Xiaoping” (p. 480).

Units “were pressed to identify 5 percent of their staff members as rightists.” A quota meant someone had to be found, whether or not anyone had spoken. Ebrey tells of a railroad engineer sent to menial labor for twenty years because someone heard him say “how bold” about a critique of the party. The party’s own count was about 550,000 rightists. Estimates run far higher, up to the almost 3 million in your textbook, because many more people were punished under other labels.

The photographs are real: Luo Longji and Zhang Bojun, ministers from a small democratic party, became the most famous rightists of all. Neither was ever cleared.

Why this matters: After 1957, few people dared to tell the party anything it did not want to hear.

Check yourself: Why did a quota make the campaign so unfair?

Q7 story · Moscow and Beijing · 1957–1958

Mao Promised to Surpass Britain in Fifteen Years

In Moscow in November 1957 Mao promised that China would pass Britain’s industry within fifteen years.
China was poor in machines and money but rich in people: their labor would be the engine.
Experts who urged caution had just been silenced as “rightists.”
In May 1958 the party launched the Great Leap Forward.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 480; Frank Dikötter, Mao’s Great Famine (2010).

Mao’s second answer to slow progress was speed. Ebrey asks the question Mao asked: “Why was China unable to find a way to use what it was rich in—labor power—to modernize more rapidly?” (p. 480).

The plan was a Great Leap Forward. “Through the coordinated hard work of hundreds of millions of people, China would transform itself from a poor nation into a mighty one.” With the masses’ creative energy unleashed, “China would surpass Great Britain in industrial output within fifteen years” (p. 480). Mao made that promise in Moscow in November 1957, in answer to Khrushchev’s boast that the Soviet Union would pass the United States. The party formally launched the Leap in May 1958.

Remember who was missing. The economists and engineers who might have said the targets were impossible had just been labeled rightists. Frank Dikötter’s history of the Leap begins with that silence.

The photograph is real: Mao signing a declaration of the world’s communist parties in Moscow, the trip where he made his promise.

Why this matters: The Leap replaced experts and money with mass labor and enthusiasm.

Check yourself: What did China have plenty of, according to Mao, and how would that make it rich?

Q7 story · Every village · 1958

Collectives Were Merged into Giant Communes with Free Mess Halls

In a few months of 1958 the collectives were merged into about 26,000 communes.
Private garden plots were banned, and peasants were organized like soldiers.
Free mess halls replaced family kitchens, a “step toward communism.”
Men marched off to dig canals and dams, and women took over much of the field work.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 481.

The Leap began in the countryside. “In 1958, in a matter of months, agricultural collectives all over the country were amalgamated into gigantic communes,” Ebrey writes (p. 481). By the end of the year there were about 26,000 of them, many with five thousand households or more.

Life was reorganized like an army. “Private garden plots were banned. Peasants were organized into quasi-military production brigades and referred to as fighters on the agricultural front.” Men were marched to work on public projects; women took over much of the field work; young people drilled in the militia (p. 481).

With everyone working long hours, there was no time to cook. So units set up mess halls “where food was free, a measure commentators hailed as a step toward communism” (p. 481). For a few months people ate as much as they liked. That used up the grain that would be needed the next spring.

Both photographs are real: villagers celebrating a new commune in 1958, and a crowded mess hall in 1959.

Why this matters: The commune took away the household’s land, its kitchen, and its control of its own time.

Check yourself: Name two things the commune took over from the family.

Q7 story · Villages, schools, and factories · Fall 1958

Backyard Furnaces Turned Pots and Pans into Useless Iron

In the late summer of 1958 Mao set out to double China’s steel output in a single year.
Communes, schools, and offices built small furnaces, and people melted down pots and tools.
Most of the iron was too poor to use; it clogged railroad yards across the country.
Farmers at the furnaces left the harvest to rot in the fields.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 481–482.

The most famous image of the Leap is the backyard furnace. “During the late summer and fall of 1958, communes, factories, schools, and other units set up ‘backyard steel furnaces’ in order to double steel production,” Ebrey writes (p. 481). The target was to make about 10.7 million tons of steel in 1958, twice the year before.

Tens of millions of people fed the furnaces day and night. They cut down trees for fuel and melted pots, woks, and even farm tools to meet their quotas. Some Leap projects lasted, such as bridges, reservoirs, and irrigation works. But many were done “with such haste and with so little technical knowledge that they did more harm than good” (p. 481). “The quality of most of the steel made in backyard furnaces was too poor to be used. Instead it filled railroad cars and clogged train yards all over the country, disrupting transportation” (p. 482).

And in the fall of 1958 the labor went to iron, not to the harvest. Grain rotted in the fields.

The photograph is real: a backyard furnace and its workers.

Why this matters: The furnaces wasted labor, fuel, and tools at the moment the harvest needed them.

Check yourself: Give two ways the furnaces made the coming food crisis worse.

Q7 story · Counties and provinces · 1958

Afraid to Disappoint Mao, Cadres Reported Harvests That Did Not Exist

Counties claimed harvests up 1,000 and even 10,000 percent.
The party announced that grain output had nearly doubled in one year.
Plans were made by local cadres eager to show zeal, not by experts.
After the Anti-Rightist Campaign, no one wanted to report failure.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, pp. 481–482.

Why did no one stop it? Because the reports said it was working. Ebrey writes that “counties claimed 1,000 and even 10,000 percent increases in agriculture production. The Central Committee announced with great fanfare that production had nearly doubled in a single year” (p. 481).

These numbers were false. With economists and engineers removed in the campaign against rightists, plans “were formulated not by experts but by local cadres eager to show their political zeal” (pp. 481–482). Some fields were plowed so deep that they were ruined. And “it was not just the legacy of the Hundred Flowers campaign that kept cadres from reporting failures” (p. 482): a cadre who reported a poor harvest could be labeled a rightist himself.

The poster is a real one from 1958. A farmer rides a wave on a giant ear of grain, with workers, machines, and a mountain of crops behind him. Posters like this told people that miracles were happening in other villages, so they had better report miracles too.

Why this matters: False reports meant the state took grain that did not exist.

Check yourself: Why were cadres afraid to report the truth? Connect your answer to Segment 1.

Q7 story · Mount Lu, Jiangxi · July 1959

Peng Dehuai Warned Mao at Lushan, and Mao Purged Him

At a party meeting in July 1959, Peng gave Mao a private letter about the Leap’s problems.
He praised the Leap, then warned of exaggeration at every level and of the free meals.
Mao circulated the letter, called it “right opportunism,” and made the leaders choose between them.
No one sided with Peng. He was dismissed, and the Leap went on.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 482; Li Rui, Lushan huiyi shilu (1989).

In July 1959 the party’s leaders met at Lushan, a mountain resort in Jiangxi. One man tried to tell the truth: Peng Dehuai, the defense minister and hero of the Korean War, whom you met in Lecture 1.

Peng put his worries in “a private letter he gave to Mao.” He began “by saying that the Great Leap was an indisputable success but pointed to the tendency to exaggerate at all levels, which made it difficult for the leadership to know the real situation.” He also warned that people wrongly thought “the food problem was solved” (p. 482). His language was polite.

“Mao’s reaction was not.” He handed out copies of the letter, denounced Peng for “right opportunism,” and “made the senior cadres choose between him and Peng, and none had the courage to side with Peng” (p. 482). Peng was dismissed. Problems were now blamed on doubters, and a new campaign hunted them. Li Rui, Mao’s secretary at the meeting, later wrote its record.

Both photographs are real: Peng in 1955, and the hall at Lushan.

Why this matters: Lushan turned a mistake into a catastrophe: the Leap was pushed harder just as famine began.

Check yourself: What did Peng warn about, and why did no other leader support him?

“Mao’s faulty economics, coupled with droughts and floods, ended up creating one of the worst famines in world history” (p. 482). That sentence is the Reading Guide anchor for Review Question 7.

Follow the chain. The 1958 harvest was “wildly exaggerated, and no one attempted to validate reports. Tax grain was removed from the countryside on the basis of the reported harvests, leaving little for local consumption.” Read the chart: grain fell from 200 million tons in 1958 to 170 million in 1959 and 144 million in 1960. By 1960 many people had less than half of what they needed to survive. And peasants “were not allowed to hit the roads, as people had always done during famines in the past” (p. 482).

Ebrey writes that census reconstructions show “on the order of 30 million ‘excess’ deaths,” meaning deaths above the normal rate. The Pierian Spring shows why historians’ estimates differ. By 1961 the leaders retreated: mess halls closed, private plots returned, and grain was bought from abroad.

Q9 story · Beijing · 1958–1959

Mao Shelled Taiwan’s Islands and Khrushchev Decided Mao Was Reckless

Mao respected Stalin but never Khrushchev, and he resented the attack on Stalin.
In August 1958 China shelled the islands near Fujian held by Taiwan; Moscow feared a war with America.
Khrushchev called the communes foolish, and Mao made light of nuclear war.
In 1959 Moscow took back its promise to help China build an atomic bomb, and would not side with China against India.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Lorenz M. Lüthi, The Sino-Soviet Split (2008).

The friendship had always been uneven. Stalin, through the Comintern, “had done as much to hinder the success of the Chinese Communist Party as to aid it,” Ebrey writes, yet Mao respected him. “Mao never had the same respect for Stalin’s successor, Khrushchev” (p. 483).

The Leap made it worse. China raised its anti-imperialist rhetoric “and began shelling the islands off the coast of Fujian still held by the Nationalists on Taiwan,” and the Russians feared China “would drag them into a war with the United States.” Khrushchev visited Beijing in 1958 and 1959 and concluded that Mao was a “romantic deviationist,” especially wrong about the communes (p. 483).

Then came the bomb. When Mao made light of nuclear weapons, saying it would not matter much if China lost half its people in a war that destroyed capitalism, “Khrushchev went back on his earlier promise to give China nuclear weapons.” Moscow also stayed neutral in China’s border disputes with India, “infuriating Mao.” Lorenz Lüthi’s history shows how ideas and pride drove the quarrel as much as interests.

The photograph is real: Mao and Khrushchev in Beijing in 1958, still smiling.

Why this matters: Each side began to see the other as dangerous, not just mistaken.

Check yourself: Name two things Khrushchev disliked about Mao’s policies.

Q9 story · Factories and building sites · July 1960

In July 1960 Moscow Called Home Its Experts and Their Blueprints

Since 1950 thousands of Soviet experts had helped build China’s factories, mines, and bridges.
In April 1960, on Lenin’s ninetieth birthday, Chinese leaders attacked Soviet foreign policy in print.
In July Khrushchev ordered the experts home, “to take their blueprints and spare parts with them.”
Hundreds of projects stopped half-built, just as the famine struck.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Shen Zhihua, A Short History of Sino-Soviet Relations (2020).

The quarrel went public in 1960. “In April 1960, Chinese leaders celebrated the ninetieth anniversary of Lenin’s birth by lambasting Soviet foreign policy,” Ebrey writes (p. 483). They did it in a series of articles claiming that they, not Moscow, understood Lenin.

Khrushchev struck back where it hurt. “In July 1960, just as famine was hitting China, Khrushchev ordered the Soviet experts to return and to take their blueprints and spare parts with them” (p. 483). That sentence is the Reading Guide anchor for Review Question 9. About 1,400 experts left within weeks, and hundreds of projects were left unfinished. Shen Zhihua, a leading Chinese historian of the alliance, describes the shock in Beijing.

The photographs are real, and they show what the experts had meant. Soviet and Chinese engineers built the first bridge across the Yangzi at Wuhan, opened in 1957, carrying the railroad from Beijing to Guangzhou. All the help that had gone into China’s industry, Ebrey notes, “seemed to have been wasted” in Soviet eyes once Mao turned to backyard furnaces.

Why this matters: China lost its main source of technology at the worst possible moment.

Check yourself: Why was the timing of the withdrawal especially painful for China?

By 1963 Each Side Called the Other a Traitor to Marxism

Mao denounced Khrushchev as a revisionist and a “capitalist roader.”
China challenged Moscow’s leadership, and communist parties everywhere split into pro-Soviet and pro-China camps.
Both sides moved troops to their long border, and clashes followed.
When the Vietnam War grew, China did not help Moscow supply Hanoi, but it sent about 320,000 of its own support troops in 1965–1969.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Chen Jian, “China’s Involvement in the Vietnam War, 1964–69,” China Quarterly 142 (1995).

By 1963 the split was complete. “Mao was publicly denouncing Khrushchev as a revisionist and capitalist roader and challenging the Soviet Union’s leadership of the international communist movement” (Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483). A capitalist roader was someone who claimed to be a communist but was leading the country back toward capitalism. Remember the term: in Lecture 3 Mao uses it against his own colleagues.

“Communist parties throughout the world soon divided into pro-Soviet and pro-China factions.” Both sides moved troops to their border, “which provoked border clashes” (p. 483).

Vietnam shows how bitter it became. China refused to cooperate with Moscow in supplying North Vietnam and blocked Soviet shipments by rail. Yet it was far from neutral. Chen Jian’s research in Chinese sources shows that about 320,000 Chinese engineering, railroad, and antiaircraft troops served in North Vietnam between 1965 and 1969, so that Vietnamese soldiers could go south to fight.

Q9 story · Lop Nur, Xinjiang · 16 October 1964

China Exploded Its Own Atomic Bomb in 1964

Without Soviet help, Chinese scientists built a bomb on their own.
Its code name, “596,” recalled June 1959, the month Moscow broke its promise.
China also dug air-raid shelters and moved defense factories deep inland.
China now stood alone, against both superpowers.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; John W. Lewis and Xue Litai, China Builds the Bomb (1988).

The split had one result Mao could be proud of. “China developed its own atomic weapons program, exploding its first nuclear device in 1964,” Ebrey writes (p. 483). The test took place on 16 October 1964 at Lop Nur, in the desert of Xinjiang.

Chinese scientists, many of them trained abroad, did the work without the Soviet help that had been promised and withdrawn. John Lewis and Xue Litai’s history of the program explains the code name. The project was called “596,” after June 1959, when Moscow told Beijing it would not supply the model bomb. Building it, the scientists said, was the answer.

Fear of attack also reshaped the country. China “built air raid shelters on a massive scale and devoted enormous resources to constructing a defense establishment in mountainous inland areas far from both the sea and the Soviet border” (p. 483). The Pierian Spring tells that story, the Third Front.

Both photographs are real: the test cloud, and Zhou Enlai announcing the news to a cheering audience in Beijing.

Why this matters: The bomb made China a nuclear power and a rival to both superpowers.

PS · Q8 story · Cities and villages · 1955–1963

Household Registration Tied Peasants to the Villages Where They Were Born

From 1955 every person was registered by hukou as rural or urban, tied to a birthplace or, for a wife, her husband’s village.
Regulations of 1958 made it law: only legal city residents got the ration coupons to buy grain in town.
When the Leap collapsed, millions of new workers were sent back to the villages.
The registration kept them, and other peasants, from coming back.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Tiejun Cheng and Mark Selden, “The Origins and Social Consequences of China’s Hukou System,” China Quarterly 139 (1994).

The Leap left one more legacy, and it lasted for decades. “Beginning in 1955 a system of population registration bound rural people to the villages of their birth or, in the case of married women, to their husbands’ villages,” Ebrey writes (p. 483). The regulations of January 1958 made it the law of the land.

The Leap had pulled millions of peasants into new city factories. When “the hasty expansion of the nation’s industrial plant was reversed, millions of unemployed workers were sent back to the countryside,” some 20 million between 1961 and 1963. To keep them from returning, “a system of urban household registration was introduced. Only those with permission to reside in a city could get the ration coupons needed to purchase grain there” (p. 483). Without coupons, a peasant in town could not buy food. Children inherited their mother’s status.

Tiejun Cheng and Mark Selden traced the system’s origins. The photograph is real: officials sent from a town to the countryside in 1957.

Why this matters: Where you were born now decided almost everything about your life.

PS · Q8 story · City and countryside · 1958–1980s

City People Got Rations, Pensions, and Schools; Villagers Got the Hoe

State factory workers had cheap housing, pensions, health care, and subsidized food.
City children stayed in school through middle school, and the brightest went further.
Villagers were made to grow grain and could only add more labor: weeding, terracing, digging ditches.
In the poorest places, the rules locked families into poverty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Jeremy Brown, City Versus Countryside in Mao’s China (2012).

Now look at what the line between city and village meant. Ebrey writes that hukou rules “had the unintended effect of locking rural communities with unfavorable man–land ratios into dismal poverty” (p. 483). That sentence is the Reading Guide anchor for Review Question 8. A man–land ratio compares the number of people to the amount of farmland.

“It is not surprising that the rural poor would want to move to the cities. Those who got jobs in state-run factories had low-cost housing, pensions, and health care, not to mention a reliable supply of subsidized food. Children in the cities could stay in school through middle school” (p. 483). Only a few rich communes came close.

In poor regions, farmers “forced by the government to concentrate on growing grain, could do little to improve their situations other than invest more labor.” They weeded more, terraced hills, and dug ditches, for little return, and “agricultural productivity (the return for each hour of labor) fell across the country” (p. 483). Jeremy Brown’s history shows how deep the divide became.

The photograph is real: new workers’ housing in Shanghai, 1952.

Why this matters: China became two societies, and the gap still shapes the country today.

No one counted the dead at the time; historians work back from the censuses of 1953, 1964, and 1982.
An “excess death” is a death above the normal rate; the answer depends on what “normal” was.
Serious estimates run from about 15 million to 45 million; your textbook uses about 30 million.

In the main lecture you read Ebrey’s figure: “on the order of 30 million ‘excess’ deaths” (p. 482). Here is why historians give different numbers.

No one counted the dead at the time. Officials who reported deaths risked being called rightists. So demographers work backward from the censuses of 1953, 1964, and 1982 and from birth and death records. An excess death is a death above the number expected in a normal year. If you assume a higher normal death rate, you get fewer excess deaths, and the reverse. Some estimates also count babies who were never born.

The results range widely: about 15 to 20 million in some Chinese government demographers’ work, about 30 million in the studies Ebrey follows, 36 million in Yang Jisheng’s Tombstone, and 45 million in Frank Dikötter’s Mao’s Great Famine.

The table shows how fast deaths rose in five provinces, from 1957 to 1960 (p. 483). In Anhui deaths rose almost ninefold.

Why this matters: Every estimate, low or high, puts this among the worst famines in history.

PS · Q9 story · Sichuan, Guizhou, and Yunnan · 1964–1980

The Third Front Moved Factories into the Mountains, Far from Any Enemy

In 1964, fearing attack by America or the Soviet Union, Mao ordered a “third front” of industry deep inland.
Steelworks, arms plants, and railways rose in the mountains of the southwest, such as the Panzhihua steel mill.
From 1964 to 1980 it took about two-fifths of all state investment.
Many factories were hidden in valleys and caves, far from markets, and were costly to run.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 27, p. 483; Covell F. Meyskens, Mao’s Third Front (2020); Barry Naughton, “The Third Front,” China Quarterly 115 (1988).

Ebrey gives the Third Front one sentence: China devoted “enormous resources to constructing a defense establishment in mountainous inland areas far from both the sea and the Soviet border” (p. 483). It was one of the biggest building projects of the Mao era.

The name came from military thinking. The coast was the first front, closest to an American attack; the interior provinces of the southwest and northwest were the third, the safest. In 1964, as the war in Vietnam grew and the split with Moscow hardened, Mao ordered factories built there. Covell Meyskens’s history describes millions of workers, soldiers, and engineers sent to remote valleys. Barry Naughton estimated the cost: about two-fifths of state investment from 1964 to 1980.

The Chengdu–Kunming Railway, opened in 1970, cut through some of the steepest mountains in China to reach the new steel city of Panzhihua. Both photographs are real: workers carving the line, and Yi villagers carrying timber for it.

Why this matters: The split with Moscow changed where China built its industry for a generation.

Check yourself: Why build factories in remote mountains? What did it cost?

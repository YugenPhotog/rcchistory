The Empress and the Boxers

Cixi, the Lost Wars with France and Japan, the Hundred Days, and the Boxer Uprising

1875–1901 · Study Buddy edition

Welcome to the last lecture on Chapter 18. It belongs to one woman, the Empress Dowager Cixi, who was the most powerful person at the Qing court for almost fifty years.

Q10 story · Beijing · June 1900

In June 1900 the Empress Dowager Declared War on Eight Foreign Powers at Once

In June 1900 armed bands called Boxers were attacking foreigners and Chinese Christians around Beijing.
Foreign troops seized the Dagu forts, and the German minister was shot dead in the street.
Cixi declared war on all eight powers at once.
How did one woman come to make that choice?

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 329–330; the Dagu forts (Esherick, 1987). Image: probably Franz Scholz, 1900.

Start at the end. In the summer of 1900, bands of young men the foreigners called Boxers were attacking missionaries and Chinese Christians across north China. "Eight foreign powers announced that they would send troops to protect missionaries. Then, on June 20, 1900, the German minister was shot dead in the street" (Ebrey, p. 329).

The Empress Dowager Cixi then "declared war on the eight powers." The textbook is blunt: "Although she had repeatedly seen China defeated when it was fighting only one of these powers, she deluded herself into thinking that if the people became sufficiently enraged, they could drive all eight out."

Instructor note: the textbook leaves out one step. On 17 June 1900, four days before Cixi's declaration, allied warships seized the Dagu forts that guarded the sea approach to Tianjin and Beijing, and the court took this as the start of war (Esherick, 1987; Cohen, 1997).

The photograph lines up soldiers from the foreign contingents that came to fight, from Britain and its empire to Japan. Count them, and imagine facing them all together.

This lecture works back to explain that decision. Reading Guide Q10 (p. 324) asks how Cixi shaped Qing politics and balanced reform and conservatism. Watch her choices as you go.

Why this matters: The war of 1900 was the last of a series of choices Cixi made over forty years.

Recap · Self-strengthening

Recap: After 1861 Reformers Built Arsenals, Schools, and a Navy

In 1861 Cixi and Ci'an became regents for her young son, with Prince Gong as chief minister · Ch. 18 L3 · regency-1861 ↗
Reformers built arsenals and translated Western books · Ch. 18 L3 · arsenal ↗
Li Hongzhang added mines, mills, steamships, and the Beiyang fleet · Ch. 18 L3 · li-projects ↗
The goal was to make China strong enough to resist the foreigners · Ch. 18 L3 · concept-self-strengthening ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322–323; Ch. 18 L3 for self-strengthening; Ci'an as co-regent (Rawski, 1998).

Recall where the last lecture left off. In 1861 the Xianfeng emperor died, and his five-year-old son came to the throne. His mother, Cixi, and the senior empress dowager, Ci'an, ruled for him as regents, with his uncle Prince Gong as chief minister. Instructor note: Ebrey (p. 322) names only Prince Gong and Cixi as regents; Lecture 3 explains the coup of 1861.

Under their rule, officials such as Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang carried out self-strengthening: the Jiangnan Arsenal, the foreign office, schools for languages and science, students sent to America, and by 1888 a modern fleet. The links take you back to those slides in the last lecture.

Now we follow the other regent. "During the self-strengthening period, the most powerful person at court was Empress Dowager Cixi" (p. 324). Everything the reformers did, they did with her permission.

Keep one question in mind from the last lecture: was self-strengthening enough? This lecture tests it in two wars.

Why this matters: The reforms you studied last time are the background for every choice Cixi made.

Q10 story · Beijing · 1875

When Her Son Died in 1875, Cixi Chose Her Three-Year-Old Nephew as Emperor

In 1875 Cixi's son, the Tongzhi emperor, died of smallpox at eighteen.
Cixi chose his three-year-old cousin, her own nephew, as the Guangxu emperor.
With another child on the throne, she could stay regent for years.
She formally retired in 1889 but still read the memorials and approved key appointments.

Names in This Story

Cixi the Empress Dowager, 1835–1908; pronounced roughly "tsih-shee"

Tongzhi her son, emperor 1861–1875

Guangxu her nephew, emperor 1875–1908

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 324, 325; Guangxu as Cixi's nephew (Ebrey, p. 324 caption). Image: Qing court painter.

Cixi's son never really ruled. "In 1875, when her son, the Tongzhi (tung-jih) emperor, was nineteen, he died of smallpox, barely having had a chance to rule on his own. Cixi chose his cousin to succeed him, who is known as the Guangxu (gwahng-shyew) emperor (r. 1875–1908). By selecting a boy of four, Cixi could continue in power as regent for many years to come" (Ebrey, p. 324). The photo caption on the same page adds that Guangxu was her nephew; his mother was Cixi's sister.

Instructor note: the textbook's "nineteen" and "a boy of four" are Chinese counts, in which a child is one at birth. By Western count Tongzhi (born 1856) died at eighteen, and Guangxu (born August 1871) was three; the slide uses Western ages, as the other decks do.

Choosing a cousin broke the usual rule that an emperor's heir came from the next generation, and some officials protested. Cixi got her way.

Even when Guangxu came of age, she held on. "Cixi officially retired in 1889… She insisted, however, on reading all memorials and approving key appointments" (p. 325).

Source beyond Ebrey: Guangxu as Cixi's nephew (Ebrey, p. 324 caption).

Why this matters: Cixi stayed in power by keeping children on the throne.

Q10 concept · Regent and empress dowager

Concept: An Empress Dowager Could Rule as Regent for a Child Emperor

Concept

Regent · Empress Dowager

A regent rules for a monarch who is too young or unable to rule. An empress dowager is the mother of a reigning emperor, honored above everyone at court except the emperor himself.

As the emperor's mother, Cixi held the highest rank at court.
As regent, she read state papers and made appointments for a child emperor.
Qing custom said women should not rule, so she listened from behind a screen.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322, 324; the screen behind the throne (Rawski, 1998). Image: Xunling, about 1903.

Two terms explain how Cixi held power. A regent rules for a monarch too young to rule. An empress dowager is the mother of an emperor, and in a Confucian court she outranked everyone except her son. Filial piety, honoring one's parents, meant that even an adult emperor owed her obedience.

Cixi was both. She became empress dowager in 1861, when her son came to the throne, and ruled as one of his two regents, alongside the senior empress dowager, Ci'an (Ebrey, pp. 322, 324; Ci'an from Rawski, 1998).

But Qing custom said a woman should not rule openly. From sources beyond Ebrey: Cixi received officials seated behind a yellow silk screen, with the child emperor on the throne in front of her. The phrase for this, "listening to government from behind the curtain," describes how she ruled for years.

The photograph shows her late in life at the Summer Palace, which she had rebuilt, surrounded by court ladies and eunuchs.

Why this matters: Cixi's power came from her place in the imperial family, not from any office, which is why she needed child emperors.

Q10 story · Cixi's politics

Cixi Defended Manchu Privilege and Played Reformers Against Their Conservative Critics

The Manchu establishment feared it was being pushed aside by Chinese officials like Li Hongzhang.
Cixi presented herself as a staunch defender of Manchu privileges.
She needed modernizers like Li and rewarded them with titles and honors.
But she kept them in check by also encouraging their conservative critics.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 324.

The Reading Guide asks you to copy this sentence for Q10: "Cixi was a skillful political operator" (Ebrey, p. 324). Here is what the textbook means.

After the Taiping war, Chinese officials such as Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang held great power in the provinces, with their own armies. Many Manchus at court worried. Cixi "recognized the Manchu establishment's fears that they were being sidelined and presented herself to them as a staunch defender of Manchu privileges."

At the same time, "she needed modernizers like Li Hongzhang and cajoled them with titles and honors, but she kept them in check by also encouraging their conservative critics." To cajole is to coax someone with flattery or rewards.

Think of it as a balance. If the reformers grew too strong, she let the conservatives attack them. If the conservatives blocked everything, she let the reformers build. No side could ever win completely, and she stayed on top.

This balance is the heart of Reading Guide Q10. Watch how it worked when war came with France.

Why this matters: Cixi's balancing kept her in power, but it also kept reform half-finished.

Q11 concept · Tributary state

Concept: Korea, the Ryukyus, and Vietnam Were Loyal Tributary States of the Qing

Concept

Tributary State

A neighboring kingdom that ruled itself but sent regular missions with gifts to the Chinese emperor, accepted his calendar and his recognition of its kings, and received gifts and trade in return.

Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, and Vietnam had been regular, loyal tributaries · Ch. 14 L2 · tribute-order ↗
To Westerners, this made them seem not fully independent countries · Ch. 16 L3 · rc-tribute ↗
Under Cixi, the old tribute system was finally dismantled.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 324; Ch. 14 L2 and Ch. 16 L3 for the tribute order. Image: Tōjō Kindai, 1848.

You met the tribute system in Chapters 14 and 16. Neighboring rulers sent envoys with gifts to the emperor, accepted his recognition of their kings, and used his calendar. In return they received gifts, trade, and sometimes protection. The links take you back to those slides.

The Reading Guide asks you to copy this sentence for Q11: "It was under Cixi's watch that the old tribute system was finally dismantled" (Ebrey, p. 324). "Three neighboring countries—Korea, the Ryukyu Islands, and Vietnam—had been regular, loyal tributaries, making them seem to Westerners not fully independent countries."

That last point matters. In European law, a country was either sovereign or it belonged to someone else. The tribute relationship fit neither box, and Japan and France used that confusion to pull these states away from China.

This Japanese map of 1848 shows Korea, the Ryukyus, and Vietnam colored as part of the Qing world.

Why this matters: Losing its tributaries meant losing China's place at the center of its own world order.

Check yourself: Why did Westerners think tributary states were not fully independent?

Q11 story · The Ryukyus and Vietnam · 1870s–1880s

Japan Took the Ryukyu Islands in the 1870s, and France Moved on Vietnam

In the 1870s Japan forced the Ryukyu Islands away from China.
Vietnam's government was closely modeled on China's, with Confucian teachings and examinations.
France gained privileges in Vietnam by treaty in 1874 and seized Hanoi in 1882.
The Vietnamese ruler asked the Qing for help.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 324; Okinawa Prefecture, 1879 (Smits, 1999). Image: Charles-Édouard Hocquard, 1884–1885.

The tributaries fell one by one. "Japan forced the Ryukyus away from China in the 1870s. In the 1880s, France forced Vietnam away" (Ebrey, p. 324). From sources beyond Ebrey: in 1879 Japan deposed the Ryukyu king and made his islands a Japanese prefecture, Okinawa.

Vietnam mattered more. "Although no part of Vietnam had been under direct Chinese rule since Tang times, Chinese influence there had remained strong. The Vietnamese government was closely modeled on the Chinese, supported Zhu Xi's Confucian teachings, and used examinations to recruit officials." The Qing saw Vietnam "as one of its most loyal vassal states, next only to Korea."

But "France was eyeing 'Indochina' as the best target for imperialist expansion, given Britain's strength in India." "In 1874, France gained privileges in Vietnam through treaties and in 1882 seized Hanoi. When the Vietnamese ruler requested Chinese help," the Qing had to decide whether to fight.

The photograph shows the palace in Hanoi's citadel, built on Chinese lines, taken by a French army doctor during the war.

Why this matters: Vietnam's appeal forced the Qing to choose between defending a tributary and avoiding a war.

Q10 story · Beijing and Fuzhou · 1883–1884

Li and Prince Gong Urged Peace, but Cixi Sided with the Hard-Liners

Realists like Prince Gong and Li Hongzhang urged the court to avoid war.
A shrill group of conservative critics insisted China must stop giving in.
Cixi hesitated, let Li negotiate, then scuttled the draft treaty after protests.
When France issued an ultimatum, Cixi sided with the hard-liners, and war began.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 324; Prince Gong's dismissal in 1884 (Eastman, 1967). Image: French newspaper engraving, 1884.

Here is Cixi's balancing act under pressure. "Realists like Prince Gong and Li Hongzhang urged avoiding war, but a shrill group of conservative critics insisted that China had to stop giving in because appeasement only encouraged the bullying of the powers" (Ebrey, p. 324). Appeasement means giving in to keep the peace.

"Cixi hesitated, called on Li Hongzhang to negotiate, and then scuttled the draft treaty when she was flooded with protests about its terms. When the French issued an ultimatum that China withdraw its forces from Vietnam or they would attack China, Cixi sided with the conservative critics."

From sources beyond Ebrey: in 1884 she also dismissed Prince Gong, her partner in power since 1861, and his whole Grand Council. With him gone, no one at court could rival her.

The engraving shows a strange scene. French warships sat at anchor beside the Chinese fleet in the Min River below Fuzhou, close enough to touch, while the talks failed.

Why this matters: Cixi chose the conservative position, and the result came the next day.

Q10 story · Fuzhou · 23 August 1884

In Half an Hour the French Fleet Destroyed China's Fuzhou Squadron and Shelled Its Shipyard

The French fleet sailed 20 miles up the Min River to Fuzhou, home of the main shipyard.
On 23 August 1884, in about half an hour, it sank nine of the squadron's eleven warships and shelled the shipyard.
Hundreds of Chinese sailors were killed.
Li Hongzhang had disobeyed Cixi's order to send his northern fleet to help.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 324; the squadron's eleven warships, the length of the action, and the death toll (Elleman, 2001). Image: French popular print, 1884.

"The French sailed their fleet 20 miles up the Min River to Fuzhou, home port of a quarter of the new Chinese navy and the site of the main shipyard. In just fifteen minutes on August 23, 1884, the French fleet destroyed the shipyard and all but two of the twenty-three Chinese warships. About three thousand Chinese were killed in the action" (Ebrey, p. 324).

Instructor note: the textbook's figures are too high. The twenty-three counts armed junks and transports as well as warships; the squadron had eleven modern steam warships, and nine were sunk or burned. An American observer saw the firing slacken after fifteen minutes, but the action lasted about half an hour. Chinese records give roughly 500 to 800 dead; Admiral Courbet's 2,000 to 3,000 included the following days, when his ships destroyed the Min River forts (Elleman, 2001). The shipyard was the Fuzhou Navy Yard of 1866, from the last lecture.

The textbook draws the lesson. "Cixi had adopted the conservative position and stood firm; the result was not only humiliating but also a fiscal disaster. The only consolation, a bittersweet one, was that Li Hongzhang had disobeyed her order to send his northern fleet to Fuzhou to help." Li saved his ships by refusing an order.

The war did not go only one way. In March 1885 Qing troops defeated a French army at Zhennanguan on the Vietnam border and drove it back through Lang Son, a defeat that brought down the French government. Even so, by the Treaty of Tianjin of June 1885 China accepted French control of Vietnam (Eastman, 1967).

Why this matters: Fuzhou showed that new ships alone could not win wars, and that Cixi's choices had costs.

Check yourself: Why does the textbook call Li's disobedience a "bittersweet" consolation?

Q11 story · Korea · 1894

In 1894 a Rising in Korea Drew China and Japan into War

Japan had modernized so successfully that it now threatened China.
When an insurrection broke out in Korea in 1894, both China and Japan sent troops.
Japan sank a steamship carrying Chinese troops, and both countries declared war.
Korea, China's most loyal tributary, was the prize.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 326; the Donghak rising and the Kowshing (Paine, 2003). Image: Japanese woodblock print, 1894.

The next blow came from a neighbor. "First came the discovery that Japan had so successfully modernized that it posed a threat to China. Japan had not been much of a concern to China since Hideyoshi's invasion of Korea in the late Ming period" (Ebrey, p. 326). Instructor note: the textbook prints the name as "Hideoyoshi"; it is Hideyoshi.

"In the 1870s, Japan began making demands on China and in the 1890s seemed to be looking for a pretext for war. … Korea provided the pretext. When an insurrection broke out in Korea in 1894, both China and Japan rushed to send troops. After Japan sank a steamship carrying Chinese troops, both countries declared war."

From sources beyond Ebrey: the insurrection was the Donghak ("Eastern Learning") rising of Korean peasants. The ship was the Kowshing, a British-owned steamer hired to carry Chinese soldiers; most of the soldiers on board drowned.

The Japanese print shows the first sea battle of the war. Japanese artists made hundreds of these prints, and they sold well at home.

Why this matters: Korea was the last and most important of China's tributaries, and Japan meant to take it.

Q11 story · Off the Yalu River · September 1894

Off the Yalu River, China Lost Five Ships and Japan Lost None

The war tested the Beiyang fleet that Li Hongzhang had built.
In the main naval battle, off the Yalu River, twelve ships fought on each side.
Five Chinese ships were sunk or ran aground and burned; the rest withdrew to port.
No Japanese ship was sunk, though Chinese shells wrecked the Japanese flagship.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 326; the date and the ships lost and damaged (Paine, 2003; Evans and Peattie, 1997). Image: Fukushima Toshimitsu, 1894.

This was the real test of self-strengthening. Li Hongzhang's Beiyang fleet had German-built battleships, bigger than any Japanese ship. "The results proved that the past decade of accelerated efforts to upgrade the military were still not enough" (Ebrey, p. 326).

"In the climactic naval battle off the Yalu River, four of the twelve Chinese ships involved were sunk, four were seriously damaged, and the others fled. By contrast, none of the twelve Japanese ships was seriously damaged." The battle was fought on 17 September 1894, a date from sources beyond Ebrey.

Instructor note: the textbook's figures are out of date, and the slide corrects them. China lost five ships: the cruisers Zhiyuan, Jingyuan, and Chaoyong were sunk, and Yangwei and Guangjia ran aground and were destroyed. Japan lost none, but a heavy shell from the battleship Zhenyuan wrecked the flagship Matsushima, killing or wounding about a hundred men, and three other Japanese ships were badly damaged and left the fight. Japanese casualties were about 300, Chinese casualties far higher (Paine, 2003; Evans and Peattie, 1997).

Why? Historians point to too few shells, slow-firing guns, poor maintenance, and commanders who did not work together. After 1888 the court bought no new ships, and some naval funds went to other uses at court. The textbook's verdict comes on a later slide.

The triptych, a print in three panels, shows Japan's victory as its own people saw it: Japanese ships firing, Chinese ships in flames.

Why this matters: The fleet that symbolized self-strengthening was beaten by a smaller Asian neighbor.

Q11 story · Weihaiwei, Shandong · February 1895

At Weihaiwei the Japanese Took the Forts and Turned China's Own Guns on Its Fleet

The Japanese went overland to take the port of Weihaiwei in Shandong.
They turned the forts' Chinese guns on the Chinese fleet in the bay.
The fleet surrendered, and its admiral took his own life.
The textbook calls it a defeat not of weapons but of organization and strategy.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 326; the surrender and Admiral Ding Ruchang (Paine, 2003). Image: Migita Toshihide, 1895.

The worst was still to come. "An even worse loss came when the Japanese went overland to take the Chinese port city of Weihaiwei (way-hy-way) in Shandong province, then turned the Chinese guns on the Chinese fleet in the bay" (Ebrey, p. 326).

Read that carefully. The Beiyang fleet had retreated to its fortified base. Japanese troops landed nearby, marched around behind the forts, and captured them from the land side. The forts' own guns, bought and installed by the self-strengtheners, then fired on the Chinese ships.

From sources beyond Ebrey: in February 1895 the fleet surrendered, and its commander, Admiral Ding Ruchang, took his own life rather than survive the defeat. The Japanese print shows the surrender talks on board ship.

The textbook's judgment is one you should remember: "This was a defeat not of Chinese weapons but of Chinese organization and strategy." China had the guns. It did not have the planning, coordination, and command to use them.

Why this matters: This sentence is key evidence for why self-strengthening fell short.

Check yourself: In your own words, what does "a defeat not of Chinese weapons but of Chinese organization and strategy" mean?

Q11 story · Shimonoseki, Japan · April 1895

In 1895 Li Hongzhang Signed a Peace That Gave Japan Taiwan and a Huge Indemnity

China sued for peace and sent Li Hongzhang to Japan to negotiate.
China ceded Taiwan and paid an indemnity of 200 million ounces of silver.
Japan also won Liaodong, which it returned under European pressure for a further 30 million.
China borrowed from foreign banks to pay, and stayed in debt to them until 1949.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 325–326; the treaty's name and Korea's independence (Paine, 2003). Image: Japanese painting, 1896.

"China sued for peace and sent Li Hongzhang to Japan to negotiate a settlement. In addition to a huge indemnity, China agreed to cede Taiwan and Liaodong (the southern tip of Manchuria) to Japan and to allow Japan to open factories in China. (Liaodong was returned to the Qing for an additional indemnity after pressure from the European powers.)" (Ebrey, p. 326). The textbook's table of reparations gives the sums: 200 million ounces of silver, then 30 million more for Liaodong (p. 325).

From sources beyond Ebrey: the agreement is called the Treaty of Shimonoseki, after the Japanese port where it was signed. It also made China recognize Korea's "independence," which meant the end of the last tribute relationship. While there, Li was shot in the face by a Japanese extremist; he survived.

The cost lasted. "China had to borrow from consortiums of banks in Russia, France, Britain, and Germany to pay the indemnity, securing the loans with future customs revenue. From this point until 1949, China was continually in debt to foreign banks, which made reform all the more difficult."

Why this matters: Defeat by Japan cost China its last tributary, its island of Taiwan, and its financial freedom.

Q11 story · The scramble for concessions · 1898

In 1898 the Powers Rushed to Grab Ports, and China Seemed About to Be Carved Up

China's helplessness set off a scramble among the powers for concessions.
Russia got a railway across Manchuria and a lease on the Liaodong Peninsula.
Germany seized Qingdao in Shandong, and Britain took Weihaiwei.
France took concessions in the south, near its colonies.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 326–327; the 1896–1897 moves (Esherick, 1987; Bickers, 2011). Image: Henri Meyer, Le Petit Journal, 1898.

After 1895 everyone could see how weak the Qing was. "European imperialism was at a high point in the 1890s, with countries scrambling to get territories in Africa and Southeast Asia. China's helplessness in the face of aggression led to a scramble among the European powers for concessions and protectorates in China" (Ebrey, p. 326).

"At the high point of this rush in 1898, it appeared that the European powers might divide China among themselves the way they had recently divided Africa. Russia obtained permission to extend the Trans-Siberian railway across Manchuria to Vladivostok and secured a leasehold over the Liaodong Peninsula. Germany seized the port of Qingdao in Shandong province, and the British stepped in to keep Russia and Germany in check by taking a port (Weihaiwei)" (pp. 326–327). France took concessions in the south.

From sources beyond Ebrey: Russia had won its railway concession across Manchuria in 1896, and Germany seized Jiaozhou Bay, with the port of Qingdao, in November 1897, after two German missionaries were murdered in Shandong. Remember that murder: Shandong is where the Boxers began. Notice too that Russia took the same Liaodong that Japan had been forced to give back. The French cartoon shows the powers as royalty carving a cake, while a Qing official behind them throws up his hands. Find Britain, Germany, Russia, France, and Japan around the table.

Source beyond Ebrey: the cartoon (Henri Meyer, Le Petit Journal, 1898).

Why this matters: The scramble showed that China could no longer defend its own coast.

Q11 evidence · Reparations, 1842–1901

The Loser Pays: Each Defeat Cost China More Silver

Each war or incident ended with a payment; here are the largest, in ounces (taels) of silver unless noted.

1842

21 million silver dollars (about 15 million ounces) to Britain, after the Opium War

1860

16 million to Britain and France, after the attack on Beijing

1895

200 million to Japan, after the Sino-Japanese War

1895

30 million more to Japan, for returning Liaodong

1901

450 million to the eight powers, after the Boxer war

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 317, 325 (table, Reparations Imposed on China); the corrected units and dates (Hsü, 2000; Paine, 2003).

The textbook prints a table called "Reparations Imposed on China (or, the Loser Pays)" (Ebrey, p. 325). Reparations, or indemnities, are payments a defeated country makes to the victor. The slide picks out the largest.

Read down the column and watch the numbers grow. 21 million silver dollars, about 15 million ounces, to Britain after the Opium War in 1842, the same figure you saw in Lecture 1. 16 million ounces to Britain and France in 1860. Then 200 million to Japan in 1895, plus 30 million more that November for the return of Liaodong. Finally, in 1901, 450 million to the powers that invaded to relieve the legations. Smaller sums paid for attacks on missionaries, the Tianjin riot of 1870, and the return of the Ili valley from Russia.

Instructor note: the textbook's table has four slips, corrected on the slide. It lists the 1842 indemnity as "21 million ounces of silver," but the treaty and the textbook's own text (p. 317) give 21 million silver dollars. It dates the Liaodong payment to 1897; the convention returning Liaodong was signed on 8 November 1895. It dates the payment to Japan after the Taiwan incursion to 1873; it was 1874. And it gives the 1901 figure as "450 million silver dollars"; the Boxer Protocol set it at 450 million taels (ounces) of silver, paid over 39 years with interest.

For comparison, the Qing government's whole yearly income in the 1890s was roughly 90 million taels. The payments after 1895 were several times that.

Source beyond Ebrey: the corrected units and dates (Hsü, 2000; Paine, 2003).

Why this matters: The table is hard evidence for Reading Guide Q11: each defeat made the next one more likely.

Check yourself: How did the size of the payments change after 1895?

Review Question 11

How did foreign imperialism and the loss of tributary states such as Vietnam and Korea demonstrate the declining international position of Qing China?

For a While China Seemed to Follow Meiji Japan, but Resistance in China Was Much Stronger

Self-strengthening had built arsenals, schools, ships, and factories · Ch. 18 L3 · li-projects ↗

Now the big question. Why did so much effort fail to save China? Q9 story · Beijing · 1867

A High Official, Woren, Said Mathematics Had Never Saved a Dynasty

In 1867 the court planned to teach mathematics and astronomy at the interpreters' college in Beijing.
The high official Woren objected that no one had ever used mathematics to save a declining nation.
Many scholars agreed and refused to enroll.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 323; the date and Woren's office (Wright, 1957).

Here is the voice of the opposition. "The high official Woren (waw-run) objected to the establishment of an interpreters' college on the grounds that 'from ancient down to modern times' there had never been 'anyone who could use mathematics to raise a nation from a state of decline or to strengthen it in times of weakness'" (Ebrey, p. 323). The textbook quotes the translation in Teng and Fairbank's China's Response to the West.

From sources beyond Ebrey: Woren was a Mongol bannerman, a grand secretary, and tutor to the young Tongzhi emperor, so his words carried great weight. In 1867 Prince Gong proposed adding mathematics and astronomy to the language school in Beijing and inviting examination graduates to study there. Read his argument fairly. He was not stupid. He believed that a dynasty rose or fell on the character of its rulers and officials, as Confucian history taught. What he could not see was that the world had changed.

A second example shows how the court treated those who learned too much. "Guo Songtao (gwo sung-tau), China's first ambassador to Britain (1877–1879), sent letters from London to Li Hongzhang praising both the British parliamentary government and its industries" (Ebrey, pp. 323–324).

Q10 story · Beijing · spring 1895

In 1895 Kang Youwei Led Examination Candidates in a Petition for an Elected Assembly

Defeat by Japan stirred fear and outrage that can be called nationalism.
Kang Youwei, a Confucian scholar, argued that Confucius himself had been a reformer.
In 1895 about six hundred candidates signed his petition for an elected assembly, but it never reached the throne.
With "400 million minds as one mind," he wrote, how could the empire fail to be strong?

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 326–327, quoting Philip A. Kuhn, Origins of the Modern Chinese State (2002); the signers and the fate of the petition (Huang, 1970; Mao, 2005). Image: Los Angeles Times, about 1905.

After 1895 a new kind of reformer stepped forward. "The mixture of fear and outrage that many of the educated class felt as China suffered blow after blow began to give rise to attitudes that can be labeled nationalism" (Ebrey, p. 326). The leader was Kang Youwei (kahng yoe-way) (1858–1927), "a committed Confucian" who "reinterpreted the classics to justify reform, arguing that Confucius had been a reformer."

In the spring of 1895, provincial graduates were in Beijing for the jinshi examination when news of the peace terms came. "Some twelve hundred signed the 'ten-thousand word petition' written by Kang Youwei. Kang called for an assembly elected by the general populace" (p. 327).

Instructor note: "some twelve hundred" is Kang's own later figure. The surviving list of signers has about six hundred names, and the petition was never presented: the treaty was ratified first, and Kang dropped it. Other groups of candidates and officials sent more than thirty petitions against the peace through regular channels (Huang Zhangjian, 1970; Mao Haijian, 2005). The slide follows the archival figures.

He promised it would unite ruler and people: "Sovereign and people will be of one body, and China will be as one family. … With 400 million minds as one mind: how could the empire be stronger?"

Compare Feng Guifen in the last lecture. Feng wanted officials to elect officials. Kang wanted the people to elect an assembly. Reform had moved from guns to government.

Why this matters: Kang's petition opened the most daring reform attempt of the dynasty, and set up Cixi's next choice.

Q10 story · Beijing · June–September 1898

In 1898 the Guangxu Emperor Issued More Than a Hundred Reform Decrees in a Hundred Days

In 1898 the young emperor invited Kang Youwei to court, and in June gave him a two-hour audience.
Over the next hundred days he issued more than one hundred decrees.
They covered the examinations, national schools, banking, the post, and patents.
Kang hoped for a constitutional monarchy, but the decrees stopped short of a constitution or parliament.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 327; the dates and scope of the reforms (Kwong, 1984); the length of the audience (Kang, China Mail, 1898). Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

Now the Guangxu emperor, Cixi's nephew, was in his twenties and wanted to act. "In January 1898 the emperor invited Kang Youwei to discuss his ideas with the high officials at court. Afterward Kang sent the emperor three memorials on constitutions, national assemblies, and political reform" (Ebrey, p. 327). He even hinted that the Qing should give up the queue, as Japan's emperor had cut his hair short.

"In June the emperor gave Kang a five-hour audience. Over the next hundred days, the emperor issued more than one hundred decrees on everything from revamping the examination system to setting up national school, banking, postal, and patent systems. He was redesigning the Qing as a constitutional monarchy with modern financial and educational infrastructures." A constitutional monarchy is one where the ruler's power is limited by written laws and an assembly. The reforms ran from June 11 to September 21, 1898, as sources beyond Ebrey explain.

Instructor note: two details in the textbook are too strong. Kang himself told the China Mail in October 1898 that the audience "lasted for two hours." And no decree created a constitution or an elected assembly; that was Kang's goal, and he later rewrote his memorials to sound more constitutional (Kwong, 1984; Huang Zhangjian, 1970).

Think about who would lose. Examination graduates, officials whose posts were abolished, Manchus who feared the queue would go. Each decree made enemies.

Cixi took back power; when Yuan Shikai revealed a plan to use his army against her, it became a purge.
She had the Guangxu emperor locked up in the palace.

Kang and his follower Liang Qichao escaped to Japan.

Cixi's answer came fast. "After three months, Empress Dowager Cixi had had enough and staged a coup with the help of Yuan Shikai's army. She had the Guangxu emperor locked up and executed those reformers she could capture. All the reform edicts were revoked. Kang and Liang, safely out of Beijing at the time, fled to Japan, where each lived for years" (Ebrey, pp. 327–328). A coup is a sudden seizure of power.

From sources beyond Ebrey: six reformers, among them Tan Sitong, who refused to flee, were beheaded on 28 September 1898. The emperor lived as a prisoner on an island in the palace lake until his death in 1908, one day before Cixi's.

Instructor note: archival work revises "with the help of Yuan Shikai's army." On 18 September Tan Sitong asked Yuan to turn his modern army against Cixi's ally Ronglu. Yuan pretended to agree and told Ronglu. But Cixi had returned to the Forbidden City on the 19th and resumed power on the 21st, before Yuan's report reached her; no troops were used. The report turned her takeover into a purge (Mao Haijian, 2005; Kwong, 1984); some historians give Yuan a larger role.

Instructor note: the chapter summary (p. 332) says Cixi "was quickly removed from power"; it was the emperor who was removed, as p. 327 says.

This ended Cixi's balancing act: faced with reforms she could not control, she chose the conservatives.

The last story of the chapter begins with water. "In the summer of 1898, while the Guangxu emperor was issuing reform edicts, Shandong province was suffering from a break in the dikes on the Yellow River, which flooded some two thousand villages and made millions of people refugees. Story · North China · 1899–1900

The Boxers Practiced Martial Arts and Spirit Possession and Called for Killing All Foreigners

Small groups began attacking the property of missionaries and their converts.
Foreigners called them Boxers because of their martial arts.
Through spirit possession, Boxers believed their gods entered them and gave them power.
Their placards urged Chinese to kill all foreigners and Chinese touched by foreign ways.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 328–330; the name "Righteous and Harmonious Fists" and the belief in invulnerability (Esherick, 1987). Image: James Ricalton, 1900.

"Not surprisingly, this region soon exploded into violence. Small groups began pillaging the property of missionaries and their converts. They were dubbed 'Boxers' by foreigners because of their martial arts practices" (Ebrey, p. 328). From sources beyond Ebrey: they called themselves the "Righteous and Harmonious Fists."

"The Boxers also practiced spirit possession, which allowed individuals to achieve direct communication with their gods and gain a sense of personal power" (pp. 328–329). Many believed the gods who possessed them made them invulnerable to swords and bullets. Their best-known slogan was "Support the Qing, destroy the foreign" (Esherick, 1987).

"After the governor of Shandong suppressed them in 1899, they began drifting into other provinces, even into the capital, where they recruited new members with placards urging the Chinese to kill all foreigners as well as Chinese contaminated by their influence. They blamed the drought on the anger of the gods at the foreign intrusion" (p. 329).

Who were they? The textbook's photo caption says most "had previously worked as laborers, porters, field hands, or the like and suffered from the deteriorating economic situation" (p. 330). These young men were photographed after their capture.

Q10 story · Beijing · June–August 1900

Cixi Backed the Boxers, and Foreigners in Beijing Were Besieged for Eight Weeks

Cixi did little to stop the Boxers, hoping an aroused people might solve her foreign problem.
Pro-Boxer Manchus told her the powers wanted her to retire and restore the emperor.
After foreign troops seized the Dagu forts and the German minister was killed, she declared war on all eight powers.
Foreigners and Chinese Christians held out in the legation quarter and the Northern Cathedral.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 329–330; the siege dates and the Dagu forts (Esherick, 1987; Cohen, 1997). Image: C. A. Killie, 1900.

Now you can understand the scene from the start of the lecture. "The foreign powers demanded that the Qing government suppress the attacks on foreigners. Cixi, apparently hoping that the Chinese people, if aroused, could solve her foreign problem for her, did little to stop the Boxers" (Ebrey, p. 329).

Then, after the German minister was shot, "Cixi, having been told by pro-Boxer Manchus that the European powers wanted her to retire and restore the emperor to the throne, declared war on the eight powers." Notice her motive: fear of losing power, the same fear behind the coup of 1898.

From sources beyond Ebrey: on 17 June allied warships had seized the Dagu forts, and the court took that as the start of war. The foreign "demand" that she retire was a forgery passed to her by pro-Boxer princes (Esherick, 1987; Cohen, 1997).

"Foreigners in the capital, including missionaries who had recently moved there for safety, barricaded themselves in the Northern Cathedral and two miles away in the legation quarter" (pp. 329–330), the district of foreign embassies. Thousands of Chinese Christians sheltered with them. From sources beyond Ebrey: the siege lasted from 20 June to 14 August 1900.

An eight-nation force, including Japan, sent twenty thousand troops to lift the siege.

Cixi and the emperor fled by cart to Xi'an hundreds of miles away" (Ebrey, p. 330). The empress who had built palaces left her capital dressed as a commoner, in a cart.

Pierian Spring · Story · Liang Qichao · 1890s–1900s

Liang Qichao Argued That China Needed Political and Cultural Change, Not Just Technology

Liang, Kang Youwei's most brilliant follower, went even further than Kang.
Self-strengthening, he said, had focused too narrowly on technology.
Scrap the examinations, build a national school system, and let the people take part in rule.
From 1895, study societies spread his ideas; Hunan alone founded fourteen.

Kang's student was even bolder. "Liang Qichao (lyang chee-chow) (1873–1929)… fifteen years younger, was Kang's most brilliant follower and went even further than Kang in advocating political change. Liang contended that self-strengthening efforts had focused too narrowly on technology and ignored the need for cultural and political change. Pierian Spring · Document · Mai Menghua, 1898

A Young Reformer Argued That China Was Weak Because Its Ruler Had Too Little Power, Not Too Much

Mai Menghua (1874–1915), a follower of Kang Youwei, wrote this essay at twenty-four.
Western states, he said, recorded every birth, taxed inheritances, and made every child go to school.
In China, the state could do none of these things.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 328–329 (Documents: Comparing the Power of China and Western Nations), from J. Mason Gentzler, Changing China (1977).

The textbook prints a remarkable essay. "This essay was written in 1898 by Mai Menghua (my mung-hwa) (1874–1915), a twenty-four-year-old follower of Kang Youwei. It responds to conservative critics who saw Kang's program as weakening the ruler's hand" (Ebrey, p. 328).

Many people said China was weak because its ruler was too powerful. Mai turned that around: "China's misfortunes arise not because the people have no power but because the ruler has no power." He listed what Western states could do: record every birth and death, tax inheritances, send every child to school, inspect buildings, license doctors, run the post, and clear land for railroads. The Chinese state could do none of it.

Instructor note: the essay as printed says "each of the 80 provinces has a different currency." China proper had eighteen provinces; this is likely a misprint or a translation slip.

The textbook asks: "Does it make sense that the Chinese government was despotic and at the same time unable to control society?" (p. 329). Despotic means ruling with unlimited power. Mai's point is that a ruler could be all-powerful in name and still weak in practice.

Why this matters: Mai saw that modern strength meant a state that reached into everyday life.

Japan's Samurai Found New Careers, but Many Qing Bannermen Could Not

The bannermen had been a hereditary military caste, in some ways like the samurai of Edo Japan · Ch. 16 L1 · banner-families ↗
When Japan abolished samurai status, many became officers or switched to skilled careers.
Many bannermen could not see how to survive without government stipends.
Many banner children were illiterate and unprepared for a modernizing country.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 326–327; Ch. 16 L1 for the banners.

The last story compares two old warrior classes. "The bannermen had been a hereditary military caste, in some ways comparable to the samurai in Edo Japan" (Ebrey, p. 326). You met the banners in Chapter 16: the Manchu, Mongol, and Chinese soldier families who conquered China in the 1600s and then lived in garrisons on state pay; the link takes you back.

In Japan the change went well. "When the special status of the samurai was abolished, samurai had not only joined the new armies in large numbers as officers, but many had successfully switched to other occupations requiring skill or learning."

In China it did not. "The hereditary military caste of the Qing did not fare as well. Many bannermen became alarmed, not seeing how the banner population could survive without government handouts. Although banner garrisons had schools for banner children, many were illiterate and unprepared to step forward as the country modernized" (p. 327).

Why the difference? The samurai were educated administrators as well as soldiers, while the bannermen had lived for two centuries on stipends, forbidden most other work. When the dynasty fell in 1911, their fear came true.

Why this matters: The comparison shows that modernizing a country also meant finding a place for its old elites.

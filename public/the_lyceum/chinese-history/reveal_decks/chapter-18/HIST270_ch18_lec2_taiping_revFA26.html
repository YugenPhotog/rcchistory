In 1837 a Failed Candidate Fell Ill and Dreamed He Fought Demons Beside an Elder Brother

Hong Xiuquan had spent years taking the civil service examinations, and kept failing.
In 1837, after another failure, he fell ill and saw visions.
A golden-bearded old man and a middle-aged man called him younger brother.

Picture a village in Guangdong in the spring of 1837. A young schoolteacher named Hong Xiuquan comes home from Guangzhou after failing the examinations yet again. He collapses, and for weeks his family fears for his life. In his fever he dreams that he is carried up to heaven.

Our textbook tells the dream in one sentence. His career as a religious leader "began with visions of a golden-bearded old man and a middle-aged man who addressed him as younger brother and told him to annihilate devils" (Ebrey, p. 318). The year, the illness, and the sword the old man gave him come from sources beyond Ebrey, from the account his cousin later gave a Swiss missionary (Theodore Hamberg, 1854) and from Jonathan Spence's biography.

Hold on to this scene. Out of it grew one of the bloodiest wars of the nineteenth century, anywhere in the world. Reading Guide Q4 (p. 318) asks how the Taiping Rebellion began and why it became so destructive. This lecture starts with the first half.

Q4 story · Hua county, near Guangzhou · 1814–1843

Hong Xiuquan Was a Hakka Village Teacher Who Failed the Examinations Four Times

Hong was born in 1814 to a humble farming family near Guangzhou.
He was a Hakka, from a Han Chinese group with its own dialect, living mostly in the far south.
His family paid for his schooling, hoping he would pass the examinations and rise.
He failed in 1827, 1836, 1837, and a final time in 1843, and earned his living as a village teacher.

Hakka "guest people": a Han Chinese group with its own dialect, mostly in the far south

Taiping "Great Peace": the name of the kingdom he would found

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the dates of the failures and his work as a teacher (Spence, 1996).

Who was this man? Hong Xiuquan was born in 1814 in a village north of Guangzhou. The textbook calls his background "humble," and it was: his family farmed, and they pooled their money so that this bright son could study for the examinations (Ebrey, p. 318).

He was a Hakka. The textbook explains that "the Hakkas were a large Han Chinese ethnic group that spoke a distinct dialect and lived predominantly in the far south." Hakka families had often arrived later than their neighbors and farmed poorer hill land, so they were often seen as outsiders.

Hong "had spent years attempting the civil service examinations." Other sources give the dates: he first failed at Guangzhou in 1827, then again in 1836 and 1837, and a last time in 1843. In between he taught village boys to read. For a family that had sacrificed for him, each failure was a disaster.

Why this matters: Hong was an educated man shut out of the system that rewarded education, which helps explain why he later attacked that system.

Q4 story · Guangzhou and Hua county · 1836–1843

Six Years After His Vision, a Christian Tract Convinced Hong He Was Jesus's Younger Brother

In Guangzhou, Hong had been handed a set of Christian tracts, and he set them aside.
After his last failure in 1843, he read them closely.
He decided the old man in his vision was God, and the middle-aged man was Jesus.
That made Hong, in his own eyes, Jesus's younger brother, sent to destroy demons.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the tracts' author, title, and dates (Spence, 1996).

The key to the vision came from a book. Back in 1836, in the streets of Guangzhou, Hong had been handed a set of Christian tracts called Good Words to Exhort the Age, written by Liang Fa, a Chinese convert who worked with Protestant missionaries. Hong glanced at them and put them away. All of that is from sources beyond Ebrey.

In 1843, after his final failure, a cousin urged him to read them. Suddenly the vision made sense. As the textbook puts it, "After reading a Christian tract that a missionary in Guangzhou had given to him, Hong interpreted his visions to mean that he was Jesus's younger brother" (Ebrey, p. 318). The golden-bearded old man was God the Father. The middle-aged man was Jesus, his elder brother. And the "devils" were the idols, and soon the Manchu rulers, of China.

Instructor note: the textbook says a missionary gave Hong the tract. The tracts were written by Liang Fa, a Chinese preacher, and accounts differ on who handed them out in 1836.

Q4 story · Guangdong and Guangxi · 1843–1847

Hong Smashed Idols and Preached Against Opium, Alcohol, Foot Binding, and Prostitution

Hong called on people to destroy idols and ancestral temples.
He preached against opium, alcohol, foot binding, and prostitution.
In 1847 he studied for two months with an American Baptist preacher and took up the Ten Commandments.
His cousin Feng Yunshan built the God Worshipping Society among the Hakka of Guangxi.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; Roberts, Feng Yunshan, and the dates (Spence, 1996). Image: Library of Congress.

Hong began to preach, and his message shocked his neighbors. He called on people "to destroy idols and ancestral temples, give up opium and alcohol, and renounce foot binding and prostitution" (Ebrey, p. 318). Smashing the statues in a village temple was an attack on the whole community, and Hong lost his teaching jobs.

Then he went to learn more. "Hong spent two months studying with a Christian preacher and adopted the Ten Commandments, monotheism, and the practice of communal prayer and hymns." The preacher, from sources beyond Ebrey, was Issachar Roberts, an American Southern Baptist in Guangzhou, in 1847. Monotheism means belief in only one god.

Meanwhile Hong's cousin and first convert, Feng Yunshan, was doing the organizing. Among poor Hakka charcoal burners and miners in the hills of Guangxi, he built up the group the textbook calls "the God Worshipping Society," which "soon attracted many followers, especially among the Hakkas".

Instructor note: the textbook says Hong "called his group the God Worshipping Society"; Feng Yunshan founded and led it in Guangxi while Hong was mostly back home.

The photograph, taken in 1875, shows a temple bell left on the ground after the rebels' wars.

Why this matters: Attacking temples and ancestors made the movement both exciting to its converts and hateful to everyone else.

Q4 concept · Heterodox sect

Concept: To the Qing State, the God Worshippers Were a Heterodox Sect

Concept

Heterodox sect

A religious group whose teachings break with the beliefs the state approves. Qing law treated such sects as dangerous because they gathered people outside the control of officials and family elders.

The textbook calls the God Worshippers an "unorthodox religious sect."
Like the White Lotus, they promised that believers would be saved and their enemies destroyed · Ch. 16 L4 · eternal-mother ↗
Unlike the White Lotus, their god came from a Christian tract.
Officials and landlords saw a sect with its own leaders, its own rules, and soon its own weapons.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; Ch. 16 L4 for the White Lotus.

Let's pause on a word. The textbook says the Taiping rebellion "had its organizational base in an unorthodox religious sect" (Ebrey, p. 318). Historians often use the word heterodox. Orthodox means the approved teaching; heterodox means a teaching that breaks with it.

Why did the Qing state care what people believed? Because a sect gathered people outside the usual chains of authority. Members obeyed their own leaders, met at night, shared money, and swore loyalty to each other. Qing law punished such groups harshly, as you saw with the White Lotus.

The God Worshippers looked a lot like the White Lotus. Both promised that believers would be saved and that the wicked would be destroyed. The big difference was the god. The White Lotus prayed to the Eternal Mother; Hong's followers prayed to the God of the Bible, as Hong understood him.

When you answer Reading Guide Q4 (p. 318), use this idea: the rebellion began as a sect, and the way the state and the sect saw each other made violence likely.

Source beyond Ebrey: the legal point.

Why this matters: A sect is a community that answers to its own leaders, which is why the state feared it.

While Hong Was Away, a Charcoal Burner Began to Speak with the Voice of God the Father

Hong was a visionary, not an organizer, and other leaders learned to manipulate him.
In 1848 an illiterate charcoal maker named Yang Xiuqing went into trances among the God Worshippers.
Yang announced that when he spoke, it was the voice of God the Father.
That put Yang above Hong, who was only the younger son.

Names in This Story

Yang Xiuqing the charcoal maker who spoke as God the Father; later the East King

Feng Yunshan Hong's cousin, who organized the God Worshippers in Guangxi

God Worshipping Society the sect's name before the kingdom was declared

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the trances and the arrest of Feng Yunshan (Spence, 1996).

The movement soon had a second strong man. In 1848, while Feng Yunshan was under arrest and Hong had gone to try to free him, the God Worshippers in Guangxi were left without their leaders. The arrest and the trances are from sources beyond Ebrey.

Into that gap stepped Yang Xiuqing, "an illiterate charcoal maker and local bully." He fell into trances, and "to claim superiority over Hong, Yang announced that when he spoke, it was the voice of God the Father, putting him above Hong, the mere younger brother" (Ebrey, p. 318). A second man, Xiao Chaogui, claimed to speak as Jesus.

When Hong returned, he accepted these voices as real. Think about what that meant. Hong was the younger brother of Jesus, but Yang could speak as God himself. "Hong was a visionary, not an organizer, and other leaders emerged who learned how to manipulate him." Remember this; it comes back later.

Instructor note: the textbook says that in 1848 Yang "elevated himself and three others to top posts." The trances began in 1848; the formal ranks of kings came later, in 1851.

Why this matters: From the start, the movement had two sources of divine authority, and that was a fault line.

Q4 story · Thistle Mountain, Guangxi · 1850

In 1850 Believers Left Their Homes, Pooled Their Money, and Gathered at Thistle Mountain

In 1850 the leaders told all God Worshippers to leave their homes.
Believers pooled their money into a common treasury.
They moved to Thistle Mountain in Guangxi, which soon became a huge military camp.
Many were Hakka villagers already fighting their neighbors over land and water.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the local feuds and the village of Jintian (Spence, 1996; Kuhn, 1970). Image: AI-generated interpretation.

In the summer of 1850 the call went out. "The Taiping leaders told all God Worshippers to leave their homes, pool their money into a common treasury, and move to Thistle Mountain in Guangxi province, a site that soon became a huge military camp" (Ebrey, p. 318).

Imagine selling your fields, carrying everything you own up a mountain path, and handing your silver to the sect's treasurers. That was a point of no return. Families who did this could not go back to their villages.

Why did so many go? Guangxi in the late 1840s was full of bandits, pirates, and feuds between Hakka settlers and older local families over land and water. For many Hakka villagers, the God Worshippers were protection as well as faith. Local militias and Qing troops began to see the camp as a rebel army, and fighting broke out around the village of Jintian.

Notice how the common treasury worked. It bound believers together and paid for weapons. A sect was turning into an army.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; Ch. 16 L1 for the queue order. Image: Gary Todd.

In January 1851, at the village of Jintian near Thistle Mountain, the sect became a state. The place and month are from sources beyond Ebrey. "In 1851, Hong declared himself king of the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace (Taiping), an act of open insurrection" (Ebrey, p. 318). He took the title Heavenly King.

The first visible sign was hair. "Men were to abandon the Manchu queue and let their hair grow long." In Chapter 16 you saw the Qing order of 1645 that made every man shave his forehead and wear the queue as a sign of loyalty. Letting your hair grow was a declaration of war. The Qing called the rebels "long-haired bandits".

The army was formidable. "Hong's true believers were brave in battle, maintained strict discipline, and seized large stores of government weapons as they campaigned." Soldiers were forbidden opium, alcohol, and looting for themselves; captured goods went to the common treasury.

The tunic in the photograph belonged to a Taiping soldier and is now in a museum in Nanjing. Its two characters announce whose army he fought in.

Why this matters: Growing their hair long turned a religious movement into an open challenge to the Manchu dynasty.

Q4 story · North China Plain · 1853–1855

A Taiping Army Marched North Toward Beijing, but Froze and Starved Near Tianjin

In 1853 the rebels sent an army north to take Beijing.
Southern soldiers met a hard northern winter far from home.
Mongol and Manchu cavalry trapped them south of Tianjin.
By 1855 the army was destroyed and its generals captured.

Sources: Spence (1996); Kuhn (1978). Image: Qing court painting.

Everything on this slide is from sources beyond Ebrey. Our textbook moves straight from Nanjing to life inside the kingdom, but this campaign explains why the Taiping never threatened Beijing again.

In May 1853, two months after taking Nanjing, the Taiping leaders sent an army north to seize the Qing capital. It marched hundreds of miles across the North China Plain and by the autumn was close to Tianjin, the port city about eighty miles from Beijing. The court was terrified.

Then winter came. The soldiers were southerners, used to rice and warm weather, now in a frozen land of wheat and millet with no reinforcements. The Qing sent cavalry under a Mongol prince, Sengge Rinchen, who will appear again in the Pierian Spring. Trapped and starving, the army fell apart. Its two commanders were captured in 1855: Lin Fengxiang in March and Li Kaifang in May.

The painting was made for the Qing court to celebrate that victory. It shows the Qing side's view: an orderly ring of soldiers around a defeated rebel camp.

Why this matters: After 1855 the Taiping held the Yangzi valley but never again came close to Beijing, so the war became a long struggle for the south.

Q4 story · Nanjing, the Heavenly Capital · 1853–1855

In Nanjing the Taiping Promised Shared Land and Separated Men from Women

A Taiping land plan of 1853 promised to divide land equally by family size.
Its slogan: "Where there is land, all shall farm it together; … no one shall be hungry or cold."
Men and women lived in separate quarters; even married couples were kept apart.
Most of the plan stayed on paper, and the kingdom still had to collect taxes.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the Land System of the Heavenly Dynasty (1853), translated for this deck; separation of the sexes and its end in 1855 (Spence, 1996).

What kind of society did the Taiping try to build? The textbook says that after making Nanjing their capital, "the Taipings announced plans for a utopian society based on the equalization of landholdings and the equality of men and women" (Ebrey, p. 318). A utopian society is an ideal one, where everyone is equal and no one is in need.

A primary text shows the dream. In 1853 the kingdom published a plan, the Land System of the Heavenly Dynasty, which promised to divide land among families by their size. One famous line, translated for this deck, says: "Where there is land, all shall farm it together; where there is food, all shall eat it together; where there is clothing, all shall wear it together; where there is money, all shall use it together; no one shall be hungry or cold." In practice, the plan was mostly never carried out, and the kingdom collected taxes much as the Qing had.

Daily life in Nanjing was strict. Men and women lived in separate quarters, and even husbands and wives were forbidden to live together until the rule was relaxed in 1855. Meanwhile the kings lived in palaces with many consorts.

The engraving shows Nanjing's Porcelain Tower, which did not survive the war.

Source beyond Ebrey: the Land System of the Heavenly Dynasty (1853), translated for this deck; separation of the sexes and its end in 1855 (Spence, 1996).

Why this matters: The Taiping promised a new world, which won them followers, but their rules also frightened many ordinary families.

How did the Western powers react to a Christian rebellion? At first, with hope. A Chinese movement that read the Bible and smashed idols looked like a gift to missionaries.

Then they looked closer. In the textbook's words, "Christian missionaries at first were excited about the prospect of revolutionaries spreading Christianity but quickly concluded that the Christian elements in Taiping doctrines were heretical" (Ebrey, pp. 318–319). Heretical means a twisted version of the faith. A man who claimed to be Jesus's brother, and a king who spoke as God, horrified them. Even Issachar Roberts, the preacher who had taught Hong in 1847, lived in Nanjing in 1860 and 1861 and left in disgust.

Trade mattered too. Shanghai was the biggest of the treaty ports, and its foreign trading houses lined the riverfront in the painting. "When the Taipings tried to take Shanghai in 1860 and 1862, the Western residents organized counterattacks" (p. 319). In August 1860 British and French troops on the city walls drove off the Taiping attack. Shanghai's merchants also paid for a new kind of army, the story of the next three slides.

Q5 story · Shanghai and Songjiang · 1860–1864

Shanghai's Merchants Paid for a New Army of Chinese Soldiers Drilled by Westerners

In 1860 Shanghai officials and the banker Yang Fang hired foreigners to defend the city.
After its first foreign mercenaries failed, the army drilled Chinese recruits with Western rifles, cannon, and bugle calls.
In 1862 the Qing court named it the "Ever Victorious Army."
It fought beside Li Hongzhang's Huai Army in the campaigns around Shanghai.

Sources: Smith, Mercenaries and Mandarins (1978); Platt (2012). Image: public domain.

The textbook says only that when the Taiping attacked Shanghai, "the Western residents organized counterattacks" (Ebrey, p. 319). Everything else on this slide is from sources beyond Ebrey. In 1860 the Taiping were closing in on Shanghai, and the Qing armies nearby had collapsed. The city's Chinese officials, and a wealthy banker named Yang Fang, decided to buy protection. They hired foreigners.

The first force was a few hundred Western sailors and drifters. In 1860 and 1861 it attacked the walled town of Qingpu and was cut to pieces. So its leaders tried something new. From 1861 they recruited Chinese men, paid them regularly, gave them Western rifles and cannon, and drilled them to Western bugle calls under Western officers. Locals mocked them as "imitation foreign devils."

By 1862 the force had several thousand Chinese soldiers, and it kept winning. In March 1862 the Qing court gave it a proud name, the Ever Victorious Army. When Li Hongzhang arrived in Shanghai that spring with his Huai Army, the two fought side by side, and Li, as governor of Jiangsu, became the army's Chinese superior.

Keep the size in mind: a few thousand men, against Zeng's Hunan Army of over a hundred thousand.

Why this matters: Western help to the Qing was real, but it came through a small army paid by Chinese money and tied to a regional leader.

Q5 story · Shanghai to Cixi · 1860–1862

An American Adventurer, Frederick Townsend Ward, Founded the Army and Died Leading It

Ward, a sailor and soldier of fortune from Salem, Massachusetts, arrived in Shanghai in 1860.
Leading the first attack on Qingpu, he was shot through the face and nearly killed.
He became a Qing subject, married Yang Fang's daughter, and received Qing official rank.
In September 1862 he was shot leading an attack at Cixi, near Ningbo, and died the next day.

Sources: Smith (1978); Caleb Carr, The Devil Soldier (1992); Platt (2012). Image: public domain.

Meet the founder, one of the Westerners who helped defend Shanghai (Ebrey, p. 319). Everything else on this slide is from sources beyond Ebrey. Frederick Townsend Ward was born in Salem, Massachusetts, in 1831. He went to sea as a teenager and drifted from one adventure to the next before arriving in Shanghai in 1860, at about twenty-eight, just as the Taiping drew near.

Shanghai's officials hired him to lead their foreign force. In the first attack on Qingpu, in August 1860, half his men were killed or wounded, and Ward was shot through the jaw. He kept going. When British officials tried to arrest him for luring sailors away from their ships, he declared himself a Qing subject. In 1862 he married Yang Changmei, the daughter of his backer Yang Fang, and the court gave him the rank of a Qing official.

On September 21, 1862, Ward led an attack on the walled town of Cixi, near the treaty port of Ningbo. He was shot in the stomach and died the next morning. He was buried at Songjiang, near Shanghai, where a shrine was later built in his honor.

The photograph was taken in 1861, between his wounds.

Why this matters: Ward shows how Westerners could serve the Qing for money and glory, inside a Chinese chain of command.

After Ward died, his American deputy, Henry Burgevine, took over, but he quarreled with his Chinese paymasters, struck Yang Fang, and was dismissed; he later joined the Taiping. In March 1863 the command went to Charles Gordon, a thirty-year-old major of the British Royal Engineers. Apart from Li Hongzhang, Zeng's protégé (Ebrey, p. 320), this story comes from sources beyond Ebrey.

Gordon saw that the waterways around Shanghai were roads, not barriers. With an armed paddle steamer and fleets of gunboats, his army moved fast along the canals and took walled town after town, working with Li Hongzhang's Huai Army.

Then came Suzhou. In early December 1863, several Taiping kings inside the city killed their own commander and surrendered. Gordon believed he had arranged for their lives to be spared. Li Hongzhang invited them to his camp and had them beheaded. Gordon was enraged; according to accounts from the time, he went looking for Li with a revolver. He refused the emperor's reward of ten thousand taels, and for two months he would not fight.

He returned to the field in early 1864, and the army was disbanded that summer. The British press called him "Chinese Gordon."

In 1856 the Taiping Leaders Turned on Each Other, and Thousands Died in the Capital

Yang Xiuqing, still speaking as God the Father, demanded more power over Hong.

Remember the fault line: Hong was Jesus's younger brother, but Yang could speak as God. In 1856 it cracked open.

The textbook says that "Hong arranged to have Yang executed. The king entrusted with this task killed not only Yang and his family but also twenty thousand followers, leading to another round of revenge killings" (Ebrey, p. 319). In the summer of 1856 Yang demanded the title "Ten Thousand Years," a title for an emperor, which would have made him Hong's equal. Hong secretly called back two generals. In September, Wei Changhui's troops broke into Yang's palace and killed him and his family, and the killing spread through his followers in the city.

Then the killing went on. Wei was himself executed within weeks, and the gifted general Shi Dakai left Nanjing with his army in 1857 and never returned. In Ebrey's words, "In time the Taipings were weakened by internal dissension."

Instructor note: the textbook gives the trigger as Yang ordering Hong beaten for mistreating a concubine. Yang had used his God-the-Father trances to humiliate Hong and order him beaten (in 1853), but historians point to the 1856 demand for the "Ten Thousand Years" title as the immediate cause.

Q4 story · The Yangzi valley · 1850–1864

The War Cost Some 20 to 30 Million Lives and Left the Lower Yangzi Ruined for a Generation

Both the Taiping campaigns and the Qing campaigns devastated the land.

Now we face the cost. The textbook says the rebellion "cost some 20 to 30 million lives" (Ebrey, p. 318), and that "the devastation wreaked both by the Taipings' campaigns and the Qing campaigns to suppress them was horrendous" (p. 321). The historian Stephen Platt says at least 20 million. For comparison, that is far more than the deaths in the American Civil War of the same years.

How did so many die? Mostly not in battle. Armies on both sides took the harvest, burned fields to deny them to the enemy, and besieged cities for months. Famine and disease, including cholera, followed.

A Western observer who travelled through the Yangzi region in 1865 described a land "strewn with human skeletons," with rivers "polluted with floating carcasses" (cited in Ebrey, p. 321). The rich lower Yangzi, the heart of the empire's wealth, "was ruined for a generation." We look at those numbers soberly: each one was a person.

Now the other side. The Taiping attacked everything the educated elite stood for: Confucian books, ancestral temples, the examinations. "The Chinese elite were horrified by the Taiping movement with its bizarre foreign gods and women soldiers" (Ebrey, p. 320). In many places, "local officials and landlords organized their own defense, repairing city walls, gathering food to withstand a siege, and arming and drilling recruits."

The Qing government saw that its banner and regular armies could not win, and that "it would have to turn to such locally raised armies." That was a big step for a Manchu dynasty that had always kept military power in its own hands.

Zeng had passed the highest examination in 1838 and served as a senior official in Beijing. In early 1853 the court ordered him to organize militia in Hunan. He was a scholar, not a soldier, and that is the point: the dynasty was saved by a man who shared the elite's horror of the Taiping.

Q5 concept · Private army

Concept: Zeng Built an "Essentially Private Army" Bound by Personal Loyalty

Concept

Private army

An army whose soldiers are loyal first to the men who recruited and pay them, rather than to the state.

Zeng recruited officers from the Confucian-educated elite.
Each officer recruited his own soldiers from farmers in his home region.
Zeng could draw on local taxes, so he paid his men well, about three times a regular soldier's pay.
Soldiers were loyal to their officers, and the officers to Zeng.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 320; the pay comparison (Kuhn, 1970). Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Here is the heart of Reading Guide Q5 (p. 320), so read it slowly. "He recruited officers from among the Confucian-educated elite and had them recruit their own soldiers from among farmers in their region" (Ebrey, p. 320). That is the sentence the Reading Guide asks you to copy.

Why did this work? Because it ran on personal ties. A soldier fought beside men from his own district, under an officer who knew his family. "Zeng was given permission to draw on local tax receipts and so could pay the soldiers and officers well. Soldiers were loyal to their officers and the officers to Zeng, creating an essentially private army." Zeng's soldiers earned about four taels of silver a month, against roughly one and a half for a regular Green Standard soldier.

Notice the danger hidden in the strength. The army belonged, in practice, to Zeng. The Qing court did not raise it, did not pick its officers, and did not fully control its money. Hold on to that; it is the second half of Q5.

Q5 story · The middle Yangzi · 1854–1861

Zeng Built 240 War Junks and Took Back the Yangzi Valley Bit by Bit

Zeng built 240 war junks so he could attack along the rivers.
He gathered some modern Western weapons, such as artillery.
A new transit tax on trade, the lijin, helped pay for the provincial armies.
In 1861 his forces took Anqing, the key city upriver from Nanjing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 320; lijin and Anqing (Kuhn, 1970; Platt, 2012). Image: Qing court painting.

The Taiping held the Yangzi River, China's great highway. Zeng understood that whoever controlled the river controlled the war. "After Zeng constructed 240 war junks so that he could attack by river and gathered some modern Western weapons such as artillery, he set about recovering Hunan province bit by bit" (Ebrey, p. 320).

It was slow, and he lost often. "The Taipings, however, also made advances, and Zeng needed twelve years and 120,000 troops before he fully defeated the Taipings."

Money was the hidden weapon. In 1853 an official near Yangzhou began taxing goods as they moved along roads and rivers. This transit tax, called lijin, spread to nearly every province by the early 1860s, and much of it paid for the new regional armies rather than going to Beijing.

The turning point, also from sources beyond Ebrey, came in September 1861, when Zeng's brother Zeng Guoquan captured Anqing after a long siege. Anqing guarded the river route to Nanjing. After it fell, the Hunan Army could move downriver toward the Taiping capital.

The Qing court painting shows a battle in this campaign, seen from the winning side.

Why this matters: River power, Western guns, and new local taxes let Zeng's army do what the dynasty's own armies could not.

Zeng did not win alone. "Generals under him, including close relatives and his protégés Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang, played major roles in the slow stranglehold placed over the Taiping capital at Nanjing" (Ebrey, p. 320). A protégé is a younger man trained and promoted by an older patron.

In 1862 Shanghai was in danger again. Zeng sent Li Hongzhang to raise a new army in his home region of Anhui, on Zeng's model. It was called the Huai Army, after the Huai River. Shanghai's merchants raised money and hired foreign steamships, which carried Li's soldiers down the Yangzi, right past Taiping-held Nanjing, to the city. There Li fought alongside the Ever Victorious Army and bought Western rifles and cannon.

Meanwhile Zuo Zongtang, another of Zeng's men, led an army into Zhejiang province and retook its capital, Hangzhou, in 1864.

In May 1862 Zeng Guoquan, Zeng's younger brother, dug in on the hill of Yuhuatai, just south of Nanjing's walls, and held on for two years.

Q5 concept · Regionalism

Concept: The Dynasty Survived, but Military Power Shifted to Regional Leaders

Concept

Regionalism

A shift of real power from the central court to regional leaders who raise their own armies and control their own revenue, while still serving the dynasty in name.

Unlike in earlier dynasties, the victorious generals did not fight each other for the throne.
Han Chinese officials, not Manchus, now held much of the dynasty's real military power.
Many of Zeng's officers became governors and governors-general in the decades after 1864.
Some historians see these armies as the seed of the warlords of the 1910s and 1920s (an interpretation) · Ch. 24 L2 · warlords ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 314, 320; the officers' later posts and the debate over regionalism (Michael, 1964; Spector, 1964; Kuhn, 1970).

What did Zeng's method mean for China in the long run? Start with the textbook's surprise: "Yet the Qing Dynasty did not fall. The generals who suppressed the rebellions did not take to fighting among themselves to see which of them could found the next dynasty, as had happened so many times before in Chinese history" (Ebrey, p. 314). Zeng stayed loyal, and he even broke up much of his army after 1864.

But the balance of power had changed. The men who now commanded armies were Han Chinese scholar-officials, not Manchu bannermen. Many of Zeng's officers became governors and governors-general in the decades that followed, and Li Hongzhang kept his Huai Army for decades. Taxes like lijin stayed largely in provincial hands.

Here is where an interpretation comes in, one that goes beyond the textbook. Historians such as Franz Michael argued that these "regional" armies began a long slide toward the warlords who divided China after 1916. Others, such as Philip Kuhn, point out that the court still appointed, moved, and dismissed these men. Treat "private armies led to warlords" as a debated argument, not a fact. Then nature struck. "Severe flooding in 1851 weakened the dikes of the Yellow River, which gave way in 1855, leading to a devastating shift in the Yellow River from south of the Shandong peninsula to north of it" (Ebrey, p. 321). Look at the map: the river's mouth moved hundreds of miles.

Disaster fed rebellion. "Those made homeless by the floods joined the Nian bands simply to survive."

Why was the Nian rebellion so hard to end? Partly because many Nian were not full-time rebels. "Many joined on a seasonal basis, staying home in the summer and winter but raiding and plundering in the autumn and spring" (Ebrey, p. 321). In other words, they farmed when the crops needed them and raided when the fields were quiet.

Then the Taiping war fed the Nian. "After the Taipings fell in 1864, some of their soldiers joined the Nian rebellion." These were hardened fighters with nowhere else to go.

The Qing had relied on the Mongol prince Sengge Rinchen, whose horsemen had destroyed the Taiping Northern Expedition. In 1865 the Nian lured him into an ambush in Shandong and killed him. "In 1865, when it was clear that the Qing regular armies had failed to suppress the Nian, Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang were assigned the long and difficult task."

Zeng tried to pen the mounted Nian in behind lines of rivers and forts. He made slow progress, and in late 1866 Li Hongzhang replaced him. Li's Huai Army, with its Western rifles, destroyed the last Nian forces in 1868.

Now we move to the far southwest. Here is the sentence the Reading Guide asks you to copy for Q6: "With the transfer of armies to the interior to fight the Taipings and Nian rebels, uprisings also got out of hand in the northwest and southwest" (Ebrey, p. 321). With the troops away, local feuds could turn into wars.

"These rebellions drew from and also exacerbated ethnic tensions and hatreds." Exacerbated means made worse. In Yunnan, "the large Muslim population had grievances based on Han Chinese settlers' moving into their territory and seizing resources such as copper, gold, and silver mines." These Muslims, known as Hui, spoke Chinese and had lived in Yunnan for centuries.

"As tensions escalated, so did feuds and violence. Han Chinese formed militias to kill Muslims, who in retaliation assassinated Chinese officials." In 1856 local officials in Kunming, the provincial capital, encouraged Han militias to attack the Muslims of the city, and thousands were killed. Across Yunnan, Muslims rose up.

The jade seal is said to have belonged to Du Wenxiu, who led the rising you meet on the next slide.

Source beyond Ebrey: the Kunming massacre (Atwill, 2005).

Why this matters: Competition for mines and land, and violence by officials, turned neighbors into enemies.

Pierian Spring · Q6 story · Dali, Yunnan · 1856–1873

Rebels Made Dali the Capital of a Muslim-Led State, and the Qing Won Only by Dividing Them

Rebels captured the city of Dali and set up a state, known to outsiders as the sultanate of Panthay.
Its leader, Du Wenxiu, ruled for sixteen years and included Han and other peoples in his government.
Yunnan's remote mountains made it hard for the Qing to send troops.
The Qing won back control in 1873 by playing Muslim factions against each other.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; Du Wenxiu, Ma Rulong, and the fall of Dali (Atwill, 2005). Image: Cangminzho.

In Yunnan, "rebels captured the city of Dali and announced the sultanate of Panthay" (Ebrey, p. 321). Their leader was Du Wenxiu, a Hui Muslim who had studied for the examinations. From 1856 he ruled western Yunnan from Dali. He took the Muslim title Sultan Suleiman, but he also gave posts to Han Chinese and to other peoples of the region.

Instructor note: "Panthay" is the name the Burmese and British used for the Yunnan Muslims. Du's state called itself the Pingnan Guo, the "Kingdom of the Pacified South".

Why did it last so long? "The remote location of Yunnan and its mountainous topography made it difficult for the Qing to send troops there."

How did it end? "The Qing was able to regain control in 1873 only because it learned to play opposing factions of Muslims against each other." Another Muslim leader, Ma Rulong, went over to the Qing and fought for them. Dali was besieged and fell at the turn of 1872–1873. Du Wenxiu took poison before surrendering, and Qing troops then killed many of the city's people.

The banner is said to come from Du's state.

Source beyond Ebrey: Du Wenxiu, Ma Rulong, and the fall of Dali (Atwill, 2005).

Why this matters: The Qing survived in Yunnan by splitting its enemies, not by force alone.

Pierian Spring · Q6 story · Shaanxi and Gansu, the northwest · 1862–1871

In the Northwest, Old Han–Muslim Hatreds and a New Sufi Order Fed a Long War

In 1862, fighting between Han militias and Muslims broke out in Shaanxi and spread to Gansu.
The textbook roots the revolt in Sufism, a mystical school of Islam.
But much of the violence came from long hatred between Han Chinese and Muslims.
A Sufi leader, Ma Hualong, held a fortress town in Gansu until 1871.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; the start in Shaanxi and Ma Hualong (Lipman, 1997).

The northwest was a second Muslim frontier. "The Muslim rebellion in the northwest (Gansu) was rooted in the spread of a mystical school of Islam known as Sufism, but much of the violence came from long-standing antagonism between the Han Chinese and the Muslims" (Ebrey, p. 321). Sufism is a form of Islam that stresses a personal path to God, often guided by a holy teacher.

The war began in 1862 in Shaanxi, when Han militias, raised to face Taiping forces nearby, clashed with Muslim villagers. Killing spread on both sides, and many Muslims fled west into Gansu. There, one of the strongest leaders was Ma Hualong, head of a Sufi order the Qing called the "New Teaching," whose stronghold was the walled town of Jinjibao.

Instructor note: the textbook says "By 1867 all of Gansu was in Muslim hands." That is too strong. Several Muslim leaders held large areas, but not all of the province, and they were not united.

This slide has no picture; few reliable images of the northwest war survive.

Source beyond Ebrey: the start in Shaanxi and Ma Hualong (Lipman, 1997).

Why this matters: Religion mattered in the northwest, but so did old hatreds and the chaos of a war elsewhere.

Pierian Spring · Q6 story · Gansu to Xinjiang · 1868–1878

Zuo Zongtang Starved Out the Northwest, Then Marched On to Win Back Xinjiang

The Qing sent Zuo Zongtang, Zeng's protégé, to retake the northwest.
Zuo classed the "New Teaching" Sufis as heterodox, like the White Lotus or the Taiping.
His campaign took five years of sieges in which the people slowly starved.
Then he marched into Xinjiang and won it back by 1878 · Ch. 16 L2 · xinjiang ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; Ch. 16 L2 for the conquest of Xinjiang; Jinjibao, Suzhou, and Yakub Beg (Lipman, 1997; Kim, 2004). Image: public domain.

The Qing turned to another of Zeng's men. It "dispatched Zuo Zongtang (1812–1885) to retake the region" (Ebrey, p. 321). You met Zuo retaking Zhejiang from the Taiping.

"Zuo classed Sufis as heterodox, like White Lotus or Taiping sectarians." Remember the word from the main lecture: to the Qing, a heterodox sect was a danger in itself. Zuo spared Muslims who surrendered, but he aimed to destroy the New Teaching. "The campaign took five years and consisted largely of sieges during which the population slowly starved." Ma Hualong's town of Jinjibao held out until 1871; Ma surrendered and was executed. When the last rebel city, Suzhou in Gansu, fell in 1873, its defenders were killed.

Instructor note: the textbook says Zuo "ordered their slaughter." Zuo's own policy separated "good" from "bad" Muslims, but his armies did carry out mass killings.

Then Zuo "marched his troops into Xinjiang, which might well have broken away from Qing control otherwise." A commander named Yakub Beg had seized much of Xinjiang, and the Qing feared Russia would take it. By 1878 Zuo had won it back. The link goes to the Qing conquest of Xinjiang in Chapter 16.

Source beyond Ebrey: Jinjibao, Suzhou, and Yakub Beg (Lipman, 1997; Kim, 2004).

Why this matters: The regional armies that beat the Taiping also rebuilt the Qing frontier.

Pierian Spring · A last story · The Vietnam border · 1865–1885

Rebels from Guangxi Crossed into Vietnam, Became the Black Flags, and Fought the French

Rebel bands from Guangxi, separate from the Taiping, fought on near the Vietnam border.
One band, led by Liu Yongfu, crossed into northern Vietnam in 1865.
Known as the Black Flags, they fought French colonial expansion.
In 1873 they killed the French officer Francis Garnier near Hanoi.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; Liu Yongfu, Garnier, and Taiwan (Davis, 2017). Image: Huard, 1887.

The Taiping story ends far from Nanjing. "Elsewhere in south China, the Taipings held out longer, with some armies relocating to Taiwan and Vietnam. In Vietnam, where they were known as the Black Flags, they took an active part in resistance to French colonial expansion" (Ebrey, p. 321).

The Black Flags were a band of fighters, including former rebels from Guangxi, led by Liu Yongfu, who crossed into northern Vietnam around 1865. They lived by controlling trade on the Red River. When the French pushed into northern Vietnam, the Vietnamese court hired the Black Flags to fight them. In December 1873 they killed the French officer Francis Garnier near Hanoi, and in the 1880s they fought the French again.

Instructor note: the textbook calls the Black Flags Taiping armies. Liu Yongfu's band came from the Guangxi insurgent armies of the 1850s and 1860s, such as Wu Yazhong's, which were tied to the Heaven and Earth Society rather than to the Taiping, and the slide follows that account (Davis, 2017).

Instructor note: the textbook also says Taiping armies relocated "to Taiwan." Liu Yongfu went to Taiwan only in 1895, after Japan was given the island, and briefly led resistance there; there was no Taiping move to Taiwan in the 1860s.

The engraving is French, made for French readers during the war in Vietnam. It shows the Black Flags as the French imagined their enemy.

Source beyond Ebrey: Liu Yongfu, Garnier, and Taiwan (Davis, 2017).

Why this matters: The violence of China's civil war spilled across its borders and into the age of European empires.

Q2 story · Humen, near Guangzhou · June 1839

Lin Zexu Had Five Hundred Men Destroy the Opium and Wash It into the Sea

Five hundred laborers worked for twenty-two days to destroy the seized opium.
They mixed it with salt and lime and washed it into the sea.
The 2.6 million pounds of opium had been surrendered by Western merchants after a six-week siege.
The destruction took place at Humen, at the mouth of the Pearl River, in June 1839.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317. Image: Hong Kong Museum of Art.

Picture the shore at Humen, near Guangzhou, in June 1839. For three weeks, crews of laborers broke open chests of a sticky brown drug, stirred it into trenches with salt and lime, and flushed the mixture out to sea. Officials watched from a pavilion. So did curious foreigners.

The textbook gives the numbers: "Lin set five hundred laborers to work for twenty-two days to destroy the opium by mixing it with salt and lime and washing it into the sea." The opium weighed "some 2.6 million pounds" (Ebrey, p. 317). The place, Humen, and the month, June 1839, come from sources beyond Ebrey.

Why would an imperial official destroy a fortune instead of selling it? Because for the Qing court this was not a business deal. It was a choice about poison, money, and loyalty, and the court made it on purpose. Today's lecture asks how the crisis grew, why the court chose to crush the trade, and what it cost. This is Reading Guide Q2 (p. 317).

The painting is by a Chinese artist of the 1800s; notice the officials in their pavilion, supervising, in control.

Why this matters: The destruction of the opium was the Qing state acting, not reacting, and it led straight to war.

Recap · The Canton system · 1700s

Recap: Europeans Could Trade Only at Guangzhou, and Paid for Tea in Silver

Europeans were permitted to trade only at Guangzhou, and only through licensed Chinese merchants · Ch. 16 L3 · guangzhou-rules ↗
The balance of trade favored China, because the British paid for their tea in silver · Ch. 16 L3 · concept-balance ↗
When Macartney asked to change the system in 1793, the Qianlong emperor saw no reason to agree · Ch. 16 L3 · no-merit ↗
By the late 1700s, the British had found something the Chinese would buy: opium.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 315; Ch. 16 L3 for the Canton system and Macartney.

You already know how this story begins. In Chapter 16 you saw the Canton system: Europeans could trade only at Guangzhou, and only through licensed Chinese merchants called the hong. The three links take you back to those slides if you need them.

Our textbook sums it up again: "In the eighteenth century, the balance of trade was in China's favor because Great Britain and other Western nations used silver to pay for steadily increasing purchases of tea. British traders found few buyers when they brought British and Indian goods to Guangzhou to sell." When Macartney asked for change, "the emperor saw no reason to approve his request" (Ebrey, p. 315).

Then the textbook turns the story with four words: "all this soon changed." The British had found something the Chinese would buy (pp. 315–316).

The portrait shows Houqua, the richest of the hong merchants, painted around 1843. His name and dates come from sources beyond Ebrey. Men like him stood between the foreigners and the Qing state.

Why this matters: The opium trade was Britain's answer to the silver problem you met in Chapter 16.

Q2 story · Across China · 1700s–1831

Smoked in Pipes, an Old Medicine Became an Addiction That Reached the Imperial Clan

Opium had been used in China as a medicine for several centuries.
Once people learned to smoke the pure sap in pipes, it became an addictive drug.
In 1831 an investigation found addicts among the imperial clan, high officials, and bannermen.
The Qing banned producing and importing opium in 1800, and in 1813 punished smoking with a hundred blows.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 316. Video: Opium usage in China, Seek Learning (YouTube, 3:52).

Opium was not new in China. Made from poppies, it "had been used in China for medicinal purposes for several centuries." What changed was the method. "Once a way was found to smoke pure opium sap in pipes," it became a drug people took "to relieve pain and boredom and to make tedious or taxing work more bearable" (Ebrey, p. 316).

The trouble was addiction. People who stopped "suffered chills, nausea, and muscle cramps," and addicts would do almost anything to keep their supply, "even pawning their clothing and selling their children."

The Qing "banned both the production and the importation of opium in 1800." In 1813 it outlawed smoking, "punishing it with a beating of a hundred blows." Then in 1831 came a shock: an investigation showed that "members of the imperial clan, high officials, and bannermen" were among the addicts. The Daoguang emperor was outraged (p. 316).

The video, Opium usage in China, shows how it happened: tobacco arrived from the Americas around 1600, people mixed opium into it and then smoked the pure drug, and opium dens became social places that made money for sellers. It ends with British opium from India and the war.

Instructor note: the unlisted excerpt runs just under four minutes; about three minutes in, it leaves China for morphine in America, so you may stop there. Some claims differ from the textbook: "one third of the country" addicted by 1800 (Ebrey's figures suggest about 2 million addicts by 1838, p. 317), and "much more accurate rifles" (most British troops carried smoothbore muskets).

Why this matters: Opium threatened the dynasty's own servants, which is part of Reading Guide Q2.

Q2 story · Bengal, British India · 1800s

In British India, the East India Company Grew the Opium and Private Traders Carried It

The opium that the British brought to China was grown in India.
The East India Company invested heavily in planting and processing opium, and held a profitable monopoly.
Once China banned the trade, the Company stopped carrying opium itself.
Instead, licensed private traders, American as well as British, carried it to the China coast.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 316–317. Image: Wellcome Collection.

Where did the opium come from? From India. "Following the British acquisition of large parts of India, the East India Company invested heavily in planting and processing opium, over which it had a profitable monopoly" (Ebrey, pp. 316–317).

Notice the trick in the next sentence. "Once China made trade in opium illegal, the company did not distribute opium itself; rather, licensed private traders, Americans as well as British, carried the drug to China" (p. 317). The Company could grow and sell the drug in India while keeping its own hands officially clean in China.

The picture shows the stacking room of the government opium factory at Patna, in Bengal, drawn by a British officer around 1850. Each ball on those shelves was opium packed for shipment; the factory and the artist come from sources beyond Ebrey. This was industry, run on a vast scale.

Keep two actors in mind. The Company and the traders wanted profit. The Qing state wanted the drug out. The next slide shows how the drug got past the ban.

Why this matters: Opium was a planned, organized business, not a few stray smugglers, which explains why the crisis was so hard to stop.

Q2 story · The Guangdong coast · 1810–1838

Smugglers and a Price War Raised Imports from 4,500 Chests to 40,000

Chinese smugglers bought opium from foreign ships anchored off the coast and passed it through middlemen.
By 1831, one to two hundred smugglers' boats worked the Guangdong coast.
A price war among the private traders drove prices down and spread addiction.
Imports rose from 4,500 chests in 1810 to 40,000 in 1838, enough for 2 million addicts.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317. Image: Wellcome Collection.

The foreign ships did not sail into Guangzhou with opium. They anchored off the coast, like the ships at Lintin on our title slide. "Chinese smugglers bought opium from British and American traders anchored off the coast, then distributed it through a series of middlemen, making it difficult for the Qing government to catch the major dealers" (Ebrey, p. 317).

So the trade had Chinese partners. "By 1831 there were between one hundred and two hundred Chinese smugglers' boats plying the Guangdong coastal waters."

Then competition made it worse. "The competition among private traders led to a price war in China that drove the price of opium down and thus spread addiction." Cheaper opium meant more smokers. Imports grew "from forty-five hundred chests smuggled into China in 1810 to forty thousand chests in 1838, enough to supply 2 million addicts" (p. 317).

Compare this painting with the opium dens in the video on the addiction slide. Here there is no couch or silk, only a plain table and thin clothes. A falling price carried the drug down the social ladder.

Why this matters: Chinese middlemen made the trade hard to police, so the court's problem was inside China as well as off its coast.

Q2 concept · Silver drain · 1820s–1830s

Concept: Now Silver Drained Out of China, and Farmers Paid the Price

Concept

Silver drain

A steady outflow of silver from a country, here because China was paying for more opium than it earned from tea and silk. Farmers felt it because taxes were set in silver but they were paid in copper coins, called cash.

The silver outflow grew from about 2 million ounces a year in the 1820s to about 9 million in the 1830s.
Taxes were assessed in silver, so the drain hurt farmers.
In 1800, paying a 1-ounce silver tax took about 1,500 copper cash; in 1830, about 2,700.
The flow of silver had reversed since the days of Macartney · Ch. 16 L3 · concept-balance ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317; Ch. 16 L3 for the earlier balance of trade.

Here is the economic heart of Reading Guide Q2 (p. 317). Remember the lopsided balance of trade from Chapter 16, when silver flowed into China to pay for tea? Opium turned it around. "By this point, it was China that suffered a drain of silver." The outflow grew "from about 2 million ounces of silver per year in the 1820s to about 9 million in the 1830s" (Ebrey, p. 317).

Why would a farmer care? Because "taxes were assessed in silver." A farmer sold his crops for copper coins, called cash, but owed his tax in silver. As silver grew scarce, it grew more expensive in copper. "A tax obligation of 1 ounce of silver took about 1,500 cash to pay in 1800, but 2,700 cash in 1830" (p. 317).

Do the arithmetic. The tax had not changed on paper, yet the farmer paid almost twice as much in real money. Angry farmers mean unrest, and unrest means less revenue for an emperor already short of funds.

Why this matters: Opium was a fiscal crisis as well as a moral one: it squeezed farmers and the treasury at the same time.

Check yourself: Explain in one sentence why the same 1-ounce tax cost a farmer more in 1830 than in 1800.

Q2 story · The Daoguang court · 1836–1838

The Daoguang Emperor Asked His Officials: Legalize Opium, or Stamp It Out?

The Daoguang emperor called for a debate on how to deal with the crisis.
Some officials urged legalizing opium and taxing it, to raise revenue and price some users out.
Others insisted that an evil like opium had to be stopped.
The memorials were written by real officials: Xu Naiji for legalizing in 1836, Huang Juezi for harsh punishment in 1838.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317.

Here is the moment to watch China's own decision. The emperor did not simply react; he asked for advice. "The Daoguang emperor called for debate on how to deal with this crisis" (Ebrey, p. 317).

One side said: be practical. Some officials "advocated legalizing the sale of opium and taxing it, which would help alleviate the government revenue shortfalls and perhaps make the drug expensive enough to deter some people from trying it." The other side said: be moral. They believed "that an evil like opium had to be stopped" (p. 317).

In 1836 the official Xu Naiji sent the emperor a memorial, a formal written proposal, arguing for legalization. In 1838 Huang Juezi answered with a memorial urging harsh punishment, even death, for smokers. Officials across the empire were asked to respond.

This is Daoguang, the frugal emperor you will meet again in the Pierian Spring. He had both options in front of him, and he chose.

Source beyond Ebrey: the memorials of Xu Naiji and Huang Juezi.

Why this matters: The decision to suppress opium was China's own choice, made after open debate at court.

Q2 story · Beijing to Guangzhou · 1838–1839

Lin Zexu Argued for Going After the Sellers, and the Emperor Sent Him to Guangzhou

Lin Zexu said the state should go after the importers and sellers, not the users.
Unless the trade was suppressed, he warned, the Qing would have no soldiers to fight and no funds for an army.
In late 1838 the emperor made him imperial commissioner, with full powers to end the trade.
In Guangzhou he arrested some 1,700 Chinese dealers and seized 70,000 opium pipes.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317.

Into the debate stepped Lin Zexu. He argued "that rather than concentrate on the users, the government should go after those who imported or sold the drug." Then comes the sentence the Reading Guide asks you to copy for Q2: "Unless trade in the drug was suppressed, he argued, the Qing would have no soldiers to fight the enemy and no funds to support an army" (Ebrey, p. 317).

See how Lin joined the two crises. Addicted soldiers cannot fight, and drained silver cannot pay them. "Lin's impassioned stand and his reputation as incorruptible led the Daoguang emperor in late 1838 to assign him the task of suppressing the opium trade." The title he was given, from sources beyond Ebrey, was imperial commissioner, an envoy with the emperor's full authority; he reached Guangzhou in March 1839.

He started at home. Once in Guangzhou, "he made rapid progress, arresting some seventeen hundred Chinese dealers and seizing seventy thousand opium pipes" (p. 317). His first targets were Chinese, not foreigners.

Instructor note: the textbook calls Lin "the governor-general"; he was governor-general of Hubei and Hunan, and was sent to Guangzhou as imperial commissioner.

The portrait is attributed to Lamqua, a Guangzhou painter who worked in Western style; the attribution is from sources beyond Ebrey.

Why this matters: Lin's plan answers the second half of Reading Guide Q2: how he tried to solve the problem.

Q2 story · The foreign factories, Guangzhou · March–May 1839

When the Merchants Refused, Lin Stopped All Trade and Besieged Their Enclave

Lin demanded that foreign firms hand over their opium, offering tea in exchange.
When appeals failed, he stopped all trade and besieged the Western merchants' enclave.
After six weeks the merchants gave up their opium, some 2.6 million pounds.
Lin also wrote to Queen Victoria: "Let us ask, where is your conscience?"

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317; Lin's letter, trans. Teng and Fairbank, China's Response to the West (1954). Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Now Lin turned to the foreigners. "He demanded that foreign firms turn over their opium stores as well, offering tea in exchange." Notice that offer: Lin was not trying to end foreign trade, only the drug. "When his appeals failed, Lin stopped all trade and placed a siege on the Western merchants' enclave. After six weeks, the merchants relented and turned over their opium, some 2.6 million pounds" (Ebrey, p. 317). That is the opium you saw destroyed on the cold-open slide.

Lin also wrote a letter to Queen Victoria, from sources beyond Ebrey. He asked how Britain could ban opium at home yet sell it to China: "Let us ask, where is your conscience?" (trans. Teng and Fairbank). It is not clear the queen ever read it.

Then Lin pressured the Portuguese to expel the British from Macao (p. 317). In August 1839 the British community retreated to their ships, anchored in the harbor of the hilly island of Hong Kong; that detail is from sources beyond Ebrey. Remember that island.

Instructor note: the textbook says the British "settled on the barren island of Hong Kong." In 1839 they lived aboard ships in its harbor; Britain occupied the island only in January 1841.

The picture imagines the siege: soldiers outside the factories, the Chinese servants leaving, the merchants watching.

Why this matters: The siege was the climax of Lin's plan, and Britain would read it as a cause for war.

Q3 story · London and the China coast · 1839–1840

Britain Called Lin's Act an Affront and Sent a War Fleet

To the British superintendent of trade, Lin's act was an affront to British dignity and cause enough for war.
The British saw China as out of step with a world of "civilized" free trade, envoys, and treaties.
Urged on by opium merchants such as William Jardine, Britain sent about forty-eight ships and 4,000 men, who arrived in June 1840.
In Parliament, the young Gladstone called it a war "more unjust in its origin" than any he knew.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317; Elliot's name, Jardine's role and dates, the size of the 1840 fleet (MIT Visualizing Cultures), and Gladstone's 1840 speech.

Now the story crosses to the British side, and the Reading Guide's Q3 begins (p. 317). "To the British superintendent of trade, Lin's act was an affront to British dignity and cause enough for war" (Ebrey, p. 317). That superintendent was Charles Elliot, from sources beyond Ebrey. He had promised the merchants that the British government would make good their losses, which turned their opium into the Crown's problem.

The British told themselves a bigger story too. They "saw China as out of step with the modern world in which all 'civilized' nations practiced free trade and maintained 'normal' international relations through envoys and treaties." Notice the quotation marks the textbook puts around "civilized" and "normal."

"With the encouragement of their merchants in China," the textbook says, the British sent "a small, mobile expeditionary force" (p. 317). William Jardine, shown in the portrait, had gone home to London and advised the foreign secretary, Lord Palmerston, on how to fight. The fleet that arrived in June 1840 counted about forty-eight warships, steamers, and transports, carrying some 4,000 men.

Instructor note: Ebrey gives "forty-two ships, many of them leased from the major opium trader Jardine, Matheson, and Company." Counts of the 1840 fleet vary (MIT Visualizing Cultures gives 48), and the sources checked for this deck do not confirm that many ships were leased from the firm, so the slide gives the documented figures.

Not every Briton agreed. In 1840 the young William Gladstone told Parliament he knew of no war "more unjust in its origin," a speech from sources beyond Ebrey.

Why this matters: The causes in Reading Guide Q3 are both Lin's action and Britain's ideas and interests.

Check yourself: Why does the textbook put "civilized" in quotation marks?

Q3 story · The China coast · 1840–1841

The Fleet Sailed North, and Both Courts Rejected the First Deal

Avoiding Lin's defenses at Guangzhou, the British occupied Zhoushan island and blockaded Ningbo in July 1840, then sailed to the forts guarding Tianjin.
In January 1841 the Qing negotiator Qishan agreed to cede Hong Kong, pay $6 million, and accept equal official contact.
Daoguang had dismissed Lin in 1840 and later exiled him to Ili in the far northwest; now he had Qishan arrested and condemned.
London rejected the deal too, recalled Elliot, and sent a larger force under Henry Pottinger.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317; Zhoushan, Ningbo, and Tianjin in 1840, the Convention of Chuenpi (January 1841), Lin's dismissal and exile to Ili, Qishan's sentence, and Pottinger. Map: Philg88, Wikimedia Commons.

Follow the map from the bottom up. "Because Lin had strengthened defenses at Guangzhou, the British sailed north," the textbook says (Ebrey, p. 317). In July 1840 they occupied Zhoushan island, blockaded the port of Ningbo, and in August anchored off the forts at the mouth of the river leading to Tianjin, the gateway to Beijing. The threat had reached the capital.

The emperor sent the Manchu official Qishan to talk, and the British agreed to return south. In January 1841, from sources beyond Ebrey, Qishan and Elliot drafted the Convention of Chuenpi: Hong Kong ceded, an indemnity of $6 million, and equal official contact between the two countries. Then, as Ebrey puts it, "In both countries, the response was outrage" (p. 317).

Watch the Qing court decide. Daoguang had already turned on Lin: he dismissed him in September 1840, and in 1841 sentenced him to exile at Ili, in the far northwest. Now he had Qishan arrested and condemned to death, a sentence later reduced. London, for its part, thought the deal far too small, recalled Elliot, and sent Henry Pottinger with more troops.

Instructor note: the textbook says the British "shut down the major ports of Ningbo and Tianjin," says Daoguang dropped Lin "as soon as the war broke out," and describes the first deal as repaying "the cost of their expedition." The slide follows the documented sequence: Zhoushan occupied, Ningbo blockaded, the fleet off Tianjin, Lin dismissed in 1840, and a $6 million indemnity in the Chuenpi terms.

Why this matters: The emperor punished both the hawk and the dove, which shows a court struggling to choose.

Q3 story · Anson's Bay, the Pearl River · 7 January 1841

At Anson's Bay in January 1841, One Iron Steamship Wrecked a Fleet of War Junks

The Qing had no navy to match Britain's warships and new shallow-draft steamers, which could sail up rivers.
On 7 January 1841, British troops stormed the Chuenpi forts at the mouth of the Pearl River.
The iron steamship Nemesis then entered shallow Anson's Bay and attacked about fifteen war junks; its first rocket blew one up.
Eleven junks were destroyed; for the whole day's fighting, Qing records counted 277 killed and 467 wounded, the British 38 wounded.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318; the battle of 7 January 1841 (W. H. Hall and W. D. Bernard, Narrative of the Voyages and Services of the Nemesis, 1844; Mao Haijian, The Qing Empire and the Opium War, 2016). Image: Royal Museums Greenwich.

Why could Britain win so far from home? The textbook is blunt: "The Opium War exposed the fact that Qing military technology was hopelessly obsolete. The Qing had no navy." Britain had "not only large men-of-war but also new shallow-draft steamships that could sail up rivers," so "the British could land troops wherever they liked" (Ebrey, p. 318).

Here is one day that shows it, from sources beyond Ebrey. On 7 January 1841, British marines and sepoys stormed the Chuenpi forts at the Bocca Tigris, the narrows leading to Guangzhou. Then the iron steamship Nemesis, drawing only a few feet of water, steamed into shallow Anson's Bay, where about fifteen war junks under Admiral Guan Tianpei lay at anchor. Its first rocket hit a junk's powder store, and the ship exploded. By midday eleven junks were destroyed. For the whole day's fighting, at the forts and in the bay, Qing records counted 277 killed and 467 wounded; the British had 38 wounded.

The painting shows that moment. It was made for a British audience; notice whose victory it celebrates.

Instructor note: Ebrey writes that "on a single day in 1841, a British steam-powered warship with long-distance artillery destroyed nine war junks, five forts, two military stations, and a shore battery." Those figures do not match 7 January; the historian Geoffrey Parker, who gives the same list, dates it to a day in February 1841 on the Pearl River.

Why this matters: Technology explains a large part of the war's outcome, one of the consequences behind Reading Guide Q3 (p. 317).

Q3 story · Qing armies · 1840s

On Land, Swords and Matchlocks Faced Well-Drilled British Musket Volleys

Even on land, Qing forces were no match for British troops.
British infantry carried muskets that fired faster and more reliably, some with new percussion locks that worked even in rain.
Most Chinese and Manchu soldiers fought with swords, spears, clubs, and arrows.
The few with firearms had matchlock muskets, each load of gunpowder lit by hand.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 318. Image: Wellcome Collection.

The gap was not only at sea. "Even when Qing forces fought on land, they were no match for the British troops." Most Chinese and Manchu soldiers "used swords, spears, clubs, and arrows" (Ebrey, p. 318). British infantry, from sources beyond Ebrey, carried smoothbore muskets drilled for rapid volleys, and some units had new percussion muskets, which fired even in wet weather when older guns failed.

Instructor note: the textbook says the British soldiers were "armed with rifles." In 1840–1842 most British infantry carried smoothbore flintlock or percussion muskets; rifles were few.

And the firearms they did have were old. "The minority with firearms had only matchlock muskets that required soldiers to ignite each load of gunpowder by hand." A matchlock fires when a slow-burning cord, the match, touches the powder. In rain or wind, it might not fire at all.

This soldier from Zhoushan island, called Chusan by the British, was drawn by William Alexander during the Macartney embassy of 1793 and published in 1805; the artist and book are from sources beyond Ebrey. Look at the long gun at his side. Forty years later, many Qing soldiers were still armed this way.

Remember the banners, too. You saw in Chapter 16 how the hereditary banner forces had declined; the Pierian Spring of this deck returns to them. Courage was not the problem; weapons and organization were.

Why this matters: Obsolete weapons explain why the Qing had "no choice but to capitulate," the textbook's own phrase for the war's end.

Q3 story · Guangzhou to the Yangzi · 1841–1842

A Larger British Force Took the Coastal Cities, Then Sailed Up the Yangzi Toward Nanjing

In May 1841 British forces attacked Guangzhou, and Qing officials paid $6 million to make them withdraw.
A larger force under Henry Pottinger then took Xiamen, Zhoushan, and Ningbo in 1841.
In 1842 it took Zhapu and Shanghai and sailed up the Yangzi River toward Nanjing.
In July it stormed Zhenjiang, where the Grand Canal meets the Yangzi, cutting the grain route to Beijing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317. Image: after Thomas Allom.

After both sides rejected the first deal, the war widened. The textbook sums it up: the British "attacked Guangzhou, occupied other ports, including Shanghai, as it proceeded up the coast, and finally sailed up the Yangzi River to Nanjing" (Ebrey, p. 317). Find the route on the map on the first deal slide.

Here is the order of events, from sources beyond Ebrey. In May 1841 British troops reached the walls of Guangzhou, and Qing officials paid $6 million to make them withdraw. Henry Pottinger then arrived with a larger force, which took Xiamen, Zhoushan, and Ningbo in 1841, and Zhapu and Shanghai in 1842.

Why the Yangzi? Because it is the main artery of China. In July 1842 the British stormed Zhenjiang, the city where the Grand Canal crosses the Yangzi, blocking the tax grain shipped north to feed Beijing. The Manchu garrison fought hard before the city fell. Nanjing was next.

Instructor note: the textbook has the "second, larger force" attack Guangzhou. In fact the attack on Guangzhou in May 1841 came before Pottinger's larger force arrived in August 1841.

The engraving, after the British artist Thomas Allom, was made for British readers, so the Qing defenders appear only as figures in the smoke.

Why this matters: By threatening the grain route and Nanjing, Britain forced the Qing court to end the war on British terms.

Check yourself: Why was the Yangzi a more dangerous target for the Qing than Guangzhou?

Q3 story · Zhapu and other garrisons · 1842

Dozens of Qing Officers Took Their Own Lives When They Could Not Repel the British

Dozens of Qing officers, both Manchu and Chinese, killed themselves when they saw they could not repel the British.
At the banner garrison of Zhapu, many who survived the fighting took their own lives afterward.
For them, failing to hold their post meant failing in loyalty to the dynasty.
One bannerman wrote a record so the dead would be honored · Pierian Spring · Guancheng ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 317, 319. Image: British plan of Zhapu.

The textbook does not hide the cost of defeat, and neither should we, though we can tell it with care. "Dozens of Qing officers, both Manchu and Chinese, committed suicide when they saw that they could not repel the British" (Ebrey, p. 317).

One place was the banner garrison of Zhapu, a port near Hangzhou. When the British returned there in the spring of 1842, its "defenses proved sorely lacking. Many of those who did not die defending Zhapu took their own lives afterward" (p. 319). The month, May 1842, comes from sources beyond Ebrey.

Why? For an officer, and especially for a Manchu bannerman, a lost post was a failure of loyalty to the dynasty. Choosing death was a way to keep faith. Their own side honored them as martyrs.

The image is a plan drawn by the attackers, not a scene of the dead; it shows how quickly the town could be surrounded from the sea. In the Pierian Spring you will meet Guancheng, a Zhapu bannerman who came home to write their story; the link goes there.

Why this matters: The war's consequences included human losses that the Qing elite felt as a crisis of honor, not only of weapons.

Q3 story · The treaty terms · 1842–1843

The Treaty Opened Five Ports to British Merchants and Consuls, Charged $21 Million, and Ceded Hong Kong

Five ports opened to British trade and residence, each with a British consul: Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317. Image: National Palace Museum, Taipei.

What did China give up? The textbook lists the main terms of the 1842 treaty and the treaties that followed it: five ports opened, a fixed tariff, an indemnity of 21 million silver dollars, and Hong Kong ceded (Ebrey, p. 317). An indemnity is a payment to the winner. A tariff is a tax on imports.

The treaty text adds detail, from sources beyond Ebrey. The five ports were Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo, and Shanghai, and Britain could station a consul in each. The 21 million dollars covered the opium Lin destroyed, debts owed by the hong merchants, and Britain's war costs. Hong Kong was ceded "in perpetuity," and the old hong monopoly ended. The treaty promised a "fair and regular" tariff; the schedule fixed in 1843 set most duties at about 5 percent, and China could not change them alone.

Instructor note: the textbook says the treaties "mandated ambassadors in Beijing." They did not; the 1842 treaty provided for consuls at the five ports, and resident envoys in Beijing came only with the treaties of 1858–1860. It also says the 21 million dollars covered "Britain's war costs"; only 12 million was for the war.

Q3 concept · Extraterritoriality · 1842–1844

Concept: Extraterritoriality Put British Subjects Beyond Chinese Law

Concept

Extraterritoriality

The right of foreigners living in a country to be tried by their own country's law and courts instead of the local ones. In China, British subjects were answerable only to British law.

Under trade regulations and a supplementary treaty of 1843, British subjects in China answered only to British law.
This held even in disputes with Chinese people.
A Chinese person harmed by a British subject had to seek justice from a British consul.
Chinese living in Britain had no matching right.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317.

Now the ideas that made the treaty "unequal." The first is a long word with a simple meaning. Extraterritoriality means "outside the territory": a foreigner carries his own country's law with him.

The textbook: "Through the clause on extraterritoriality, British subjects in China were answerable only to British law, even in disputes with Chinese" (Ebrey, p. 317).

Imagine a fight on a Guangzhou street between a British sailor and a Chinese boatman. Before the war, the Qing magistrate would judge it. After the treaties, the British sailor would be tried by a British consul under British law. No such right was given to Chinese people living in Britain. That one-sidedness is the point.

The right was written out in the General Regulations of Trade of July 1843, made part of the supplementary Treaty of the Bogue that October, and spelled out further in the American treaty of 1844.

Instructor note: the textbook presents extraterritoriality among the terms of the Nanjing settlement; it was not in the 1842 treaty itself but in the 1843–1844 treaties ("this set of treaties," in Ebrey's words).

Source beyond Ebrey: the 1843 General Regulations of Trade and Treaty of the Bogue, the consular courts, and the lack of a matching right.

Why this matters: Extraterritoriality took away a basic power of any state, to judge what happens on its own soil, which is part of why Reading Guide Q3 calls the treaty unequal.

Check yourself: In your own words, what right did extraterritoriality give British subjects?

Q3 concept · Most favored nation · 1843–1844

Concept: The Most-Favored-Nation Clause Meant One Power's Gain Was Every Power's Gain

Concept

Most favored nation

A treaty clause promising that any privilege a country grants to one foreign nation will automatically be granted to the nation holding the clause too.

In the 1843 Treaty of the Bogue, China agreed that any privilege it granted another nation would go to Britain too.
In 1844 the United States and France signed their own treaties with China.
They gained the same clause, so each new concession spread to all the powers.
China, in return, gained no such rights in their countries.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317; the Treaty of the Bogue (1843), the treaties of Wangxia and Whampoa (1844), and the spread of the clause.

The second idea sounds friendly but worked like a ratchet. "The most favored nation clause meant that whenever one nation extracted a new privilege from China, it was extended automatically to Britain" (Ebrey, p. 317).

Think of it this way. If France won a lower fee at a port, Britain got it too, without asking. And the textbook tells you the treaty "was soon followed by an amended agreement and treaties with the United States and France." Those powers wrote the same clause into their own treaties. So every concession to any one of them became a concession to all of them.

Notice the word "extracted." Privileges were pulled out of China, one at a time, and the clause made sure none was ever given to just one nation. Nothing in the treaties gave China the same advantage abroad.

The clause appeared in the supplementary Treaty of the Bogue of October 1843, and the American Treaty of Wangxia and the French Treaty of Whampoa, both of 1844, carried their own versions. Instructor note: Ebrey groups it with the Nanjing terms; it was not in the 1842 treaty itself. This clause matters for the rest of the chapter. As more powers arrived, each new treaty widened the privileges of all of them.

Why this matters: The most-favored-nation clause multiplied every loss, which helps explain why the treaty system grew so fast after 1842.

Check yourself: If the United States won a new right in China in 1844, what happened to Britain's rights?

Q3 concept · Unequal treaty · 1842

Concept: Concluded at Gunpoint, the Treaty Gave Benefits to Britain but Not to China

Concept

Unequal treaty

A treaty imposed by force that gives one side rights and benefits without giving the same in return. Chinese writers later used the term for the whole series of treaties that began at Nanjing.

The Treaty of Nanjing was concluded at gunpoint.
It provided benefits for Britain but not for China, making it "unequal."
With the treaties of 1843–1844, China lost control of its tariffs, its courts over foreigners, and an island, and got nothing in return.
The textbook's verdict: "Western imperialism had its first victory in China."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 317.

Put the pieces together and you have the second half of Reading Guide Q3 (p. 317). The guide asks you to copy this sentence: "Concluded at gunpoint, the 1842 Treaty of Nanjing provided benefits for Britain but not for China, making it 'unequal'" (Ebrey, p. 317).

There are two tests in that sentence. First, how it was made: at gunpoint, with British guns on the river at Nanjing. Second, what it gave: everything ran one way. Look back at the treaty terms slide, the extraterritoriality slide, and the most-favored-nation slide, and ask of each term: what did China get back? The answer is nothing.

The phrase "unequal treaties" became a rallying cry for Chinese nationalists in the twentieth century, and the system it named lasted until the 1940s.

The textbook ends the section with a verdict worth remembering: "Western imperialism had its first victory in China" (p. 317).

Source beyond Ebrey: the later Chinese use of the term.

Why this matters: "Unequal treaty" is the key term for the rest of the course; every later treaty in this chapter builds on Nanjing.

Start with the numbers. "By the beginning of the nineteenth century, China had a population of about 300 million and was continuing to grow, reaching about 400 million by 1850" (Ebrey, pp. 314–315).

Pierian Spring · Q1 story · Roads, rivers, and cities · 1800s

Hard Times Meant Fewer Daughters, Unmarried Sons, and a Floating Population on the Roads

Hard times led to more female infanticide, and so to a shortage of marriageable women.
Many young men took to the road and became a floating population of unemployed.
They worked as boatmen, charcoal burners, and night-soil collectors, or as sedan carriers, beggars, or thieves.
Poverty fed the traffic in women: poor families sold their daughters for cash.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 315.

Poverty changed families. "Hard times also led to increased female infanticide because families felt they could not afford to raise more than two or three children and saw sons as necessities" (Ebrey, p. 315). Fewer girls meant fewer brides, and "a shortage of marriageable women" left young men with less reason "to stay near home and do as their elders told them."

So they left. Many "became part of a floating population of unemployed, moving around in search of work," as "boatmen, charcoal burners, night soil collectors, and the like." In cities they "might become sedan chair carriers, beggars, or thieves." Remember the boatmen; you will see them again on the Grand Canal.

Women's fate was different but no kinder. "Poverty fed the traffic in women; poor families sold their daughters for cash, perhaps expecting them to become rich men's concubines, though many ended up as prostitutes" (p. 315).

The photograph was taken in Beijing in 1874, a generation later; it is from sources beyond Ebrey. These are the kinds of men the textbook means.

Why this matters: Rootless men without families became recruits for the rebellions of the next lecture.

Pierian Spring · Q1 story · The Grand Canal · 1824–1855

The Grand Canal was the empire's spine. The canal the Qing inherited "was 1,747 kilometers long and crossed five major rivers," climbing 138 feet over the hills of Shandong through locks and sluice gates. "More than fifty thousand hereditary boatmen and migrant laborers worked moving the tax grain" (Ebrey, p. 316). You saw the Ming rebuild it in Chapter 14; the link goes there.

It needed constant repair, and repairs need money. Late in 1824, storms tore breaches in the dikes of Hongze Lake, and the canal silted up where it crossed the Yellow River. The grain fleet bound for Beijing was stranded. Daoguang "ordered repairs," yet "more and more tax grain had to be sent by sea, exacerbating unemployment in north China" (p. 315). In 1826 officials, among them Qishan, the negotiator you met in the main lecture, shipped a year's grain by sea from Shanghai to Tianjin.

The final blow came later: in 1855 the Yellow River broke its banks and shifted north, cutting the canal. "Unemployed boatmen were prominent among those who joined the Nian rebellion in the 1850s" (p. 316).

Instructor note: the textbook says that "by 1850 the canal was largely abandoned." The decisive break came with the Yellow River's shift in 1855.

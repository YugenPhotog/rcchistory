Large and Yet Weak

The Second Opium War, the Burning of the Summer Palace, and China's Self-Strengthening

1856–1893 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome to the third lecture on Chapter 18. This one has two halves, and the title links them.

In the first half, Britain and France went to war with China again, from 1856 to 1860, and ended by burning the emperor's favorite palace. In the second half, a scholar named Feng Guifen asked a painful question: "Why are Western nations small and yet strong? Why are we large and yet weak?" A group of officials then tried to answer it with arsenals, schools, and ships (Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 321–323).

Q7 story · The Yuanmingyuan, outside Beijing · October 1860

In October 1860 British Troops Set Fire to the Emperor's Summer Palace

Northwest of Beijing lay the Yuanmingyuan, the Qing emperors' great park of palaces and gardens.
In October 1860 British and French soldiers looted it of porcelain, robes, and furniture.
Then British troops set the whole complex on fire, and it burned for two days.
How had a trade war come to this?

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; the photographer and the length of the fire (Beato; Hevia, 2003). Image: Felice Beato, 1860.

Picture the hills northwest of Beijing in October 1860. Here, for over a century, the Qing emperors had built their favorite home, the Yuanmingyuan, the "Garden of Perfect Brightness": lakes, pavilions, libraries, and halls full of art. Westerners called it the Summer Palace.

The textbook tells what happened next. British and French troops "marched to the summer palace located northwest of the city, a complex of two hundred or so buildings. They looted the buildings of furniture, porcelains, robes, and whatever else attracted them and then torched the entire" complex (Ebrey, p. 322). The fire, set by British soldiers on the orders of the British envoy, Lord Elgin, burned for two days.

The photograph was taken by Felice Beato, a photographer traveling with the British army, in the days just before the fire. Few pictures of the palace as it stood survive.

Reading Guide Q7 (p. 321) asks for the causes and outcomes of this war. Keep this scene in mind; the first half of the lecture explains how it came about.

Source beyond Ebrey: the photographer and the length of the fire (Beato; Hevia, 2003).

Why this matters: The burning of the Summer Palace became a lasting symbol in China of humiliation by foreign powers.

Recap · The first Opium War

Recap: In 1842 the Treaty of Nanjing Opened Five Ports and Gave Foreigners Special Rights

Britain won the first Opium War with steamships and modern guns · Ch. 18 L1 · nemesis ↗
The Treaty of Nanjing opened five ports, ceded Hong Kong, and fixed China's tariffs · Ch. 18 L1 · treaty-terms ↗
Foreigners in China were tried by their own consuls, not by Chinese courts · Ch. 18 L1 · concept-extraterritoriality ↗
Signed at gunpoint, it was the first of the unequal treaties · Ch. 18 L1 · concept-unequal ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 317–318; Ch. 18 L1 for the first war.

Before the second war, recall the first. In the first lecture of this chapter you watched a single British steamship destroy a fleet of war junks, and British forces sail up the Yangzi to Nanjing. The links take you back to those slides.

The Treaty of Nanjing of 1842, and the treaties with other powers that followed, opened five ports to foreign trade and residence, ceded Hong Kong to Britain, fixed China's tariff on imports at about 5 percent, and gave foreigners extraterritoriality, the right to be tried by their own consuls. Most-favored-nation status meant that whatever one power won, the others got too (Ebrey, pp. 317–318).

Historians call these "unequal treaties" because China gave up rights and received nothing in return, and because they were signed under threat of force.

Hold on to these terms. The second war was fought to widen them.

Why this matters: The second war built on the first; each new treaty took more of China's control over its own affairs.

Q7 story · The Amur River and the Pacific coast · 1858–1860

Russia, Seeing China's Weakness, Took the Lands North of the Amur and Down to Vladivostok

In 1689 the Qing and Russia had fixed their border far north of the Amur River · Ch. 16 L2 · steppe-closed ↗
In the 1850s, seeing China's weakness, Russia pushed into the Amur River valley.
Treaties of 1858–1860 gave Russia the lands north of the Amur and the coast down to Vladivostok.
Russia won all this without a war, while Britain and France did the fighting.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; Ch. 16 L2 for the 1689 border. Map: AoifeIves.

The first power to take advantage of China's troubles did not fire a shot. The Reading Guide asks you to copy this sentence for Q7: "Russia, seeing China's weakness, penetrated the Amur River valley, violating the borders agreed to in 1689" (Ebrey, p. 321).

In Chapter 16 you saw the Kangxi emperor's treaty with Russia in 1689, which kept the Russians far to the north. Now, with the Qing fighting the Taiping, Russian officers and settlers moved down the Amur River. "In new treaties of 1858–1860, Russia gained the maritime provinces of eastern Manchuria down to Vladivostok." Vladivostok means "ruler of the East" in Russian.

Find the striped areas on the map. That is a huge territory, larger than France and Germany together, and it is still part of Russia today. It also cut Manchuria, the Manchus' homeland, off from the Sea of Japan.

The textbook adds that fear of Russian expansion was "a large part of the reason" the Qing later sent an army into Xinjiang.

Source beyond Ebrey: Map: AoifeIves.

Why this matters: Russia showed that a weak Qing could lose land without even losing a war.

Q7 story · Guangzhou · 1856

Britain and France Wanted the Treaties Rewritten, and a Small Ship Called the Arrow Gave Britain a Pretext

Britain and France both wanted the trade terms of the Opium War renegotiated.
They said China had failed to carry out all the terms agreed a decade earlier.
In October 1856 Qing officers boarded the Arrow, a Chinese-crewed ship sailing under a British flag, and arrested its crew.
Britain called it an insult to its flag and attacked; the French joined in.

Names in This Story

Second Opium War also called the Arrow War, 1856–1860

Guangzhou the southern port Westerners called Canton

Harry Parkes British consul at Guangzhou

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; the boarding, Parkes, and the French missionary (Wong, 1998).

Why another war? "Both sides wanted the trade agreement reached after the Opium War renegotiated, though for different reasons" (Ebrey, p. 321). Britain wanted more ports, envoys in Beijing, and access to the interior. France wanted protection for its Catholic missionaries. They claimed China had failed to keep the earlier promises, and "decided to make swift, brutal coastal attacks, a repeat of the Opium War".

The spark was small. In October 1856, at Guangzhou, Qing officers boarded the Arrow, a Chinese-owned ship registered in Hong Kong, and arrested twelve of its Chinese crew as pirates. The British consul, Harry Parkes, said they had pulled down a British flag. In fact the ship's British registration had expired, and whether the flag was flying is still disputed. France joined in after a French missionary, Auguste Chapdelaine, was executed in Guangxi. These details are from sources beyond Ebrey.

That is why the British "called it the Arrow War, from the name of a ship that gave the British a pretext for war." A pretext is an excuse, not the real reason.

Why this matters: The real cause was the wish for bigger treaty rights; the Arrow was the excuse.

Q7 story · Guangzhou · 1857–1858

At the End of 1857 the Allies Took Guangzhou, and Its Governor-General Was Shipped Off to India

The war was delayed in 1857 when British troops were sent to put down a revolt in India.
At the end of 1857 British and French forces easily captured Guangzhou.
They held the city for three years.
They captured its governor-general, Ye Mingchen, and shipped him to Calcutta, where he died.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 321; the delay, Ye's capture and death (Wong, 1998). Image: Robert Morrison, 1858.

The first blows fell in the south. "Guangzhou was easily captured at the end of 1857 and held for three years" (Ebrey, p. 321). The attack came late because in 1857 a great revolt broke out against British rule in India, and the troops meant for China were sent there first.

Guangzhou's governor-general was Ye Mingchen, a tough official who had crushed rebels in the south and refused to meet the British on their terms. When the city fell in January 1858, British soldiers found him hiding in an office and took him prisoner. They shipped him to Calcutta in India, where he died in 1859. These details come from sources beyond Ebrey.

Think about what that meant. A senior official of the emperor, ruler of two provinces, was carried off like a trophy, and an allied council ran China's great southern port.

The portrait was painted while Ye was a prisoner, by a British artist. Notice whose eyes we see him through.

Why this matters: Taking Guangzhou showed again that the Qing could not defend even its largest ports.

Q7 story · Tianjin · 1858

In 1858 Allied Gunboats Took the Tianjin Forts, and Qing Envoys Signed New Treaties

British and French ships sailed north and took the forts guarding Tianjin, near Beijing.
Threatened with a march on Beijing, the Qing negotiator agreed to foreign envoys living there.
Ten new ports opened, and Westerners, including missionaries, could travel anywhere in China.
China paid 4 + 2 million ounces of silver, and a new tariff made opium a legal import.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 321–322; the opium tariff of 1858 (Wong, 1998). Image: engraving, 1860.

Then the allies went north. "By mid-1858, the French and British ships were in the north and took the forts at Tianjin" (Ebrey, p. 321). Tianjin is the port about eighty miles from Beijing, where the Beihe River reaches the sea, so this was a threat to the capital itself.

The court sent senior officials to bargain. "When the British threatened to march on Beijing unless they were allowed permanent diplomatic representation in Beijing, the hard-pressed Manchu negotiator conceded." Permanent representation means an ambassador living in the capital, like any other country's envoy, instead of a tribute mission that came and went.

The treaties of 1858 also opened "ten new ports," gave "permission for Westerners, including missionaries, to travel through China," set "a fixed transit tariff for foreign goods within China of no more than 2.5 percent," and demanded "an indemnity of 4 million ounces of silver for the British and 2 million for the French" (Ebrey, pp. 321–322). An indemnity is money paid by the loser to cover the winner's costs.

One more outcome is missing from the textbook. In November 1858 a tariff agreement signed at Shanghai set a duty of 30 taels a chest on opium, which made the drug a legal import (Wong, 1998). The war over trade that began with opium in 1839 ended with opium legalized.

The engraving was made for British readers, to show a triumph.

Why this matters: These terms reached deep inside China: its capital, its rivers, and its villages.

Q7 story · The mouth of the Beihe · June 1859

In 1859 Qing Forts at the River Mouth Drove the British Back

The emperor opposed foreign ambassadors in Beijing, calling them little better than spies.
In 1859 the British came back to sign, and insisted on sailing up the Beihe River toward Beijing.
The Qing wanted them to travel overland; when the British forced the river, Qing guns drove them off.
Several British gunboats were sunk or crippled, a rare Qing victory.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; Sengge Rinchen and the losses (Wong, 1998). Image: public domain.

The treaties had to be ratified, approved by both rulers, and the envoys were to "return in a year for the signing" (Ebrey, p. 322). But "the Qing emperor was strongly opposed to allowing ambassadors to reside in Beijing, viewing them as little better than spies".

In June 1859 the British came back with a fleet and "insisted on taking their ships up the Beihe (bay-huh) River toward Beijing instead of going overland, as the Qing wanted them to." This time "Qing forces withstood them." The Qing had rebuilt the forts at the river mouth, blocked the channel, and placed heavy guns. When British gunboats tried to force their way in, several were sunk or crippled, and hundreds of men were killed or wounded.

The commander was Sengge Rinchen, a Mongol prince you may remember from the last lecture's Pierian Spring, where he fought the Nian. The numbers and his name come from sources beyond Ebrey.

For a moment the court thought it had won. It had not.

Why this matters: One Qing victory persuaded Britain and France to send a much bigger army, aimed at Beijing itself.

Q7 story · From the coast to Beijing · August–October 1860

In 1860 an Army of 11,000 British and 6,700 French Troops Fought Its Way to Beijing, and the Emperor Fled

A new expedition of 11,000 British and 6,700 French soldiers landed in August 1860.
It took the river forts from behind and beat the Qing cavalry on the road to Beijing.
The Xianfeng emperor fled north to his hunting palace beyond the Great Wall.
When Qing officials did not give way on every point, the allies charged into Beijing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; the landing, the battles, and the emperor's flight to Rehe (Hevia, 2003). Image: Illustrated London News, 1860.

Britain and France answered the defeat with force. "A new expedition was then dispatched with eleven thousand British soldiers and sixty-seven hundred French ones" (Ebrey, p. 322).

From sources beyond Ebrey: in August 1860 the army landed north of the river mouth, took the forts from the land side, and marched inland. Sengge Rinchen's cavalry, the best horsemen of the Qing, charged the allied lines near Beijing in September and were cut down by rifles and artillery. The engraving shows one of those fights, drawn for London readers. The Xianfeng emperor fled north, beyond the Great Wall, to his hunting palace at Rehe, leaving his younger brother, Prince Gong, to deal with the invaders.

The talks broke down. "When Qing authorities did not let them have their way on all matters, they charged into Beijing." In October the allies stood inside the capital of the Qing empire.

Why this matters: For the first time, a European army occupied the Chinese capital and the emperor ran from it.

Q7 story · The Yuanmingyuan · 6–19 October 1860

The Invaders Looted the Summer Palace, Then Burned It to Punish the Emperor

The Russian envoy talked the British out of burning the palace inside Beijing.
French and British soldiers looted the Summer Palace first, carrying off treasures by the cartload.
The British envoy, Lord Elgin, ordered it burned in revenge for prisoners who had died in Qing hands.
Many of the palace's treasures are still in Western museums and private collections.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; the prisoners, Elgin's order, and the fate of the loot (Hevia, 2003). Image: Godefroy Durand, 1860.

Why burn a palace? In September the Qing had seized a British and French party, led by Harry Parkes, who came to negotiate under a flag of truce. Some of the prisoners were tortured, and about twenty died. The British envoy, Lord Elgin, wanted a punishment that would hurt the emperor himself, not ordinary people; the army itself was commanded by General Sir James Hope Grant. That background is from sources beyond Ebrey.

The textbook tells the rest. "The Russian ambassador, already in residence in Beijing, talked the British out of burning the palace in retaliation" (Ebrey, p. 322), meaning the Forbidden City. So the British and French went to the Summer Palace. French troops arrived first and began the looting, and the British soon joined in. Then, on 18 and 19 October, British soldiers set the fires.

Instructor note: the textbook's "two hundred or so buildings" and "10-square-mile complex" are rough figures. The Yuanmingyuan itself covered about 3.5 square kilometers (1.3 square miles), and the fires also spread to neighboring imperial parks.

The engraving is French, and it shows the looting as a busy, almost cheerful scene.

Why this matters: Looting and burning turned a dispute over treaties into a lasting wound.

Q7 story · Beijing · October–November 1860

Prince Gong Accepted the Terms: Sixteen Million Ounces of Silver and Kowloon for Britain

The Russian envoy then persuaded the Qing court to accept the allies' terms.
China confirmed the treaties of 1858, envoys in Beijing included.
The indemnity rose to 16 million ounces of silver, and Britain gained the Kowloon peninsula opposite Hong Kong.
Russia, the go-between, took its own reward: the coast down to Vladivostok.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 321–322; Prince Gong's role and the dates of the conventions (Hevia, 2003). Image: Felice Beato, 1860.

With the palace in ashes, the court gave in. "The Russian ambassador this time approached the Qing court and talked them into accepting the offered terms, which included having to pay a larger indemnity of 16 million ounces of silver and transfer of the Kowloon peninsula opposite Hong Kong island to Britain" (Ebrey, p. 322).

The man who signed for the Qing in late October 1860 was Prince Gong, the emperor's 27-year-old brother, left behind in Beijing. He is the young man in this photograph, taken by the same Felice Beato who photographed the Summer Palace. Remember his face; you will meet him again in a few minutes as a reformer.

The Russian envoy did not help for free. In November 1860 Russia's own treaty confirmed its hold on the lands east of the Ussuri River, down to Vladivostok, the territory you saw on the map.

Add up the outcomes of the war: envoys in the capital, more open ports, missionaries in the interior, a low transit tax, legal opium, a huge indemnity, Kowloon, the right to recruit Chinese laborers for work abroad, and Russia's new lands.

Source beyond Ebrey: Prince Gong's role and the dates of the conventions (Hevia, 2003).

Q7 story · After 1860

After 1860 the Western Powers Decided That a Weak Qing Dynasty Was Worth Keeping

The unequal treaties gave the Western powers many advantages inside China.
A collapsing dynasty could not pay indemnities or enforce treaties.
So after 1860 the powers increasingly propped up the Qing against the Taiping · Ch. 18 L2 · eva ↗
Foreign-run customs, begun at Shanghai in 1854, spread to every treaty port · ps-customs ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322, 325; Ch. 18 L2 for the Ever Victorious Army.

Here is the twist. Having humiliated the Qing, Britain and France now wanted it to survive. "Because the Western powers gained many advantages through these unequal treaties, after 1860 they increasingly saw propping up the faltering Qing Dynasty as in their interest" (Ebrey, p. 322).

Think about why. The treaties were contracts with the Qing government. If the Taiping won, who would pay the indemnities, protect the ports, or honor the tariff? A weak dynasty that kept its promises suited the powers better than a strong new one that might tear them up.

You saw the result in the last lecture: Western officers drilled the Ever Victorious Army, and British guns helped defend Shanghai. Foreign inspectors, first appointed at Shanghai in 1854, also took over collecting China's sea customs at every treaty port, which the Pierian Spring explains. The links take you to both.

So the same war that weakened the dynasty also tied its survival to foreign help.

Why this matters: From 1860 on, the Qing survived partly because foreign powers found it useful.

Q7 concept · Sovereignty

Concept: The War Took Away More of China's Sovereignty

Concept

Sovereignty

A state's right to rule its own territory and people without taking orders from outside: to make its own laws, set its own taxes, guard its own borders, and decide whom to receive.

Foreign ambassadors now lived in Beijing whether the emperor liked it or not.
Foreigners and missionaries traveled the interior under their own consuls' law.
China could not raise its own tariffs or transit taxes without foreign consent.
Land passed to Britain and Russia by treaty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 317, 321–322.

Reading Guide Q7 (p. 321) ends with a hard word: how did the war "further weaken Qing sovereignty?" Sovereignty is a state's right to rule its own land and people without outside orders. A sovereign state makes its own laws, sets its own taxes, guards its borders, and decides who may enter.

Now test the outcomes against that definition. Envoys in Beijing: the emperor could no longer choose whom to receive. Missionaries in the interior, protected by extraterritoriality: Chinese law no longer reached everyone in China. A fixed transit tax of 2.5 percent: China could not set its own taxes on goods moving inside its own country. Kowloon and the Russian lands: territory given away by treaty (Ebrey, pp. 321–322).

The first war had started this in 1842 with five ports and a fixed tariff. The second war spread it to the capital and the whole interior. That is what "further" means in the question.

Why this matters: Sovereignty is the measuring stick for this question; each outcome is a piece of it given away.

Check yourself: Pick one outcome of the war and explain how it limited Qing sovereignty.

Q8 story · Beijing · 1861

In 1861 the Emperor Died, and the Child Emperor's Mother and Uncle Seized Control

The Xianfeng emperor died in 1861, still in exile beyond the Great Wall.
His five-year-old son became emperor.
After a palace coup, his mother Cixi and the senior empress dowager, Ci'an, ruled as regents, with his uncle Prince Gong as chief minister.
A new reign brought new officials, and a chance for new policies.

Names in This Story

Xianfeng the emperor who fled in 1860 and died in 1861

Tongzhi his young son, emperor 1861–1875

Prince Gong the boy's uncle, who signed the 1860 peace

Cixi, Ci'an the boy's mother and the late emperor's empress, both empresses dowager

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; the emperor's death at Rehe and Tongzhi's age (Wright, 1957); the coup of 1861 and Ci'an (Rawski, 1998). Image: Qing court portrait.

The second half of the lecture begins with a death. "In 1861 the Xianfeng (shyen-fung) emperor died and was succeeded by a young son. The child's uncle, Prince Gong (gung), and his mother, Empress Dowager Cixi (tsih-shee), served as regents" (Ebrey, p. 322). A regent rules for a monarch who is too young. The emperor died at his hunting palace at Rehe, never having returned to Beijing, and his son, the Tongzhi emperor, was five years old; these details come from sources beyond Ebrey.

Instructor note: the textbook simplifies the regency. Xianfeng named eight regents, led by Sushun. In November 1861 Cixi, the senior empress dowager Ci'an, and Prince Gong overthrew them in a palace coup, and Sushun was beheaded. The two empresses dowager then ruled together, "listening to government from behind the curtain," until 1873, and Ci'an shared power until her death in 1881. Prince Gong held the title prince-counselor, the leading minister, not regent (Rawski, 1998).

Why did this matter? "A change in emperor normally meant a change in chancellors and other high officials, making it easier for the court to take new directions." And "new policies were needed": the rebellions had laid waste the richest provinces, and "the British and French had only recently left Beijing after extracting new concessions."

Prince Gong was the man who had signed the peace in 1860. He had seen foreign power up close. Cixi, only twenty-five, would become the most powerful person at court for the next half century; the next lecture is about her.

Why this matters: The new regents gave reformers the opening they needed.

Check yourself: Why did a change of emperor make new policies easier?

Q8 story · Shanghai · about 1860

In Shanghai the Scholar Feng Guifen Watched Westerners Defend the City Against the Taiping

Feng Guifen (1809–1874) was a scholar from Suzhou who had passed the highest examination.
He fled to Shanghai when the Taiping took his home region.
There he watched British and French troops, with modern guns, defend the city.
In 1861 he wrote essays that made the case for wide-ranging reform.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; Feng's degree, his home in Suzhou, and the title of his essays (de Bary and Lufrano, 2000). Image: John Thomson, 1869.

Now meet the man whose question gives this lecture its title. "In that same year, a scholar named Feng Guifen (fung gway-fuhn) (1809–1874) wrote a set of essays presenting the case for wide-ranging reforms. He had taken refuge in Shanghai during the Taiping War and there had seen how the Westerners defended the city" (Ebrey, p. 322).

From sources beyond Ebrey: Feng came from Suzhou, the elegant city you saw fall to the Taiping in the last lecture, and he had earned the highest examination degree, the jinshi. He was an insider, a trained Confucian scholar, not a rebel or a foreigner. His essays were collected under the title Protests from the Study of Jiaobin.

In Shanghai Feng saw something new. A few thousand foreign soldiers, with rifles and steamships, held off Taiping armies that had beaten the Qing again and again. Shanghai itself was booming, with foreign banks, warehouses, and ships, as the photograph from a few years later shows.

Reading Guide Q8 (p. 322) asks what Feng and the other reformers hoped to achieve. His experience in Shanghai is where it starts.

Why this matters: The push for reform came from inside the scholar class, from men who had seen Western power at work.

Q8 source · Feng Guifen's essays · 1861

"Why Are Western Nations Small and Yet Strong? Why Are We Large and Yet Weak?"

Feng noted that China was a hundred times bigger than France and two hundred times bigger than Britain.
Yet small Western nations had beaten it twice.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322, quoting de Bary and Lufrano, Sources of Chinese Tradition (2000). Image: Yang Pengqiu, 20th century.

Here is Feng's question in his own words, as the textbook quotes it. "In his essays he pointed out that China was a hundred times bigger than France and two hundred times bigger than Great Britain. 'Why are Western nations small and yet strong? Why are we large and yet weak?'" (Ebrey, p. 322). The translation is from de Bary and Lufrano's Sources of Chinese Tradition, a standard collection of primary sources.

Read the quotation aloud. It is a hard question for a proud civilization to ask. For centuries Chinese scholars had seen their country as the center of the civilized world. Feng did not say that China's culture was wrong. He said that China lacked certain practical skills, and that it could learn them.

And he was confident. "China has many brilliant people. There must be some who can learn from the barbarians and surpass them." Notice that he still uses the word "barbarians" for Westerners. He wanted to learn from them in order to beat them.

The portrait is a later painting, made long after his death, so treat it as a likeness, not a photograph.

Why this matters: Feng's question became the starting point for all Chinese reform for the next fifty years.

Check yourself: In your own words, what was Feng asking?

Q8 story · Feng Guifen's proposals

Feng Proposed Hiring a Few "Barbarians" to Build Shipyards and Arsenals, and Translating Western Books

Hire a few foreigners to set up shipyards and arsenals in each major port.
Reward the Chinese managers with examination degrees if their ships and guns matched the foreigners'.
Set up translation bureaus for Western books on mathematics and the sciences.
Hire Westerners to teach groups of boys Western languages.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322. Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

What did Feng want to do? The Reading Guide asks you to copy the sentence that begins "He called for…". Here it is: "He called for hiring a few 'barbarians' to help set up shipyards and arsenals in each major port" (Ebrey, p. 322). An arsenal is a factory that makes guns and ammunition.

Then he solved a practical problem. Who would run these places? Educated men wanted to pass the examinations, not run factories. So Feng "proposed rewarding them with examination degrees if the ships and weapons produced were as good as those of the foreigners." That was a bold idea: an engineer could earn the same honor as a scholar.

He also "proposed setting up translation bureaus to translate Western books on mathematics and the sciences," and wanted Westerners "hired to teach groups of boys Western languages." His argument was simple: "many Westerners had learned the Chinese language and much about the country; surely there should be Chinese people just as capable."

These are the specific reforms Reading Guide Q8 asks you to list.

The picture is an AI-generated interpretation made for this deck, not a historical source. Use it to imagine the scene, not as evidence. It imagines the kind of shipyard Feng proposed, with a hired foreign engineer.

Why this matters: Feng's plan joined Western technology to the old system of examination rewards.

Q8 story · Feng Guifen's proposals

Feng Even Proposed That Officials Elect Their Superiors, and That the Votes Be Counted

To improve officials' morale, high officials would be elected by lower-ranking ones.
Local elites would nominate local officials.
The votes were to be counted, probably an idea from foreign elections.
This went far beyond guns and ships, and it was never carried out.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322–323. Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

Feng's boldest ideas were about government, not weapons. "To improve the morale of officials, he proposed subjecting high officials to election by lower-ranking officials. Local elites would be given the power to nominate local officials, thus broadening political participation considerably" (Ebrey, pp. 322–323).

And one more detail: "Undoubtedly influenced by what he had learned of foreign election practices, he specified that the votes were to be counted." In the Qing system officials were appointed from above, by the emperor and his ministers. Feng wanted some voice from below.

Think about how radical this was. Feng was not proposing democracy for everyone; only officials and local elites would vote. But it meant that power would come partly from colleagues and local leaders, not only from the throne.

None of these political ideas was adopted. The reformers who followed Feng took up his technical proposals, shipyards, arsenals, translation, and schools, and left the elections aside. Keep that gap in mind; the next lecture asks why reform fell short.

The picture is an AI-generated interpretation made for this deck, not a historical source. Use it to imagine the scene, not as evidence. It shows Feng's plan as he imagined it. No such vote ever took place.

Why this matters: Feng's goals reached into politics, but the reforms that followed stayed mostly technical.

Q8 concept · Self-strengthening

Concept: Self-Strengthening Meant Learning Western Skills to Defend the Chinese Way

Concept

Self-Strengthening

The reform movement of about 1861–1895, led by officials such as Prince Gong, Zeng Guofan, and Li Hongzhang, that built arsenals, shipyards, schools, and industries on Western models in order to make the Qing strong enough to resist the foreign powers.

Goal: make China strong enough to resist the foreigners.
Method: borrow Western weapons, science, and industry.
Limit: keep the emperor, the examinations, and Confucian values.
Leaders: provincial officials, with Prince Gong's support at court.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322–323; the movement's dates and its "strong" goal (Teng and Fairbank, 1979).

The textbook gives this period the heading "Self-Strengthening" (Ebrey, p. 322). The term came from an old Chinese phrase about making oneself strong, and the reformers used it for their program. Historians usually date the movement from about 1861 to China's defeat by Japan in 1895, as sources beyond Ebrey explain.

The idea had a clear logic. The goal was to make the Qing strong enough to resist the foreign powers. The method was to borrow what the West did best: weapons, ships, science, and later industry. The limit was to change as little else as possible. The emperor, the examinations, and Confucian values would stay.

"An important minority of officials, including Zeng Guofan and Li Hongzhang, were more and more persuaded by these sorts of arguments. Prince Gong sided with them" (Ebrey, p. 323). You met Zeng and Li in the last lecture as the generals who crushed the Taiping. Their armies had used Western guns; now they wanted China to make its own.

Why this matters: Self-strengthening is the answer to the "goals" half of Reading Guide Q8.

Check yourself: What did the self-strengtheners want to change, and what did they want to keep?

Q8 story · Beijing · 1861–1880

A Foreign Office Run by Prince Gong Handled Diplomacy, and by 1880 China Had Embassies in Seven Capitals

In 1861 a new foreign office, the Zongli Yamen, took charge of diplomacy, with Prince Gong at its head.
A school in Beijing taught European languages and international law.
Envoys went abroad for the first time.
By 1880 China had embassies in London, Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Washington, Tokyo, and St. Petersburg.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 323; the office's name, the 1862 language school (the Tongwen Guan), and the photographer (Thomson; Wright, 1957). Image: John Thomson, about 1871.

Self-strengthening started with diplomacy. Before 1860 the Qing had no foreign ministry; foreigners were handled as tribute envoys or traders. Now foreign ambassadors lived in Beijing, and someone had to deal with them. "Changes were made not only in how soldiers were trained and weapons produced but also in the conduct of foreign affairs… schools opened to teach European languages and international law, and a foreign office established to manage diplomatic affairs, with Prince Gong in charge" (Ebrey, p. 323).

The textbook just says "a foreign office." Its name, the Zongli Yamen, "office for general management," and the founding of the Beijing language school in 1862 come from sources beyond Ebrey. The photograph shows some of its members.

Then China began to send envoys out. "By 1880, China had embassies in London, Paris, Berlin, Madrid, Washington, Tokyo, and St. Petersburg." For an empire that had treated all other states as tribute-bearers, this was a real change: China was now one state among many.

Why this matters: The foreign office and embassies were a reform in how China dealt with the world, not just in weapons.

Q8 story · Shanghai · 1865

In 1865 Zeng and Li Opened the Jiangnan Arsenal at Shanghai, with a Bureau to Translate Western Books

Zeng and Li had used Western guns to crush the Taiping · Ch. 18 L2 · proteges ↗
In 1865 they founded the Jiangnan Arsenal in Shanghai to make guns and steamships.
Its translation bureau turned Western books on science and engineering into Chinese.
In 1866 Zuo Zongtang opened the Fuzhou Navy Yard, with French engineers and a naval school.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 322–323; the first steamship and the translator John Fryer (Elman, 2005); the Fuzhou Navy Yard (Pong, 1994). Image: photograph before 1900.

The most famous self-strengthening project was a factory. "1865: Established Jiangnan Arsenal at Shanghai with a translation bureau attached, jointly with Zeng Guofan," says the textbook's table of Li Hongzhang's projects (Ebrey, p. 323). Jiangnan means "south of the river," the lower Yangzi region.

Compare this with Feng's plan: shipyards and arsenals in the major ports, with foreign help, and bureaus to translate Western books on mathematics and science. Here they were, in one place.

From sources beyond Ebrey: the arsenal launched its first steamship in 1868 and became the largest arms factory in East Asia. Its translators, led by the Englishman John Fryer working with Chinese scholars, put well over a hundred Western books on chemistry, mathematics, and engineering into Chinese.

A second great yard followed. In 1866 Zuo Zongtang founded the Fuzhou Navy Yard on the Min River, run by Shen Baozhen with French advisers led by Prosper Giquel; it built steam warships and trained officers in its naval school (Pong, 1994). Remember Fuzhou: in the next lecture a French fleet destroys the squadron anchored in front of it.

The photograph shows workers moving one of the arsenal's guns. Notice the mix: a modern weapon, moved by human muscle.

Why this matters: The Jiangnan Arsenal shows Feng's specific proposals becoming real.

Q8 story · San Francisco · 1872

In 1872 the First Chinese Boys Sailed to America to Study

In 1872 Li and Zeng recommended sending teenagers to study in the United States.
Some 120 boys went, in four groups, led by Yung Wing, who had studied at Yale.
They lived with families in New England and went to American schools.
Officials feared they were becoming too American, and in 1881 the court called them home.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 323; the Chinese Educational Mission, Yung Wing, and its recall (LaFargue, 1942). Image: photograph, 1872.

One of the most surprising entries in the textbook's table is for 1872: Li, "jointly with Zeng recommended sending teenagers to study in the U.S." (Ebrey, p. 323). Feng had wanted Chinese boys to learn Western languages from Westerners. This went further: the boys would go to the West.

From sources beyond Ebrey: the program was called the Chinese Educational Mission. Its leader, Yung Wing, had himself graduated from Yale in 1854, the first Chinese graduate of an American college. Between 1872 and 1875, 120 boys, most of them about twelve years old, sailed to America. They lived with families in Connecticut and Massachusetts, studied in American schools, played baseball, and some entered Yale.

Conservative officials worried that the boys were cutting off their queues and forgetting Chinese ways. In 1881 the court ordered them home. Many later became engineers, naval officers, and diplomats.

These six boys from the first group were photographed soon after they landed in California.

Why this matters: The mission shows both how far self-strengthening could reach and how quickly conservatives could pull it back.

Q8 story · Beyond guns and ships · 1870s–1890s

Li and Other Reformers Came to See That Guns and Ships Rested on Industry

Guns and ships, the reformers found, were only the surface of Western strength.
To catch up, China would need new industries: railways, steamships, mines, telegraphs, and cotton mills.
Newspapers in Shanghai and Hong Kong began to report on the world.
By 1888 China had a modern navy, the Beiyang fleet, with German-built battleships.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 323; the ship's German builder and dates (U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command). Image: U.S. Naval History and Heritage Command.

Over time the reformers' goals grew. "As the Chinese learned more about Western ways, Li and other modernizers came to recognize that guns and ships were merely the surface manifestation of the Western powers' economic strength. To catch up with the West, they argued, China would have to initiate new industries, which in the 1870s and 1880s included railway lines, steam navigation companies, coal mines, telegraph lines, and cotton-spinning and weaving factories" (Ebrey, p. 323).

Knowledge grew too. "By the 1890s knowledge of the West had improved considerably. Newspapers covering world affairs had begun publication in Shanghai and Hong Kong, and more and more Western works were being translated."

The battleship in the photograph is the Dingyuan, built in Germany and delivered in 1885, and flagship of the Beiyang ("northern seas") fleet that Li founded in 1888. On paper it was one of the strongest navies in Asia. These ship details come from sources beyond Ebrey. The next lecture tells what happened when it was tested.

Why this matters: The goals of self-strengthening widened from weapons to the economy that made weapons.

Pierian Spring · Story · The Yuanmingyuan · 1740s–1860

The Ruins You Saw at the Start Were European-Style Palaces Designed by Jesuits for the Qianlong Emperor

The Qianlong emperor had Jesuit missionaries at court design a corner of the park in European style · Ch. 16 L2 · jesuits-court ↗
Stone palaces, mazes, and fountains were built from the 1740s on.
One fountain clock had twelve bronze animal heads that spouted water in turn, each for one of the day's twelve two-hour periods.
In 1860 these buildings burned with the rest, and only their stone frames survived.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 322; Ch. 16 L2 for Jesuits at the Qing court; the European palaces, the engravings, and the bronze heads (Wong, 2001; Barmé, 1996). Image: Qing court engraving, 1786.

Go back to the photograph on the title slide. Those broken white arches do not look Chinese, and they are not. They are the ruins of the Xiyang Lou, the "Western mansions," a small corner of the Yuanmingyuan built in European style. The details on this slide come from sources beyond Ebrey.

In the 1740s the Qianlong emperor saw a picture of a European fountain and asked the Jesuit missionaries at his court to build him some. The Italian painter Giuseppe Castiglione and the French Jesuit Michel Benoist designed stone palaces, a maze, and great fountains. You met the Jesuits at the Qing court in Chapter 16; the link takes you back.

The most famous fountain was a water clock: twelve bronze heads of the animals of the Chinese zodiac, each spouting water for two hours of the day. In 1786 court artists made a set of copperplate engravings of the European palaces, like this one.

In 1860 they burned with the rest of the park. Because they were stone, their frames survived, which is why they are the ruins people visit today. Several of the bronze heads were taken abroad, and their return has become a cause in China.

Why this matters: The palace burned in 1860 held a sign of an earlier, friendlier curiosity about Europe.

Pierian Spring · Story · The treaty ports · 1860–1900

In the Treaty Ports, Foreign Concessions Had Their Own Police, Courts, and Taxes

Foreign concessions were districts carved out of Chinese cities.
They had foreign police, foreign law courts, and their own taxes, though most residents were Chinese.
By 1900 about thirty treaty ports were open; Shanghai, Tianjin, Hankou, Guangzhou, and Dalian were the largest.
Foreign warships anchored at the docks, ready to make a show of force.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 324–325; the number of treaty ports (Feuerwerker, 1983). Image: John Thomson, 1869.

The treaties of 1842 and 1860 created a new kind of city. "The foreign concessions at treaty ports were areas carved out of existing Chinese cities. They had foreign police and foreign law courts and collected their own taxes, a situation the Qing accepted with little protest, even though most of the population within the concessions continued to be Chinese" (Ebrey, p. 324).

"By 1900 there were one hundred treaty ports, but only Shanghai, Tianjin, Hankou, Guangzhou, and Dalian (at the southern tip of Manchuria) became major centers of foreign residence" (p. 325). Hong Kong was a colony, not a treaty port.

Instructor note: the textbook's "one hundred" is too high for 1900. The Maritime Customs listed about thirty treaty ports open at the end of 1899; the total passed ninety only by about 1917, counting ports China opened on its own (Feuerwerker, 1983). The slide uses the lower figure.

Life there could be impressive: "the streetlights and tall buildings in the Western-dominated parts of these cities showed Chinese what Western 'progress' was all about." But "the Chinese in these cities also felt the disdain of the Westerners toward China and the Chinese."

And behind it all was force. "Foreign warships anchored at the docks of the treaty ports, ready to make a show of force." When missionaries or merchants got into conflicts, gunboats were sent to threaten retaliation, "a practice termed gunboat diplomacy."

Why this matters: The concessions made the loss of sovereignty visible on the streets of China's biggest cities.

Check yourself: Why were the concessions a loss of sovereignty, even though most people living in them were Chinese?

Pierian Spring · Story · The Imperial Maritime Customs · 1854–1911

Westerners Ran China's Customs Service, and an Irishman, Robert Hart, Led It for Over Forty Years

When the Taiping disrupted tariff collection at Shanghai, the British and American consuls collected the duties themselves.
This grew into the Imperial Maritime Customs, staffed at its higher levels by Westerners.
Robert Hart, an Irishman, served as its Inspector General from 1863 to 1911.
It collected the tariffs for the Qing, published trade reports, and built postal and telegraph services.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, p. 325; Robert Hart and his years in office (Bickers, 2011). Image: J. J. E. Mayall, 1866.

Here is one of the strangest results of the treaties. "When the disorder of the Taiping Rebellion disrupted tariff collection in Shanghai and Amoy, the British and American consuls there collected the tariffs themselves, a practice later regularized into a permanent Imperial Maritime Customs staffed at its higher level by Westerners" (Ebrey, p. 325).

Its work went beyond taxes: "In addition to recording and collecting tariffs, the customs published annual reports on the outlook for trade at each port and undertook projects to improve communications, such as telegraph and postal systems."

From sources beyond Ebrey: the customs was led for almost half a century by Robert Hart, an Irishman from Ulster who went to China at nineteen as a British consular interpreter. He became Inspector General in 1863 and held the post until 1911. Hart was a Qing official, not a British one: he served the emperor, spoke Chinese, and advised Prince Gong. His service gave the Qing a large and honest stream of revenue, much of it later used to pay indemnities.

Why this matters: The customs shows how foreigners became part of the Qing state itself, helping prop it up.

Pierian Spring · Story · Missionaries · 1860–1905

After 1860 Missionaries Spread Through the Countryside, Running Schools, Hospitals, and Orphanages

The treaty of 1860 let missionaries travel and live anywhere in China, and they came in large numbers.
Unlike merchants, they lived among ordinary, often poor, Chinese.
Orphanages helped produce converts, but rumors said missionaries bought babies for evil purposes.
Over time many turned to medicine and education, which the Chinese received better than preaching.

3,900

missionaries in China by 1900 (886 Catholic, about 3,000 Protestant), more than half of them women

250

mission hospitals and dispensaries by 1905, treating about 2 million patients

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 325–326. Image: AI-generated illustration made for this deck.

Away from the treaty ports, most Chinese never met a merchant. They met missionaries. "Once China agreed in the treaty of 1860 to allow missionaries to travel through China, they came in large numbers. Unlike merchants in the treaty ports, missionaries had no choice but to mix with the local population, and they spent much of their time with ordinary, poor Chinese, finding the best opportunities for conversion among them" (Ebrey, p. 325).

That closeness caused trouble. "Missionaries often ran orphanages, a 'good work' that also helped produce converts, but the Chinese suspected that they were buying babies for nefarious purposes." Nefarious means wicked. "Widely circulated antimissionary tracts were often filled with inflammatory charges of this sort."

Missionaries and their converts also turned to their consuls, protected by extraterritoriality, when they quarreled with neighbors. That made local people see them as agents of foreign power.

The numbers grew large. "By 1900 there were 886 Catholic and about 3,000 Protestant missionaries in China, more than half of them women." By 1905, "the 250 mission hospitals and dispensaries treated about 2 million patients" (p. 326).

Why this matters: Missionaries brought both schools and hospitals and anger, and that anger fed the Boxers.

Pierian Spring · Story · Tianjin · June 1870

In June 1870 Rumors About an Orphanage in Tianjin Ended in a Riot That Killed the French Consul, Nuns, and Priests

The French had built a cathedral on the site of a Chinese temple, and nuns there ran an orphanage.
When an epidemic killed many orphans, rumors spread that the children were being murdered.
The French consul fired a pistol at Chinese officials, and an angry crowd killed him and about twenty other foreigners, with several dozen Chinese converts.
China paid reparations, punished rioters, and sent a mission of apology to France.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 18, pp. 325–326; Zeng Guofan's role in the settlement (Cohen, 1963). Image: John Thomson, 1870s.

The tensions of the last slide exploded in Tianjin "on a June day in 1870" (Ebrey, p. 325). The French had made many enemies: troops based there in 1860–1863, a former palace taken for their consulate, and a cathedral "at the site of a former Chinese temple" (p. 326).

Nuns at the cathedral ran an orphanage and paid small sums for dying children, to baptize them before death. "When an epidemic swept through the orphanage in June 1870, so many orphans died that rumors spread that they were being killed for their body parts." When a local official came to search, a fight broke out.

Then the French consul, "carrying two pistols," charged into the official's office and fired at him, missing, and later shot at the city magistrate in the street. "The crowd then killed the consul and the officer with him, as well as twelve priests and nuns, seven other foreigners, and several dozen Chinese converts," and burned the cathedral and four other churches.

"Although the French consul had incited the violence, it was the Chinese who had to pay reparations as well as punish members of the mob and send a mission of apology to France." The official who settled the case was Zeng Guofan, who was bitterly criticized for it, as sources beyond Ebrey explain.

Why this matters: Tianjin in 1870 was a warning of the larger anti-foreign rising, the Boxers, thirty years later.

HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 12 · Lecture 3

Displaced Elites and the Collapse of Yuan Rule

Scholars Found New Roles under the Mongols, and Floods, Money and Rebels Brought the Dynasty Down

1279–1368 · Loyalists, exams, drama, painting and decline · Study Buddy edition

After Qian Xuan, Ode on Returning Home, handscroll, 14th–15th century, a close copy of a composition by Qian Xuan. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 13.220.124. Public domain.

Welcome to the last lecture on the Yuan! This deck is your Study Buddy for two big stories: what educated Chinese men did when the Mongol conquest took away their usual careers, and why Mongol rule in China fell apart in the 1350s and 1360s.

The painting behind the title is a later copy. The Metropolitan Museum dates it to the 14th or 15th century and catalogs it as "after Qian Xuan," a close copy of a composition by Qian Xuan, a southern scholar who lived through the fall of the Song and, according to later accounts, never took a Yuan post. So the brushwork you see is the copyist's, but the design, the subject and the poem go back to Qian.

The composition shows the poet Tao Yuanming (365–427) sailing home after quitting office, a scene every educated reader knew. Choosing that scene after 1279 could carry a personal meaning for Qian, but the composition itself does not tell us exactly what he intended. The fact that people kept copying it suggests later viewers valued the theme.

Why this matters: the scholars in this lecture lost the path their families had planned for them. How they responded, from refusing to serve to writing plays, changed Chinese culture for centuries.

The puzzle · One life, 1269–1354

Huang Gongwang Was a Clerk, a Prisoner, a Daoist and a Painter

No exams. Huang grew up in the south after 1279, when the regular examinations that had made officials were gone.
A clerk's career. He worked as a clerk, and around 1315 he was jailed when his superior fell in a corruption case.
A new life. Afterward he joined a Daoist order, told fortunes, taught and painted.
A masterpiece. In his late seventies he painted a long scroll for a Daoist friend, finishing around 1350.

The puzzle: how did educated men build lives when the usual road to office narrowed, and what else helped drama and painting thrive?

This slide gives you one life that sums up much of this lecture. Huang Gongwang was born in 1269 in Changshu, near the Yangzi delta. He was educated for office, but in his lifetime the Yuan held no regular examinations until 1315, and southerners had few ways into official posts.

So he became a clerk, a paid assistant who handled paperwork for officials. Biographies report that around 1315 he was imprisoned after his superior, Zhang Lü, was arrested in a scandal over a land survey. After his release Huang joined the Quanzhen school of Daoism. He supported himself through fortune telling and teaching, and he painted.

The scroll was later damaged. In 1650 a collector who loved it ordered it burned at his death; a relative pulled it from the fire, and it survives in two pieces. This short opening piece is in Hangzhou, and the long main section is in Taipei. You will see that section later.

Why this matters: Huang's life is one example behind Review Questions 6 and 10. Restricted official opportunities encouraged some educated men to pursue other roles. Drama and painting also grew from existing artistic traditions, networks of friends and patrons, and city audiences. Career loss was one contributing condition, not the whole story.

Literati (shi)

The literati were men educated in the Confucian classics, history and poetry. Under the Song, the examinations turned many of them into officials, and the rest still shared the same education and values.

Being literati was about learning and status, not only about holding office. That is why a man could stay literati even when he had no job in government.

Q6 story · The closed door

The Conquest Took Away the Path from Study to Office

1274 to 1315

Southern Song held its last examination in 1274, and the Yuan held no regular one until 1315, a gap of forty years.

Shared posts. The Yuan staffed its government with Mongols, semu and northern Chinese, so fewer posts were open to southern scholars.
Other ways in. Many officials entered through family privilege, recommendation or clerical service rather than the civil service examinations.
Few in the realm study the Confucian classics, and many who obtain office do so as clerks of the brush.Advisers to Khubilai in 1284, as recorded in the examination treatise of the Yuan shi, ch. 81. Translated for this deck.
Lost status. For Song families, office had been the proof of success. Without it, a scholar had to find a new answer to the question "What am I for?"

Not everyone suffered. Many southern landlords kept their land and wealth, and taxes in the south were often light. The loss was mainly one of careers and prestige.

×

Semu ren

You met this label in Lecture 2. Semu ("various kinds") covered peoples who were neither Mongol nor Chinese, such as Uyghurs, Tanguts, Persians and Arabs.

Semu officials held many posts in finance and administration, which is one reason fewer posts were open to Chinese scholars.

This slide gives you the core problem behind Review Question 6. Under the Song, a talented boy from a good family studied for the civil service examinations. Passing them was the main road to office and honor. After the conquest, that road was closed.

The dates matter. The Southern Song held its last examination in 1274, two years before the Mongols took its capital. North China had seen only one Mongol-era test, in 1238, and its graduates did not get a lasting system. So for about forty years, educated men in the south had no regular exam to aim at.

The quotation comes from a debate at Khubilai's court in 1284. His advisers complained that few people studied the classics and that many officials rose from clerkships, the "clerks of the brush." They proposed reviving the exams, and Khubilai agreed, but nothing happened in his lifetime. The quotation tells you that men at the court itself saw the problem.

Keep the picture balanced. Losing office did not mean losing everything. Many southern families stayed rich landowners, and some did well under the Yuan. What they lost was the career that gave their education its purpose and their families their prestige.

Why this matters: Q6 asks how the conquest disrupted the roles of the educated elite. The missing exams and the sharing of posts with non-Chinese officials are the heart of your answer.

Q6 story · Refusing to serve

Some Scholars Stayed Loyal to the Fallen Song and Refused Yuan Office

Zheng Sixiao. Zheng painted orchids without soil, and later writers said the land had been stolen by foreigners.
Xie Fangde. Xie refused repeated offers of office, and when he was taken to Dadu in 1289 he starved himself to death.

Heaven and Earth have a righteous spirit; mixed and scattered, it takes on many forms.Wen Tianxiang, opening lines of the Song of the Righteous Spirit, written in prison in Dadu, 1281. Translated for this deck.
×

The rootless orchid

The orchid was an old symbol of a pure and loyal gentleman. Zheng Sixiao (1241–1318) painted it with no earth around the roots.

Later writers reported that when asked why, he answered that the land had been taken by foreigners. Other stories say he always sat facing south, toward the lost Song. These are reports written after his death, so treat them as part of his legend.

This slide gives you the first and most dramatic response to the conquest: loyalism. Song loyalists (in Chinese, yimin, "left-over subjects") believed that a man who had served or studied under one dynasty should not serve the dynasty that destroyed it.

Zheng Sixiao is famous for the orchid. The painting is real and dated 1306. The explanation of the missing soil is a story recorded by later writers. It fits his poems, which mourn the Song, but we cannot be sure he said it. A careful answer says "later writers reported."

Xie Fangde may be familiar from Lecture 2, where he joked about ten grades of people with scholars near the bottom. Yuan officials pressed him to serve. When he was taken north to the capital in 1289, he refused food and died.

Wen Tianxiang you met in Lecture 1. He led Song resistance, was captured, and spent about three years in prison in Dadu before his execution in 1283. His fame grew after his death. Ming emperors honored him with shrines, and his poem became something schoolchildren memorized.

Loyalists were a minority. Most educated men did not starve themselves; they found ways to live under the new rulers, as the next slide shows.

Why this matters: Q6 asks about the paths scholars took. Refusing office was one path, and it shaped how later Chinese remembered the Yuan.

Q6 story · New ways to live

Educated Men Became Teachers, Clerks, Doctors, Monks and Writers

Teaching

Many scholars taught in family schools, local schools and private academies, where they kept Neo-Confucian learning alive.

Clerks

Some took clerical jobs in Yuan offices. The work paid and could lead to minor posts, but scholars had long looked down on it.

Medicine

Medicine became an honorable choice for "Confucian doctors." Zhu Zhenheng studied Neo-Confucianism and then became a famous physician.

Religion

Others joined Daoist or Buddhist orders, which the Yuan favored and which gave a life outside government.

Writing and art

Some wrote plays, songs and stories, and others painted for friends or sold their work.

Private life

Men with land could live as gentlemen at home, writing poetry and leading their local communities.

×

Academies (shuyuan)

Shuyuan were schools, often in quiet places, where scholars studied and taught the classics. Zhu Xi had made them central to Neo-Confucian learning in the Song.

The Yuan government recognized and supported many academies and appointed teachers to them. Those teaching posts gave scholars a modest income and a respected role.

This slide gives you the "alternative paths" that Review Question 6 asks about. None of them was new, but under the Yuan many more educated men took them, and some gained new respect.

Teaching was the most common choice. Neo-Confucian teachers in academies and local schools trained the next generation, which is one reason Zhu Xi's teachings were ready to become the exam standard in 1315.

Clerks were the people who actually ran Yuan offices day to day. Huang Gongwang, from the opening slide, was one. Song scholars had seen clerical work as low, but under the Yuan it was one of the few routes into government.

Medicine rose in status. Zhu Zhenheng (1281–1358), also called Zhu Danxi, studied with a leading Neo-Confucian teacher, then turned to medicine to help his sick mother and others. He became one of the most influential doctors in Chinese history.

Religion offered a community and an income. The Yuan court supported Buddhist and Daoist clergy, and Daoist orders such as Quanzhen attracted educated men.

Why this matters: a strong Q6 answer names several paths and shows that scholars adapted, not just suffered.

Q6 story · The collaborator debate · 1286–1322

Zhao Mengfu Served the Mongols, and Critics Still Argued about Him Centuries Later

Recruited. In 1286 a Yuan envoy brought Zhao, a Song royal descendant, to Khubilai's court, and he rose to high office.
Useful at court. Biographies say he helped bring down the hated minister Sangha in 1291.
In the mountains it is called ‘far ambition’; out of the mountains it is just ‘little grass.’Zhao Mengfu, from his poem "My Fault Was Coming Out." The line puns on the names of one medicinal herb. Translated for this deck.

This slide gives you the most debated life in this block. Zhao Mengfu (1254–1322) was a descendant of the Song imperial family. In 1286 he was among the southern scholars recruited for Khubilai's court. He served for decades and reached the top of the Hanlin Academy.

His service was not empty. His biography in the Yuan shi reports that he helped expose Sangha, a finance minister whom many officials hated, who fell in 1291. Zhao also helped bring Chinese scholars into the court.

The poem shows he felt the cost. The herb yuanzhi means "far ambition," and its sprouts were called "little grass." In the hills a man is a noble recluse; once he comes out to serve, he is just grass. The title he gave the poem speaks of guilt.

The painting was made in 1295 for Zhou Mi, a friend who refused to serve the Yuan and whose family came from Shandong in the north, which he had never seen. Zhao painted those northern mountains for him. Some critics later praised Zhao's art and condemned his choice to serve. Others defended him. The debate tells us as much about later loyalty ideals as about Zhao himself.

Why this matters: Q6 is not only about refusers. Zhao shows that serving was also a path, with real benefits and a real cost to one's reputation.

Check yourself: Give one argument a critic might make against Zhao, and one argument a defender might make for him.

Q7 story · The exams return · 1313–1315

Emperor Renzong Restored the Examinations and Made Zhu Xi's Commentaries the Standard

A ruler with Chinese teachers. Ayurbarwada, known as Renzong (r. 1311–1320), had studied with Chinese tutors and favored Confucian advisers.
The edict. In the eleventh lunar month of 1313, Renzong issued the restoration edict, setting provincial exams for 1314 and metropolitan exams for 1315.
One school of thought. All candidates had to answer on the Four Books using Zhu Xi's commentaries.
In selecting men, virtuous conduct should come first; in testing their skills, the classics come first and literary composition second. Flowery words beyond substance We do not want.Renzong's restoration edict of the eleventh lunar month of 1313, in the examination treatise of the Yuan shi, ch. 81. Translated for this deck.
×

Zhu Xi and the Four Books

Zhu Xi was the great Neo-Confucian thinker of the Southern Song. He grouped four short texts, the Great Learning, the Analects, the Mencius and the Doctrine of the Mean, as the Four Books and wrote commentaries on them.

The 1313 rules made his commentaries required for every candidate. Ming and Qing exams kept that rule until the exams ended in 1905.

This slide gives you the turning point of Review Question 7. Renzong, whose Mongol name was Ayurbarwada, was raised with Chinese tutors such as Li Meng and is often described as one of the Yuan rulers most comfortable with Chinese learning. Be careful with the common claim that he was the first Mongol emperor who could read Chinese. We cannot test it well, so say instead that he was well versed in Chinese learning.

Khubilai's advisers had urged a revival as early as 1284, and later emperors issued orders that went nowhere. Renzong's ministers told him that someone must be blocking the plan. In the eleventh lunar month of 1313, Renzong issued the restoration edict. It set provincial exams for 1314 and the metropolitan exams for 1315.

The quotation shows what kind of exam he wanted. Song exams had prized poetry and elegant prose. Renzong's rules dropped much of that and stressed moral conduct and the classics. Most important, every candidate had to use Zhu Xi's commentaries. This made Zhu Xi's version of Confucianism the state orthodoxy for the next six centuries, a much longer legacy than the Yuan itself.

The portrait is a later imagined likeness, not a picture made in Zhu Xi's lifetime.

Textbook note: Ebrey (p. 206) dates the revival both to 1313 and to 1315. Both are right: the edict came in 1313 and the first exams in 1314–1315. Source beyond Ebrey: Yuan shi ch. 81.

Why this matters: Q7 asks you to evaluate the restored exams. One big result was not about the Yuan at all: it fixed Zhu Xi's teachings at the center of Chinese education.

Q7 story · Quotas and separate tracks

The New Exams Gave Each of the Four Groups the Same Number of Places

Essential: equal shares for unequal groups

75 each

Provincial exams sent 300 men to the capital: 75 Mongols, 75 semu, 75 Hanren and 75 Nanren.

25 each

The capital exam was to pass 100 men, 25 from each group, listed on two separate lists.

Unequal requirements. Mongols and semu sat two sessions with shorter answers, while Hanren and Nanren sat three harder ones.
Unequal populations. The Nanren were by far the largest group but had the same quota as the small Mongol and semu groups.

Supporting evidence: only 56 men received the jinshi in 1315, and the full quota of 100 was first reached in 1333. About 1,100 men earned the degree in the 16 exams held between 1315 and 1366, and Bayan halted the exams from 1335 until the 1340s.

×

The right and left lists

Successful candidates were posted on two lists. The right list, the more honored side, held Mongols and semu. The left list held Hanren and Nanren.

Each list had its own top graduate, so the Yuan named two "first-place" winners in a single exam.

This slide gives you the numbers Review Question 7 asks about. The gold and red boxes are the essentials: equal shares of places for the four groups, unequal exam requirements and very unequal populations. The muted line at the bottom is supporting evidence about how small the results were. They come from the examination treatise of the Yuan shi, which lists the quotas in detail, down to how many men each province could send.

Be careful with a figure you may see elsewhere: that southerners got "25 percent." Each group did get one quarter of the places, but that equal share is the point. The four groups were very unequal in size. Nanren were the great majority of the population, Mongols and semu a small minority. An equal quota therefore gave a Mongol candidate far better odds than a southerner.

The exams were also unequal in content. Mongols and semu answered questions on the Four Books and one essay on current affairs. Hanren and Nanren had to write on the classics, compose formal prose and answer a longer policy question.

Finally, the numbers stayed small. Around 1,100 degree holders over half a century is tiny for an empire of tens of millions. Most officials still entered by other routes. Graduates were a small share of officials: about 2 percent of posts in Ebrey (p. 206), just over 4 percent of ranked civil officials in Hsiao, Cambridge History of China 6, p. 517 (source beyond Ebrey).

Why this matters: Q7 asks how the quotas reflected Yuan hierarchies. Equal quotas for unequal groups, separate lists and easier tests for Mongols and semu all mirror the four-group order from Lecture 2.

Check yourself: Explain in your own words why "equal quotas" could still mean unequal chances.

Q9 story · A new kind of theater

Zaju Drama Filled City Stages, and Scholars Began Writing the Scripts

The form. A zaju play usually had four acts, with songs set to popular tunes and one lead singer.
The stage. Troupes played in city theaters and at temple festivals, and many star performers were women.
The writers. Some educated men formed writing clubs and sold scripts to troupes, and the scripts began to circulate as texts.
A record. In 1330 Zhong Sicheng listed the playwrights he knew in his Register of Ghosts.
×

Zaju ("variety plays")

Zaju grew out of earlier Song and Jin entertainments. By the Yuan it had a fixed form: four acts, each built on a suite of songs in one musical mode, with spoken dialogue between.

Only the lead role sang. That role could be male or female, so a famous actress might carry a whole play.

This slide gives you the setting for Review Question 9. Zaju was the great theater of the Yuan, performed in the big cities of the north, such as Dadu, and later in the south as well.

The mural is precious evidence. It was painted in 1324 on a wall of a temple hall dedicated to a water god in Hongtong, Shanxi. A banner at the top of the original, mostly cut off in this view, announces that the actress Zhongdu Xiu and her troupe "performed here." You can see costumes, painted faces, false beards and musicians. The photo was taken in the Shanxi Museum in Taiyuan, which displays what appears to be a full-size copy; the original remains in the temple hall.

Playwrights included scholars without posts, clerks and some minor officials. They joined writing societies that produced scripts for professional troupes. Zhong Sicheng's Register of Ghosts (1330) named well over a hundred writers of plays and songs. "Ghosts" was partly a joke: many of the writers he admired were already dead, and he hoped their names would live on.

Why this matters: Q9 asks about political and social factors. Restricted official opportunities encouraged some educated men to pursue other roles. Q9 story · Two famous plays

Guan Hanqing Wrote of Injustice, and Wang Shifu Wrote of Love

Earth, you cannot tell good from bad: how can you be Earth? Heaven, you mistake the wise for fools: you are Heaven in name only!Dou E, before her execution, in Guan Hanqing's Injustice to Dou E, Act 3, as printed in a Ming edition. Translated for this deck.
Love against the rules. In Wang Shifu's play, the student Zhang and Cui Yingying fall in love and meet in secret, helped by the clever maid Hongniang.

About the texts. Most Yuan plays survive in Ming editions that editors revised, so exact wording may not be Yuan.

This slide gives you the two plays most often linked to Review Question 9.

Guan Hanqing was one of the most prolific Yuan playwrights. Later lists credit him with dozens of plays, of which a much smaller number survive; counts differ. In Injustice to Dou E, a poor young widow is falsely accused of murder by a villain called Donkey Zhang. A corrupt official tortures her into confessing, and she is executed. Before she dies she makes three vows: her blood will fly up onto a white banner, snow will fall in summer, and drought will strike for three years. All three come true, and in the end her father, now an honest official, clears her name.

Dou E's speech is one of the most famous in Chinese drama. She blames Heaven and Earth, the moral order itself, for letting the innocent suffer. Many readers see criticism of Yuan justice here. That is a reasonable interpretation, but the play is set in an unnamed past and never names the Mongols, so present it as a reading, not a proven message.

Wang Shifu's Story of the Western Chamber retells a Tang love story. Its young lovers defy their families, and generations of readers adored it, while some moralists disapproved.

Why did some scholars write plays? Restricted official opportunities encouraged some educated men to pursue other roles. Drama and painting also grew from existing artistic traditions, networks of friends and patrons, and city audiences. Some writers needed income, and others simply enjoyed the craft. Plays let them show off learning and poetry, and they could also mock corrupt officials, greedy merchants and pompous scholars.

Notice that both pictures are much later prints made in the Ming and Qing periods. They show how these plays were loved long after the Yuan, not how Yuan stages looked.

Why this matters: for Q9 you need at least one play you can describe and explain. Either of these will do.

Q10 story · Painting for oneself and one's friends

Southern Scholars Turned Painting into Personal Expression for Friends

Qian Xuan. Qian (c. 1235–before 1307), a southern scholar, is reported never to have served the Yuan and to have lived partly by selling his paintings.
Looking back. Qian and Zhao Mengfu revived older styles from the Tang and Five Dynasties instead of copying the Song court painters.
Words and image. Literati painters wrote poems on their paintings, joining poetry, calligraphy and painting.
Rocks like ‘flying white,’ trees like seal script: to paint bamboo, you must master the eight strokes of writing.Zhao Mengfu, poem on his own painting Elegant Rocks and Sparse Trees. Translated for this deck.
×

Literati painting (wenrenhua)

Literati painting emphasized scholarly identity, expressive brushwork, and exchange among friends. Its ideal contrasted with professional craftsmanship, although in practice some literati sold paintings or served at court.

The ideal went back to Song scholars such as Su Shi. Literati painters often joined the "three perfections" of poetry, calligraphy and painting on one scroll.

This slide gives you the starting point for Review Question 10. Under the Song, many of the most admired paintings came from the court academy. After 1279, court and professional painting continued, including at the Yuan court, but scholar-painters working outside government gained new prominence.

Qian Xuan belonged to the generation that saw the fall of the Song. Later accounts say he refused to serve the Yuan and lived by his brush. His poems on paintings like this one can be read with a sad, nostalgic tone, but many of his subjects, like birds and flowers, were also ordinary favorites. A good answer says his loyalist reputation shaped how later viewers read him.

Zhao Mengfu, from the Q6 block, pushed painting in a new direction. He said brushwork in painting should come from calligraphy. "Flying white" is a dry, streaky stroke, and "seal script" is an ancient form of writing. He also revived older blue-green and archaic styles, as in his Autumn Colors.

Notice the irony: the collaborator and the refuser both helped create the new style. Painting was a shared language among southern scholars, whatever their politics.

Why this matters: Q10 asks how painting evolved. A strong answer can name three changes: scholar-painters gain new prominence alongside continuing court and professional painting, brushwork becomes like writing, and poems join the image.

Check yourself: What did Zhao Mengfu mean when he compared painted rocks and trees to kinds of calligraphy?

Q10 story · Two of the Four Masters

Huang Gongwang and Wu Zhen Painted from Lives Spent Outside Government

Huang Gongwang (1269–1354). His long scroll of the Fuchun River unrolls hills built from soft, dry, repeated brushstrokes, painted bit by bit over about three years.
Wu Zhen (1280–1354). Wu lived quietly near Jiaxing, reportedly telling fortunes, and painted bamboo, fishermen and landscapes with poems of his own.

This slide gives you two of the painters later called the Four Masters of the Yuan. That label was created by Ming critics; the four men did not see themselves as a group.

Huang Gongwang is the clerk turned Daoist from the opening slide. He wrote on his scroll that he painted it for a Daoist friend over several years, adding to it when the mood took him. The result is a landscape that seems to come from the painter's mind as much as from the real Fuchun River near Hangzhou. Later painters copied and imitated it for centuries.

Wu Zhen never held office. Accounts say he lived simply and supported himself partly by fortune telling. He loved painting bamboo, a plant that bends in the wind but does not break, a long-standing symbol of the upright gentleman. He also painted fishermen, a classic image of the free life of a recluse.

Do these paintings protest against the Mongols? They certainly celebrate a life away from government. Whether that was protest, a necessity or simply a preference is a matter of interpretation, and it probably differed from painting to painting.

Why this matters: Q10 asks how painting helped scholars cope. Painting gave scholars, with or without office, a respected activity, a circle of friends and a way to express their values. Restricted official opportunities encouraged some educated men to pursue other roles. Drama and painting also grew from existing artistic traditions, networks of friends and patrons, and city audiences.

Check yourself: Why might a scholar without office choose to paint bamboo or a fisherman? Give two possible reasons.

Q10 story · The other two masters

Ni Zan Painted Empty Shores, and Wang Meng Painted Crowded Mountains

Ni Zan (1301–1374). A rich landowner, Ni gave away much of his property in the 1350s and spent years traveling by boat around Lake Tai.
What I call painting is no more than a few careless strokes. I do not seek likeness; I paint only to amuse myself.Ni Zan, letter to a friend. Translated for this deck.
Wang Meng (c. 1308–1385). Zhao Mengfu's grandson painted dense, restless mountains, and he later died in a Ming prison.

Read with care. Political readings of these paintings are interpretations. The paintings rarely say so themselves.

This slide gives you the last two of the Four Masters, whose styles could hardly be more different.

Ni Zan came from a wealthy family near Wuxi. As the Yuan collapsed and taxes and rebels threatened the region, he gave away or abandoned much of his property and lived for years on boats and with friends. His paintings use a near shore with thin trees and an empty pavilion, a band of water, and distant hills. The emptiness has been read as purity, loneliness or withdrawal from a corrupt world. Ni himself, in the letter quoted here, says he paints to please himself.

Notice the date of The Rongxi Studio: 1372. That is four years into the Ming dynasty. So not every "Yuan" painting reacts to Mongol rule; Ni kept painting the same way after the Yuan fell.

Wang Meng was a grandson of Zhao Mengfu. His mountains are packed and twisting, almost unsettled. Some art historians, such as James Cahill in Hills Beyond a River, connect the anxious mood of works like this to the turmoil of the 1360s. That is an interpretation, not a fact written on the painting. Wang served briefly under the Ming and died in prison in 1385, caught up in a purge.

Your reading describes these painters as working for a small circle of like-minded friends and sometimes using allusion to make political statements. Keep the word "could." Some paintings carry such meanings, and many do not show them clearly.

Why this matters: Q10 asks how painting was used to cope with lost political power. These four men show several ways: withdrawal, friendship, self-expression and sometimes allusion.

Check yourself: Ni Zan's 1372 painting was made under the Ming. What does that suggest about reading every empty landscape as a protest against the Mongols?

The War of the Two Capitals (1328)

When Emperor Yesün Temür died at the summer capital Shangdu, his supporters there enthroned his young son. In Dadu, the general El Temür backed a rival prince, Tugh Temür.

The two sides fought for about two months, and Dadu won. In 1329 Tugh Temür's elder brother Khoshila arrived to claim the throne and died suddenly days after meeting him. Many at the time suspected poison.

This slide gives you the first of the separate causes in Review Question 11: political instability. The Mongols had no fixed rule for succession. Any male descendant of Chinggis Khan with enough support could claim the throne, and after Khubilai's grandson Temür died in 1307, rival princes and generals fought over it again and again.

The result was a court that spent its energy on plots. Strongmen such as El Temür and then Bayan dominated young emperors. Bayan halted the examinations in 1335 and, according to the Yuan shi, distrusted Chinese officials. His nephew Toghto turned against him in 1340, restored the exams and tried to rebuild the government. The historian John Dardess, in Conquerors and Confucians, describes this as a struggle between Mongol groups over how Chinese the government should become.

The edict in the image dates from 1362, late in Toghon Temür's reign, when his government still issued orders in Mongolian from Dadu. It shows that the court kept working even as the empire shrank; it is not evidence of any specific crisis.

Why this matters: Q11 asks for a combination of causes. Instability at the top meant that when floods and rebels came, the court was divided and slow to respond.

Q11 story · Economic mismanagement · 1350s

The Court Printed More and More Paper Money, and Prices Soared

A paper system. The Yuan had made paper money the main currency, as Lecture 2 showed, and it worked for decades.
The main driver. Civil war raised military spending while shrinking the revenue base, which pushed the government to print more money.
Other costs. Earlier gifts to princes and temple building had already strained the treasury.
A failed fix. In 1350 Toghto's government issued new notes and copper coins, but soon prices soared.
Before long, prices leapt more than tenfold. In the capital, ten ingots of paper notes could not buy a peck of grain.Treatise on economics of the Yuan shi, ch. 97, compiled by Ming scholars. Translated for this deck.

This slide gives you the second cause in Review Question 11: economic mismanagement. Your reading points out that the Mongols had difficulty regulating paper money and that inflation grew serious in the fourteenth century.

Paper money only works if people trust it. In the early Yuan the government limited how much it printed and let people exchange notes for silver. The main driver of the late crisis was war. Civil war raised military spending while shrinking the revenue base, which pushed the government to print more money. Rebels cut off taxes, salt revenue and grain shipments from the south just as the court needed to pay more soldiers. When the new notes of 1350 appeared just as rebellions broke out, the court printed them in huge quantities.

Other spending mattered too, but as a secondary cause. Gifts to Mongol princes and support for Buddhist temples had strained the treasury for decades before the wars began.

The quotation comes from the Yuan shi, written by Ming scholars soon after the dynasty fell. It reports that people stopped using notes and went back to barter. The general picture is well supported, but treat the exact figures as the report of a later official history.

The note in the picture is an earlier Zhiyuan series note, not one of the 1350 issues, so use it as an example of Yuan paper money in general.

Why this matters: inflation hurt ordinary people, ruined the government's finances, and made it harder to pay the troops needed to fight rebels.

Check yourself: Why would printing more money to pay soldiers make the government's problems worse rather than better?

Q11 story · Environmental crisis · 1344–1351

The Yellow River Broke Its Dikes, and the Repair Gathered a Huge Labor Force

Heavy rain fell for more than twenty days. The Yellow River overflowed, the water stood about two zhang deep on level ground, and it broke through the Baimao dike to the north.River treatise of the Yuan shi, ch. 66, on the 5th month of 1344. Translated for this deck.
A disaster zone. Floods spread across western Shandong and threatened the Grand Canal and the salt works that paid for the state.
The repair. In 1351 Jia Lu was given 150,000 laborers and 20,000 soldiers to force the river back into its old bed.
Success and danger. The work finished within the year, but it gathered many hungry men in one region.

This slide gives you the third cause in Review Question 11: environmental crisis. The Yellow River carries so much silt that it builds up its own bed and tends to break out of its banks. Dikes held it in place, but they needed constant care.

In 1344, after weeks of rain, the river broke the dikes and poured north and east. A zhang was about three meters, so the report describes water over six meters deep. Whole districts were flooded for years, and the Grand Canal, which carried southern grain to the capital, was put at risk. There were also droughts, famines and epidemics in the 1340s and 1350s, recorded across many regions. Historians debate their exact causes and how they were connected, so avoid naming one single disease or climate event.

Toghto took on the problem. His engineer Jia Lu chose the most ambitious plan, to send the river back to its southern course. The Yuan shi reports 150,000 laborers and 20,000 soldiers. The project worked: by late 1351 the river was back in its old bed. But a huge labor draft in a hungry region was risky, and rebellion broke out nearby that same year.

The map shows many courses over many centuries. Use it to see how far the river could move, not as a precise record of 1344.

Why this matters: Q11 lists environmental crises separately from economic and political ones. The floods were a cause of their own, and the government's response linked them to the rebellion.

Check yourself: Was the 1351 river project a failure? Explain why the answer is more complicated than yes or no.

Q11 story · Rebellion · 1351–1360s

The Red Turbans Rose, and Rival Warlords Divided Central and South China

A religious spark. In 1351 Han Shantong and Liu Futong led a rising that preached the coming of Maitreya, the future Buddha.
A Song revival. The rebels wore red headcloths and claimed Han Shantong descended from the Song emperors.
Do not say the stone man has only one eye; once he stirs the Yellow River, the realm will rebel.Rhyme that later writers said was spread before a one-eyed stone figure was "found" by the river workers in 1351. Translated for this deck.
Warlords. Others carved out their own states, among them Zhang Shicheng, a salt trader, Fang Guozhen, a sea raider, and Chen Youliang.
A lost chance. In 1354 Toghto was besieging Zhang Shicheng at Gaoyou when rivals at court had him dismissed, and his army broke up.
×

Maitreya and the White Lotus

Maitreya is the Buddha of the future. Some popular Buddhist groups, often linked with the White Lotus tradition, taught that his arrival would end a corrupt age and bring a world of peace.

The Red Turbans mixed this hope with Manichaean ideas of a "Prince of Light" and with loyalty to the Song. The Yuan had banned such groups, which made them natural centers of resistance.

This slide gives you the event that turned the crises into a collapse. In 1351, as the river workers labored, Han Shantong and Liu Futong launched a rebellion in the Huai River region. Han was captured and killed early, but the movement spread. In 1355 its leaders proclaimed Han's son, Han Lin'er, emperor of a revived Song dynasty.

The rhyme about the one-eyed stone man is a famous story. Later writers said rebels buried the statue where the workers would dig, so that "finding" it would seem to fulfill a prophecy. Treat it as a report: it may be true, or it may be a story that grew later.

Not every rebel was a Red Turban. Fang Guozhen raided the coast from 1348 and threatened the sea route that brought rice to the capital. Zhang Shicheng, a salt smuggler and trader, took Gaoyou in 1353 and later ruled from Suzhou. Chen Youliang built a state on the middle Yangzi.

The Gaoyou story shows how the political cause made things worse. The Yuan shi reports that Toghto's huge army was close to victory when court rivals persuaded the emperor to dismiss him. The army scattered, and many soldiers joined the rebels.

Why this matters: Q11 asks for a combination. Here you can see all three causes meet: floods and labor drafts, inflation that ruined trust, and a court that removed its own best general.

Check yourself: Name two rebel leaders who were not Red Turbans, and say what each controlled.

Q11 story · From orphan to emperor · 1328–1368

Zhu Yuanzhang Rose from a Poor Orphan to Found the Ming

1344Famine and disease kill most of Zhu's family, and he enters a Buddhist temple as a novice.
1352He joins a Red Turban army under Guo Zixing at Haozhou.
1356He captures Nanjing and makes it his base.
1363He defeats Chen Youliang in a great battle on Poyang Lake.
1367He defeats Zhang Shicheng at Suzhou.
1368He proclaims the Ming dynasty, his general Xu Da takes Dadu, and Toghon Temür flees north.

This slide gives you the man who ended Yuan rule in China. Zhu Yuanzhang was born in 1328 to a poor farming family in the Huai region. In 1344, the same year as the great flood, famine and epidemic struck his home, and his parents and most of his family died. He became a novice in a Buddhist temple and spent years begging on the roads.

In 1352 he joined the Red Turban army of Guo Zixing and rose quickly. From his base at Nanjing he defeated his rivals one by one: Chen Youliang on Poyang Lake in 1363 and Zhang Shicheng in 1367. In early 1368 he proclaimed the Ming dynasty, with the reign name Hongwu.

Later that year his general Xu Da marched north. Toghon Temür fled Dadu with his court to the steppe, and Ming troops entered the city. The Yuan court survived in Mongolia, which later historians call the Northern Yuan. The loss of Dadu marks the conventional end of the Yuan dynasty in China, although Yuan forces held out in places such as Yunnan until 1381.

The portrait is a formal court image. Other Ming portraits show Zhu with a long, jutting jaw and a pitted face. No one knows which is closer to the truth, so do not use either as a photograph.

Why this matters: Zhu's story connects every cause on the last four slides. Famine and epidemic devastated Zhu's family; rebellion later provided his route into military leadership. And a divided Yuan court could not stop him.

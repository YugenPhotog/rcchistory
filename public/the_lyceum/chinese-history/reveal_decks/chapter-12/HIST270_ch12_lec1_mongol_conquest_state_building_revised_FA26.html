Welcome! This deck is your Study Buddy for the Mongol conquest of China. It is built for you to work through on your own, at your own pace, as many times as you like. There is no clock running.

Here is the big story in one breath. In 1206 a steppe leader named Temüjin became Chinggis Khan (you may know him as Genghis Khan). His riders tore into North China in 1211. Seventy years later, in 1279, his grandson Khubilai Khan ruled all of China as emperor of a Chinese-style dynasty called the Yuan. Conquerors who started out burning cities ended up running tax offices, building a capital and holding Chinese court rituals.

Paiza (travel pass)

A paiza was a metal tablet that Mongol rulers gave to officials and envoys. Showing it let the holder demand horses, food and lodging at relay stations along the road.

Khan and Great Khan

A khan is a ruler of steppe peoples. A Great Khan (qaghan) is the supreme ruler over many khans and tribes.

"Chinggis" probably means something like "fierce" or "oceanic, universal." Scholars still debate the exact sense.

×

Keshig (the khan's guard)

The keshig was the khan's personal bodyguard, which grew to about 10,000 men. Guardsmen protected the khan's tents, but they also served as cooks, scribes, quiver-bearers and stewards.

Sons of leading families served in it, so it doubled as a training school for future generals and governors, and as a way to hold those families' loyalty.

This slide gives you the man behind the name. Temüjin was born around 1162 on the grasslands of Mongolia. When he was about nine, enemies from the Tatar people poisoned his father. The clan's followers rode away and left the family to live on roots and fish. As a teenager he was seized by a rival clan and escaped at night while still locked in a heavy wooden collar.

Those hardships matter for the story. A boy abandoned by his own clan learned that kinship alone did not guarantee loyalty. As a leader he rewarded people for service, and he broke up old tribes and mixed their men into new units. That made his followers loyal to him personally. In 1206 a great assembly by the Onon River named him Chinggis Khan, ruler of all the peoples who lived in felt tents.

Most of what we know about his early life comes from The Secret History of the Mongols, a Mongol account written down in the thirteenth century. It is dramatic and loyal to his family, so read it as a heroic story with real history inside it. (There is a whole Pierian Spring slide on it at the end.)

The portrait was painted for the Yuan court after Chinggis died, so the artist never saw him. Think of it as an official image, like a family's portrait of a respected ancestor.

Why this matters: Chinggis built a state around loyalty to himself and his family, not around cities or written laws. That is a very different starting point from a Chinese dynasty.

AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source. A Mongol horse archer at the gallop.

×

Decimal units

Mongol soldiers were grouped by tens: an arban (10), a jaghun (100), a mingghan (1,000) and a tümen (10,000).

Because Chinggis often put men from different tribes in the same unit, a soldier's loyalty ran up the chain of command to the khan, not back to his old chief.

This slide gives you the reasons the Mongol army won so often, even against states with far more people.

Remounts are spare horses. Steppe horses were small and tough and could live on grass, so an army did not need long supply wagons. With several horses per rider, a Mongol force could cover distances that astonished its enemies.

The feigned retreat is a fake escape. It only works with strict discipline: if riders really panicked, the trick would turn into a real rout. Discipline came from the decimal units, where every ten men answered for each other. Because Chinggis mixed men from different tribes in the same unit, their loyalty ran to him, not to an old chief.

Terror mattered too. Cities that resisted were often destroyed, and that reputation made other cities surrender without a fight.

Why this matters: this army was superb at winning battles in open country. Walled cities, rivers and taxes would be different problems, and that is where the story goes next.

Q1 story · North China · 1211–1219

Mongol Armies Crushed the Jin in the Field in 1211 and Took Their Capital, Zhongdu, in 1215

1211. At Yehuling pass, Mongol cavalry shattered a large Jin frontier army.
1213–1214. Riders looted the North China Plain; the Jin bought a truce.
1214–1215. The Jin court fled south to Kaifeng, and Zhongdu was burned.
1217–1219. General Muqali took over in China; Chinggis turned west.

Not just plunder. Chinggis already placed darughachi overseers over some conquered towns.

This slide gives you the first Mongol war in China, against the Jin dynasty. The Jin were Jurchens, a people from Manchuria who had conquered North China about a century earlier. The Song dynasty still ruled the south.

On paper the Jin should have won. Your textbook gives Chinggis about 50,000 bowmen, plus a similar army under his sons, against some 150,000 Jin cavalry and more than 300,000 Chinese infantry. Yet Mongol tactics frustrated them. The Mongols would take a city, plunder it and withdraw, leaving the Jin to deal with the hunger and ruin. When the Jin emperor moved his court south to Kaifeng in 1214, Chinggis treated it as a broken promise. The capital, Zhongdu (modern Beijing), fell in 1215 and was sacked and burned.

Early campaigns emphasized plunder and submission, but they were not empty of government. Chinggis already placed darughachi overseers over some towns, and from 1217 his general Muqali ran both the war and the Mongol-held parts of North China, using Khitan and Chinese officials. In 1219 Chinggis himself rode west against the Khwarazmian Empire in Central Asia.

The painting shows Zhongdu with bright blue walls, which tells you more about Persian painting style than about Beijing. Still, it shows the key problem: horsemen could not ride over a city wall. Sieges took months, so the Mongols recruited Chinese and Muslim engineers who knew how to break walls.

Textbook note: Ebrey (p. 199) says these armies "only plundered." Source beyond Ebrey for the darughachi and Muqali: Allsen, Cambridge History of China 6, pp. 357–58, 373.

Why this matters: keep this "before" picture in mind as a matter of degree. Later rulers made administration thicker and more systematic.

Q1 story · The Tangut Xia · 1209–1227

The Tangut Xia Kingdom Submitted in 1209, Defied Chinggis, and Was Wiped Out in 1227

1209. After a botched Mongol flood attack, Xia submitted and sent a princess.
1219. Asked for troops, a Xia minister mocked the khan's strength.
1226–1227. Chinggis destroyed Xia city by city and died during the siege.

This slide gives you the fate of the Xia, also called the Western Xia or Tangut kingdom. The Tanguts ruled a rich corridor west of the Jin, along the trade routes into Central Asia. Find Western Xia on the left side of the map.

In 1209 the Mongols tried to flood the Xia capital by damming the Yellow River, but the dike burst and soaked their own camp. Xia submitted anyway. In 1219, when Chinggis demanded troops for his Central Asian war, a Xia minister mocked him: if his army was too weak, why call himself khan? That story comes from The Secret History of the Mongols. Whether or not the words were exact, the point is clear: submission meant permanent obedience, including sending soldiers. Refusing was treated as rebellion.

Chinggis came back in 1226. When Xia soldiers fought well, it only enraged him, and his generals destroyed Xia city by city. He died during the final siege in August 1227. Nobody knows the cause; the sources disagree, so be cautious with any confident claim. The Xia ruler who surrendered was put to death.

Why this matters: Xia shows early Mongol rule in action. Submit and obey, or be destroyed.

Why the census gap misleads

A census counts only the households a government can find and tax. After 1211, millions of people in North China fled to the Song south, hid in the hills, or became captives and private servants of Mongol nobles, who did not report them.

So the gap between the two counts mixes deaths with people who simply disappeared from the records. Historians agree the losses were enormous, from killing, famine, disease and flight, but they do not agree on how many died.

This slide gives you the human cost of the early wars, and it asks you to think like a historian about numbers.

Start with the poem. Yuan Haowen was one of the great poets of the Jin dynasty. In 1233 the Jin capital Kaifeng surrendered, and he and other officials were taken north across the Yellow River as captives. His poem is a real eyewitness voice, full of grief. (Your textbook prints a different translation of a poem from the same days; compare them if you like.) Notice the twist at the end: after saying everyone is dead, he sees smoke from a few homes. Even the poet admits that his first line is not literally true.

Your textbook describes these years bluntly: Mongol armies did not try to control territory, they plundered it, and sometimes slaughtered or enslaved whole towns. Now the numbers. The drop from about 8.4 million households (roughly 45 to 53 million people) to under 2 million is shocking, and you will sometimes see it quoted as a death count. It is not. A census is a list of people the state can find and tax. War destroyed the record keepers, sent people fleeing, and turned many into captives of Mongol nobles.

Why this matters: the damage was so great that it hurt the conquerors too. Empty farms pay no taxes. That problem pushes the story toward administration.

×

Yam (relay stations)

The yam was a chain of relay stations with fresh horses, food and shelter. Envoys and messengers carrying a paiza could ride from station to station across the empire.

Nearby households had to supply the horses and food, which made the yam a heavy burden on local people.

This slide gives you the first big step from raiding toward ruling. Chinggis died in 1227. His son Ögödei was chosen Great Khan in 1229 and sent the main Mongol army to finish the Jin. The last Jin emperor killed himself at Caizhou in 1234 as Mongol and Song troops broke in.

A census is a count of households, made so they can be taxed and drafted. A permanent walled capital, a census and a network of relay stations are signs that a ruler means to hold land for the long term, not just loot it and leave. Think of it as a change of degree, not a sudden switch from "no government" to "government."

The portrait comes from the same Yuan court album as the Chinggis portrait. It is an official likeness made for the dynasty's ancestral rites, not a sketch from life.

Why this matters: conquered land is worth more if its people stay alive and keep paying. Ögödei's reign is where that idea starts to show up in policy.

×

Darughachi (overseer)

A darughachi (the word comes from "to press a seal") was an official the khan placed over a conquered town or region. Chinggis already used them; his heirs used them more widely and more routinely.

He watched local leaders, oversaw the census and taxes, and reported back. Local people did the daily work of government, but the darughachi made sure the khan got his share.

×

Tamma (garrison armies)

A tamma army was a permanent garrison force made of soldiers drawn from many units and peoples. It was posted on the edge of newly conquered land to keep it under control.

A raiding army leaves after the loot is taken. A tamma army stays, which shows the Mongols now meant to hold the land.

This slide gives you the next stage. Ögödei's nephew Möngke became Great Khan in 1251. He ordered a new, stricter census in 1252 and pulled tax collection under the Great Khan's own officials, so that princes and local strongmen could not simply keep what they collected.

Two tools held the conquered land more systematically. Darughachi were resident overseers, already used under Chinggis, who watched local leaders and made sure the khan got his share. Tamma armies were garrison forces posted to stay. A raiding army leaves after the loot is taken; a garrison stays, which tells you the Mongols now meant to keep the land.

Be careful with one idea. Ruling farmers did not mean becoming farmers. The khans still moved their courts with the seasons, kept their herds and held steppe assemblies. They governed settled people without giving up their own way of life.

The painting was made in Shiraz, Iran, almost two centuries later for a Persian history of the Mongols. It shows how Persian readers pictured the Great Khan's court.

Why this matters: Möngke also handed North China to his brother Khubilai, who becomes the hero of the Q3 story.

Yelü Chucai (耶律楚材)

Yelü is the family name of the Khitan royal clan that ruled the Liao dynasty (907–1125) before the Jin conquered it. Your textbook spells his name Yelü Qucai; it is the same person.

The Khitans were a people from the Mongolian and Manchurian borderlands. By Yelü's time many Khitan families had lived among Chinese for generations and served the Jin as officials.

This slide gives you the hero of the next part of the story. Yelü Chucai (your textbook spells it Yelü Qucai) was born in 1190 in Zhongdu, the Jin capital. His family were Khitans, descended from the royal house of the Liao dynasty, which the Jin had destroyed. He was raised on Chinese classics, astronomy and Buddhism.

When the Mongols took Zhongdu in 1215 he was a young Jin official. In 1218 Chinggis summoned him and expected gratitude: the Jin had destroyed Yelü's ancestors' dynasty, and now the Mongols had crushed the Jin. Yelü's answer was a surprise. He said he could not call his own former ruler an enemy. Chinggis admired that loyalty and kept him close. Your textbook quotes the same exchange.

The calligraphy is real and in his own hand, written in 1240 near the end of his career. Big, confident brushwork like this was a mark of a cultured Chinese scholar.

A caution: most of what we know about Yelü comes from a memorial inscription written for his family after he died, which later became his official biography. It makes him look as wise and influential as possible.

Why this matters: Yelü stood between two worlds. He understood Mongol power and Chinese government, and he tried to persuade the khans that the second could serve the first.

Taels, bolts and shi

A tael (liang) was a weight of silver, a little over an ounce. A bolt was a standard length of silk cloth, which worked almost like money. A shi was a large measure of grain, roughly a big sack's worth.

You do not need exact conversions. The point is that a taxed farming population could produce a steady, enormous income every single year.

This slide gives you the most famous argument of the whole period. It happened around 1230, soon after Ögödei became Great Khan. Your textbook tells it this way: Ögödei's Mongol advisers proposed turning much of North China into pastureland, and Yelü answered that the farmers should stay because "great wealth could be extracted from them through equitably collected taxation." The quotations here come from Yelü's later official biography, a flattering source written to honor him, so it may sharpen the drama.

Yelü answered in the khan's own language: income. He did not say "killing farmers is wrong." He said "living farmers will pay you, every year." That is a clever move. He turned a moral argument into a practical one that a conqueror could accept. (Your textbook, p. 199, converts his grain figure to more than 20,000 tons; the biography gives it as 400,000 shi. Same promise, different units.)

Ögödei's answer was short: try it. The test is on the next slide.

Why this matters: this debate is a window into the Mongol choice between treating China as a place to plunder and treating it as a place to govern.

Q2 story · The test · 1230–1231

Yelü's Tax Offices Delivered the Promised Silver in 1231, and Ögödei Put Him in Charge of the Secretariat

1230. Yelü set up ten tax bureaus across North China.
Scholars, not soldiers. He staffed them with Confucian-educated former Jin officials.
1231. The registers, silver and silk were laid out before a delighted khan.
You never left my side, yet you have made the state's revenues sufficient.Ögödei to Yelü in 1231, from the Yuan shi. He then named Yelü head of the Secretariat.

This slide gives you the result of Yelü's experiment. He created ten tax bureaus, offices that collected regular taxes in each region, starting at Yanjing (the old Zhongdu). He staffed them with Confucian scholars, many of them former Jin officials who had lost everything in the war. That gave educated Chinese a reason to work with the new rulers, and it put people who knew how to keep registers in charge of the registers.

In 1231 the results were displayed for Ögödei at Yunzhong (near modern Datong). The khan was thrilled and made Yelü head of the Secretariat, the chief office for running civil government. It sounds like a total victory, but notice that Mongol princes and generals still held most real power.

Your textbook adds a warning: Yelü's plan was approved, "but before it had much chance to show its benefits," rivals offered the khan something even more tempting. Hold that thought for two slides.

Why this matters: for a few years, Chinese-style regular taxation looked like the winning plan.

This slide gives you the moment Yelü's money argument saved a city. Kaifeng was the last great Jin capital, and it had held out against the Mongols for months. By Mongol custom, a city that resisted was massacred. The general Subutai wanted exactly that.

Yelü made the same argument he had made about pasture: people are what make land valuable. Ögödei agreed and limited the punishment to the Jin royal clan. Your textbook adds a related detail: the poet Yuan Haowen, whom you met on slide H, wrote to Yelü asking that fifty-four men of letters be spared.

The skeptic line matters. Yelü's biography was based on an inscription written to honor him, so it may give him credit that others shared. Read the figure of 1,470,000 people saved as a sign of how huge the city was, not as a careful count.

The photo shows Kaifeng's Iron Pagoda, a Buddhist tower from 1049. It is not really iron; its brown glazed tiles only look rusty. It was standing when the Mongols arrived, and it is still there.

Why this matters: this is the Q2 argument in action. A conqueror who expects to tax a city has a reason not to destroy it.

This slide gives you the competing plan that beat Yelü's. Yelü's taxes worked, but they had limits: fair, fixed rates meant a steady income, not a huge one. A merchant named Abd al-Rahman, a Central Asian Muslim, offered 2.2 million taels of silver, double the existing income, for the right to collect North China's taxes himself.

Your textbook describes the same move: Yelü's rivals convinced Ögödei that a more lucrative way to raise revenue was to let Central Asian Muslim merchants bid against each other for licenses to collect taxes. Yelü wept and argued that collectors would squeeze the farmers to make their profit. According to his biography, Ögödei snapped, "Do you want to fight over this?" and then, "Let it be tried."

Notice why the bid was tempting: the khan got a big, guaranteed sum with no effort. The cost fell on the farmers. To Chinese subjects, your textbook says, these tax farmers seemed even more oppressive than the Mongol lords. Remember, though, that the Mongol rulers made the choice.

×

Tax farming

Tax farming means contracting for the right to collect specified revenues. The bidder pays the ruler a fixed sum up front, then collects from the people and keeps whatever is left after covering that payment.

The ruler gets money fast with little effort. Collectors often squeeze the people harder, because their profit depends on taking more than they paid.

×

Ortogh (merchant partnerships)

An ortogh (from a Turkic word for "partner") was a merchant association, often of Central Asian Muslims or Uighurs, that pooled capital from the court, princes or other investors.

Ortogh partners shared commercial risk on long-distance trade and finance across Eurasia. They also sometimes collected revenues for their patrons. High-interest lending, including the crushing "lamb's interest" named in Chinese sources, was one part of that wider business, not the whole of it.

×

Appanage (princely grant)

An appanage was an allocation of households or revenues to a member of the ruling elite, such as a prince or general. The 1236 grants in North China are a famous example.

Appanages are not the same thing as tax farming or as ortogh partnerships. They gave elite holders a claim on local people and income, which complicated any plan for a single state tax system.

This slide gives you the second half of Review Question 2: the "alternative revenue-generating methods" that competed with Yelü's vision. Keep the three ideas separate, because they are easy to blur.

Tax farming is a contract. A bidder pays the ruler a fixed sum up front, then collects from the people and keeps whatever he can take beyond that. Ortogh (from a Turkic word for "partner") were merchant partnerships, often of Central Asian Muslims or Uighurs, who used capital from the court and princes for long-distance trade, high-interest loans and sometimes tax collection. Appanages were grants of households or revenues to members of the ruling family and top generals, like the big grants Ögödei made in North China in 1236.

All three pulled money away from a single state tax system run by trained officials, which was Yelü's plan. He won a compromise that let state officials collect some taxes from appanage lands, but the princes kept pressing for more. After Ögödei died in 1241, Abd al-Rahman ran North China's finances, and Yelü died, sidelined, in 1244.

The ingot is real Yuan-era silver. Mongol rulers loved silver, and that is a big part of why merchants who promised it had their ear.

Why this matters: several ways of making money from China were competing, and the steady Chinese-style option was not the most tempting one.

Q3 story · The dynasty · 1264–1272

In 1271 Khubilai Named His Dynasty Yuan, an Idea from the Book of Changes, and Ruled from a New Capital, Dadu

A new kind of name. Yuan means "primal origin," an idea, not a place.
A new capital. Dadu, "Great Capital," rose where Beijing stands today.
×

Yuan (元), "origin"

The name comes from the Book of Changes (Yijing), a classic of Chinese thought. Its first line praises qianyuan (乾元), the creative power of Heaven: "Great is qianyuan! All things owe their beginning to it."

So "Great Yuan" claimed that Khubilai's rule was as universal as Heaven itself. It also avoided naming the dynasty after the Mongols' homeland, which made it easier for Chinese subjects to accept.

×

Dadu (大都), the Great Capital

Dadu was the Yuan winter capital, on the site of modern Beijing. Liu Bingzhong planned it on a grid following an ancient Chinese ideal of a royal city, with the palace in the center, the ancestral temple to the east, and markets to the north.

In summer the court moved north to Shangdu on the steppe, so the emperor lived part of each year in each world.

This slide gives you the moment the Mongol state became a Chinese dynasty in name. In December 1271 Khubilai issued an edict giving his state a dynastic name, Da Yuan, "Great Yuan." Your textbook puts it this way: he adopted the Chinese name Yuan ("primal"), "casting it as a dynasty to the Chinese," and explained the choice with a passage from the ancient Book of Changes.

Why was that clever? Earlier dynasties took their names from places: Qin and Han from the regions where they first rose. Yuan came from a Chinese classic and meant "origin." That told educated Chinese that this ruler belonged in the line of legitimate dynasties. The Mandate of Heaven, the old idea that Heaven grants the right to rule, now covered a Mongol.

At the same time Khubilai built Dadu, his Great Capital, where Beijing is today. Your textbook dates the decision to 1264; work on the new walled city began in 1267 (Rossabi, Cambridge History of China 6, pp. 454, 458, source beyond Ebrey). Liu Bingzhong planned it. The city was Chinese in layout, but its palace grounds had room for Mongol tents, and the court still spent its summers on the steppe at Shangdu.

The court scene is AI-generated. Notice how it mixes Mongol, Tibetan and Chinese figures, because Khubilai's court really was that mixed.

Why this matters: names and capitals are claims to legitimacy. Khubilai used Chinese ideas to tell Chinese subjects that he was a proper emperor.

Phags-pa script

In 1269 Khubilai ordered a new alphabet, designed by the Tibetan Buddhist lama Phags-pa, his imperial preceptor. It was based on Tibetan letters and was meant to write Mongolian, Chinese and other languages of the empire.

It appears on seals, coins, paper money and passes like the paiza at the start of this deck. Most people kept using Chinese characters, and the script faded after the Yuan fell.

×

Can we trust Marco Polo?

Partly. Polo's book was written in prison in Genoa with a writer of romances, Rustichello of Pisa, and it mixes accurate details about Yuan China with boasting and borrowed stories.

No Chinese record mentions him, and he leaves out things like tea drinking and foot binding. Most scholars think he did reach China, but they read his claims, especially about his own importance, with care. The Pierian Spring section has a whole slide on him.

This slide gives you the double life of Khubilai Khan. To his Chinese subjects he was the Son of Heaven, an emperor with a dynastic name, Chinese-style ministries and Confucian rituals. Your textbook adds that the Yuan kept the old Chinese counties and prefectures and added a new, powerful level above them, the province. To Mongols he was the Great Khan, a descendant of Chinggis who hunted on horseback, kept Mongol law for Mongols and spent every summer at Shangdu.

The painting shows this. It was painted in 1280 by Liu Guandao, a Chinese court artist, in fine Chinese silk-painting style. But the subject is a Mongol hunt, with Khubilai in white furs among bows and hunting animals. A Chinese art form celebrating Mongol life: that is the Yuan court in one image.

The Phags-pa script is another example. In 1269 Khubilai decreed that it be used for government documents, so one alphabet could write Mongolian, Chinese and other languages. It never caught on. Source beyond Ebrey: Endicott-West, CHC 6 (1994), p. 605.

Marco Polo's description is probably fairly accurate, since Khubilai's official portrait fits it. But open the popup before trusting Polo. You will meet one of his tallest tales in the Xiangyang story.

Why this matters: Khubilai did not simply "become Chinese." He used Chinese traditions where they helped him and kept Mongol ways where they mattered to his power.

Xiangyang and its twin city Fancheng faced each other across the Han River in modern Hubei. Thick walls, a wide moat and the river itself made them the strongest fortress on the Song frontier.

×

Thunder-crash bombs

Gunpowder was a Chinese invention. By the 1200s, Jin and Song armies had bombs with iron or ceramic casings that burst with a deafening bang and threw out fragments and fire.

They were thrown by hand or launched by catapult. The Mongols learned to use them from Chinese experts and carried them as far as Japan.

This slide gives you the longest siege of the war. Xiangyang and Fancheng sat on opposite banks of the Han River. As long as Song boats could carry food and soldiers along the river, the cities could hold out. Your textbook says both sides saw the city as the key to the Yangzi, so the siege lasted five years, with thousands of boats and tens of thousands of troops on each side.

So the Mongols did something new: they built a navy. With Chinese shipbuilders, Chinese sailors and the strategy of the Song defector Liu Zheng, they closed the river with fleets, forts and chains. Yuan records claim about 5,000 boats and 70,000 sailors and marines. Those figures may be rounded up, but the point stands: steppe horsemen became a river power.

The 1272 breakthrough is one of the great stories of the siege. Two officers, Zhang Shun and Zhang Gui, ran the blockade with about a hundred paddle boats and 3,000 men, cutting through chains under fire. Zhang Shun was killed, and his body was later found floating in the river, still in armor. Their courage brought supplies in, but it did not break the siege.

Why this matters: by the 1260s the Mongols could fight on water as well as on land, using Chinese technology and Chinese crews. That changed what they could conquer.

Counterweight trebuchet

A trebuchet is a giant throwing arm on a pivot. Chinese armies had long used traction trebuchets, pulled down by teams of people hauling on ropes.

A counterweight trebuchet uses a huge box of earth or stones instead. When the box drops, the arm whips up and throws a heavier stone farther and more accurately than human muscle could. It was well known in the Middle East but new to China.

This slide gives you the weapon that ended the siege. After years of stalemate, Khubilai reached across his family's empire. His relative Abaqa ruled the Ilkhanate, the Mongol state in Iran, and sent two Muslim engineers, Ismail and Ala al-Din, whose machines were among the most advanced in the world.

The key idea is the counterweight. Instead of a crew pulling ropes, a falling weight powers the throw. That meant heavier stones hitting the same spot again and again. How heavy? Your textbook says "up to a hundred pounds"; the Yuan shi says 150 jin, closer to 90 kilograms. Sources often disagree on numbers like this, which is why the slide says "a hundred pounds or more." The engineers first battered Fancheng, which fell early in 1273. Then a machine was set up against Xiangyang, and after a few shots the Song commander Lü Wenhuan surrendered in March 1273. He then joined the Yuan side and helped persuade other Song cities to give up.

The quotation comes from the official Yuan shi. Treat it as the court's dramatic report of the machine's psychological impact, not as an engineering measurement. The lower picture is a real Persian painting made in Mongol-ruled Iran around 1306 to 1314. It shows what this technology looked like to artists in the engineers' own world.

About Marco Polo: his book says the Polos had stone-throwers built for Xiangyang. Chinese and Persian sources name Muslim engineers, and the dates show the siege was over before the Polos reached China. It is a good example of Polo making himself the hero.

Why this matters: the Mongol empire connected Iran and China. Technology from one end of Eurasia decided a battle at the other end.

Pierian Spring · The Mongols' own story

The Secret History of the Mongols Is the Only Account of Chinggis Written by Mongols

Written down in the 1200s, probably within a generation of Chinggis's death.
Survived only as Chinese characters spelling out the Mongolian sounds.
Honest about flaws. It even tells how young Temüjin killed his half-brother.
Still a family story. It praises Chinggis's line and its heroes.

This slide gives you the most important Mongol source for the life of Chinggis Khan, the one behind the stories on slides D and G. The Secret History of the Mongols was written in Mongolian in the thirteenth century, probably within a generation of Chinggis's death. Scholars still argue over the exact date.

Its survival is a story in itself. The original Mongolian text in Uighur-style script was lost. What survived was a version made in early Ming China, in which Chinese characters were used to spell out the Mongolian sounds, with a Chinese translation alongside. Scholars rebuilt the Mongolian from that. Imagine reading English written phonetically in Chinese characters, and you get a sense of the puzzle.

What makes it special is its frankness. It tells how the boy Temüjin and his brother shot their half-brother over a stolen fish and how furious their mother was. An official court history would usually hide something like that. But it is still a story told by and for Chinggis's family, so heroes are heroic and enemies are wicked.

The picture is AI-generated. It imagines how a story told from memory might have been written down. No image of the original writing survives.

Why this matters: most of what we know about the Mongols comes from the people they conquered: Chinese, Persians, Russians. The Secret History lets the Mongols speak for themselves.

Pierian Spring · A traveler's tale

Marco Polo Made Khubilai Famous in Europe, but His Book Mixes Real Detail with Boasting

c. 1275–1292. Polo says he served Khubilai for seventeen years.
Accurate details. Paper money, the relay roads and Hangzhou's size check out.
Strange gaps. No tea, no foot binding, and no Chinese record of him.
The verdict. Most historians think he went, but exaggerated his role.

This slide gives you the most famous European witness to Khubilai's China, and a lesson in how to judge a source. Marco Polo was a young Venetian who traveled east with his father and uncle and said he served Khubilai from about 1275 to 1292. Back in Europe he was captured in a war with Genoa, and in prison he told his story to Rustichello of Pisa, a writer of romances. That partnership explains a lot of the book's style.

Much of it checks out against Chinese sources: paper money, the relay-station roads, the huge size of Hangzhou, the design of Khubilai's capitals. But there are odd gaps. He never mentions tea, foot binding or Chinese writing, and no Chinese record names him. Some writers have even argued that he never went at all. Most historians think he did, because he knows details that he could hardly have invented, but that he inflated his own importance. You saw one example on slide Z, where he claims credit for the Xiangyang trebuchets.

The painting was made in France about two centuries later. The artist had never seen China, so Khubilai looks like a European king. That is evidence of how Europeans imagined the khan, not of what his court looked like.

Why this matters: a source can be partly reliable. The historian's job is to sort the parts, not to accept or reject the whole thing.

Pierian Spring · The limits of conquest · 1274 and 1281

Khubilai Twice Sent Fleets Against Japan, and Both Invasions Failed

1274

A Mongol, Chinese and Korean force landed in Kyushu, then withdrew.

1281

A much larger fleet, many ships from the former Song, was wrecked by a typhoon.

Kamikaze

Japanese later called the storms the "divine wind."

This slide gives you the place where the Mongol war machine finally met its limit: the sea between Korea and Japan. Khubilai demanded that Japan submit, and when it refused, he sent two invasions.

In 1274 a force of Mongol, Chinese and Korean troops landed in northern Kyushu. You saw a scene from that year on slide AA, with a bomb bursting over a samurai. After a day of fighting the invaders returned to their ships and sailed away; a storm may have struck the fleet. In 1281, after conquering the Song, Khubilai sent a much bigger invasion, including a huge fleet built in former Song territory. For weeks the Japanese held a defensive wall along the shore and raided the ships at night in small boats, like the scene above. Then a typhoon destroyed much of the fleet.

The Japanese came to call these storms kamikaze, "divine wind," a sign that the gods protected Japan. Underwater archaeologists studying wrecks off Takashima have suggested that some Yuan ships were river boats pressed into ocean service, which may have made them easier for the storm to break.

The scroll is real. Takezaki Suenaga, a samurai who fought in both invasions, had it painted to prove his deeds so he could claim a reward from the shogun's government.

Why this matters: the navy that won at Xiangyang and Yashan was built for rivers and coasts. The open ocean was a different problem.

Pierian Spring · An alphabet for an empire · 1269

Khubilai Ordered One New Alphabet to Write Every Language of His Empire

The designer. The Tibetan lama Phags-pa, Khubilai's imperial preceptor.
The model. Letters based on Tibetan, written top to bottom like Mongolian.
The goal. One script for Mongolian, Chinese and other languages.
The result. Used on seals, coins and passes, then faded after 1368.

This slide gives you one of Khubilai's boldest ideas. In 1269 he ordered a new writing system designed by Phags-pa, a Tibetan Buddhist lama who served as his imperial preceptor, the emperor's chief religious teacher. You have already seen the script twice: on the paiza travel pass on slide C, and in the popup on slide V.

Chinese characters stand for whole words or syllables. Phags-pa is an alphabet: its letters stand for sounds. It was based on Tibetan letters but written in vertical columns, like Mongolian. Khubilai wanted one script that could write Mongolian, Chinese, Tibetan and other languages of his empire, so that edicts, seals and money could look the same everywhere.

It was used officially on seals like this one, on paper money, coins and passes. But most people kept writing their own languages in their own scripts, and Phags-pa faded after the Yuan fell in 1368. Some scholars think it may have influenced the design of the Korean alphabet, hangul, in the 1440s, though that idea is debated.

The seal is a real Yuan object. A seal like this was pressed in red ink onto documents to prove they were genuine.

Why this matters: Khubilai thought of himself as ruler of a whole Eurasian empire, not only of China. Phags-pa was his attempt to give that empire one written face.

Background essay: the historians' debates behind this lecture ↗

This slide gives you the sources behind the stories. The quotations come from real historical texts. The Yuan shi is the official history of the Yuan dynasty, written by Ming-dynasty scholars soon after the Yuan fell, using Yuan records. Like any official history, it has a point of view, which is why this deck flags numbers that look inflated.

If you want to go deeper, Morris Rossabi's biography of Khubilai is very readable, and Tonio Andrade's book is great on gunpowder and the siege of Xiangyang. The background essay linked at the bottom of this slide explains what historians still argue about: how "Chinese" Khubilai became, how far to trust Yelü Chucai's biography, and how big the death toll in North China really was.

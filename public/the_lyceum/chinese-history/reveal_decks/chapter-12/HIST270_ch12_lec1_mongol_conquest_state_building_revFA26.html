Here is the big story in one breath. In 1206 a steppe leader named Temüjin became Chinggis Khan (you may know him as Genghis Khan). His riders tore into North China in 1211. Seventy years later, in 1279, his grandson Khubilai Khan ruled all of China as emperor of a Chinese-style dynasty called the Yuan. Conquerors who started out burning cities ended up running tax offices, building a capital, and holding Chinese court rituals.

Q1 story · The steppe · c. 1162–1206

Temüjin Survived a Brutal Steppe Youth and Was Proclaimed Chinggis Khan in 1206

Abandoned. When Temüjin was about nine, enemies from the Tatar people poisoned his father. The clan's followers rode away and left the family to live on roots and fish.
Captured. As a teenager he was seized by a rival clan and escaped at night while still locked in a heavy wooden collar.
Rising. He won followers by sharing loot and rewarding loyalty instead of noble birth, and he turned on old allies who stood in his way.
Proclaimed. In 1206 a great assembly by the Onon River named him Chinggis Khan, ruler of all the peoples who lived in felt tents. His personal guard, the keshig, became the core of his government.
×

Khan and Great Khan

A khan is a ruler of steppe peoples. A Great Khan (qaghan) is the supreme ruler over many khans and tribes.

"Chinggis" probably means something like "fierce" or "oceanic, universal." Scholars still debate the exact sense.

×

Keshig (the khan's guard)

The keshig was the khan's personal bodyguard, which grew to about 10,000 men. Guardsmen protected the khan's tents, but they also served as cooks, scribes, quiver-bearers and stewards.

Sons of leading families served in it, so it doubled as a training school for future generals and governors, and as a way to hold those families' loyalty.

This slide gives you the man behind the name. Most of what we know about Temüjin's early life comes from The Secret History of the Mongols, a Mongol account written down in the thirteenth century, probably within a generation of his death. It is dramatic and loyal to his family, so read it as a heroic story with real history inside it.

The hardships matter for the story. A boy who was abandoned by his own clan learned that kinship alone did not guarantee loyalty. As a leader he rewarded people for service, and he broke up old tribes and mixed their men into new units. That habit made his armies unusually loyal to him personally.

The portrait was painted for the Yuan court after Chinggis died, so the artist never saw him. Think of it as an official image, the way a family might commission a portrait of a respected ancestor.

Why this matters: Chinggis built a state around loyalty to himself and his family, not around cities or written laws. That is a very different starting point from a Chinese dynasty.

Q1 story · The army

Speed, Discipline and Spare Horses Made the Mongol Army the Most Feared of Its Age

Spare horses. Each rider brought several remounts, so a column could switch horses and keep moving for days.
Units of ten. Men served in units of 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000, often mixed across tribes.
Fake retreats. Riders pretended to flee, drew enemies into a chase, then turned and shot from the saddle.

×

Decimal units

Mongol soldiers were grouped by tens: an arban (10), a jaghun (100), a mingghan (1,000) and a tümen (10,000).

Because Chinggis often put men from different tribes in the same unit, a soldier's loyalty ran up the chain of command to the khan, not back to his old chief.

This slide gives you the reasons the Mongol army won so often, even against states with far more people.

Remounts are spare horses. Steppe horses were small and tough and could live on grass, so an army did not need long supply wagons. With several horses per rider, a Mongol force could cover distances that astonished its enemies.

The feigned retreat is a fake escape. It only works with strict discipline: if riders really panicked, the trick would turn into a real rout. Discipline came from the decimal units, where every ten men answered for each other.

The historian David Morgan, in his short book The Mongols, argues that organization and discipline, more than sheer numbers, explain Mongol victories. Most historians agree that terror also mattered: cities that resisted were often destroyed, and that reputation made others surrender.

Why this matters: this army was superb at winning battles in open country. Walled cities, rivers and taxes would be different problems, and that is where the story goes next.

Q1 story · North China · 1211–1219

Mongol Armies Crushed the Jin in the Field in 1211 and Took Their Capital, Zhongdu, in 1215

1211. At Yehuling, the "Badger Mouth" pass, Mongol cavalry shattered a large Jin army that was defending the northern frontier.
1213–1214. Mongol columns looted the North China Plain while the walled capital, Zhongdu (modern Beijing), held out. The Jin finally bought peace with a princess, horses, gold and silk.
1214–1215. When the Jin emperor moved his court south to Kaifeng, Chinggis called it a betrayal. Starving Zhongdu fell in 1215 and was looted and burned.
1217. Chinggis entrusted the continuing Jin war to Muqali. In 1219 he began his major campaign against the Khwarazmian Empire.

Early rule. Plunder and submission came first, but Chinggis already used darughachi overseers and local intermediaries, and Muqali governed through Khitan and Chinese officials.

This slide gives you the first Mongol war in China, against the Jin dynasty. The Jin were Jurchens, a people from Manchuria who had conquered North China about a century earlier. The Song dynasty still ruled the south.

Early campaigns emphasized plunder and submission. Administration developed unevenly during Chinggis's reign and became more systematic under his successors. Chinggis already placed darughachi overseers over some conquered towns and worked through local intermediaries. From 1217, his general Muqali ran both the continuing Jin war and the administration of Mongol-held North China, using Khitan and Chinese officials alongside Mongol commanders.

The painting shows Zhongdu with bright blue walls, which tells you more about Persian painting style than about Beijing. Still, it shows the key problem: horsemen could not simply ride over a city wall. Sieges took months, and the Mongols soon learned to recruit Chinese and Muslim engineers who knew how to break walls.

Why this matters: the early years lean toward plunder and terror, but they are not empty of government. Keep that "before" picture in mind as a matter of degree, because later rulers make administration thicker and more systematic.

Q1 story · The Tangut Xia · 1209–1227

The Tangut Xia Kingdom Submitted in 1209, Defied Chinggis, and Was Wiped Out in 1227

1209. The Mongols tried to flood the Xia capital by damming the Yellow River, but the dike burst and soaked their own camp. Xia submitted anyway and sent a princess.
1219. When Chinggis demanded troops for his Central Asian war, a Xia minister mocked him: if his army was too weak, why call himself khan?
1226–1227. Chinggis came back for revenge. He died during the campaign in August 1227, and the Xia ruler who surrendered was put to death.

This slide gives you the fate of the Xia, also called the Western Xia or Tangut kingdom. The Tanguts ruled a rich corridor west of the Jin, along the trade routes into Central Asia.

The insult in 1219 comes from The Secret History of the Mongols. Whether or not the words were exact, the point is clear: submission to the Mongols meant permanent obedience, including sending troops. Refusing was treated as rebellion.

Chinggis Khan died in August 1227 while the Xia were being destroyed. Nobody knows the cause. The Secret History says he was hurt in a fall from his horse while hunting, and other stories (illness, an arrow wound, even poison) appear in later sources. Be cautious with any confident claim.

The Xia state, its royal family and much of its capital were destroyed. The Tangut writing system survived in books and inscriptions, which is how modern scholars can still read Xia texts.

Why this matters: Xia shows the early Mongol rule in action. Submit and obey, or be destroyed. Conquest came first; thicker administration of farming populations grew more slowly and unevenly.

Why the census gap misleads

A census counts only the households a government can find and tax. After 1211, millions of people in North China fled to the Song south, hid in the hills, or became captives and private servants of Mongol nobles, who did not report them.

So the gap between the two counts mixes deaths with people who simply disappeared from the records. Historians agree the losses were enormous, from killing, famine, disease and flight, but they do not agree on how many died.

This slide gives you the human cost of the early wars, and it asks you to think like a historian about numbers.

Start with the poem. Yuan Haowen was one of the great poets of the Jin dynasty. In 1233 the Jin capital Kaifeng surrendered, and he and other officials were taken north across the Yellow River as captives. His poem is a real eyewitness voice, full of grief. Notice the twist at the end: after saying everyone is dead, he sees smoke from a few homes. Even the poet admits that his first line is not literally true.

Now the numbers. The drop from about 8.4 million households to under 2 million is shocking, and you will sometimes see it quoted as if it were a simple death count. It is not. A census is a list of people the state can tax. War destroyed the record keepers, sent people fleeing, and turned many into captives held by Mongol nobles.

Why this matters: early Mongol warfare in North China was devastating, and the damage was so great that it hurt the conquerors too. Empty farms pay no taxes. That problem pushes the story toward administration.

Q1 story · The heirs · 1229–1259

Chinggis's Heirs Finished Off the Jin and Began Counting, Taxing and Garrisoning North China

Ögödei, 1229–1241

He destroyed the last Jin stronghold at Caizhou in 1234, where the Jin emperor killed himself.

He ordered a census of North China, walled a capital at Karakorum in 1235, and expanded the relay stations of the yam.

Möngke, 1251–1259

He ordered a new, stricter census in 1252 and pulled tax collection under the Great Khan's own officials.

He put his brother Khubilai in charge of North China and died in 1259 while besieging a Song fortress at Diaoyu.

Darughachi overseers, already used under Chinggis, and tamma garrison armies, posted to stay, held the conquered land more systematically.

Ruling settled farming populations did not require Mongol rulers to abandon mobile courts or pastoral traditions.

×

Yam (relay stations)

The yam was a chain of relay stations with fresh horses, food and shelter. Envoys and messengers carrying a paiza could ride from station to station across the empire.

Nearby households had to supply the horses and food, which made the yam a heavy burden on local people.

×

Darughachi (overseer)

A darughachi (the word comes from "to press a seal") was an official the khan placed over a conquered town or region. Chinggis already used them; his heirs used them more widely and more routinely.

He watched local leaders, oversaw the census and taxes, and reported back. Local people did the daily work of government, but the darughachi made sure the khan got his share.

×

Tamma (garrison armies)

A tamma army was a permanent garrison force made of soldiers drawn from many units and peoples. It was posted on the edge of newly conquered land to keep it under control.

A raiding army leaves after the loot is taken. A tamma army stays, which shows the Mongols now meant to hold the land.

This slide gives you the next stage in a change of degree, not a sudden switch from "no government" to "government." Chinggis died in 1227. His son Ögödei was chosen Great Khan in 1229 and finished the war against the Jin. The last Jin emperor killed himself at Caizhou in 1234 as Mongol and Song troops broke in.

A census is a count of households, made so that they can be taxed and drafted. A permanent capital, relay stations and more systematic use of resident overseers are signs that rulers meant to hold the land for the long term. Ögödei's nephew Möngke, Great Khan from 1251, pushed this further with a new census and tighter control over who collected taxes.

The historian Thomas Allsen, in Mongol Imperialism, showed how Möngke's censuses and centralizing reforms tied conquered lands more tightly to the Great Khan. Ruling farmers did not mean becoming farmers. As Ruth Dunnell shows in "The Rise of Chinggis Khan and the United Empire," in The Cambridge History of the Mongol Empire, Mongol rulers governed settled populations while keeping mobile courts and pastoral traditions.

Why this matters: conquered land is worth more if its people stay alive and keep paying. The heirs of Chinggis made that idea more systematic, without giving up the steppe way of life.

Yelü Chucai, a Khitan Scholar Who Had Served the Jin, Became the Khans' Chief Adviser on North China

Who he was. Yelü Chucai came from a Khitan noble family descended from the old Liao royal house, trained in Confucian classics, astronomy and Buddhism.
Summoned in 1218. Chinggis called the tall scholar Urtu Saqal, "Longbeard," and kept him as a scribe and astrologer.
Liao and Jin are enemies from generation to generation. I have taken revenge for you. My father and grandfather both served the Jin. As their subject, how could I dare to treat my ruler as an enemy? Chinggis Khan and Yelü Chucai at their first meeting in 1218, as told in Yelü's biography in the Yuan shi, the official history of the Yuan compiled in 1369–1370. Translated for this deck.
×

Yelü Chucai (耶律楚材)

Yelü is the family name of the Khitan royal clan that ruled the Liao dynasty (907–1125) before the Jin conquered it. You may also see his name spelled Yelü Qucai, which is the same person.

The Khitans were a people from the Mongolian and Manchurian borderlands. By Yelü's time many Khitan families had lived among Chinese for generations and served the Jin as officials.

This slide gives you the hero of the next part of the story, and he is a fascinating one. Yelü Chucai was born in 1190 in Zhongdu, the Jin capital. His family were Khitans, descended from the royal house of the Liao dynasty, which the Jin had destroyed. He was raised on Chinese classics and became a devoted Buddhist.

When the Mongols took Zhongdu in 1215 he was a young Jin official. In 1218 Chinggis summoned him. Chinggis expected gratitude: the Jin had destroyed Yelü's ancestors' dynasty, and now the Mongols had crushed the Jin. Yelü's answer was a surprise. He said he could not call his own former ruler an enemy. Chinggis admired that loyalty and kept him close.

The calligraphy is real and in his own hand, written in 1240 near the end of his career. Big, confident brushwork like this was a mark of a cultured Chinese scholar.

A caution: most of what we know about Yelü comes from a memorial inscription written for his family after he died, which later became his official biography. It makes him look as wise and influential as possible. The scholar Igor de Rachewiltz, who studied him closely, accepted his importance but warned that these sources flatter him.

Why this matters: Yelü stood between two worlds. He understood Mongol power and Chinese government, and he tried to persuade the khans that the second could serve the first.

Taels, bolts and shi

A tael (liang) was a weight of silver, a little over an ounce. A bolt was a standard length of silk cloth, which worked almost like money. A shi was a large measure of grain, roughly a big sack's worth.

You do not need exact conversions. The point is that a taxed farming population could produce a steady, enormous income every single year.

This slide gives you the most famous argument of the whole period. It happened around 1230, soon after Ögödei became Great Khan. The quotations come from Yelü's later official biography in the Yuan shi. That biography is a flattering source, written after his death to honor him, so it may sharpen the drama of the debate.

Some courtiers favored converting conquered land to pasture; others saw greater value in agricultural taxes. Bie-die's proposal stands for the first view. Yelü's reply stands for the second.

Yelü answered in the khan's own language: income. He did not say "killing farmers is wrong." He said "living farmers will pay you, every year, forever." That is a clever move. He turned a moral argument into a practical one that a conqueror could accept.

Ögödei's answer was short: try it. The test is on the next slide.

Why this matters: this debate is a window into the Mongol choice between treating China as a place to plunder and treating it as a place to govern.

Q2 story · The test · 1230–1233

Yelü's Tax Offices Delivered the Promised Silver in 1231, and Ögödei Put Him in Charge of the Secretariat

1230. Yelü set up ten tax bureaus across North China, starting at Yanjing (the old Zhongdu), and staffed them with Confucian scholars instead of soldiers.
1231. At Yunzhong the registers, silver and silk were laid out before the khan, who laughed with delight.
You never left my side, yet you have made the state's revenues sufficient.Ögödei to Yelü in 1231, from the Yuan shi. He then named Yelü head of the Secretariat.
Our soldiers have been in the field for decades. What they want is land and people. Land without people, what use is it?Yelü in 1233, urging Ögödei not to massacre Kaifeng after it surrendered. The khan spared the city.

Be skeptical. These scenes come from a flattering memorial to Yelü. His biography claims his plea saved 1,470,000 people in Kaifeng, a number that is probably inflated.

This slide gives you the result of Yelü's experiment. He created ten tax bureaus, offices that collected regular taxes in each region. He staffed them with Confucian scholars, many of them former Jin officials who had lost everything in the war. That gave educated Chinese a reason to work with the new rulers.

In 1231 the results were displayed for Ögödei at Yunzhong (near modern Datong). The khan was thrilled and made Yelü head of the Secretariat, the chief office for running civil government. It sounds like a total victory, but notice that Mongol princes and generals still held most real power.

The second quotation is from 1233. The Mongol general Subutai wanted to massacre the people of Kaifeng, which by Mongol custom was the price of resisting. Yelü argued that land without people is worthless. Ögödei agreed and limited the punishment to the Jin royal clan.

The skeptic box matters. Yelü's biography was based on an inscription written to honor him, so it may give him credit that others shared. The figure of 1,470,000 people saved should be read as a sign of how big the city was, not as a count.

Why this matters: for a few years, Chinese-style taxation looked like the winning plan. The next slide shows why it did not stay that way.

×

Tax farming

Tax farming means contracting for the right to collect specified revenues. The bidder pays the ruler a fixed sum up front, then collects from the people and keeps whatever is left after covering that payment.

The ruler gets money fast with little effort. Collectors often squeeze the people harder, because their profit depends on taking more than they paid.

×

Ortogh (merchant partnerships)

An ortogh (from a Turkic word for "partner") was a merchant association, often of Central Asian Muslims or Uighurs, that pooled capital from the court, princes or other investors.

Ortogh partners shared commercial risk on long-distance trade and finance across Eurasia. They also sometimes collected revenues for their patrons. High-interest lending, including the crushing "lamb's interest" named in Chinese sources, was one part of that wider business, not the whole of it.

×

Appanage (princely grant)

An appanage was an allocation of households or revenues to a member of the ruling elite, such as a prince or general. The 1236 grants in North China are a famous example.

Appanages are not the same thing as tax farming or as ortogh partnerships. They gave elite holders a claim on local people and income, which complicated any plan for a single state tax system.

This slide gives you the competing plan that beat Yelü's. Keep three ideas separate. Tax farming is a contract for the right to collect specified revenues. Ortogh are merchant partnerships in trade, finance and sometimes revenue collection. Appanages are allocations of households or revenues to members of the ruling elite, such as the 1236 grants.

Yelü's taxes worked, but they had limits: fair, fixed rates meant a steady income, not a huge one. A merchant named Abd al-Rahman, a Central Asian Muslim, offered 2.2 million taels for a tax farm, double the existing income. Yelü wept and argued that collectors would squeeze the farmers to make their profit. According to his biography, Ögödei snapped, "Do you want to fight over this?" and then, "Do you want to weep for the people? Let it be tried."

Mongol rulers liked merchants. Ortogh partners shared commercial risk, carried trade across Eurasia and sometimes collected revenues for the court and princes. High-interest lending was part of that world, but not the only part. Columbia's Asia for Educators essay "Merchant Associations" is a clear short guide. Many Chinese writers blamed foreign financiers for ruining farmers, and there is truth in that, but remember the Mongol rulers made the choice.

The 1236 appanages worked against Yelü too. He won a compromise that let state officials collect some taxes, but the princes kept pressing for more.

Why this matters: several ways of making money from China were competing. One looked to Chinese-style regular taxation, and others looked to contracts, merchant capital and elite grants.

Q3 story · The prince · 1242–1256

Prince Khubilai Gathered Chinese Advisers and Learned That Sparing Cities Won More Than Burning Them

1242. The Buddhist monk Haiyun visited Khubilai and brought a brilliant younger monk, Liu Bingzhong, who stayed on as his adviser for thirty years.
His mother's lesson. Sorghaghtani Beki, a Christian princess of the Kereit people, ran her North Chinese lands by protecting farmers, not squeezing them.
1251. His brother Möngke became Great Khan and put Khubilai in charge of North China.
Last night you told the story of Cao Bin, who conquered without killing. I can do that.Khubilai to his adviser Yao Shu in 1253, marching on the Dali kingdom in Yunnan, from Yao Shu's Yuan shi biography. Khubilai spared Dali's people. Translated for this deck.

This slide gives you the young Khubilai and the people who shaped him. Khubilai was born in 1215, a grandson of Chinggis. He grew up as a Mongol prince: a rider, a hunter and a soldier. What made him different was the circle around him.

Liu Bingzhong (1216–1274) was a Chinese scholar who had become a Buddhist monk. He knew the classics, mathematics, astronomy and city planning. Yao Shu and other Chinese scholars joined him, and together they taught Khubilai how Chinese emperors had governed. Khubilai never learned to read Chinese well, but he learned to listen.

His mother, Sorghaghtani Beki, mattered just as much. She held lands in North China and saw that farmers who were treated fairly paid more over time. She also made sure her sons were educated and well connected.

The quote is from 1253, during Khubilai's campaign against the kingdom of Dali in Yunnan. Cao Bin was a famous early Song general who conquered the southern kingdom of Southern Tang in 975 without letting his troops massacre its people. When Dali's leaders killed Mongol envoys, the generals wanted revenge, and Khubilai's advisers reminded him of his promise. He spared the city. Remember Cao Bin, because his name comes back later.

A note on the woodblock print: it was made in 1609, three centuries later, so it shows how Ming readers imagined Liu Bingzhong, not what he looked like.

Why this matters: Khubilai's Chinese advisers gave him a model of rule that promised more power, not less, if he governed the way Chinese emperors did.

Q3 story · The throne · 1259–1264

Khubilai Seized the Throne in 1260 and Won a Four-Year Civil War with the Wealth of North China

1259Möngke dies in the Song war, and Khubilai, besieging Ezhou on the Yangzi, makes a quick truce and hurries north.
1260His supporters proclaim him Great Khan at Kaiping, and he takes a Chinese reign title, Zhongtong, "Central Rule."
1260In Mongolia, his younger brother Ariq Böke is proclaimed Great Khan as well.
1262Li Tan, a Chinese warlord in Shandong, rebels and is crushed, and Khubilai grows wary of Chinese officials.
1264Ariq Böke, cut off from Chinese grain, surrenders.

This slide gives you the crisis that made Khubilai emperor. In 1259 Möngke died while besieging a Song fortress in Sichuan. Khubilai was far away, attacking the Song city of Ezhou (part of modern Wuhan). He made a quick deal with the Song minister Jia Sidao and rushed north.

Two brothers were then proclaimed Great Khan in 1260. Ariq Böke was backed by many princes in the Mongolian homeland. Khubilai was backed by his own followers at Kaiping, his new city on the edge of the steppe. Khubilai also announced a Chinese-style reign title, a name for the years of his rule, which no Great Khan had done before. That was a message to his Chinese subjects.

The war was decided by supplies. Karakorum, the Mongol capital, depended on grain shipped from North China, and Khubilai controlled North China. When he cut off the grain, Ariq Böke's side starved and gave up in 1264.

Notice the warning sign in 1262. A Chinese general, Li Tan, rebelled, and one of Khubilai's top Chinese ministers was executed for links to him. From then on Khubilai relied on Chinese advisers but balanced them with Mongols, Central Asians and others.

Why this matters: Khubilai won because he controlled the farms and cities of China. Chinese wealth made him khan, and he did not forget it.

Q3 story · The dynasty · 1267–1272

In 1271 Khubilai Named His Dynasty Yuan, an Idea from the Book of Changes, and Ruled from a New Capital, Dadu

We may establish the dynastic name Great Yuan, taking its meaning from "qianyuan" in the Book of Changes.Khubilai's edict of December 1271, preserved in the Yuan shi. Liu Bingzhong is credited with suggesting the name. Translated for this deck.
A new kind of name. Qin and Han were named for home regions. Yuan was named for an idea, the great origin of all things.
A new capital. Dadu, "Great Capital," rose from 1267 beside the ruins of Zhongdu. Mongols called it Khanbaliq, "City of the Khan."
×

Yuan (元), "origin"

The name comes from the Book of Changes (Yijing), a classic of Chinese thought. Its first line praises qianyuan (乾元), the creative power of Heaven: "Great is qianyuan! All things owe their beginning to it."

So "Great Yuan" claimed that Khubilai's rule was as universal as Heaven itself. It also avoided naming the dynasty after the Mongols' homeland, which made it easier for Chinese subjects to accept.

×

Dadu (大都), the Great Capital

Dadu was the Yuan winter capital, on the site of modern Beijing. Liu Bingzhong planned it on a grid following an ancient Chinese ideal of a royal city, with the palace in the center, the ancestral temple to the east, and markets to the north.

In summer the court moved north to Shangdu on the steppe, so the emperor lived part of each year in each world.

This slide gives you the moment the Mongol state became a Chinese dynasty in name. In December 1271 Khubilai issued an edict giving his state a dynastic name, Da Yuan, "Great Yuan."

The edict explains its own choice. Earlier dynasties took their names from places: Qin and Han from the regions where they first rose, Sui and Tang from noble titles tied to territory. Yuan was different. It came from the Book of Changes, and it means "origin" or "primal." Choosing a word from a Chinese classic told educated Chinese that this ruler belonged in the line of legitimate dynasties. The Mandate of Heaven, the old idea that Heaven grants the right to rule, now covered a Mongol.

At the same time Khubilai built Dadu, his Great Capital, where Beijing is today. Liu Bingzhong, the monk-adviser from earlier, planned both Shangdu and Dadu. The city was Chinese in layout, but its palace grounds had room for Mongol tents, and the court still spent its summers on the steppe.

The court scene is AI-generated. Notice how it mixes Mongol, Tibetan and Chinese figures, because Khubilai's court really was that mixed.

Why this matters: names and capitals are claims to legitimacy. Khubilai used Chinese ideas to tell Chinese subjects that he was a proper emperor.

Q3 story · Two roles · 1260–1294

Khubilai Ruled as a Chinese Emperor and a Mongol Great Khan at the Same Time

Chinese emperor

He set up Chinese-style offices for civil affairs, the army and inspection.

He honored ancestors with Chinese temple rites.

Mongol khan

He hunted and held court on the steppe every summer.

He ordered a new Phags-pa script for every language in his empire.

He is of a good stature, neither tall nor short, but of a middle height. His complexion is white and red, the eyes black and fine.Marco Polo, a Venetian who said he served Khubilai from about 1275 to 1292, in his Travels (Yule translation). Can we trust him?
×

Phags-pa script

In 1269 Khubilai ordered a new alphabet, designed by the Tibetan Buddhist lama Phags-pa, his imperial preceptor. It was based on Tibetan letters and was meant to write Mongolian, Chinese and other languages of the empire.

It appears on seals, coins, paper money and passes like the paiza at the start of this deck. Most people kept using Chinese characters, and the script faded after the Yuan fell.

×

Can we trust Marco Polo?

Partly. Polo's book was written in prison in Genoa with a writer of romances, Rustichello of Pisa, and it mixes accurate details about Yuan China with boasting and borrowed stories.

No Chinese record mentions him, and he leaves out things like tea drinking and foot binding. The historian Frances Wood even doubted he reached China. Most scholars think he did, but they read his claims, especially about his own importance, with care.

This slide gives you the double life of Khubilai Khan. To his Chinese subjects he was the Son of Heaven, an emperor with a dynastic name, Chinese-style ministries and Confucian rituals. To Mongols he was the Great Khan, a descendant of Chinggis who hunted on horseback, kept Mongol law for Mongols, and spent every summer at Shangdu.

The painting shows this perfectly. It was painted in 1280 by Liu Guandao, a Chinese court artist, in fine Chinese silk-painting style. But the subject is a Mongol hunt, with Khubilai in white furs and his companions with bows and hunting animals. A Chinese art form celebrating Mongol life: that is the Yuan court in one image.

The Phags-pa script is another example. Khubilai wanted a writing system for his whole empire, not just for Chinese. It was a bold idea that never fully caught on.

Marco Polo's description is fun and probably fairly accurate, since Khubilai's official portrait fits it. But open the popup before you trust everything Polo says. We will meet one of his tallest tales in the Xiangyang story.

Why this matters: Khubilai did not simply "become Chinese." He used Chinese traditions where they helped him and kept Mongol ways where they mattered to his power.

Q3 story · The plan for the south · 1260–1274

Khubilai Aimed His Song War at One River Fortress and Promised Mercy to Cities That Surrendered

A hard target. The Song south was rich, crowded and laced with rivers and rice paddies where cavalry struggled.
A grievance. In 1260 the Song minister Jia Sidao locked up Khubilai's envoy, the scholar Hao Jing, and held him for sixteen years.
A defector's advice. Liu Zheng, a Song general who switched sides in 1261, urged Khubilai to build a navy and strike Xiangyang first.
Long ago Cao Bin pacified the south without a love of killing. Understand my heart, and be my Cao Bin.Khubilai to his commander Bayan in 1274, from Bayan's Yuan shi biography. Translated for this deck.
×

Xiangyang (襄陽)

Xiangyang and its twin city Fancheng faced each other across the Han River in modern Hubei. Thick walls, a wide moat and the river itself made them the strongest fortress on the Song frontier.

The Han River flows south into the Yangzi. Whoever held Xiangyang held the gate to the Yangzi valley, the heartland of Song China.

This slide gives you the plan for conquering the Southern Song, the Chinese dynasty that still ruled the south. It was the richest state in the world at the time, with great cities, a huge population and strong walls. Earlier Mongol attacks had failed, and Möngke died trying.

Khubilai needed a cause and a plan. The cause came from the Song side: Jia Sidao detained Khubilai's envoy Hao Jing, which Khubilai could present as an insult that justified war. The plan came from a Chinese defector, Liu Zheng. Liu knew the Song frontier from the inside. He argued that the Mongols must learn to fight on water and must take Xiangyang, the key to the Yangzi.

Notice how much depends on Chinese people here: a Chinese defector's strategy, Chinese shipbuilders and, soon, Chinese sailors. Khubilai's Song war was not just Mongols against Chinese.

The quote echoes Cao Bin from the Dali story. In 1274, as his army prepared to sail down the Yangzi, Khubilai told his commander Bayan to be "my Cao Bin." A conqueror who spared cities would find it easier to rule them later, and cities that knew they would be spared were more likely to surrender.

Why this matters: Khubilai used a Chinese hero as his model for conquering China. That is the link between legitimacy and conquest.

Q4 story · The siege · 1268–1273

For Five Years a Mongol-Built Navy and a Ring of Forts Strangled Xiangyang by Land and River

1268. The Mongol general Aju and the defector Liu Zheng ringed the twin cities of Xiangyang and Fancheng with forts and walls.
A navy from nothing. The Mongols built about 5,000 boats and trained about 70,000 sailors and marines, most of them Chinese.
1272. Two Song officers named Zhang led about 100 paddle boats and 3,000 men through the blockade at night. They broke in, but the city stayed trapped.
Fire and noise. Both sides hurled gunpowder "thunder-crash" bombs.
×

Thunder-crash bombs

Gunpowder was a Chinese invention. By the 1200s, Jin and Song armies had bombs with iron or ceramic casings that burst with a deafening bang and threw out fragments and fire.

They were thrown by hand or launched by catapult. The Mongols learned to use them from Chinese experts and carried them as far as Japan.

This slide gives you the longest siege of the war. Xiangyang and Fancheng sat on opposite banks of the Han River. As long as Song boats could carry food and soldiers along the river, the cities could hold out forever.

So the Mongols did something new: they built a navy. With Chinese shipbuilders, Chinese sailors and the strategy of the Song defector Liu Zheng, they closed the river with fleets, forts and chains. The figures of 5,000 boats and 70,000 marines come from Yuan records and may be rounded up, but the point stands: steppe horsemen became a river power.

The 1272 breakthrough is one of the great stories of the siege. Two officers, Zhang Shun and Zhang Gui, ran the blockade with about a hundred paddle boats, cutting through chains under fire. Zhang Shun was killed, and his body was later found floating in the river, still in armor. Their courage brought supplies in, but it did not break the siege.

Why this matters: by the 1260s the Mongols could fight on water as well as on land, using Chinese technology and Chinese crews. That changed what they could conquer.

Q4 story · The machines · 1271–1273

Muslim Engineers' Counterweight Trebuchets Broke Xiangyang's Nerve, and It Surrendered in March 1273

1271. Khubilai asked his relative Abaqa, the Mongol ruler of Iran, for siege experts. Two Muslim engineers, Ismail and Ala al-Din, came east.
A new machine. Their counterweight trebuchets hurled stones of about 90 kilograms. Fancheng fell first, early in 1273.
When the machine was released, its sound shook heaven and earth. Whatever it struck was smashed, and the stone sank seven feet into the ground. The Song commander Lü Wenhuan was afraid and surrendered the city.The official Yuan shi report (ch. 203), biography of the engineer Ismail. This is the dynasty's dramatic account of the machine's impact, not a measured technical observation. Translated for this deck.

Tall tale. Marco Polo later claimed that he, his father and his uncle built these machines. The siege ended before they arrived.

×

Counterweight trebuchet

A trebuchet is a giant throwing arm on a pivot. Chinese armies had long used traction trebuchets, pulled down by teams of people hauling on ropes.

A counterweight trebuchet uses a huge box of earth or stones instead. When the box drops, the arm whips up and throws a heavier stone farther and more accurately than human muscle could. It was well known in the Middle East but new to China.

This slide gives you the weapon that ended the siege. After years of stalemate, Khubilai reached across his family's empire. His relative Abaqa ruled the Ilkhanate, the Mongol state in Iran. Abaqa sent two Muslim engineers, Ismail from Hülegü's lands and Ala al-Din from a place called Mufali, whose machines were the most advanced in the world.

The key idea is the counterweight. Instead of a crew pulling ropes, a falling weight powers the throw. That meant heavier stones, about 150 jin or roughly 90 kilograms, hitting the same spot again and again. The engineers first battered Fancheng, which fell in early 1273. Then Ismail set up a machine at the southeast corner of Xiangyang. After a few shots, the Song commander Lü Wenhuan surrendered on 14 March 1273. He then joined the Yuan side and helped persuade other Song cities to give up.

The quotation that the machine's sound "shook heaven and earth" and that its stone sank seven feet into the ground comes from the official Yuan shi, chapter 203. Treat it as the court's dramatic report of the psychological and political impact, not as a modern engineering measurement. The story still links Ala al-Din and Ismail's machines to Lü Wenhuan's surrender.

The lower picture is a real Persian manuscript painting made in Tabriz, in Mongol-ruled Iran, around 1306 to 1314. It shows a trebuchet attacking a fortress in an older story, but it tells you what this technology looked like to artists in the engineers' own world.

About Marco Polo: his book says the Polos had a German and a Nestorian Christian build stone-throwers for Xiangyang. Chinese and Persian sources name Muslim engineers, and the dates show the siege was over before the Polos reached China. It is a good example of Polo making himself the hero.

Why this matters: the Mongol empire connected Iran and China. Technology from one end of Eurasia decided a battle at the other end.

Q4 story · Down the Yangzi · 1274–1276

Bayan's Multiethnic Army and Fleet Swept Down the Yangzi, and Hangzhou Surrendered in 1276

A mixed army

Mongols rode beside northern Chinese, Jurchen and Khitan troops, Uighur and Persian experts, and thousands of Song soldiers who changed sides.

1275

At Dingjiazhou, Bayan's fleet and catapults routed Jia Sidao's army. At Changzhou, which resisted, his troops massacred the people.

1276

Grand Empress Dowager Xie surrendered Hangzhou for the small boy Emperor Gong, and Bayan entered the city in good order.

This slide gives you the fall of the Song capital. After Xiangyang, the road to the Yangzi was open. In 1274 Bayan, a brilliant Mongol general who had served in Iran, led a huge army and fleet down the Han River and the Yangzi.

Look at who was in that army. Ethnic Mongols were a minority. There were northern Chinese soldiers and sailors, Jurchens and Khitans, Uighur and Persian specialists, and a growing flood of Song commanders who surrendered and kept their troops. The Yuan conquest of the Song was in large part Chinese fighting Chinese, under Mongol command.

Bayan followed Khubilai's "be my Cao Bin" advice when it worked and ignored it when a city resisted. Changzhou refused to surrender and was sacked with great slaughter, which frightened other cities into opening their gates. In early 1276 the Song court at Hangzhou (also called Lin'an) surrendered. Grand Empress Dowager Xie, ruling for the young Emperor Gong, handed over the imperial seal. The boy emperor was taken north and treated with respect.

The Japanese scroll is from a different campaign in the same year, 1274: a Yuan force of Mongol, Chinese and Korean troops attacked Japan, and a samurai named Takezaki Suenaga later had scrolls painted to record his deeds. Notice the bomb bursting in the air. The scroll shows related military abilities, such as gunpowder and multiethnic forces, but it cannot tell us the makeup or equipment of Bayan's army on the Yangzi.

Why this matters: naval power, gunpowder, siege engines and a multiethnic army, much of it Chinese, made the conquest of the south possible.

Q4 story · Yashan · 1279

The Song Dynasty Died at Sea off Yashan in 1279

Loyalists chained about 1,000 Song ships together into one floating fortress.
Zhang Hongfan, a northern Chinese general leading the Yuan fleet, cut off their water and attacked.
Lu Xiufu leapt into the sea carrying the boy emperor, Zhao Bing, about seven years old.
Since ancient times, who has lived and not died? Let me leave a loyal heart to shine in the pages of history.Wen Tianxiang, a Song minister captured in 1278, in a poem written as a prisoner on a Yuan ship. He was executed in 1283.

This slide gives you the end of the Song dynasty, and it is one of the most dramatic scenes in Chinese history. After Hangzhou surrendered, loyal ministers fled south with two young princes, crowning first one and then the other as emperor. They moved by sea along the southern coast.

In 1279 the last Song fleet made its stand at Yashan, an island at the mouth of the Pearl River near modern Guangzhou. The commander Zhang Shijie chained his ships together so no one could flee. The Yuan fleet, commanded by Zhang Hongfan, a Chinese general from the north, blockaded them, cut off fresh water and attacked. When the battle was lost, the minister Lu Xiufu took the boy emperor on his back and jumped into the sea. Many officials and soldiers followed.

Wen Tianxiang had been captured a few months earlier. When Zhang Hongfan asked him to write a letter urging the loyalists to surrender, Wen gave him a poem instead, ending with the lines on this slide. Khubilai himself later offered him high office. Wen refused and was executed in Dadu in 1283. Chinese have remembered him ever since as a model of loyalty.

Notice again who did the fighting: Chinese commanders on both sides of the last battle. The portrait of Wen is a Ming painting from long after his death.

Why this matters: the conquest of China, begun in 1211, ended at sea. The Mongols won the final battle of their land empire with a navy.

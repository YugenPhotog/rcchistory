HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 12 · Lecture 2

Yuan Hierarchy and Global Exchange

The Yuan Sorted Its People by Origin While Goods and Ideas Crossed Eurasia

1260s–1350s · Ranks, registers, roads and blue-and-white · Study Buddy edition

A caravan bound for Cathay, detail of the Catalan Atlas, Majorca, 1375, attributed to Abraham Cresques. Bibliothèque nationale de France. Public domain, Wikimedia Commons.

Welcome back! This deck is your Study Buddy for life inside Yuan China after the conquest. You can work through it on your own, at your own speed, as many times as you like.

Here is the big story in one breath. Once Khubilai Khan ruled all of China, his government had to manage tens of millions of people who did not share Mongol language, religion or customs. It sorted them by origin and by hereditary occupation. At the same moment, Mongol rule across Asia made it easier for merchants, missionaries, envoys and craftsmen to travel, and one result was a style of porcelain painted with imported cobalt blue that buyers across Asia came to prize. Earlier Chinese blue-decorated ceramics existed as far back as the Tang, but Yuan Jingdezhen developed the major production tradition that became internationally influential.

The picture behind the title is a real historical image. It comes from the Catalan Atlas, a world map made on the island of Majorca in 1375. The mapmaker had never been to Asia. He drew this caravan from travelers' reports, including Marco Polo's book, so it shows what Europeans imagined about the road east.

Why this matters: hierarchy and exchange can look like opposite stories, one about walls between people and one about bridges. In Yuan China they happened together, under the same rulers.

The puzzle · 1287 and 1345

A Monk from Dadu Reached Rome, and a Judge from Morocco Reached Quanzhou

Going west. In 1287 Rabban Sauma, a Christian monk born near the Yuan capital, met kings in Paris and Bordeaux, and in 1288 he met the pope in Rome.
Going east. Around 1345 the Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta stepped ashore at Quanzhou, a southern port crowded with Muslim merchants.
Staying put. Meanwhile, Yuan officials sorted the people of China into ranked labels and hereditary household registers.

The puzzle: how could one government keep people in fixed places at home while moving people, money and goods across a continent?

This slide gives you the two journeys that frame the lecture, plus the problem of hierarchy that sits between them.

Rabban Sauma traveled from China all the way to Western Europe, about two centuries before any European ship reached China. Ibn Battuta, a legal scholar from Tangier, traveled the other way, through India and Southeast Asia to the Chinese coast. We will meet both of them properly later in the deck.

The map is a good reminder of how little Europeans knew. The Catalan Atlas mixes real place names picked up from merchants with legends. It is real evidence, but evidence about European knowledge in 1375, not a survey of Asia.

Why this matters: Yuan rule produced both sharp lines between groups at home and unusually open roads abroad. Keeping both in view is the skill this lecture builds.

Q5 story · The problem of numbers

A Small Mongol Minority Ruled Tens of Millions of Chinese Subjects

about 59 million

people in about 13 million households appear in the Yuan count of 1290, and many more were never registered.

Few rulers. Mongols were a small minority in China. Historians can only estimate their numbers, but every estimate is tiny next to the Chinese population.
Many helpers. Khubilai governed through Mongol nobles, Central Asian and Middle Eastern specialists, and many Chinese officials and clerks.
Divided subjects. Northerners had lived under Jurchen rule for a century, while southerners had been Song subjects until 1276.

This slide gives you the basic arithmetic of Yuan rule. The census figure comes from the geography treatise of the Yuan shi, the official history compiled by Ming scholars in 1369–1370 from Yuan records. Census numbers in imperial China counted taxpayers, not every human being, so treat the figure as a floor, not a head count.

The Mongols had a real problem. They held the army and the throne, but they could not staff every office, speak every language or read every tax register themselves. So they relied on people from many backgrounds. Some were Uyghurs, Tanguts and Muslims from Central and West Asia. Many were Chinese.

Notice the split inside the Chinese population too. North China had been ruled by the Jurchen Jin dynasty since the 1120s and fell to the Mongols in 1234. South China stayed under the Song until 1276. Those were two different histories, and the Yuan treated them as two different groups.

Why this matters: any system of labels the Yuan used was a tool for a minority to rule a majority. Ask who it helped and how well it worked.

Check yourself: Why would a ruling minority need help from people outside its own group?

Q5 story · Four labels

Yuan Rules Sorted People into Mongols, Semu, Hanren and Nanren

Mongols. Members of the Mongol tribes held the throne, commanded the armies and claimed the top posts.
Semu ren. "People of various kinds" included Uyghurs, Tanguts, Kipchaks, Persians, Arabs and others from the west.
Hanren. People of the former Jin lands in the north, including Chinese, Khitans and Jurchens, formed the third group.
Nanren. Southerners, the former subjects of the Southern Song, came last and were the largest group.
Mongols shall serve as the darughachi of every circuit, Han as the governors, and Muslims as the associate governors. Let this be a permanent rule.Order of 1265 recorded in Khubilai's annals, Yuan shi ch. 6. Translated for this deck. Note that it names Muslims, not the broader label semu.
×

Semu ren

Semu literally means "various kinds" or "assorted categories." It was a catch-all for peoples who were neither Mongol nor Chinese, mostly from Central and West Asia.

The historian Funada Yoshiyuki points out that the term appears in Chinese-language sources, not in Mongolian or Persian ones. He argues that it was an administrative label for people governed by their own customs rather than by Chinese law.

×

Hanren (northerners)

In Yuan usage, Hanren did not simply mean "ethnic Chinese." It covered most people of the old Jin realm in North China: Chinese, but also Khitans, Jurchens and some Koreans.

A late Yuan scholar, Tao Zongyi, listed eight kinds of Hanren and more than thirty kinds of semu. His lists repeat and overlap, which suggests that even educated people of the time did not share one precise definition.

×

Nanren (southerners)

Nanren meant people of the lands conquered from the Southern Song in the 1270s. Mongols sometimes called them Manzi, a slighting term that Marco Polo also used for South China.

Southerners were the most numerous group and included the richest landlords and the most famous scholars in the empire.

This slide gives you the four labels that Review Question 5 calls the "four-grade" classification. Learn them in order, because some Yuan rules really did treat them in that order.

The quotation is one of the clearest examples. In 1265 Khubilai ordered that in every circuit (a regional unit of government) the darughachi, the overseer with final authority, should be a Mongol, the governor should be Han, and the associate governor should be a Muslim. In practice the court soon allowed exceptions, and later rules spoke of Mongols and semu together. The order shows the logic: put a Mongol at the top and spread other posts across groups, so no single group controlled a region.

The labels were not about skin color or "race" in a modern sense. They were about political history: who had joined the Mongols early, and who was conquered late. Northerners surrendered decades before southerners did, and southerners were labeled last.

The seal belonged to a centurion (a commander of a hundred) in an imperial guard unit. Its square letters are Phags-pa, the script Khubilai adopted for official use, which we will meet again on another slide.

Why this matters: to answer Q5 you need the four labels and a clear sense of how they were used, not just a ranking chart.

Check yourself: Which of the four groups was the largest, and why might the Yuan have placed it last?

Q5 story · The labels in law

Some Yuan Laws Treated Mongols and Chinese Differently

When a Mongol quarrels with a Han and strikes him, the Han must not strike back. He may bring a complaint to the authorities.Yuan legal provision preserved in the law treatise of the Yuan shi, ch. 105. Translated for this deck.
A Mongol who kills a Han in a quarrel or while drunk shall be sentenced to military service on campaign and pay the full burial money.Same treatise. Its general rule in the same section is that whoever kills a person shall die.
Han who carry weapons are forbidden to do so. Han who serve as soldiers are not.Same treatise, from its section on prohibitions.
Unequal protection. These rules gave Mongols lighter penalties and asked Chinese victims to wait for a court instead of defending themselves.
Disarming the many. Bans on weapons, hunting gear and large gatherings were aimed at preventing revolt among the Chinese majority.
Exceptions built in. The weapons ban exempted Chinese soldiers, so a man's registered job could matter as much as his label.

About the source. The law treatise is a Ming summary of Yuan codes. It tells us what rules said, not how often courts enforced them.

×

The Yuan shi law treatise

The Yuan shi was written in 1369–1370 by scholars working for the new Ming dynasty. Its treatise on punishments condenses Yuan legal collections into short rules.

That makes it a valuable record of what Yuan law said. It does not tell us how many cases were tried, or whether local officials applied the rules evenly. Ming writers also had reasons to make Mongol rule look harsh.

This slide gives you the strongest evidence that the four labels had real legal teeth. All three quotations come from the treatise on punishments in the Yuan shi, translated for this deck.

"Burial money" was a payment to the victim's family for funeral costs. Compare the two outcomes in the second quotation: a Mongol killer could be sent to fight in the army and pay compensation, while the general rule in the same section reads: whoever kills a person shall die. That is a real double standard.

Notice the word Han in these rules. In legal texts it usually meant all Chinese subjects, northern and southern, not only the Hanren label from the last slide. Yuan terms were not always used consistently, which is one reason historians argue about them.

Notice too the exception in the weapons rule. The Yuan needed Chinese soldiers, so Chinese men registered as soldiers could be armed. Keep that in mind for the slide on households.

Textbook note: Ebrey (p. 205) says a Mongol killer "merely had to pay a fine." The code also sent him on campaign. Source beyond Ebrey: Yuan shi ch. 105.

Why this matters: Q5 asks how the system served Mongol interests. Disarming the majority and protecting the ruling minority in court are clear examples, as long as you remember these are rules on paper.

Check yourself: What does the weapons exemption for Chinese soldiers tell you about how the Yuan balanced fear of revolt with the need for troops?

Q5 story · Historians disagree

Historians Debate How Rigid the "Four Classes" Really Were

The classic view. In 1938 the Chinese historian Meng Siming described Yuan society as a fixed four-class ladder, and many general histories still repeat his picture.
The challenge. Funada Yoshiyuki and others note that no single Yuan law created a "four classes" system. The labels appear in particular rules on offices, quotas, punishments and weapons.
Daily practice. Elizabeth Endicott-West shows that Mongol overseers depended on Chinese clerks, and Frederick Mote calls many controls harsh but unevenly enforced.
People who rose anyway. The northerner Shi Tianze and the southerner Zhao Mengfu reached high office while keeping their origins.

A fair summary: the labels were real and could hurt, but they worked through specific rules, and wealth, office, registration and personal ties often mattered as much.

×

Zhao Mengfu (1254–1322)

A descendant of the Song imperial family, Zhao was recruited to serve Khubilai in 1286. He rose to high honorary posts and became the most admired painter and calligrapher of his age.

Some later critics condemned him for serving the conquerors. You will meet him again in Lecture 3.

This slide gives you the debate that sits inside Review Question 5. The question calls the stratification "rigid." Historians today are divided about how rigid it was, and a strong answer shows that you know the evidence on both sides.

The neat ladder of "four classes" was popularized by Meng Siming in a 1938 study. The Japanese historian Funada Yoshiyuki has argued that Yuan documents never set out such a system in one law, and that "semu" was an administrative category created in Chinese documents. Elizabeth Endicott-West, in Mongolian Rule in China, shows how Mongol overseers depended on Chinese clerks who actually ran local paperwork. Frederick Mote, in Imperial China 900–1800, describes many controls as harsh on paper but inconsistently enforced.

None of these historians says the labels were harmless. Southerners did face real limits, especially in reaching high office. The disagreement is about how complete and how fixed the system was.

Two cases help. Shi Tianze, a northern Chinese commander, became one of Khubilai's chief ministers. Zhao Mengfu, a southerner and Song royal descendant, became a high-ranking scholar at court. They were exceptions. Their careers show that origin did not completely determine access to high office. They rose politically while retaining their backgrounds.

Why this matters: you can answer Q5 as it is asked and still weigh how rigid the system was in practice. That balance is what makes an answer strong.

Check yourself: Name one piece of evidence that supports the word "rigid" and one that complicates it.

Q5 story · Hereditary households

Families Were Registered by Occupation, and Sons Inherited the Duty

Military households. Junhu owed soldiers to the army generation after generation, and in return got some tax relief.
Artisan households. Weavers, potters and metalworkers were registered as jianghu and worked for government workshops.
Confucian households. Ruhu were scholar families excused from many labor duties so they could study and teach.
Other duties. Relay-station, salt, hunting, medical and clergy households all had their own registers under the name zhuse huji.

Two different systems. The four labels sorted people by origin. Household registers sorted them by duty, and every group had them.

×

Junhu (military households)

A military household had to supply a soldier, and often his equipment, whenever called. If the soldier died, the family sent another son.

There were Mongol, semu and Chinese military households. The Ming dynasty kept this hereditary system after 1368.

×

Jianghu (artisan households)

During the conquests, Mongol armies often spared skilled craftsmen and moved them to new workshops. Under the Yuan, their families were registered as artisan households and owed work to government or princely workshops.

Many lived poorly and tried to escape the register, but the system gave the court a steady supply of luxury goods.

×

Ruhu (Confucian households)

Confucian households were scholar families recognized by the state. They were excused from many labor services, and their sons were expected to keep studying.

In the north, many families won this status through a test held in 1238. Registration protected scholars even when there were no regular civil service examinations.

×

Zhuse huji ("households of all categories")

Yuan documents use the phrase zhuse huji for the many kinds of registered households. Besides ordinary farmers, they included military, relay-station, artisan, Confucian, medical, salt-making, hunting and falconry, and boat households.

There were also registers for Buddhist monks, Daoists, Christian clergy, Muslim clergy and ortogh merchants.

This slide gives you the second half of Review Question 5: the "hereditary occupational categories." Keep these separate from the four ethnic labels. A family could be Nanren by origin and also registered as a military household or a Confucian household.

The basic idea is simple. The state wanted certain jobs done forever: soldiers for the army, horses and riders for the relay stations, salt for the salt monopoly, silk and metalwork for the court. So it attached each duty to a family, and the duty passed from father to son. Leaving a register was hard and usually needed official permission.

The tapestry is a real court commission, woven in silk around 1330–32. The Met identifies the small kneeling figures as Yuan rulers and their consorts. The deck does not claim to know which workshop made it, but objects like this are exactly what registered artisans were organized to produce.

Why this matters: hereditary registration served Mongol interests by guaranteeing labor and soldiers without bargaining with each generation. It also gave some groups, like Confucian households, real privileges.

Check yourself: What is the difference between a family's origin label and its household registration? Give one example of each.

Q5 story · A famous saying, checked

"Ninth Scholars, Tenth Beggars" Began as a Bitter Joke, Not a Yuan Law

A great wit, mocking Confucians, said: "Under the statutes of our Great Yuan, people come in ten grades. First officials, second clerks. Seventh artisans, eighth prostitutes, ninth Confucians, tenth beggars."Xie Fangde, a Song loyalist who refused to serve the Yuan, in a farewell preface dated 1288. Translated for this deck.
Who said it. Xie himself presents the list as a jester's joke. He used it to complain that scholars had lost their old prestige.
A second version. A similar ten-grade list appears in the Xinshi, a loyalist text whose authenticity many scholars doubt.
What the laws show. No surviving Yuan code ranks people in ten grades. Registered Confucian households in fact enjoyed labor exemptions.

The real grievance was loss of status and office for scholars, especially in the south. That story belongs to Lecture 3.

×

The Xinshi (Heart History)

This anti-Mongol text is credited to the Song loyalist Zheng Sixiao. It was supposedly sealed in an iron box, sunk in a well in Suzhou, and found in 1638, near the end of the Ming dynasty.

Several Qing scholars already suspected it was a late Ming forgery. Use anything quoted from it with care.

This slide gives you a famous saying that you will see repeated online and in older books: under the Yuan, scholars ranked ninth, just above beggars. It is a good test of reading a source carefully.

The earliest datable version comes from Xie Fangde, a Song official who fought the Mongols and later starved himself to death rather than serve them. Read his words closely. He does not say the Yuan passed such a law. He says a "great wit" made the joke to mock Confucians. Xie used the joke to protest how far scholars had fallen.

The other version is in the Xinshi, a text attributed to another loyalist, Zheng Sixiao. Its strange discovery story in 1638 makes many scholars suspect a forgery.

Meanwhile the real Yuan laws point the other way. Registered Confucian households were excused from many labor duties. The grievance was real, but it was about lost careers and prestige, not a legal ranking below prostitutes.

Why this matters: one bitter remark by an angry loyalist is not a description of a whole society. Q5 answers are stronger when they use rules and cases rather than slogans.

Check yourself: Who does Xie Fangde say made up the ten-grade list, and why does that matter for how we use it?

Q5 story · Semu financiers · 1262–1282

Khubilai's Finance Minister Ahmad Raised Revenue, Made Enemies, and Was Murdered in 1282

Rise. Ahmad, a Muslim from Fanākat in Central Asia, ran Khubilai's finances from 1262. He expanded state monopolies on salt, iron and other goods.
Enemies. Chinese officials and the heir apparent accused him of greed, of selling offices, and of placing his sons and allies in power.
Murder. In April 1282, while Khubilai was away at Shangdu, a Chinese officer named Wang Zhu lured Ahmad out at night and killed him with a bronze mace.
Wang Zhu was right to kill him.Khubilai, as reported in Ahmad's biography in the Yuan shi, ch. 205, which places him among the "treacherous ministers." Translated for this deck.

Hostile sources. Nearly everything we know about Ahmad comes from his enemies. Read the charges as accusations.

×

Who tells Ahmad's story?

The Yuan shi was written by Confucian scholars of the early Ming, who disliked "profit-seeking" ministers and placed Ahmad among the "treacherous ministers". A long account of the murder in Marco Polo's book appears only in Ramusio's Italian edition of 1559, and it says the Chinese hated being ruled by "Saracens."

The Persian historian Rashid al-Din also describes the affair. All of these writers had reasons to tell it their way.

This slide gives you a real person behind the label semu. Ahmad (often called Ahmad Fanākatī, after his hometown near modern Tashkent) was one of the most powerful men in Yuan China for twenty years. His job was to raise money for an emperor who was building a capital, fighting the Song and paying princes.

He was good at it, and that made him hated. Chinese officials trained in Confucian ideals saw tax-raising ministers as greedy. They accused Ahmad of corruption, of selling offices and of filling the government with his relatives. Some charges were probably true. But notice who is speaking.

After the murder, Khubilai first had Wang Zhu executed. Then, the official history reports, investigations exposed Ahmad's crimes, and Khubilai said the words on this slide. The same biography reports that he had Ahmad's body dug up and thrown to the dogs. These are reports in a hostile official history, so read them as the Ming historians' version.

Do not assume all finance ministers were semu. One of Ahmad's successors, Lu Shirong, was Chinese, and he was executed in 1285. The later minister Sangha was executed in 1291. Running Yuan finances was a dangerous job for anyone.

Why this matters: Ahmad shows how the Yuan used semu experts to raise revenue, and how those experts became lightning rods for Chinese resentment.

Check yourself: Why should you be careful about believing every accusation against Ahmad?

Q5 story · One script for the state · 1269

Khubilai Ordered a New Script So Official Documents Could Write Every Language

The maker. The Tibetan lama Phags-pa designed the script from Tibetan letters, and Khubilai made it official in 1269.
The goal. One alphabet could spell Mongolian, Chinese and other languages, so edicts, seals and passes looked the same across Yuan China.
The limits. Officials kept using Chinese characters and the older Uyghur-style Mongolian script, and Phags-pa faded after the Yuan fell.

This seal is a small picture of Yuan rule: a Chinese title, written in a Tibetan-based script, for a Mongol state.

×

Phags-pa Lama (1235–1280)

Phags-pa was a leader of the Sakya school of Tibetan Buddhism. Khubilai made him his imperial preceptor, the chief Buddhist cleric of the realm, and gave him authority over Buddhist affairs and Tibet.

The script named after him is sometimes called the "square script" because of its blocky letters.

This slide gives you the Phags-pa script, which you first saw on the travel pass in Lecture 1. Here it helps answer Q5, because writing was part of how the Yuan ranked and ruled people.

Khubilai wanted a single "state script" that belonged to the dynasty rather than to any one conquered people. In 1269 he ordered that Phags-pa be used for official edicts. Because it was an alphabet, clerks could use it to spell Chinese words by sound, as on this seal, whose letters spell a Chinese military title.

Be careful about scope. The script was ordered for Yuan official documents. It was not imposed across the other Mongol khanates in Iran or Central Asia, and even in Yuan China most people never learned it. Chinese characters remained the working script of local government.

Why this matters: a state script showed who stood at the top. Its limited use also shows that the Yuan could not run China without Chinese writing and Chinese clerks.

Check yourself: Why might Khubilai want a script that was neither Chinese nor the older Mongolian one?

Q8 story · Feeding the capital · 1280s–1290s

Grain Reached Dadu by a Rebuilt Grand Canal and by Fleets That Sailed the Open Sea

A straighter canal. Yuan engineers cut new channels across Shandong in 1283 and 1289, so boats no longer had to detour west through the old Sui route.
The last link. In 1293 Guo Shoujing finished the Tonghui canal, which brought barges right into Dadu.
Pirates turned admirals. Zhu Qing and Zhang Xuan, former smugglers, led sea transport of southern rice to the north from 1283.

Scale. The Yuan shi reports more than three million shi of grain carried by sea in peak years, though storms sank many ships.

×

Sea transport (haiyun)

Fleets loaded tax rice near the Yangzi mouth and sailed north along the coast to Zhigu, near modern Tianjin. From there the grain went on to Dadu.

The sea route was faster and cheaper than the canal, but much riskier. Zhu Qing and Zhang Xuan grew enormously rich and were brought down in 1303.

This slide gives you the plumbing of the Yuan empire. The capital, Dadu (modern Beijing), sat in the dry north, but the rice surplus grew in the Yangzi delta, more than a thousand kilometers south. Every Chinese dynasty with a northern capital faced this problem.

The old Grand Canal of the Sui dynasty bent far west toward Luoyang. The Yuan straightened it by cutting the Jizhou and Huitong canals through Shandong, and Guo Shoujing, the engineer and astronomer you will meet again, designed the Tonghui canal into the city. The modern canal largely follows this Yuan line.

The canal's upper sections were often short of water, so the Yuan also tried something bold: shipping grain by sea. Its organizers, Zhu Qing and Zhang Xuan, had been smugglers and pirates on the coast. The grain figures come from the Yuan shi treatise on food and money. Treat them as official records of what was planned and delivered, with losses that the same source admits.

The bridge in the photo stands on the old canal route in central Beijing. It has been rebuilt over the centuries, so it marks the place rather than showing a Yuan structure untouched.

Why this matters: the same routes that fed the capital also carried merchants, goods and ideas. Q8 is about networks, and these are the domestic ones.

Check yourself: Why did the Yuan need both a canal and a sea route to move grain north?

Q8 story · The great port · Zaytun

Quanzhou, Called Zaytun by Foreigners, Was One of the Busiest Ports on Earth

For one shipload of pepper that goes to Alexandria or elsewhere, destined for Christendom, there come a hundred such, aye and more too, to this haven of Zayton.Marco Polo's book, tr. Henry Yule. Polo wanted to astonish European readers, and his reliability is debated.
Pu Shougeng. This merchant from a Muslim family ran the port's trade office under the Song, and he handed Quanzhou to the Yuan in 1276.
Many faiths. Mosques, churches and a Hindu temple stood in the city, and their stones survive.
×

How far to trust Marco Polo

Polo's book was written down in prison in 1298 by a romance writer, Rustichello of Pisa, and copied in many different versions. Frances Wood has argued that Polo may never have reached China.

Most specialists think he did, because many details about Yuan salt, money and officials check out. But his numbers are often round and boastful. Use him as a vivid witness, not a statistician.

This slide gives you the port that linked Yuan China to the Indian Ocean. Chinese called it Quanzhou. Arab and Persian merchants called it Zaytun, which means "olive" in Arabic, though no olives grew there.

Quanzhou was already a great port under the Song. Its trade office was run by Pu Shougeng, from a family of Muslim merchants of Arab or Central Asian origin. When the Mongols advanced in 1276 he turned against the Song and surrendered the city, and the Yuan rewarded him with high office. His story shows how the Yuan relied on foreign merchant communities that were already in place.

The three objects come from different communities. The Qingjing Mosque gate is the city's best-known Islamic monument. Andrew of Perugia was a Franciscan friar sent from Rome who became bishop of Zaytun; the stone in the photo is a museum copy of his tombstone. The round relief comes from a Hindu temple built by Tamil merchants; its carvings were later reused at a Buddhist temple. Hundreds of Arabic, Persian and Syriac gravestones from the same period also survive in Quanzhou.

Polo's pepper line is surely exaggerated. A hundred ships to one is a storyteller's ratio, not a count. Ibn Battuta, whom you will meet soon, also called Zaytun one of the largest harbors in the world, so the general picture holds.

Why this matters: Q8 asks how Eurasian networks shaped trade. Quanzhou is the place where those networks touched Chinese soil.

Check yourself: Name two religious communities that left evidence in Yuan Quanzhou.

Q8 story · From Dadu to Rome · 1270s–1294

Rabban Sauma Left China as a Pilgrim and Reached Europe as a Mongol Ambassador

Pilgrims. In the 1270s Sauma and his younger disciple Markos, Christians of the Church of the East, set out from near Dadu for Jerusalem.
A surprise election. War blocked the road, and in 1281 Markos was chosen to lead their whole church as Patriarch Yahballaha III.
An embassy. In 1287 the Ilkhan Arghun, the Mongol ruler of Iran, sent Sauma west to seek allies against the Mamluks of Egypt.
Europe. He met the Byzantine emperor, Philip IV of France and Edward I of England, and in 1288 Pope Nicholas IV.
×

Church of the East

The Church of the East was an Asian Christian church with its own patriarch, based in Iraq and Iran. It used Syriac in its services. Europeans often called it "Nestorian."

It had communities across Central Asia and among some Mongol and Turkic peoples, including the Öngüt and Kerait. Several Mongol princesses were Christians of this church.

This slide gives you the traveler who went the "wrong" way, from China to Europe. Rabban Sauma ("rabban" means master or teacher) was born in or near Dadu, probably to an Öngüt or Uyghur Christian family. He became a monk, and his student Markos joined him on a pilgrimage to Jerusalem.

They never got there. Fighting between the Mongols and the Mamluks closed the road. But in Iraq, church leaders elected Markos as patriarch in 1281, partly because he knew Mongol language and customs. Now the head of the Church of the East was a man from Inner Mongolia.

The Mongol ruler of Iran, Arghun, wanted European help against the Mamluks of Egypt, so he sent Sauma west in 1287. Sauma visited Constantinople, Rome, Genoa and Paris, met Edward I in Gascony, and returned to Rome, where the new pope gave him communion in 1288. No alliance came of it. Sauma died in Baghdad in 1294.

His story survives in a Syriac account of the two men's lives, which says it drew on Sauma's own notes. It is friendly to its heroes, so read its praise of Sauma's reception with that in mind. Arghun's 1289 letter in the French archives is independent evidence that the embassy happened. The red seals are in Chinese characters, a sign of links between the Ilkhans and the Yuan court.

Why this matters: Mongol power linked China, Iran and Europe closely enough that one monk could cross all three. Sauma also shows that exchange was not only about trade. It moved people, faiths and diplomacy.

Q8 story · A Moroccan in China · c. 1345

Ibn Battuta Marveled at Zaytun's Harbor and at Paper Money That Replaced Gold and Silver

The harbour of Zaitun is one of the greatest in the world. I am wrong: it is the greatest! I have seen there about one hundred first-class junks together.Ibn Battuta's Rihla, dictated in Morocco in 1355, tr. Henry Yule.
Their buying and selling is carried on exclusively by means of pieces of paper, each of the size of the palm of the hand, and stamped with the sultan's seal.Same work, tr. H. A. R. Gibb. He adds that no shop would take a gold or silver coin until it was changed for paper.
Doubts. His accounts of northern China are vague, and some of his events never happened, so scholars question how far north he went.
×

Did he really go everywhere he says?

Ibn Battuta dictated his book years later to a scribe, Ibn Juzayy, who polished it and borrowed some passages from older travel books.

His description of the coast fits other evidence well. His trip to Khanbaliq (Dadu) is more doubtful: he reports that the emperor was killed fighting a rebel relative, and no such event happened in the 1340s.

This slide gives you the most traveled man of the fourteenth century. Ibn Battuta was a legal scholar from Tangier in Morocco. He spent nearly thirty years on the road, served as a judge in Delhi, and was sent by the sultan of Delhi on an embassy to China. After a shipwreck and many detours he reached the Chinese coast around 1345.

Both quotations come from his Rihla (travel book). The first is in Henry Yule's older translation, the second in H. A. R. Gibb's. Ibn Battuta found Muslim communities in the port cities, with their own judges and mosques, which made China feel partly familiar to him.

His report on paper money is one of the best outside descriptions of Yuan currency. He even explains that worn notes could be exchanged for new ones at an office like a mint. The photo shows a real Yuan note in the National Museum of China. Remember from Lecture 1 that paper money worked across Yuan China but failed when Mongol Iran tried it in 1294.

Treat his northern travels with care. His book was written down long afterward, partly by an editor, and his story about the emperor's death in battle is wrong. Most scholars accept that he saw the southern ports.

Why this matters: Ibn Battuta shows that Muslim merchant networks stretched from Morocco to China. Those same networks carried goods like cobalt and porcelain, which is where Q8 goes next.

Q8 story · Persian blue, Chinese clay

Jingdezhen Potters Painted Fine White Porcelain with Imported Cobalt Blue

The clay. Jingdezhen, in Jiangxi, had hard white porcelain stone, and in 1278 the Yuan set up a porcelain bureau there.
The blue. Persian potters had long painted with cobalt. Scientific analyses support imported cobalt for much Yuan blue, with Iranian deposits among the proposed sources.
The carriers. Muslim merchant networks connected Mongol Iran, Indian Ocean ports, and Yuan China, helping move pigment east and finished wares west.
The result. By the 1320s to 1350s Jingdezhen was producing confident, crowded blue designs on a large scale.

Not instant fame at home. A Chinese connoisseur's guide of 1388 still called the new blue-painted wares "very vulgar."

×

Fuliang Porcelain Bureau

Jingdezhen lay in Fuliang county. The Yuan shi records that a Fuliang Porcelain Bureau was set up in 1278 to supervise production for the court.

Most kilns were still private workshops. They made wares for the court, for Chinese buyers, and increasingly for export.

×

Cobalt and its fingerprints

Cobalt oxide turns blue when fired. Laboratory studies find that most Yuan blue is rich in iron and low in manganese, which matches ores from Iran better than most Chinese ores. Exact sources are still debated.

A Persian treatise on pottery written in 1301 by Abu'l-Qasim Kashani describes cobalt mined near Kashan. Later Chinese texts call imported cobalt "Muslim blue."

This slide gives you the heart of Review Question 8. Blue-and-white porcelain brought together a Chinese material and a West Asian color.

The material was Chinese. Jingdezhen potters had a white porcelain body, high-firing kilns and centuries of skill. Under the Song they were famous for pale bluish-white wares. Earlier Chinese blue-decorated ceramics existed as far back as the Tang, but Yuan Jingdezhen developed the major production tradition that became internationally influential. The Yuan government took an interest early, setting up a porcelain bureau in 1278.

The color was a West Asian specialty. Potters in Iran had used cobalt blue for centuries. Scientific analyses support imported cobalt for much Yuan blue, with Iranian deposits among the proposed sources. Exact sources are still debated, and some local cobalt may have been used too, so say "much," not "all."

The connection was the network. Under Mongol rule, Muslim merchant networks connected Mongol Iran, Indian Ocean ports, and Yuan China. The Yuan state used Muslim merchants as ortogh partners, and Quanzhou had large Muslim communities. Those networks helped move pigment east to the kilns in Jiangxi and finished wares west.

The 1388 comment comes from the Gegu yaolun, a guide for Chinese collectors. Its scorn reminds you that blue-and-white was at first made largely for export, and Chinese elite taste took time to change.

Textbook note: Ebrey (p. 205) calls the Yuan decoration a new style, which is right; Tang blue-and-white came earlier. Source beyond Ebrey: Regina Krahl, "Tang Blue-and-White," in Shipwrecked (2010).

Why this matters: this is a case study of Q8 in one object. Each ingredient came from a different part of the Mongol world.

Q8 story · Made for West Asian buyers

Big Dishes and Dense Designs Suited Buyers in West Asia

Size. Dishes up to about 45 cm across fit West Asian meals, where many diners shared food from one platter.
Style. Some shapes and decorative arrangements, such as dense bands, echo West Asian metalwork and suited West Asian buyers, while potters also drew on established Chinese motifs such as dragons, phoenixes and lotus.
Survivors. Many of the finest Yuan pieces survive in the Ardabil Shrine and Topkapı Palace collections.
It is exported to India and other countries, even reaching as far as our own lands in the West.Ibn Battuta on Chinese porcelain, tr. Gibb. He wrongly thought it was made only near the ports.
×

The Ardabil Shrine collection

In the early 1600s the Safavid ruler Shah Abbas I gave his Chinese porcelain to the shrine of Sheikh Safi al-Din at Ardabil, in northwest Iran. Most of the surviving pieces are now in the National Museum of Iran.

The gift came long after the Yuan, so the collection shows that Yuan pieces were treasured in Iran for centuries. It does not show exactly when each piece arrived.

×

The Topkapı Palace collection

Long after the Yuan, Ottoman sultans gathered more than ten thousand Chinese ceramics through purchase, gifts and war booty. The collection is kept today at Topkapı Palace in Istanbul. A few dozen are Yuan blue-and-white.

In the 1950s the American scholar John Alexander Pope compared Topkapı and Ardabil pieces with the dated David Vases to work out which ones were Yuan.

This slide gives you the buyers. Much early blue-and-white was shaped by what customers in West Asia wanted.

Look at the scale and the decoration. Huge dishes suited communal meals where food was served on one great platter. Some shapes and the dense arrangement of decoration in bands echo West Asian metalwork and suited West Asian buyers. The motifs themselves, such as dragons, phoenixes and lotus, were long established in China. Think of it as selective borrowing and adaptation: Jingdezhen potters took what sold abroad and combined it with their own repertoire. Notice the octagonal jar: someone in Iran added brass mounts to it, a sign of how prized it was. Its photo was taken at a 2012 exhibition of British-owned pieces in Beijing, so the deck does not claim to know where it is kept today.

The Ardabil and Topkapı collections are the two greatest storehouses of Yuan blue-and-white outside China. Be careful with dates. Both collections were assembled later, by Safavid shahs and Ottoman sultans, so they prove long-lasting demand. They do not tell you that a particular dish was shipped to Iran in, say, 1340. The Topkapı photo is labeled honestly too: it shows Chinese blue-and-white there, but the deck does not claim each dish is Yuan.

Ibn Battuta's line shows the reach of the trade, all the way to Morocco. His claim that porcelain was made only near Zaytun and Canton is wrong. He probably saw it in the ports where it was shipped.

Why this matters: Q8 asks about international trade. Here the market abroad helped decide what Chinese potters made.

Q8 story · A dated pair · 1351

The David Vases Carry a Date, 1351, That Anchors the Whole Story of Yuan Blue-and-White

Zhang Wenjin, a disciple of the gods, gladly offers an incense burner and a pair of flower vases, praying for the peace of his whole family. The fourth month of Zhizheng 11.Inscription on the vases, shortened and adapted from the British Museum's translation.

Why it matters. Few Yuan pieces carry a date, so experts compare others with these two.

×

A yardstick for dating

Before the 1950s, many collectors assumed that fine blue-and-white began in the Ming dynasty. The dated David Vases proved that mature blue-and-white existed by 1351.

John Alexander Pope used them to pick out a "fourteenth-century style" in the Topkapı and Ardabil collections. Pieces that match their painting and shapes are now called Yuan.

This slide gives you the most important pair of vases in the history of Chinese porcelain. They are named after Sir Percival David, the collector who brought them together, and they are now in the British Museum.

The neck inscription records that a man named Zhang Wenjin, from Yushan county in Jiangxi, offered the vases with an incense burner to a temple, praying for his family's safety. The date, the fourth month of the eleventh year of the Zhizheng reign, corresponds to 1351. The quotation is shortened from the museum's translation, so check the full version if you use it in writing.

Notice two things. First, these vases were made for a Chinese temple altar, not for export. By 1351 at least some Chinese buyers wanted blue-and-white, even if elite connoisseurs still sniffed at it. Second, the dragons, phoenixes and bands are painted with great confidence. This was not an experiment; it was an established craft.

Because so few pieces carry dates, scholars use these two as a yardstick. If a dish now kept in Istanbul or Tehran has the same style of painting, it is probably from the same decades.

Why this matters: for Q8, the vases prove that the new style was fully developed under the Yuan, and they show it was used inside China as well as abroad.

Q8 story · Measuring the sky · 1267–1281

Persian Astronomers Brought Instruments to Khubilai, and Guo Shoujing Built a New Calendar

1267. Jamal al-Din, a Persian astronomer, presented seven instruments and a calendar to Khubilai.
1271. The court set up a separate Muslim astronomical office beside the Chinese one, and the two kept their own records.
1276–1280. Guo Shoujing and Wang Xun built new instruments, sent observers to stations across Yuan China, and produced the Shoushi calendar.

Borrowing or parallel work? Historians debate how much Guo learned from the Persian instruments. His methods and calendar were mostly Chinese in design.

×

Jamal al-Din

A Persian astronomer who probably had ties to the great Ilkhanid observatory at Maragha in Iran. The Yuan shi lists his seven "Western Region" instruments, including an armillary sphere, a globe of the earth and an astrolabe.

He also helped compile a geography of the whole empire.

×

Shoushi calendar (1280)

The name means "Granting the Seasons." It set the year at 365.2425 days, the same value as the Gregorian calendar adopted in Europe in 1582.

The Ming dynasty kept it, with small changes, under the name Datong calendar.

This slide gives you an exchange of knowledge, not goods. A Chinese emperor needed an accurate calendar, because predicting eclipses and seasons showed that he ruled with Heaven's favor.

Jamal al-Din arrived from the Islamic world and gave Khubilai a set of instruments in 1267. The court soon ran two astronomical offices, one Chinese and one Muslim, side by side. That is a typical Yuan arrangement: experts from different traditions working in parallel for the same ruler.

Guo Shoujing was a brilliant engineer (you met his canal on an earlier slide). With Wang Xun he designed new bronze instruments, including the abridged armilla, a simplified set of rings that made sightings easier. The Yuan shi reports observations at twenty-seven stations. Their new calendar was issued in 1280 and took effect in 1281.

Be precise about the photo. The instrument at Purple Mountain is a Ming copy cast in the fifteenth century, based on Guo's design. Guo's original does not survive.

Did Guo copy the Persians? Some historians see influence in his instrument designs; others find little direct borrowing. A safe answer: Chinese and Islamic astronomical traditions worked alongside one another at the Yuan court; the extent of particular borrowings remains debated.

Why this matters: Q8 asks how networks influenced Chinese culture. Here experts from different traditions met through people, not just objects.

Q8 story · Doctors and historians · 1270–1318

Muslim Doctors Served the Yuan Court, and a Persian Vizier Wrote a History of China

Doctors in Dadu. In 1270 the Yuan created an office for Muslim physicians who prepared Western medicines for the court and the guards.
A go-between. Bolad, a Mongol official trained at Khubilai's court, went to Iran in 1285 and stayed, sharing what he knew about China and the Mongols.
A world history. Rashid al-Din, chief minister of Mongol Iran, used Bolad and Chinese informants to write a history of China into his Compendium of Chronicles.
Medicine going west. Under Rashid's direction, Chinese medical texts on the pulse were translated into Persian in a work called the Tanksuqnama.
×

Guanghui Office ("Office for Extending Kindness")

The Yuan shi records this office, founded in 1270, as responsible for "Muslim medicines" for the palace and the imperial guards. Muslim pharmacies were later set up in the two capitals.

A Chinese-language collection called Muslim Prescriptions (Huihui yaofang), which survives only in part, is full of Arabic and Persian drug names.

This slide gives you two more kinds of exchange: medicine and historical knowledge. They show that the Mongol networks were not only about trade.

At the Yuan court, the Guanghui Office employed Muslim doctors and pharmacists. Like the two astronomy offices, it sat beside the Chinese medical bureau rather than replacing it. The court used both.

Knowledge also flowed west. Bolad was a Mongol who had served Khubilai in Chinese-style offices. He was sent as an envoy to Iran and never went home. The historian Thomas Allsen, in Culture and Conquest in Mongol Eurasia, calls him one of the key cultural brokers of the age. He worked closely with Rashid al-Din, a Persian physician who became chief minister of the Ilkhans and organized the first history to cover China, India, Europe and the Islamic world together.

Look at the painting. Rashid's history of China lines up emperors in rows, a layout that seems to imitate Chinese printed chronicles. The painter was Persian, so these are Persian images of Chinese rulers, made far from China. They show how China was pictured in Iran, not what these emperors looked like.

Why this matters: Q8 focuses on porcelain, but a strong answer can mention that the same networks moved medicine, astronomy and history. That shows you see the pattern, not just one object.

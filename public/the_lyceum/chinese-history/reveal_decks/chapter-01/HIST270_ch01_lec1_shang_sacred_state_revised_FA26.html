The puzzle · Old bones, older writing

Bones Sold as Medicine Turned Out to Hold China's Oldest Writing

Dragon bones. Chinese druggists ground old bones into medicine.
Carved signs. Around 1899, scholars noticed writing on some of them.
The source. Farmers near Anyang had been digging them up.
The surprise. The writing named kings of the Shang dynasty.

The puzzle: how did Shang kings hold power, and how do these bones help us know?

This slide gives you the detective story that opened up Shang history. For centuries, farmers near the town of Anyang in north China plowed up old bones and turtle shells and sold them to drugstores as dragon bones, which were ground into medicine. Around 1899 a scholar noticed that some pieces carried ancient writing. Your textbook says only that scholars linked the bones to the Shang kings after 1898; the famous version about a sick scholar named Wang Yirong is told in the Pierian Spring section at the end.

Once scholars could read some of the writing, they found the names of Shang kings that already appeared in much later Chinese histories. That proved the Shang were real. Since then, rubbings of about 48,000 bone fragments have been published.

The photo is real. It shows pit YH127 at Anyang, found in 1936, packed with thousands of inscribed turtle shells. You are looking at a royal archive about 3,000 years old.

Why this matters: these bones are the Shang kings' own records. Almost everything in Act II of this deck comes from them.

A rubbing is a copy made by laying damp paper over a carved surface and dabbing it with ink. The flat surface turns black and the carved lines stay white, so faint writing becomes easy to read.

Loess

Loess is fine, yellowish soil blown in by the wind over thousands of years. It covers much of north China, is very fertile, and is soft enough to farm with simple tools.

It also washes into the Yellow River and gives the river its color and its name.

This slide gives you the stage the Shang story plays on. Find the Yellow River across the north of the map and the Yangzi River through the middle. The Shang lived in the north, on the plain where the Yellow River leaves the hills.

The north is colder and drier; many areas get less than 20 inches of rain a year, so the crops were millet (a hardy grain) and later wheat. The soil is loess, soft enough to work with stone tools. The catch is the river: it carries so much silt that its bed rises, and once people built dikes, a broken dike meant a huge flood. The south is warmer and wetter, good for rice and for travel by boat.

The map is a modern relief map, not an ancient one. Look at the brown and white band of mountains and deserts to the west and north. Your textbook says these mountains, deserts and grasslands separated China from the other early civilizations, which is why Chinese writing grew up on its own. (That is Review Question 1. The next slide tells that part of the story, and a Your Turn slide closes Act I.)

A good way to say it: the environment did not dictate history, but it set the menu of choices. Dry northern plains rewarded millet farmers who could organize to hold back floods; the wet south rewarded rice farmers and boatmen.

Why this matters: a farming people living close together on a flood-prone plain needed leaders who could organize labor and defense. Keep that in mind when you meet the Shang kings.

Q1 story · A world apart

Mountains, Deserts and Grasslands Kept China Apart, but Never Completely

Barriers. Himalayas and Tibet to the west; deserts to the northwest.
Distance. Far from Mesopotamia, Egypt and India.
Steppe. Northern grasslands linked China to herders, not cities.
Borrowing. Wheat and chariots still arrived from West Asia.
Result. Chinese civilization grew mostly on its own path.

This slide gives you the second half of Review Question 1 in your Reading Guide, which asks how geography shaped early Chinese farming and China’s contact with outside cultures. Your textbook says that mountains, deserts and grasslands separated China proper (the farming heartland) from the other early centers of civilization in Mesopotamia, Egypt and India.

Look at the map. To the west rise the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau, the highest land on earth. To the northwest lie the Taklamakan and Gobi deserts. To the north stretch the grasslands, or steppe, where people lived by herding animals rather than farming. Inside these barriers, the Yellow River and Yangzi River valleys formed a large, rich region where farming peoples could trade and borrow from each other.

But isolation was never total. Wheat was first grown in West Asia, and the horse-drawn chariot reached the Shang around 1200 BCE through the steppe. So the best answer is “mostly separate, with important borrowing.” That is why Chinese writing, cooking and religion developed along their own lines.

A caution about one piece of evidence: your textbook mentions Shang silk found in Egypt. That find, from a burial of about 1000 BCE, is now doubted by specialists, so do not lean on it.

One more caution. Historical “China proper” was smaller than the modern country, and its edges shifted constantly, so this isolation argument applies to the farming heartland, not to a fixed modern map.

Why this matters: geography explains why later Chinese thinkers often saw their land as the center of the civilized world.

Setting · Before the kings

Farming Villages Came First; Kings and Walled Towns Came Thousands of Years Later

8000Farmers in north China begin growing millet (all dates BCE).
4000Distinct regional cultures appear.
2500Some settlements build walls; burials show rich and poor.
2000Bronze Age civilization begins soon after this date.
1500The Shang dynasty rises, about this time.

This slide gives you the long road to the Shang. All the dates are BCE (before the Common Era), so the numbers get smaller as time moves forward. The first firm evidence of farming comes from north China around 8000 BCE, about ten thousand years ago, when villagers at sites such as Cishan were growing broomcorn millet. Along the Yangzi, people were cultivating rice by about 6000 BCE, though fully domesticated rice took thousands more years to develop. Over the next four thousand years, villages multiplied and different regions developed their own styles of pottery and burial.

A note on the date: your textbook says China had agriculture “from about 10,000 B.C.E.” Archaeologists now place the first true farming a little later, around 8000 BCE; people around 10,000 BCE were already gathering wild grains and making pottery, but they had not yet become farmers. The textbook’s round number may also be a slip for “about 10,000 years ago.” For an exam, “about 8000 BCE, millet in the north” is the safer answer.

Two clues tell archaeologists that society was changing by about 2500 BCE. First, some settlements built walls, which means there was something worth defending and someone able to organize the work. Second, some graves were much richer than others, a sign that a few families had become powerful. Soon after 2000 BCE people began making bronze, a mix of copper and tin, and the first true states appeared.

The photo shows loess hills in north China today. Farmers have cut terraces into this soft soil for thousands of years.

Why this matters: the Shang did not appear out of nowhere. They built on thousands of years of farming villages and growing inequality, which helps explain how a few people could rule so many.

Setting · The first dynasties

Old Books Name the Xia as China’s First Dynasty, but Only the Shang Can Be Proven

Tradition. Early Chinese texts list a Xia dynasty and its kings.
No proof. Early Bronze Age sites have no writing to confirm the Xia.
Shang proven. Oracle bones name Shang kings known from later histories.
ca. 1500–1045 BCE. The Shang ruled the North China Plain.

This slide gives you a key idea about evidence. Chinese tradition says the first dynasty was the Xia, and later books even list its kings. Some of the earliest Bronze Age sites, like Erlitou, may be connected to the Xia, but they contain no writing, so nobody can prove or disprove it.

The Shang are different. They are known from two kinds of texts: transmitted texts, which were copied and recopied for centuries, and excavated texts, which archaeologists dig up, like the oracle bones. When both kinds agree on the names of kings, historians can be confident. Your textbook says no one today doubts that the Shang existed.

The photo shows real early bronze and pottery wine cups from Erlitou. Notice the long spouts and thin legs; this shape, called a jue, was used to warm and pour wine in rituals. The Shang kept making it for their own ancestor rituals.

Why this matters: history needs evidence. A dynasty that left writing can be studied in a way that a legendary one cannot.

Rammed earth

Rammed earth (hangtu) is soil poured into wooden frames and pounded, layer by layer, until it is almost as hard as concrete.

The Shang used it for palace platforms, city walls and tomb fill. It needed little material but huge amounts of labor.

This slide gives you the city at the heart of the story. Tradition says the Shang kings ruled from five successive cities. The best known is the last one, near the modern city of Anyang. The Shang called it Yin, so the site today is called Yinxu, the Ruins of Yin. The kings ruled from here from about 1200 BCE until the dynasty fell in 1045 BCE.

At the center stood large palaces, temples and altars. They were built of wood on platforms of rammed earth, pounded soil as hard as concrete. The wood rotted away long ago, so archaeologists find only the earth platforms and the holes where pillars stood.

The photo shows the excavated palace area today. The red posts are modern markers placed where the ancient wooden pillars stood. Chinese archaeologists dug here from 1928 to 1937, one of the first great excavations in China.

Why this matters: nearly all the evidence in this lecture, the oracle bones and the royal tombs, comes from this one city. When historians talk about the Shang, they mostly mean late Shang Anyang.

Setting · Kings at war

Shang Kings Were War Chiefs Who Fought, Hunted and Took Captives

Armies. Kings regularly sent 3,000 to 5,000 men on campaigns.
Hunts. Between wars, royal hunts could last for months.
Shifting map. Vassals turned into enemies; enemies became allies.
Captives. Prisoners could be enslaved or sacrificed, a key source of royal wealth.
Chariots. Horse-drawn chariots came into use around 1200 BCE.

This slide gives you the first side of a Shang king: the warrior. Your textbook calls the Shang kings military chieftains. They regularly sent out armies of three to five thousand men, and when they were not at war they went on hunts that could last for months. A vassal is a lesser ruler who promises loyalty to a stronger one. Shang vassals often rebelled, and enemies sometimes became allies, so the map kept changing.

Bronze gave Shang warriors better weapons: bronze-tipped spears and dagger-axes. Around 1200 BCE they began to use spoke-wheeled chariots, pulled by two or four horses, which gave commanders a moving platform to direct their troops. Prisoners of war mattered a great deal: they could be made slaves or killed as sacrifices, and your textbook calls war booty, especially captives, an important source of the king’s revenue.

The photo is a real chariot burial at Yinxu. The wood rotted long ago, but it left its shape in the hardened soil, so you can see the spokes of the wheels. The horses were buried with it. (Where chariots came from is a Lecture 2 story.)

Why this matters: armies alone did not explain why people obeyed the king. Many chiefs could fight. The next act shows the power only a Shang king had.

×

Di (帝)

Di was the highest god the Shang worshipped. Di could command rain, thunder and wind, the forces that decided whether crops grew.

Di was remote. The royal ancestors, who were closer to the living king, could ask Di for help on his behalf.

This slide gives you the Shang view of the world, which you need before the oracle bones make sense. The Shang believed the world was full of powerful spirits. At the top was Di, a high god who could command rain, thunder and wind. The king also made offerings to nature spirits: the sun and moon, the Yellow River, the winds of the four directions and certain mountains.

Most important of all were the royal ancestors, the dead kings and queens of the Shang line. They were not resting quietly; they were active. They could send curses and dreams, help the king in battle, and plead with the distant Di. Worshipping them is called ancestor worship: honoring and feeding dead family members so they will help the living.

The bronze cauldron is a real Shang ritual vessel. Food for the spirits was cooked or offered in vessels like this. The staring animal face on its side is called a taotie; nobody is sure what it meant.

Why this matters: this belief system is the first piece of Review Question 2. If ancestors control the harvest and the war, then whoever speaks to the ancestors holds real power.

Q2 story · Reading the cracks

To Question the Ancestors, Diviners Heated Bones Until They Cracked

Prepare a cattle shoulder blade or a turtle under-shell.
State the question aloud to the ancestors or to Di.
Apply heat until the bone cracks with a sharp pop.
The king reads the cracks as the spirits’ answer.
Carve the question, and sometimes the outcome, into the bone.

This slide gives you how an oracle bone worked. Diviners used the flat shoulder blades of cattle (called scapulae) or the flat under-shells of turtles (called plastrons). They carved small hollows into the back so the bone would crack in a controlled way. Then they announced the question and pressed heat into a hollow. The bone cracked, often with a sharp sound, and the shape of the crack was read as the answer. The Chinese character for divination, 卜 (bu), even looks like a crack.

After the reading, a scribe often carved the question into the bone, and sometimes what actually happened later. That is why these bones are written sources and not just ritual leftovers.

The image is a real plastron from Anyang. Look closely and you can see both the cracks and the neat columns of carved characters running beside them.

The next slide has a two-minute video that shows the whole process.

Why this matters: for Review Question 2, divination is the tool. It is how the king “talked” with the ancestors, and it left a written record of royal decisions.

This slide gives you the questions themselves. King Wu Ding ruled at Anyang around 1200 BCE, and more oracle bones survive from his reign than from any other. Your textbook lists what his diviners asked the high god Di or the ancestors about: rain, the harvest, military expeditions, dreams, floods, tribute payments, sacrifices and even a toothache.

The quotation comes from one of the most famous oracle bones. The diviner predicted that the childbirth of Lady Hao, one of Wu Ding’s wives, would be “good.” The king read the cracks himself and added conditions. The scapula in the picture is real and comes from Wu Ding’s reign. Each group of carved characters is a separate question.

Q2 story · Feeding the ancestors

The King Fed His Ancestors with Wine, Grain, Animals and Human Victims

Offerings. Millet, wine, cattle, sheep and grain went to the ancestors.
Human victims. Captives were offered to the royal ancestors too.
Bronze. Great vessels held the food and wine at sacrificial ceremonies.
Rank. Nobles owned ritual bronzes, but kings owned many more.

This slide gives you the other half of the relationship. Ancestors helped the living, but they had to be fed. The king addressed his ancestors in prayers and made offerings of millet, wine, cattle, sheep and grain. He also offered human victims, usually captives taken in war. Your textbook is plain about this, and Act III shows how large the practice became.

Most surviving Shang bronzes are ritual vessels: cups, goblets, steamers and cauldrons that held food and wine for the spirits. Both kings and nobles owned them, but kings had many more. So the number of bronzes a family owned showed its rank.

The photo shows a real sacrificial pit at Yinxu, the Shang capital at Anyang, now kept under protective glass. Several people were laid side by side in it; you can see their skulls and long bones. Victims like these were offerings to the royal ancestors, most of them captives taken in war. It is a hard image, and it is here because it is evidence: the oracle bones say the king offered human victims, and the ground at Anyang confirms it.

The largest bronze vessel of all, the Houmuwu ding, appears on the Your Turn slide at the end of this act. It weighs about 830 kilograms (your textbook gives an older figure of 875 kg). Lecture 2 tells how it was cast.

Why this matters: feeding the ancestors was a royal duty and a royal privilege. It tied religion to wealth, war captives and bronze, all things the king controlled.

Q2 story · The king as high priest

The King Was Also the High Priest, the Person Best Qualified to Reach the Spirits

High priest. Oracle bones show the king leading sacrifices to ancestors and Di.
His ancestors. The royal ancestors were the most powerful spirits of all.
Final word. The king himself often read the cracks and announced the meaning.

This slide gives you the key idea for Review Question 2. Your textbook says Shang power did not rest only on armies. The oracle bones show that the king was also the high priest: the person best qualified to offer sacrifices to the royal ancestors and to Di.

Why him? Because the most powerful ancestors were his ancestors, the dead kings of the Shang line. A noble family could honor its own dead, but the king spoke for the royal ancestors who could reach Di. And the king did not simply wait for the diviners: he often read the cracks himself and announced what they meant. The carved bones then kept a record of his questions and his predictions.

The picture is an AI-generated illustration, not evidence. Q2 story · Why religion meant power

Each Answered Prayer Made the King’s Right to Rule Look Stronger

Ask. The king consults the ancestors before acting.
Record. Scribes carve the question, and sometimes the result.
Proof. A good harvest or a victory shows his link works.
Excuses. Failures could be blamed on the wrong offering, not the king.
Result. No rival could claim to speak for the royal ancestors.

This slide gives you the reason religion and power fit together so well. Follow the cycle. Before a big decision, the king asks his ancestors. The answer is carved into bone. If the harvest is good or the war is won, that seems to prove that the king’s link to the spirits works, and his authority grows. If things go badly, the blame can fall on a missed offering or an angry ancestor, which calls for more ritual, not a new king.

Historians call this a feedback loop: each turn of the cycle makes the next turn stronger. A rival might raise an army, but he could not claim to speak for the royal ancestors. The scholar K. C. Chang argued that in early China, control of contact with the spirit world was the very basis of political authority. The Shang king is the clearest example.

Think it through: why is control over contact with the spirits also a political weapon? Because it made its own proof. A good harvest, a won battle or a survived illness all counted, after the fact, as evidence that the king’s channel to the ancestors worked, while failures were blamed on the wrong ritual, not on the system. Round after round, the system manufactured proof of the king’s fitness to rule. One careful word: older versions of this lecture called this a “monopoly.” Your textbook says the king was the person best qualified to reach the spirits, not the only one, so “advantage” is the safer word.

The image repeats the plastron from slide J on purpose. Every record like this was both a question and a reminder of who held the right to ask.

Why this matters: this is the heart of a strong answer to Review Question 2. Do not just list divination, ancestor worship and the king’s authority. Explain how each one fed the others.

This independent-study slide gives you the detective story behind slide B. For centuries, Chinese pharmacies sold “dragon bones,” ground up as medicine. Farmers near Anyang dug them up in their fields. A famous story says that in 1899 a scholar named Wang Yirong, sick with malaria, noticed ancient writing on bones bought for his medicine. Your textbook says more carefully that the bones were connected to the Shang “after 1898.” The Wang Yirong story may be partly legend, but scholars were collecting inscribed bones by around 1899–1900.

Dealers kept their source secret so they could control the price. Only around 1908 did scholars trace the bones to villages near Anyang. Scientific excavations there from 1928 to 1937 uncovered palace foundations, royal tombs and thousands more bones. In 1936, one pit called YH127 held about 17,000 pieces, a buried royal archive (a detail from the excavation record, not from your textbook).

Today scholars have published rubbings of about 48,000 fragments, the number your textbook gives.

Pierian Spring · What historians argue

Historians Agree Shang Power Was Sacred, but Argue About How Far to Push the Idea

David Keightley

Divination was how the king decided, and proved, his rule.

K. C. Chang

Control of contact with the spirits was the path to power.

Li Feng

Roderick Campbell

Violence and kinship held the Shang state together.

← Back to the lecture: why religion meant power (slide N)

This independent-study slide gives you the big arguments behind the story you just learned. Historians agree that religion sat at the center of Shang kingship, but they push the idea in different directions.

David Keightley, the leading Western scholar of oracle bones, argued that divination was a political tool as much as a religious one. It showed the king’s special access to the spirits, committed the court to decisions, and created written records, an early form of government paperwork. He also described Shang religion as a kind of ranked order of ancestors who received scheduled offerings, a mirror of the king’s court. K. C. Chang, in Art, Myth, and Ritual (1983), went further: whoever controlled contact with the spirit world, through ritual, divination and bronze vessels, controlled political authority. Your textbook is more careful, calling the king the person “best qualified” to make sacrifices, not the only one.

Li Feng, in Early China (2013), stresses that the Shang were one center among many regional cultures in contact with each other and with the steppe, which is why “independent development” and “borrowed chariots” can both be true. Roderick Campbell argues that violence and family ties together built the Shang state, with sacrifice as public proof of the king’s power over life and death.

The background essay linked from the title and sources slides explains these debates in more depth.

Why this matters: history is an argument about evidence. Knowing that scholars disagree helps you write answers that are confident but careful.

Background essay: the historians’ debates behind this lecture ↗

This slide gives you the sources behind the stories. Most of the facts in this deck come from your textbook, Chapter 1. The words of the oracle bones come from translations by David Keightley, the scholar who did more than anyone to make these inscriptions readable in English. They have been simplified for this deck, so check his book if you want the exact wording.

If you want to go deeper, Li Feng’s Early China is the clearest recent overview, and Roderick Campbell’s book explains how violence and family ties built the Shang state. The background essay linked at the bottom of this slide explains what historians still argue about: how “sacred” the Shang king really was, whether the followers in royal tombs chose to die, and why the oracle bones show us only the king’s view.

Why this matters: every claim in a history deck should be traceable to a source you could check yourself.

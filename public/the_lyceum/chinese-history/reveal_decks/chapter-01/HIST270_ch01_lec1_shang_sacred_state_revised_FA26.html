Carved signs. Around 1899, scholars noticed writing on some of them.
The source. Farmers near Anyang had been digging them up.
The surprise. The writing named kings of the Shang dynasty.

This slide gives you the detective story that opened up Shang history. For centuries, farmers near the town of Anyang in north China plowed up old bones and turtle shells and sold them to drugstores as dragon bones, which were ground into medicine. Around 1899 a scholar noticed that some pieces carried ancient writing. Your textbook says scholars linked the bones to the Shang kings after 1898 (p. 10). The famous story of a scholar named Wang Yirong comes from outside your textbook; it is told in the Pierian Spring at the end.

Once scholars could read some of the writing, they found the names of Shang kings that already appeared in much later Chinese histories. That proved the Shang were real. Since then, rubbings of about 48,000 bone fragments have been published.

The photo is real. It shows pit YH127 at Anyang, found in 1936, packed with thousands of inscribed turtle shells. You are looking at a royal archive about 3,000 years old.

Why this matters: these bones are the Shang kings' own records. Almost everything in Act II of this deck comes from them.

Rubbing

A rubbing is a copy made by laying damp paper over a carved surface and dabbing it with ink. The flat surface turns black and the carved lines stay white, so faint writing becomes easy to read.

Scholars have published rubbings of about 48,000 oracle bone fragments.

Loess

Loess is fine, yellowish soil blown in by the wind over thousands of years. It covers much of north China, is very fertile, and is soft enough to farm with simple tools.

It also washes into the Yellow River and gives the river its color and its name.

This slide gives you the stage the Shang story plays on. Find the Yellow River across the north of the map and the Yangzi River through the middle. The Shang lived in the north, on the plain where the Yellow River leaves the hills.

The north is colder and drier; many areas get less than 20 inches of rain a year (p. 9), so the crops were millet (a hardy grain) and later wheat. The soil is loess, soft enough to work with stone tools. The catch is the river: it carries so much silt that its bed rises, and once people built dikes, a broken dike meant a huge flood. The south is warmer and wetter, good for rice and for travel by boat.

The map is a modern relief map, not an ancient one. Look at the brown and white band of mountains and deserts to the west and north. Your textbook says these mountains, deserts and grasslands separated China from the other early civilizations, which is why Chinese writing grew up on its own. That is the second half of Reading Guide Q1 (p. 10); the Pierian Spring slide on a world apart tells it in full.

A good way to say it: the environment did not dictate history, but it set the menu of choices. Dry northern plains rewarded millet farmers who could organize to hold back floods; the wet south rewarded rice farmers and boatmen.

Why this matters: a farming people living close together on a flood-prone plain needed leaders who could organize labor and defense. Keep that in mind when you meet the Shang kings.

This slide gives you the long road to the Shang. All dates are BCE (before the Common Era), so the numbers get smaller as time moves forward.

The first farming in north China was the growing of millet, a hardy grain. Archaeologists date it to about 8000 BCE; your textbook gives about 10,000 BCE (p. 10). Either way, farming began thousands of years before the first kings. (The 8000 date comes from outside your textbook; if a quiz asks for the textbook's figure, it is 10,000.) Over the next four thousand years villages multiplied, and by 4000 BCE distinct regional cultures had appeared.

Two clues show society changing by about 2500 BCE: some settlements built walls, so someone could organize the work and had something to defend, and some graves were far richer than others. Soon after 2000 BCE people began making bronze, a mix of copper and tin, and the first states appeared.

The photo is real: loess hills in north China today, terraced by farmers for thousands of years.

Why this matters: the Shang built on thousands of years of farming and growing inequality, which helps explain how a few could rule so many.

×

Rammed earth

Rammed earth (hangtu) is soil poured into wooden frames and pounded, layer by layer, until it is almost as hard as concrete.

The Shang used it for palace platforms, city walls and tomb fill. It needed little material but huge amounts of labor.

This slide gives you the city at the heart of the story. Tradition says the Shang kings ruled from five successive cities. The best known is the last one, near the modern city of Anyang. The Shang called it Yin, so the site today is called Yinxu, the Ruins of Yin. The kings ruled from here from about 1200 BCE until the dynasty fell in 1045 BCE (p. 10).

At the center stood large palaces, temples and altars. They were built of wood on platforms of rammed earth, pounded soil as hard as concrete. The wood rotted away long ago, so archaeologists find only the earth platforms and the holes where pillars stood.

The photo shows the excavated palace area today. The red posts are modern markers placed where the ancient wooden pillars stood. Chinese archaeologists dug here from 1928 to 1937, one of the first great excavations in China.

Why this matters: nearly all the evidence in this lecture, the oracle bones and the royal tombs, comes from this one city. When historians talk about the Shang, they mostly mean late Shang Anyang.

Setting · Kings at war

Shang Kings Were War Chiefs Who Fought, Hunted and Took Captives

Armies. Kings regularly sent 3,000 to 5,000 men on campaigns.
Hunts. Between wars, royal hunts could last for months.
Shifting map. Vassals turned into enemies; enemies became allies.
Captives. Prisoners could be enslaved or sacrificed, a key source of royal wealth.
Chariots. Horse-drawn chariots came into use around 1200 BCE.

This slide gives you the first side of a Shang king: the warrior. Your textbook calls the Shang kings military chieftains (p. 10). They regularly sent out armies of three to five thousand men, and when they were not at war they went on hunts that could last for months. A vassal is a lesser ruler who promises loyalty to a stronger one. Shang vassals often rebelled, and enemies sometimes became allies, so the map kept changing.

Bronze gave Shang warriors better weapons: bronze-tipped spears and dagger-axes. Around 1200 BCE they began to use spoke-wheeled chariots, pulled by two or four horses, which gave commanders a moving platform to direct their troops. Prisoners of war mattered a great deal: they could be made slaves or killed as sacrifices, and your textbook calls war booty, especially captives, an important source of the king’s revenue.

The photo is a real chariot burial at Yinxu. The wood rotted long ago, but it left its shape in the hardened soil, so you can see the spokes of the wheels. The horses were buried with it. (Where chariots came from is told in the Lecture 2 Pierian Spring.)

Why this matters: armies alone did not explain why people obeyed the king. Many chiefs could fight. The next act shows the power only a Shang king had.

Q2 story · The spirit world

Nature spirits. The sun, moon, Yellow River, winds and mountains received offerings.
Ancestors. Dead kings could curse, send dreams and help in battle.

×

Di (帝)

Di was the highest god the Shang worshipped. Di could command rain, thunder and wind, the forces that decided whether crops grew.

Di was remote. The royal ancestors, who were closer to the living king, could ask Di for help on his behalf.

This slide gives you the Shang view of the world, which you need before the oracle bones make sense. The Shang believed the world was full of powerful spirits. At the top was Di, a high god who could command rain, thunder and wind (p. 10). The king also made offerings to nature spirits: the sun and moon, the Yellow River, the winds of the four directions and certain mountains.

Most important of all were the royal ancestors, the dead kings and queens of the Shang line. They were not resting quietly; they were active. They could send curses and dreams, help the king in battle, and plead with the distant Di. Worshipping them is called ancestor worship: honoring and feeding dead family members so they will help the living.

The bronze cauldron is a real Shang ritual vessel. Food for the spirits was cooked or offered in vessels like this. The staring animal face on its side is called a taotie; nobody is sure what it meant.

Why this matters: this belief system is the first piece of Review Question 2. If ancestors control the harvest and the war, then whoever speaks to the ancestors holds real power.

Q2 story · Reading the cracks

To Question the Ancestors, Diviners Heated Bones Until They Cracked

Prepare a cattle shoulder blade or a turtle under-shell.
State the question aloud to the ancestors or to Di.
Apply heat until the bone cracks with a sharp pop.
The king reads the cracks as the spirits’ answer.
Carve the question, and sometimes the outcome, into the bone.

This slide gives you how an oracle bone worked. Diviners used the flat shoulder blades of cattle (called scapulae) or the flat under-shells of turtles (called plastrons). They carved small hollows into the back so the bone would crack in a controlled way. Then they announced the question and pressed heat into a hollow. The bone cracked, often with a sharp sound, and the shape of the crack was read as the answer. The Chinese character for divination, 卜 (bu), even looks like a crack.

After the reading, a scribe often carved the question into the bone, and sometimes what actually happened later. That is why these bones are written sources and not just ritual leftovers.

The image is a real plastron from Anyang. Look closely and you can see both the cracks and the neat columns of carved characters running beside them.

The next slide has a two-minute video that shows the whole process.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q2 (p. 10), divination is the tool. It is how the king “talked” with the ancestors, and it left a written record of royal decisions.

This slide gives you the questions themselves. King Wu Ding ruled at Anyang around 1200 BCE, and more oracle bones survive from his reign than from any other. Your textbook lists what his diviners asked the high god Di or the ancestors about: rain, the harvest, military expeditions, dreams, floods, tribute payments, sacrifices and even a toothache (p. 10).

The quotation comes from outside your textbook, from one of the most famous oracle bones. The diviner predicted that the childbirth of Lady Hao, one of Wu Ding’s wives, would be “good.” The king read the cracks himself and added conditions. Then, thirty-one days later, a scribe recorded the result: “she gave birth; it was not good; it was a girl.” It tells you that the Shang royal family wanted sons, and it shows that the bones sometimes recorded when a prediction failed. Keep Lady Hao’s name in mind: she returns in Act III as a general.

The scapula in the picture is real and comes from Wu Ding’s reign. Each group of carved characters is a separate question.

Why this matters: notice that every question is state business. Weather, war and the royal family were all decided with the ancestors’ help. That is what Review Question 2 means by religion establishing political power.

Q2 story · Feeding the ancestors

The King Fed His Ancestors with Wine, Grain, Animals and Human Victims

Offerings. Millet, wine, cattle, sheep and grain went to the ancestors.
Human victims. Captives were offered to the royal ancestors too.
Bronze. Great vessels held the food and wine at sacrificial ceremonies.
Rank. Nobles owned ritual bronzes, but kings owned many more.

This slide gives you the other half of the relationship. Ancestors helped the living, but they had to be fed. The king addressed his ancestors in prayers and made offerings of millet, wine, cattle, sheep and grain (p. 10). He also offered human victims, usually captives taken in war. Your textbook is plain about this, and Act III shows how large the practice became.

Most surviving Shang bronzes are ritual vessels: cups, goblets, steamers and cauldrons that held food and wine for the spirits. Both kings and nobles owned them, but kings had many more. So the number of bronzes a family owned showed its rank.

The photo shows a real sacrificial pit at Yinxu, the Shang capital at Anyang, now kept under protective glass. Several people were laid side by side in it; you can see their skulls and long bones. Victims like these were offerings to the royal ancestors, most of them captives taken in war. It is a hard image, and it is here because it is evidence: the oracle bones say the king offered human victims, and the ground at Anyang confirms it.

The largest bronze vessel of all, the Houmuwu ding, weighs about 830 kilograms (your textbook: 875 kg, p. 14). You will see it at the end of this lecture, and Lecture 2 tells how it was cast.

Why this matters: feeding the ancestors was a royal duty and a royal privilege. It tied religion to wealth, war captives and bronze, all things the king controlled.

Q2 story · The king as high priest

The King Was Also the High Priest, the Person Best Qualified to Reach the Spirits

High priest. Oracle bones show the king leading sacrifices to ancestors and Di.
His ancestors. The royal ancestors were the most powerful spirits of all.
Final word. The king himself often read the cracks and announced the meaning.
Records. Carved bones preserved royal questions, answers and results.

This slide gives you the key idea for Review Question 2. Your textbook says Shang power did not rest only on armies (p. 10). The oracle bones show that the king was also the high priest: the person best qualified to offer sacrifices to the royal ancestors and to Di.

Why him? Because the most powerful ancestors were his ancestors, the dead kings of the Shang line. A noble family could honor its own dead, but the king spoke for the royal ancestors who could reach Di. And the king did not simply wait for the diviners: he often read the cracks himself and announced what they meant. The carved bones then kept a record of his questions and his predictions.

The picture is an AI-generated illustration, not evidence. It imagines the scene the oracle bones describe: the king holding a cracked ox shoulder blade, a diviner heating a rod to make the cracks, and a scribe with a knife ready to carve the question and answer. One detail is wrong on purpose to notice: the king’s flat crown with hanging strings of beads is an anachronism, something from the wrong time. That style of crown belongs to later Zhou and Han court dress, not to the Shang.

A careful word: your textbook says “best qualified,” not “the only person allowed.” Others made offerings too. The king’s advantage was that he stood at the top of the most powerful line of ancestors.

Q2 concept · Legitimacy

Concept: Legitimacy Is the Belief That a Ruler Has the Right to Rule

Concept

Legitimacy

People’s belief that a ruler has the right to rule, not just the power to force obedience. A ruler seen as legitimate needs less force to stay in charge.

Force alone. Armies can seize power, but force is costly and rivals can try it.
Shang answer. The king spoke for the royal ancestors, so his rule seemed right.
Keeping it. Legitimacy must be renewed, or a rival may claim it instead.
Zhou answer. The next dynasty would claim Heaven’s approval instead (Lecture 3).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 10 and 17; the general definition is a standard historians’ term.

Before you see why religion made the Shang king so hard to replace, you need one word historians use constantly. Legitimacy is people's belief that a ruler has the right to rule, not just the power to make them obey. A ruler seen as legitimate needs fewer soldiers, because most people accept his orders as proper.

Every government has to answer the question "Why should we obey you?" Force can win a throne, but force is expensive, and any rival with a bigger army can use it too. A shared belief is much harder to defeat.

The Shang answer came from religion. Your textbook says the king was the person best qualified to make sacrifices to the royal ancestors and to Di (p. 10). If the ancestors controlled rain and victory, then the man who spoke for them had a right to rule that no rival could easily claim. In Lecture 3 you will see the Zhou give a different answer: Heaven itself chose them, an idea your textbook says legitimated every later change of dynasty (p. 17).

There is no image here: a concept slide gives you a tool, and the next slide puts it to work.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q2 asks how religion helped establish and maintain Shang power; legitimacy is the idea that links those two verbs.

This slide gives you the reason religion and power fit together so well. Follow the cycle. Before a big decision, the king asks his ancestors. The answer is carved into bone. If the harvest is good or the war is won, that seems to prove the king's link to the spirits works, and his authority grows. If things go badly, the blame can fall on a missed offering or an angry ancestor, which calls for more ritual, not a new king.

Historians call this a feedback loop: each turn of the cycle makes the next turn stronger. A rival might raise an army, but he could not claim to speak for the royal ancestors. The scholar K. C. Chang argued that in early China, control of contact with the spirit world was the basis of political authority (an argument from outside your textbook).

One careful word: your textbook calls the king the person best qualified to reach the spirits, not the only one (p. 10), so "advantage" is safer than "monopoly."

Q3 story · What survived

Shang Palaces Rotted Away, but Their Royal Tombs Survived Underground

Palaces. Wooden halls vanished; only earth platforms remain.
Tombs. Kings lay in huge cross-shaped pits with long ramps.
Cemetery. The royal graves sit across the river from the palaces.
Robbed. Almost every royal tomb was looted long ago.

This slide opens Act III and gives you the second big kind of evidence: tombs. Shang palaces were splendid, but they were made of wood and other materials that rot, so nothing of them remains above their foundations. What survived are the lavish underground tombs built for Shang kings and their wives (p. 11).

This slide is hard but important, so take it slowly. Besides objects, the Shang buried people in royal tombs, sometimes dozens of them. Your textbook separates two groups (p. 12). This slide gives you the scale of Shang sacrifice, and it is grim. Early Shang graves rarely held more than three victims or followers, but the practice grew. One late Shang king’s tomb held ninety followers plus seventy-four human sacrifices, along with twelve horses and eleven dogs (p. 12).

Sacrifice was not only for funerals. Oracle bone texts mention ceremonies where three to four hundred captives were killed. In 1976, archaeologists found about twelve hundred victims in 191 pits near the royal tombs, apparently from many separate ceremonies of a few dozen victims each. Animals were sacrificed too, sometimes one, two or three hundred cattle, sheep, pigs or dogs at a time.

The photo shows a real sacrificial pit preserved under glass at the Yinxu site museum. It is dim and distant on purpose; you do not need to look closely to understand what it is.

This slide gives you the bottom of the pyramid, which the tombs only hint at. Shang nobles lived in large houses built on platforms of rammed earth. Mountains, Deserts and Grasslands Kept China Apart, but Never Completely

← Back to the lecture: two rivers

This slide gives you the second half of Review Question 1 in your Reading Guide, which asks how geography shaped early Chinese farming and China’s contact with outside cultures. Your textbook says that mountains, deserts and grasslands separated China proper (the farming heartland) from the other early centers of civilization in Mesopotamia, Egypt and India (p. 10).

Look at the map. To the west rise the Himalayas and the Tibetan Plateau, the highest land on earth. To the northwest lie the Taklamakan and Gobi deserts. To the north stretch the grasslands, or steppe, where people lived by herding animals rather than farming. Inside these barriers, the Yellow River and Yangzi River valleys formed a large, rich region where farming peoples could trade and borrow from each other.

But isolation was never total. Wheat was first grown in West Asia, and the horse-drawn chariot reached the Shang around 1200 BCE through the steppe. So the best answer is “mostly separate, with important borrowing.” That is why Chinese writing, cooking and religion developed along their own lines. (Your textbook also reports Shang silk in an Egyptian tomb, p. 12; specialists dispute that identification.)

One more caution. Historical “China proper” was smaller than the modern country, and its edges shifted constantly, so this isolation argument applies to the farming heartland, not to a fixed modern map.

Why this matters: geography explains why later Chinese thinkers often saw their land as the center of the civilized world.

This independent-study slide gives you a key idea about evidence. Chinese tradition says the first dynasty was the Xia, and later books even list its kings. Some early Bronze Age sites, such as Erlitou, may be connected to the Xia, but they contain no writing, so nobody can prove or disprove it (p. 10).

The Shang are different. They are known from two kinds of texts: transmitted texts, copied and recopied for centuries, and excavated texts, which archaeologists dig up, like the oracle bones. When both kinds name the same kings, historians can be confident. Your textbook says no one today doubts that the Shang existed.

This independent-study slide gives you the detective story behind the dragon bones that opened the lecture. For centuries, Chinese pharmacies sold "dragon bones," ground up as medicine, and farmers near Anyang dug them up in their fields.

A famous story, from outside your textbook, says that in 1899 a scholar named Wang Yirong noticed ancient writing on bones bought for his medicine. Your textbook says only that the bones were connected to the Shang "after 1898" (p. 10). The story may be partly legend, but scholars were collecting inscribed bones by around 1899–1900.

This independent-study slide gives you the big arguments behind the story. Historians agree that religion sat at the center of Shang kingship, but they push the idea in different directions. All four views come from outside your textbook.

David Keightley, the leading Western scholar of oracle bones, argued that divination was a political tool as much as a religious one: it showed the king's special access to the spirits, committed the court to decisions and created written records. K. C. Chang, in Art, Myth, and Ritual (1983), went further: whoever controlled contact with the spirit world controlled political authority. Your textbook is more careful, calling the king the person "best qualified" to make sacrifices (p. 10).

Li Feng stresses that the Shang were one center among many regional cultures in contact with each other and with the steppe, a theme of Lecture 2. Roderick Campbell argues that violence and family ties together built the Shang state, with sacrifice as public proof of the king's power over life and death.

Background essay: the historians’ debates behind this lecture ↗

This slide gives you the sources behind the stories. Most of the facts in this deck come from your textbook, Chapter 1 (pp. 8–19). Facts from other sources are marked "outside the textbook" on the slides or in the notes. The words of the oracle bones come from translations by David Keightley, the scholar who did more than anyone to make these inscriptions readable in English. They have been simplified for this deck, so check his book if you want the exact wording.

If you want to go deeper, Li Feng’s Early China is the clearest recent overview, and Roderick Campbell’s book explains how violence and family ties built the Shang state. The background essay linked at the bottom of this slide explains what historians still argue about: how “sacred” the Shang king really was, whether the followers in royal tombs chose to die, and why the oracle bones show us only the king’s view.

Why this matters: every claim in a history deck should be traceable to a source you could check yourself.

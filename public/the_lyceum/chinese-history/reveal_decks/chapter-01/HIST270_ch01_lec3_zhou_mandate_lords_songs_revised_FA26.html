A Bronze Pot Recorded the Morning the Zhou Destroyed the Shang

The attack. King Wu of Zhou marched east against the Shang king.
One morning. The Zhou army won on a jiazi day, the cycle’s first day.
A reward. Days later, an official named Li received metal from the king.

Lecture 2 · where we left the Shang ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 15; the Li gui inscription is described in the background essay.

This slide drops you into the moment the Shang world ended. In 1045 BCE, your textbook says, a frontier state called Zhou rose against the Shang and defeated it (p. 15). You already met the Shang as a powerful dynasty with oracle bones, bronze and armies. Now you watch it fall.

The vessel in the photograph remembers that day. It is a bronze food bowl called a gui, found in 1976. Inside, 32 cast characters say that King Wu attacked the Shang on a jiazi morning, the first day of the sixty-day cycle you met in Lecture 2, and won. Seven days later the king rewarded an official named Li with metal, and Li cast this vessel to honor his own ancestor. (The inscription's details come from beyond your textbook.)

Notice what Li chose to do with a war prize: turn it into a ritual vessel that carried a written record. Writing and bronze, the two Shang skills from Lecture 2, now served a new dynasty.

Why this matters: conquest was only the first step. The Zhou still had to persuade millions of former Shang subjects that their victory was right, the question behind Reading Guide Q10.

Q10 story · The western frontier · before 1045 BCE

A Frontier State West of the Shang Shared Its Culture, Then Overthrew It

The Qiang. Fierce peoples to the far west probably spoke an early Tibetan.
The Zhou. A frontier state between them shared most Shang material culture.
1045 BCE. The Zhou rose against the Shang and defeated it.
Two Zhous. Western Zhou ruled from near Xi’an; Eastern Zhou later ruled from Luoyang.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 15 and Map 1.1.

This slide tells you who the Zhou were. Your textbook says the Shang were surrounded by allied and rival peoples. To the far west lived the fierce Qiang, who probably spoke an early form of Tibetan. Between the Shang capital and the Qiang was a frontier state called Zhou, which shared most of the material culture of the Shang (p. 15). In other words, the Zhou were not strangers: they used similar bronzes and similar weapons, and they knew the Shang well.

In 1045 BCE the Zhou rose against the Shang and defeated it. Historians call the first part of the dynasty the Western Zhou (1045–771 BCE) because its capital lay in the west, near modern Xi'an. After 771 the capital moved east, near modern Luoyang, so the later period is the Eastern Zhou (p. 15).

Read the map from left to right: the Zhou homeland in the Wei River valley, the red arrows of the march east, and the battle near the Shang capital at Yin (Anyang). It is a modern teaching map, so the routes are approximate.

Why this matters: the Zhou shared Shang culture, so they could not simply call the Shang barbarians. They needed a different argument, which you meet on the next slides (Reading Guide Q10).

Names box

King Wen (Wen = cultured): father of King Wu. King Wu (Wu = martial): the conqueror; not the Shang king Wu Ding. Duke of Zhou (Zhou Gong, personal name Dan): Wu’s brother and regent.

Western Zhou (1045–771 BCE), capital near Xi’an; Eastern Zhou (770–256 BCE), capital near Luoyang.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 15–16.

This slide introduces the three men early Zhou writers credited with the conquest (pp. 15–16). King Wen (his name means "cultured" or "lettered") expanded the Zhou domain. His son King Wu ("martial") conquered the Shang. Wu's brother, the Duke of Zhou, consolidated the conquest and served as regent, a person who rules on behalf of a king too young to govern, for Wu's heir.

Notice the pattern in the names: one founder stands for culture, one for war, and one for loyal service. Later Chinese thinkers, Confucius above all, held up the Duke of Zhou as the model minister because, in the traditional story, he handed power back to the young king when the king came of age. You will meet Confucius in Chapter 2. Watch the names: King Wu of Zhou is not Wu Ding, the Shang king from Lecture 1.

The portraits are real paintings, but they were made almost three thousand years after these men lived. They show how later Chinese imagined their heroes, not what the founders looked like.

Why this matters: the story of virtuous founders is part of the Zhou argument for their right to rule (Reading Guide Q10).

Q10 concept · Mandate of Heaven

Concept: The Mandate of Heaven Said Heaven Lets a King Rule Only While He Rules Well

Concept

Mandate of Heaven

The belief that Heaven grants a ruler the right to rule only as long as he rules in the interests of the people. A bad ruler can lose the mandate to a worthier one.

Mandate. An order or authority given from above, here by Heaven.
Heaven. A divine force the Zhou called Tian, meaning Sky or Heaven.
Conditional. The right to rule depends on good rule, not on birth alone.
Recap. Lecture 1 · legitimacy ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 16–17 and 19.

Before you see how the Zhou explained their victory, you need the idea at the heart of it. The Mandate of Heaven is the belief that Heaven grants a ruler the right to rule, but only as long as he rules in the interests of the people (p. 17). A mandate is an order or authority given from above. Heaven (Tian in Chinese, also translated "Sky") was the divine force the Zhou worshipped alongside their ancestors (p. 16).

The key word is conditional. Your textbook's summary puts it plainly: if a king did not rule in the interests of the people, Heaven could take away his mandate and give it to a worthier person (p. 19). So the right to rule is earned, and it can be lost.

In Lecture 1 you learned that legitimacy means people's belief that a ruler has the right to rule; the recap link reopens that concept slide in a new tab. The Mandate of Heaven is one answer to the legitimacy question, and it lasted three thousand years.

There is no image here: a concept slide gives you a tool, and the next slides put it to work.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q10 asks you first to explain this concept. Make sure you can state it in one sentence of your own.

Check yourself: What makes the Mandate of Heaven conditional, and who decides when a king has lost it?

Q10 story · Heaven and the king · ca. 1040 BCE

Shang Kings Fed Their Ancestors; Zhou Kings Also Served Heaven as Its Son

Shang kings. They sacrificed to Di and the royal ancestors.
Zhou kings. They sacrificed to their ancestors and also to Heaven.
Son of Heaven. The king claimed a close, personal bond with Heaven.
All under Heaven. The ideal was one ruler over one united world.

Lecture 1 · the Shang spirit world ↗ Lecture 1 · religion and power ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 10 and 16–17; He zun inscription (see the background essay).

This slide shows how the Zhou changed the religious basis of kingship. In Lecture 1 you saw that Shang kings sacrificed to the high god Di, to nature spirits and to the royal ancestors (p. 10). Like the Shang kings, the Zhou kings sacrificed to their ancestors, but they also sacrificed to the divine force called Heaven (p. 16).

The Book of Documents, the subject of the next slide, assumes a close relationship between Heaven and the king, who was called the Son of Heaven (tianzi). The Zhou also promoted the ideal of political unity for "all under Heaven": one rightful ruler over the whole known world (p. 17).

The image is a real rubbing, ink pressed onto paper laid over the bronze, of the inscription inside the He zun, the vessel on the title slide. In it a Zhou king recalls that King Wu, after conquering the Shang, reported the victory to Heaven. That detail is from beyond your textbook, but it shows the idea on a bronze cast within a generation of the conquest.

Why this matters: Shang power rested on the Shang ancestors. By appealing to Heaven, a power above every family, the Zhou could claim a right that did not depend on Shang ancestry (Reading Guide Q10).

Check yourself: Why would worshipping Heaven help a new dynasty more than worshipping its own ancestors alone?

Q10 story · The Zhou version · Book of Documents

The Book of Documents Told the Conquest as Heaven Punishing an Evil King

Earliest book. The Book of Documents is the earliest transmitted Chinese text.
Speeches. It records Zhou speeches, pronouncements and reports.
The heroes. Just and noble Zhou warriors, supported by Heaven.
The villains. A decadent Shang court led by an evil king.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 16.

This slide gives you the Zhou side of the story, told in the Zhou's own words. Your textbook calls the Book of Documents (in Chinese Shujing or Shangshu) the earliest transmitted text, a book copied by hand generation after generation rather than dug up by archaeologists (p. 16).

Its speeches, pronouncements and reports portray the Zhou founders and their age. They depict the conquest as the victory of just and noble warriors, supported by Heaven, over the decadent Shang court led by an evil king (p. 16). Notice the shape of the story: good men, a bad king, and Heaven on the side of the good.

Ask yourself who is telling it. The Book of Documents preserves the winners' account. It does not record what Shang loyalists thought. A careful historian reads it as evidence of what the Zhou wanted people to believe.

The image is a real page from a book printed in the 1100s CE, a commentary on the Book of Documents. Even this "old" copy was made two thousand years after the conquest; that is how transmitted texts reach us.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q10 asks how the Zhou used the Mandate to justify the overthrow. This book is their main justification.

Q10 story · Mandate as argument · ca. 1000 BCE

The Mandate Justified the Zhou Victory, and Every Later Change of Dynasty

New idea. The Mandate seems to have had no place in Shang beliefs.
Propaganda? Some scholars think early Zhou rulers built it to win over Shang subjects.
On bronze. A Zhou cauldron says King Wen “received Heaven’s great mandate.”
Long life. It legitimated many later changes of dynasty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 17; Da Yu ding inscription, trans. adapted from Edward L. Shaughnessy (see the background essay).

This slide asks you to look at the Mandate of Heaven as an argument with a purpose. Your textbook notes that the theory does not seem to have had any place in Shang beliefs, so some scholars think the early Zhou rulers developed it as propaganda (information spread to win people over to a cause) to win over the conquered subjects of the Shang (p. 17). Notice the hedge: "some scholars think." It is a strong interpretation, not a proven fact.

Think about why it worked. The conquered Shang could accept the Zhou without believing their own ancestors had failed them; the problem was one bad king who had lost Heaven's favor. And the idea promoted the ideal of political unity for "all under Heaven" (p. 17).

The image is a real bronze cauldron, the Da Yu ding. Its inscription, from beyond your textbook, has a Zhou king recall that King Wen "received Heaven's great mandate" and that the Shang lost the mandate because its lords and officials were drunk on wine. The argument was being cast in bronze within a few generations of the conquest.

Why this matters: your textbook says the Mandate legitimated many later changes of dynasty (p. 17). You will see it used again and again in this course.

Check yourself: Why did the Mandate of Heaven appeal to conquered Shang subjects, and why do some scholars call it propaganda?

Q11 concept · Feudal and quasi-feudal

Concept: In a Feudal System a King Grants Land to Lords Who Owe Him Loyalty

Concept

Feudalism · fief · vassal

A system in which a ruler grants land (a fief) to a lord (a vassal) in return for loyalty and service. “Quasi-feudal” means “feudal-like”: similar, but not the same.

Fief. Land granted by a ruler, often with the people who farm it.
Vassal. The lord who receives the fief and owes loyalty and service.
Decentralized. Power is spread out to many lords, not held by one court.
Quasi. Historians borrowed the European word; the Zhou version differed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 8, 17 and 18.

Before you see how the Zhou governed, you need the words your textbook uses for it. Feudalism is a system in which a ruler grants land, called a fief, to a lord, called a vassal, in return for loyalty and service. A decentralized system spreads power out among many lords instead of keeping it in one central court.

Your textbook calls the Zhou arrangement a "decentralized, quasi-feudal system" (p. 17) and, earlier, "feudal-like" (p. 8). Quasi means "almost" or "resembling." Historians first used the word feudal for medieval Europe, more than fifteen centuries later, so they add "quasi" to warn you that the Zhou system was similar but not identical. One difference you will see: Zhou lords were usually the king's own relatives, bound to him by family rituals.

Your textbook also says land was held on feudal tenures in a manorial economy, with farmers treated as serfs (p. 18); you will meet them in Act IV.

There is no image here: a concept slide gives you a tool, and the next slides put it to work.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q11 uses both words, decentralized and quasi-feudal. Be ready to define each.

Check yourself: What is the difference between a fief and a vassal, and why does the textbook add "quasi"?

Q11 story · The garrisons · ca. 1040–1000 BCE

Zhou Kings Sent Relatives East to Build Walled Garrisons in Conquered Lands

The problem. The conquered land was too large to rule from the west.
The answer. Kings sent relatives and trusted followers to build walled garrisons.
The center. The king headed the royal lineage and the nobility.
The glue. Ancestor rituals and court visits kept lords loyal.
×

Lineage

A lineage is a group of families who trace their descent from one shared ancestor. The Zhou king was its ritual head: he led the sacrifices to the royal ancestors that every royal relative honored.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 17.

This slide shows the Zhou solution to an enormous problem. Rather than attempting to rule all their territories directly, your textbook says, the early Zhou rulers sent out relatives and trusted subordinates to establish walled garrisons, fortified settlements of soldiers and settlers, in the conquered territories (p. 17). That created the decentralized, quasi-feudal system you just defined.

At the center stood the Zhou king, who was at the same time the ritual head of the royal lineage (a family line tracing descent from one ancestor) and the supreme lord of the nobility (p. 17). So the king was both head of the family and head of the government. His authority was kept up by rituals of ancestor worship, where lords honored the same royal ancestors, and by court visits, where lords came to the capital to serve and be seen.

The map is a course-made teaching graphic carried over from an earlier version of this lecture. The arrows show broad directions, not real routes. Many Zhou lords ended up hundreds of miles from the capital.

Why this matters: this slide is the core of Reading Guide Q11. Note the two things that held the system together: family and ritual.

Q11 story · An appointment ceremony · ca. 800 BCE

Kings Wrote Appointments on Bamboo, and Families Recast Them in Bronze

The document. Court scribes wrote appointments to offices or fiefs on bamboo strips.
The ceremony. An official read the document aloud on the king’s behalf.
The copy. A copy was handed to the person appointed.
The bronze. His family later had it cast in bronze, to pass down.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 16; Song ding inscription (see the background essay).

This slide shows how a Zhou appointment actually happened. Your textbook says court scribes prepared written documents on bamboo or wooden strips to name people to offices or fiefs. Later, during a court ceremony, an official read the document aloud on behalf of the king, and a copy was handed to the person appointed, who later had it reproduced in bronze so that it could be passed down in his family (p. 16).

The bamboo has rotted, but the bronzes survive, which is why bronze inscriptions are one of the most important sources for the early Zhou period. The vessel in the photograph is real: the Song ding. Its inscription, from beyond your textbook, describes a ceremony in a royal temple at dawn. Song entered and stood in the courtyard, a scribe read out the king's command putting him in charge of storehouses, the king gave him robes and chariot gear, and Song bowed and left wearing the document on his sash.

Notice how personal this was: an office came from the king's own word, before witnesses, and the family kept the record forever.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q11, this is how the king tied lords and officials to himself one by one.

Check yourself: Why did families copy their appointment documents into bronze?

The order. The king sent Yu to drive back attacks by the Southern Huai.
The proof. Yu returned with 100 enemy heads and 40 bound prisoners.

×

Ancestral temple

An ancestral temple is a hall where a family honors its ancestors with offerings. Rewarding Yu there made the royal ancestors witnesses to the bargain.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 18.

This slide gives you one real exchange of service for reward. Your textbook describes a bronze inscription that recorded the rewards given to a man named Yu for obeying the king's command to drive back attacks by the Southern Huai, peoples the Zhou called barbarians (p. 18).

After Yu's successful return, the king brought him into the ancestral temple, the hall where the royal ancestors were honored, and gave him two bronze ritual vessels, fifty strands of cowrie shells and one hundred fields, in return for one hundred enemy heads and forty bound prisoners. The inscription ends with Yu praising the Son of Heaven and hoping that his sons' sons and grandsons' grandsons would treasure the vessel for ten thousand years (p. 18).

The photograph shows real cowrie shells, the small sea shells used as money in Shang and Zhou China.

Notice the chain: royal command, military service, reward in land and wealth, and a bronze to remember it. Land, the most valuable gift, came straight from the king.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q11, this inscription shows the personal bond of service and reward that held the Zhou system together.

Q11 story · A new state · 806 BCE

In 806 BCE a Royal Son Was Made a Duke and Founded the State of Zheng

806 BCE. Prince You, a younger son of King Li, was made a duke.
His fief. He founded the state of Zheng, near the royal lands.
Still at court. He and his heirs served the king as high ministers.
Later. Zheng’s lords moved east onto marshy land that had to be drained.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 17; the founder’s parentage follows Sima Qian, Shiji 42 (see the background essay).

This slide shows a Zhou fief being created, with a date. Your textbook uses Zheng as its example (p. 17). In 806 BCE a younger royal son was made a duke, a high noble title, and given the new state of Zheng. His personal name was You; he was a younger son of King Li and the half-brother of the reigning King Xuan. (Don't confuse Prince You with King You, the last Western Zhou king.)

Your textbook stresses a surprising detail: the duke and his successors spent much of their time at the Zhou court, serving as high ministers (p. 17). A fief did not take a lord away from the king; it tied him closer. The textbook also says Zheng lay on swampy ground that had to be drained. Zheng's lords moved east into such land near the Yellow River in the last years of the Western Zhou, and their state flourished there for centuries.

The photograph shows a real bronze from a Zheng lord's tomb at Xinzheng, made around 600 BCE, long after this story. It shows the wealth Zheng's rulers later enjoyed.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q11, Zheng shows a fief granted to a relative who still served at court.

Q11 story · Fiefs become family property · by 800 BCE

By 800 BCE Two Hundred Lords Passed Their Lands and Offices to Their Sons

Inheritance. A vassal could usually pass his position to a son.
Hereditary fiefs. Over time the domains became family property.
By 800 BCE. About two hundred lords; only about twenty-five really mattered.
Offices too. Lords’ officers passed their posts and lands to their sons.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 17–18.

This slide shows the system changing over time. A Zhou vassal was generally able to pass his position on to a son, so in time the domains became hereditary fiefs, lands handed down within one family (p. 17). By 800 BCE there were about two hundred lords with domains large and small, but only about twenty-five were large enough to matter much in politics between the states (p. 18).

The same thing happened one level down. Each lord appointed officers to serve him in ritual, administrative or military jobs, and these posts and their titles also tended to become hereditary. Each domain came to have noble families with inherited offices and lands (p. 18).

The vessel in the photograph belonged to a lord of Jin, one of the large states. It comes from a cemetery where, generation after generation, the lords of Jin and their wives were buried side by side (a detail from beyond your textbook). It is a family's power, cast in bronze.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q11, inheritance made the system stable at first, but it also meant that, generation by generation, lords owed more to their own families than to a distant king. Keep that in mind for 771 BCE.

Check yourself: How did fiefs and offices become hereditary, and what risk did that create for the king?

Q11 story · Outsiders brought in · ca. 1000–800 BCE

Rong and Yi Chiefs Could Join the Zhou Order, if They Used Chinese Writing

Outsiders. Yi groups lived in the east; Rong groups in the north and west.
Not primitive. They spoke other languages, but had similar technology.
Recognized. Zhou kings made their chiefs lords of their own lands.
The price. For matters of state, they had to use Chinese writing.

Lecture 2 · one script, many tongues ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 18.

This slide shows the Zhou order reaching beyond the Zhou people. As in Shang times, groups the Zhou viewed as alien lived among and beyond the Zhou states. Various Yi groups ("eastern barbarians") lived scattered through the east, and Rong groups ("northern barbarians") lived in the north and west (p. 18). These were Zhou labels, not names the peoples chose. They spoke distinct languages, but your textbook stresses they were not necessarily more primitive than the Zhou in technology.

Over the nearly three centuries of Western Zhou rule, the kings drew many of these groups into the Zhou political order by recognizing their chiefs as the lords of their domains. To take part, though, they had to use the Chinese writing system for matters of state (p. 18).

The image is a real early Zhou bronze lid whose long inscription records orders sent to officials: the kind of written business every member of the Zhou order had to handle.

Why this matters: this is the last piece of Reading Guide Q11, and it links forward to Q12: shared writing slowly turned outsiders into participants in one culture.

Check yourself: What did the Zhou kings offer Rong and Yi chiefs, and what did they require in return?

Q13 concept · Transmitted and excavated texts

Concept: Some Ancient Texts Were Copied Down the Centuries; Others Were Dug Up

Concept

Transmitted text · excavated text

A transmitted text survived because people copied it, generation after generation. An excavated text survived in the ground and was found by archaeologists.

Transmitted. The Book of Documents and Book of Songs, copied for three thousand years.
Excavated. Oracle bones and bronze inscriptions, written at the time.
Risk. Copied texts can be edited; buried ones are often short.
Recap. Lecture 1 · texts versus archaeology ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, pp. 8, 15–16 and 19.

Before you read the two Zhou books as evidence, you need one distinction historians use all the time. A transmitted text is a text that survived because people kept copying it, generation after generation. An excavated text is one that survived buried in the ground until archaeologists found it.

Your textbook uses this language. It says the early Zhou is known not only from archaeological evidence but also from transmitted texts, which give the Zhou version of their righteous victory over the decadent Shang (p. 8). The Book of Documents, the earliest transmitted text (p. 16), and the Book of Songs are transmitted. The oracle bones of Lecture 1 and the bronze inscriptions of Act II are excavated.

Each kind has strengths and risks. Transmitted texts are long and full of ideas, but copyists and editors could change them over the centuries. Excavated texts are untouched since they were buried, but they are usually short and limited to what their makers cared about. In Lecture 1 you saw this same problem with the Xia, known only from later texts.

There is no image here: a concept slide gives you a tool, and the next slides put it to work.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q13 asks what historians can learn from two transmitted books. Knowing what kind of source they are tells you how far to trust them.

This slide gives you the social ladder of the Western Zhou. Your textbook calls it a highly aristocratic society, ruled by a hereditary nobility, in which inherited ranks placed people in a hierarchy (p. 18). At the top was the king. This independent-study slide tests the Mandate of Heaven against a famous story. Your textbook says only that in 771 BCE the king was killed by Rong warriors and Zhou vassals (p. 18). Later writers, beginning with the historian Sima Qian about 100 BCE, told a livelier version, from beyond your textbook. King You (not the Prince You of Zheng) loved a beautiful consort named Bao Si, who never smiled. To amuse her he lit the beacon fires that summoned his lords to defend the capital. They rushed in, found no enemy, and she laughed. He did it again and again, so when real enemies came, no one answered.

Historians doubt it. A bamboo text called the Xinian ("Annals"), held by Tsinghua University since 2008, gives a plainer account: King You attacked the lord of Shen, his former queen's family, and Shen with its allies, including Western Rong, killed him. That fits your textbook's alliance of Rong and Zhou vassals.

The image is an 18th-century imagining of Bao Si, made about 2,500 years after her time.

Why did the legend last? A last ruler ruined by a woman and by pleasure fits the Mandate story perfectly, just as the Shang's last king was blamed for drink and cruelty.

This independent-study slide shows historians arguing about a word. Your textbook calls the Zhou system "quasi-feudal" and "feudal-like" and speaks of feudal tenures and serfs (pp. 8, 17–18). Why the hedges?

Some historians, from beyond your textbook, find the European word useful: in both cases a ruler granted land for loyalty, fiefs became hereditary, and farmers were bound to lords. Others reject it. One correction you should know about: your textbook calls the founder of Zheng "a younger son of King You." The standard ancient account, Sima Qian's, makes him a younger son of King Li and brother of King Xuan, who made him a duke in 806 BCE; King You was his nephew. This deck follows that account. The background essay explains the difference.

If you want to go deeper, Li Feng's Landscape and Power in Early China is the best study of how the Western Zhou rose and fell, and Edward Shaughnessy's Sources of Western Zhou History explains how historians read the bronzes.

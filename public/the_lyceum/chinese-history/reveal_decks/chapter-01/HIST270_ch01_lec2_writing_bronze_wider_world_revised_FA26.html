×

Houmuwu or Simuwu?

The three characters cast inside the cauldron name a royal mother called Wu. Scholars disagree whether the first character means “queen” (hou) or “to sacrifice to” (si), so the vessel is also called the Simuwu ding.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 14; the 1939 discovery story is from People’s Daily (2015) (outside the textbook).

This slide gives you a true story to start with, told in sources from outside your textbook. In March 1939, during the Japanese occupation of north China, villagers in Wuguan village near Anyang probed the ground and struck metal deep below a field. Over three nights, about forty of them hauled up a bronze cauldron taller than a child and heavier than a small car. According to the villagers' own accounts, an antiques dealer wanted it sawn into pieces; they managed to cut off only one handle before stopping. When Japanese troops came looking, the villagers reburied it under a stable.

In 1946, after the war, officials dug it up again. It was presented to the Chinese leader Chiang Kai-shek and shown in Nanjing in 1948, which is the moment in this photograph. Today it stands in the National Museum of China as the Houmuwu ding, the largest surviving Shang bronze vessel (p. 14). Scholars still argue how to read its first character, so you may also see it called the Simuwu ding; the popup explains.

The photo is a real 1948 news photograph, a modern primary source about the discovery, not about the Shang. You will see the cauldron itself in Act II.

Why this matters: the cauldron raises the two big questions of this lecture. What kind of society could cast a bronze this big (Review Question 7)? And what do the three characters cast inside it tell us about Shang writing and about the woman it honored (Review Question 5)?

This slide gives you the evidence half of Review Question 5: what the Shang actually wrote down. Your textbook says the inscribed bones show that writing was already a major element of Chinese culture by 1200 BCE (p. 12). The same inscriptions that asked the ancestors about rain also recorded enemies slain, booty taken and animals bagged in royal hunts, and King Wu Ding's diviners asked about tribute payments too.

Notice what kind of information this is. It is the data a government needs: counts, results, payments, dates. In the Shang state, the religious archive and the government archive were the same archive.

The records also name people. From inscribed bones, historians know that Lady Hao, one of King Wu Ding's wives, led several military campaigns, once with 13,000 troops against the Qiang to the west. Writing preserved the commands of a powerful woman, not just of kings.

Q5 story · Keeping time

Scribes Dated Every Record with a Sixty-Day Cycle That Lasted Three Thousand Years

Lunar months. The year was divided by the phases of the moon.
Ten-day weeks. Ten “day signs” repeated, like our seven weekdays.
Sixty-day cycle. Paired signs gave every day its own name.
Why it matters. Records can be compared only if everyone dates them alike.
×

Ten-day and sixty-day cycles

The Shang named days with two sets of signs: ten “heavenly stems” and twelve “earthly branches.” Pairing them in order gives sixty unique day names before the pattern repeats.

Versions of this cycle ran continuously through the rest of Chinese history, and it is still used for the traditional calendar today.

This slide gives you one of the quiet tools of Shang government: the calendar. Your textbook says Shang scribes recorded dates using lunar months and ten-day and sixty-day cycles (p. 12). Each day had a name made by pairing a sign from a set of ten with a sign from a set of twelve. Run through the pairs in order and you get sixty different day names before the cycle starts over. This is called the sexagenary cycle (sexagenary means “based on sixty”).

The image is a real Shang ox scapula, catalogued as Heji 37986. It is not a divination at all: a scribe carved the complete table of sixty day names on it, probably as a reference chart or a practice piece. Read it top to bottom in columns.

A shared calendar sounds dull, but it is essential state machinery. A king can only compare this year's tribute with last year's, or check when a campaign began, if every record uses the same dates. The same cycle later dates the Zhou conquest of the Shang, as you will see near the end of this deck.

Why this matters: for Review Question 5, the calendar shows writing doing administrative work: organizing time so records can be stored, found and compared.

Q5 story · The writing we lost

Writing Is Older Than the Bones, but the Earliest Books Rotted Away

Mature by 1200 BCE. The oracle-bone script was already a full writing system.
Earlier roots. Writing must have been invented before the surviving bones.
Perishable. Daily writing likely used bamboo, wood or silk.
A clue. The Shang graph for “book” pictures tied bamboo strips.

This slide gives you a puzzle about what survives. Writing appears on the oracle bones fully formed, with thousands of different characters. Your textbook concludes that writing must have been invented earlier, but its early stages cannot be traced, probably because everyday writing was done on perishable materials like wood, bamboo or silk that have rotted away (pp. 12–13).

The image supports that idea. It is a modern drawing of a real Shang character, 冊 (ce), which means a written document or book. It pictures vertical strips tied together with cord, exactly how later Chinese books of bamboo strips were made. So the Shang probably already had bamboo books, even though none survive.

Your textbook adds that only about 40 percent of the roughly five thousand characters on Shang divination texts have been deciphered. Even so, scholars no longer doubt that the Shang language and script are the direct ancestors of later Chinese.

Why this matters: for Review Question 5, this is a lesson about evidence. The bones show only the durable part of Shang writing, so the Shang court probably wrote much more than we can read.

Q5 story · Writing and control

Literacy Was an Ally of Political Control Across an Expanding Realm

Distance. Written orders carried the king’s words where he could not go.
Counting. Records made tribute, campaigns and labor countable, and collectable.
Restricted. Years of training kept literacy in elite hands.
Permanent. Words cast in bronze announced claims meant to last forever.

Takeaway: like divination, writing gave the king an advantage few could share.

This slide gives you the causal core of Review Question 5. Your textbook puts it in one sentence: “Literacy is an ally of political control, facilitating communication across an expanding realm” (p. 12). Ask three questions about any writing system: who could write, what did they write about, and whose power grew as a result?

Writing let a ruler do things speech cannot. It stores information beyond human memory, carries exact orders across distance, and lets the center check what officials and allies claim. Once tribute and campaigns are written down, the court can compare this year with last year and notice who is falling short. Because learning the script took years, literacy belonged to a small trained elite, and controlling writing meant controlling the state's information. That is the same pattern you saw in Lecture 1, where the king was best qualified to reach the most powerful ancestors.

The image is a real bronze lid from around the time the Zhou replaced the Shang, with its inscription cast into the metal. Bones were filed away; bronze inscriptions were displayed in ancestral temples so that descendants would read them for generations.

Why this matters: a strong Review Question 5 answer is causal. Say what writing let the Shang do, then explain how restricted access turned those abilities into political control.

Q6 concept · Logographic script

Concept: A Logographic Script Uses Characters That Stand for Whole Words

Concept

Logographic script

A writing system in which each character stands for a word, not a sound. Chinese is the great surviving example; most of the world now writes with phonetic scripts.

Phonetic. Symbols stand for sounds; two dozen letters can spell any word.
Logographic. Each character stands for a whole word, with its meaning.
Same family. Early Egyptian and Sumerian writing worked this way too.
Key effect. Readers grasp meaning without sharing the same pronunciation.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 1, p. 13.

Before you see how Chinese characters work, you need one term. A logographic script is a writing system in which each character stands for a whole word. The word comes from Greek: logos, "word," and graphein, "to write."

Compare the alphabet you are reading now. English uses a phonetic script, where symbols stand for sounds; about two dozen letters can spell any word, as long as you know how it is pronounced. Your textbook says the Shang script was logographic, like the early writing of ancient Egypt and Sumer, and that in western Eurasia those systems were eventually changed into or replaced by phonetic ones. In China that never happened (p. 13).

The key effect is on the right of the slide. Because a character carries meaning, a reader can understand it without knowing how the writer pronounced the word. Hold on to that idea; it explains why one script could serve speakers of different dialects, and readers living centuries apart.

There is no image here: a concept slide gives you a tool, and the next two slides put it to work.

Q6 story · How characters work

Chinese Characters Stand for Words, Not Sounds, and Most Combine Meaning and Sound

Logographic. Each character stands for a whole word.
Pictures. Some characters began as drawings, like horse and sun.
Borrowing. Some graphs were borrowed for words that sounded alike.
Combining. Over half of oracle-bone graphs join a meaning part and a sound part.
The cost. Basic literacy needs two to three thousand characters.
×

Logographic vs. phonetic scripts

In a phonetic script, like the English alphabet, symbols stand for sounds, and about two dozen letters can spell any word.

In a logographic script, each character stands for a whole word. Chinese is the great surviving example, similar in principle to ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs and Sumerian cuneiform. It takes longer to learn, but a reader can understand a character without knowing how someone else pronounces it.

This slide gives you how Chinese characters actually work. You now know that in a logographic script each character stands for a whole word. But how do you make thousands of characters (p. 13)?

Characters were made in several ways. Some began as pictures, like the horse with its mane and legs and the sun as a circle with a dot in the chart. Some graphs were borrowed for a different word that sounded the same. And the most common method joined two graphs, one hinting at meaning and one at sound. More than half of the characters on the oracle bones already combine parts this way.

The chart is course-made from public-domain drawings of real ancient forms. Read it left to right: oracle bone, bronze, seal script, modern. The shapes change, but it is one continuous script.

There is a cost. Your textbook says basic literacy requires knowing the characters for two or three thousand common words. Because characters are built from a few hundred parts, it is easier than it sounds, but it still takes much longer than learning an alphabet.

Why this matters: for Reading Guide Q6, knowing how characters were built explains both their cost and their staying power.

Q6 story · One script, many tongues

One Script Held China Together Across Different Dialects and Many Centuries

Across space. Speakers of different dialects could read the same texts.
Across time. Readers could still understand writing from centuries earlier.
Kept, not replaced. Western Eurasia switched to phonetic scripts; China never did.
Beyond China. Korea, Japan and Vietnam all began writing with Chinese characters.

Cost and benefit: years of study for a few; one written language for all readers.

This slide gives you the payoff of the logographic system. In western Eurasia, logographic scripts were eventually changed into or replaced by phonetic ones. That never happened in China. Why keep a script that takes years to learn?

Your textbook's answer: because Chinese characters carry meaning regardless of pronunciation, people who could not understand each other's speech could still read each other's letters and books. As spoken Chinese split into mutually unintelligible regional varieties (meaning speakers could not understand each other), the written language stayed one. And because characters stayed recognizable even as pronunciation changed over centuries, educated readers could read texts written long before without translation. In the textbook's words, the script “played a large role in holding China together and fostering a sense of connection with the past” (p. 13).

The map is a course-made teaching map. The arrows are broad and schematic: Korea, Japan and Vietnam each adopted and adapted Chinese characters over many centuries, not in a single event.

Weigh the cost too. Years of study were paid by the families who could afford to train a son instead of sending him to work; the benefits went to rulers, who governed through written orders, and to the educated, whose skill made them indispensable. That is why the writing story keeps bringing you back to power.

Why this matters: this is the heart of Reading Guide Q6 and it strengthens your Q5 answer. The script did more than serve Shang kings; it became a lasting bond for Chinese civilization.

Q7 story · How a bronze was cast

Shang Artisans Cast Bronze in Clay Piece-Molds, Step by Careful Step

Shape a clay model of the finished vessel.
Press clay around it to make mold sections; carve the decoration.
Fire the piece-mold sections, then fit them around a clay core.
Pour molten bronze into the gap and let it harden.
Break away the mold, then finish and polish the vessel.
×

Piece-mold casting

In piece-mold casting, a vessel is cast inside a mold made of separate fired-clay sections fitted together around a core.

Because the decoration was carved into the mold sections, every line had to be planned before the metal was poured. The method rewarded precision and specialization.

This slide gives you how the work was done. The Chinese method is called piece-mold casting: artisans made a clay model of the vessel, built mold sections of clay around it, fired them hard, and fitted them around a clay core. Molten bronze was poured into the narrow gap between core and mold. After it cooled, the mold pieces were broken off and the vessel was finished.

Your textbook stresses that the high precision and standardization of surviving vessels means there must have been considerable division of labor: skilled artisans to make clay models, others to build the ceramic piece-molds, others to assemble and finish each vessel, and managers overseeing the whole process (p. 13). (Your textbook's Figure 1.1 shows one of these molds.)

The image is a real fragment of a clay mold from the royal foundry at Anyang. Notice the animal-face pattern carved into it. The decoration you see on Shang bronzes was cut, in reverse, into molds like this one.

Why this matters: for Review Question 7, the steps show specialization. No single person could do every job, so bronze proves the Shang could organize experts.

Q7 story · Ritual, not war

This slide gives you the surprise of Shang bronze. You might expect a warlike state with chariots and bronze-tipped spears to put most of its metal into weapons. That choice is itself evidence. When a society spends its scarcest high-tech material on ceremonies for the dead, it is telling you where it believes real power lives. Bronze was the equipment of the sacred state you met in Lecture 1.

Q7 story · The face no one can explain

Wild Animals Cover Shang Bronzes, and Scholars Still Argue About What They Mean

Wild, not farm. Shang art favors wild animals, not crops, kings or slaves.
Cicadas. Insects reborn from the earth may suggest rebirth among ancestors.
Birds. They may stand for messengers to realms in the sky.
Taotie. A monster, a mask, a sacrifice symbol, or just design?
×

Taotie (饕餮)

The taotie is the stylized, symmetrical animal face that dominates Shang bronze decoration, with two staring eyes, horns and curling jaws.

Scholars do not agree on its meaning. Some see a monster that scared away evil, some a ritual mask, others a sign of animal sacrifice, totemism or shamanism, and some think it was hardly more than a design.

This slide gives you a mystery, and an honest lesson about evidence. Your textbook notes that in the art of ancient Egypt, Assyria and Babylonia, pictures of farm crops, animals, kings, priests and slaves match what we know about those societies (p. 14). Shang bronzes are different: wild animals dominate.

Some meanings are easy to guess. Cicadas spend years underground before emerging, so they probably suggested rebirth in the world of the ancestral spirits. Birds suggest messengers who can reach the sky. The hardest case is the most common image, the stylized animal face called the taotie (say “tao-tyeh”). Is it a monster to scare off evil, a ritual mask, a sign of animal sacrifice, totemism or shamanism, or hardly more than a design? Scholars have not agreed.

The image is a real Shang bronze wine jar, a you, now in the Musée Cernuschi in Paris. Find the pair of staring eyes on the belly of the jar, then trace the horns and jaws outward; a smaller face repeats on the lid. Each face is split down the middle and mirrored, so it can also be read as two animals in profile meeting nose to nose.

This slide gives you the detective work. Your textbook says both pits were filled in layers: small objects on the bottom, then larger ones, then the elephant tusks, then rammed earth (p. 15). Many objects in Pit 1 had been burned before being placed there, and others had been purposely broken. Click “Gods or ancestors?” on the slide to see the full list of what each pit held.

So what happened? One possibility is that the pits are the remains of a huge sacrifice. But unlike major Shang sacrifices, there is no sign of human victims. Some scholars speculate that the bronze figures of humans were used to replace humans in a sacrificial ceremony. The bronze heads were probably once attached to wood or clay statues and could have represented gods or ancestors. It is even possible the local people decided, for some reason, that those gods or their images had to be burned and buried.

The image is the real bronze standing figure from Pit 2, about 2.6 meters tall with its base, one of the largest bronze human figures surviving from this era anywhere in the world. His huge hands once held something, perhaps an ivory tusk; nobody knows for certain.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the spread of an idea, technology or crop from one people to another, often step by step through neighbors, rather than by one group inventing it independently.

← Back to the lecture: the Northern Zone

This independent-study slide gives you Review Question 9: what technologies and crops reached China through diffusion (the spread of ideas or things from one people to another) from other parts of Eurasia (p. 10).

Start with the chariot, because your textbook makes an argument, not just a claim. Technologies usually leave a trail of development: simple carts before fine chariots, solid wheels before spoked ones. In China there is no such trail. Your textbook says there is no evidence of animals pulling loads, or of wheels of any kind, before the chariot, which leads to the conclusion that the chariot arrived by diffusion across Asia. This independent-study slide shows how fast archaeology can overtake a textbook. Between 2020 and 2022, archaeologists reopened the Sanxingdui sacrificial area and found six new pits, numbered 3 to 8, right beside the two found in 1986. They produced nearly 13,000 numbered finds, including bronzes, ivory, jade, traces of silk and a gold mask from Pit 5 weighing about 280 grams. Radiocarbon dates announced in 2022 placed Pits 3, 4, 7 and 8 between roughly 1131 and 1012 BCE, at the very end of the Shang. Your textbook also says no later sites of the Sanxingdui culture had been found (p. 15). That has changed too. In 2001, construction in the city of Chengdu uncovered Jinsha, a rich site with gold, jade and ivory in a similar style, which flourished after Sanxingdui declined. Many archaeologists now see Jinsha as Sanxingdui's successor, so the culture may have moved rather than vanished.

This independent-study slide gives you the big arguments behind the story. David Keightley, the leading Western scholar of oracle bones, showed that the divination records worked like an early government archive: dated, formulaic and centered on the king. William Boltz argues that Chinese writing developed through the same stages as other early scripts (pictures, then sound-borrowing, then combined characters) but never became fully phonetic, which preserved the word-based system that held China together.

Robert Bagley stresses that piece-mold casting shaped everything about Chinese bronzes and that the foundries show management as impressive as the art. He is also a strong voice for decentering: the Shang-centered picture, he argues, partly reflects where archaeologists dug first and which society left texts. Sarah Allan reads bronze decoration like the taotie through Shang myth and sacrifice, while admitting the evidence cannot force one answer. Li Feng treats early China as a participant in Bronze Age Eurasia, with the steppe as a corridor for metal, chariots and horses.

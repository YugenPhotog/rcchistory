# HIST 270 | Chapter 8 | Lecture 1
# Song China Shared Power with Strong Northern States

**Format:** Detailed slide outline for a future Reveal.js deck  
**Duration:** 35 minutes, including two five-minute classroom activities  
**Audience:** First-year community college students  
**Assigned source:** Ebrey, *A History of China*, Chapter 8, especially pp. 129–131 and 138–139; concluding interpretation on p. 147  
**Sequence:** Lecture 1 of five; followed by “Agriculture and Trade Sustained Song Prosperity”

## Historical argument

Song rulers stabilized their dynasty by subordinating military commanders to civilian government, but they could not dominate neighboring states. Treaties and payments made coexistence possible. When Song allied with the rising Jurchen Jin to defeat Liao, the regional balance changed against Song: the dynasty lost the north but survived in the south. Liao and Jin were organized states with their own administrative systems and claims to imperial legitimacy.

**Framing question:** How could a powerful Chinese dynasty survive without controlling all of the territory it claimed?

## Learning targets

By the end of the lecture, students should be able to:

- Explain how Taizu reduced the danger of internal military takeovers.
- Compare Liao’s government of its Khitan and Chinese populations.
- Explain why Song rulers negotiated peace and why some officials opposed treaty payments.
- Trace the sequence from Jin’s founding to the fall of Northern Song in 1127.
- Explain how Jin used Chinese institutions while maintaining its own authority and identity.
- Distinguish Northern Song at Kaifeng from Southern Song at Hangzhou.

## Detailed slide outline

### Slide 1 | Song China Shared Power with Strong Northern States

**Time:** 0:00–0:30  
**Format:** Title slide with a restrained regional-map backdrop or no image

**Student-facing text**

- HIST 270 | Chapter 8 | Lecture 1
- Northern Song and Southern Song
- Framing line: “Military rivals, negotiated peace, and a dynasty that survived the loss of its northern capital.”

No presenter notes are needed for the title slide. Introduce the framing question orally, then move immediately to the map.

### Slide 2 | Song Ruled Alongside Liao and Xia

**Time:** 0:30–2:00  
**Format:** Map with a compact identification key  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 129–131; Map 8.1, p. 131

**Student-facing bullets**

- Song controlled a large Chinese population, but not every Chinese-speaking region.
- Khitan Liao held territory to the northeast, including the Beijing region.
- Tangut Xia controlled territory to the northwest.
- These neighboring states had rulers, governments, and military power of their own.

**Map labels:** Song, Liao, Xia, Kaifeng, the Beijing region, and the approximate Sixteen Prefectures area. Use the textbook’s ca. 1050 map for this stage of the lecture.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Separate the names of ruling peoples from dynastic names: Khitan is the people, Liao the dynasty; Tangut is the people, Xia the dynasty. Northern Song refers to the phase when Song’s court was based at Kaifeng. The lecture title’s “shared power” means that several states coexisted and negotiated; they did not jointly administer a single government or possess equal resources. Do not label Jin on a map of ca. 1050.

### Slide 3 | Taizu Put Generals Under Civilian Supervision

**Time:** 2:00–3:30  
**Format:** Three-step institutional sequence with a short callout  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 130

**Student-facing bullets**

- Zhao Kuangyin, later Emperor Taizu, founded Song in 960.
- His troops had placed him on the throne.
- He retired or rotated generals and assigned civilian officials to supervise them.
- The goal was to prevent commanders from replacing the ruler.

**Callout:** “Control the commanders to protect the dynasty.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Connect briefly to the military fragmentation that followed the Tang. A warlord is a commander whose military base lets him exercise political power with limited central control. Taizu understood the danger because his own rise demonstrated it. Civilian supervision did not abolish Song’s army; it changed who exercised authority over its commanders. Do not present this policy as a sufficient explanation for every later military defeat.

### Slide 4 | Liao Held the Northern Defenses Song Wanted to Recover

**Time:** 3:30–5:00  
**Format:** Focused map detail with three explanatory bullets  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 130

**Student-facing bullets**

- Liao had acquired the Sixteen Prefectures during the Five Dynasties period.
- The region included mountains, passes, and the line of the Great Wall.
- Taizu and Taizong tried unsuccessfully to recover it.
- A large population did not guarantee victory over Liao’s mounted forces.

**Presenter-note guidance**

A prefecture was an administrative territory. Explain why these particular territories mattered: holding defensive terrain could affect military access to the plains farther south. Avoid treating the Great Wall as the modern tourist monument or as an impenetrable barrier. Song’s claims combined strategic concerns with the conviction that the region belonged within its realm.

### Slide 5 | Liao Used Different Institutions for Different Populations

**Time:** 5:00–7:00  
**Format:** Two-column comparison  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 130–131  
**Review alignment:** Q2

**Student-facing comparison**

| Northern territories | Southern territories |
|---|---|
| Predominantly Khitan pastoral populations | Large settled Chinese populations |
| Mobile court moved seasonally | Chinese-style civil bureaucracy |
| Khitan social and military organization remained important | Examinations and agricultural tax revenue |

**Callout:** “One dynasty could govern different populations through different institutions.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Abaoji united Khitan tribes and borrowed hereditary succession from Chinese monarchy. His successors did not have to erase Khitan practices to use Chinese administrative methods. Liao’s southern administration also included territories granted to imperial relatives and senior officials, so it was not a complete copy of Song government. Define sinification as adopting Chinese cultural practices and institutions, then emphasize that such adoption could be selective. This comparison supplies the evidence for Q2 without turning it into a separate activity.

### Slide 6 | Song Bought Peace After Failing to Defeat Liao

**Time:** 7:00–9:00  
**Format:** Short event sequence plus payment callout  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 130

**Student-facing bullets**

- A Liao invasion in 1004 approached Kaifeng.
- Song agreed to annual payments in exchange for peace.
- The initial payments were 100,000 ounces of silver and 200,000 bolts of silk.
- The treaty preserved Song rule without recovering the disputed frontier.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Use the source’s sequence: the invasion of 1004 led to a settlement; the chapter does not supply a separate formal treaty date here. Explain a bolt as a length of cloth. Describe payments as a negotiated means of managing a military rival. The guide’s word “unprecedented” should not become a claim that Chinese rulers had never previously paid or negotiated with neighbors. Emphasize Song’s accommodation to a rival it could not dominate.

### Slide 7 | Treaty Payments Cost Less Than Maintaining the Army

**Time:** 9:00–10:30  
**Format:** Paired numerical callouts with a brief disagreement panel  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 130

**Student-facing bullets**

- By 1022, the military consumed about three-quarters of state tax revenues.
- Peaceful relations with Liao cost no more than about 2–3 percent of annual revenues.
- Advocates of peace emphasized expense and stability.
- Advocates of war emphasized lost territory and humiliation.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The figures compare the military budget with the costs of maintaining peaceful relations with Liao; they are not a precise comparison of one campaign’s cost with one treaty payment. Keep the moral and political disagreement visible even when the financial argument seems compelling. The chapter also explains that some silver returned to Song through purchases of Chinese exports; reserve the fuller trade discussion for Lecture 2. Do not reveal a polished answer to Q1 before the activity.

### Slide 8 | Xia Also Forced Song to Negotiate

**Time:** 10:30–12:00  
**Format:** Three bullets beside a northwest map detail  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 131

**Student-facing bullets**

- Tangut rulers consolidated Xia in the northwest.
- Xia controlled important horse-trading connections and developed its own script.
- After unsuccessful fighting, Song accepted a treaty with Xia in 1044 and made payments.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Under Yuanhao, Xia asserted its dynastic identity and demanded recognition as a sovereign state. Its institutions drew on several regional models, and Buddhism was its state religion. Mention this religious detail briefly; this is not a religion lecture. Xia reinforces the geographical point: Song faced more than one organized neighbor. Avoid implying that Liao and Xia had identical relationships with Song or with each other.

### Slide 9 | Pause & Process: Explain Song’s Responses to Military Threats

**Time:** 12:00–17:00  
**Format:** Classroom activity slide  
**Source:** Reading guide, Review Question 1; textbook, p. 130

**Original review question**

> How did the founders of the Song Dynasty resolve the threat of internal warlordism, and what unprecedented diplomatic strategies did they use to manage the Khitan Liao Empire to the north?

**Student-facing task**

- Write a brief response of 3–4 sentences.
- Identify one measure used to control Song’s commanders.
- Identify one measure used to manage the Liao threat.
- Explain why these different problems called for different responses.

**Five-minute routine**

1. **Two minutes:** Write individually using lecture evidence.
2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; check that both internal and external threats are explained.
3. **One minute:** Share a response and revise one sentence if needed.

Do not require students to recopy the reading guide’s evidence sentence. Keep instructor response criteria in the appendix, not on this slide. No presenter notes are needed for the activity.

### Slide 10 | The Jurchens Founded Jin Before Northern Song Fell

**Time:** 17:00–18:30  
**Format:** Northeast map detail plus a prominent date  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 138

**Student-facing bullets**

- Jurchen communities lived northeast of Song and challenged Liao authority.
- Their economy combined hunting, fishing, animal husbandry, and farming.
- Wanyan Aguda proclaimed the Jin Dynasty in 1115.
- Jin’s rise changed the balance among Song, Liao, and Xia.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Distinguish Jurchens from Khitans, Tanguts, and Mongols. They were not simply another name for Liao. The date 1115 establishes that Jin existed before the events of 1127: conquest of Song did not create the dynasty. Horse raising and regional contacts help explain Jurchen military capacity without reducing them to a stereotype of purely nomadic outsiders.

### Slide 11 | Song Allied with Jin to Recover Its Northern Frontier

**Time:** 18:30–20:00  
**Format:** Three-step diplomatic sequence  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 138

**Student-facing bullets**

- Song still wanted to recover the Sixteen Prefectures.
- Song and Jin agreed to cooperate against Liao.
- The proposed division of Liao territory promised Song its disputed frontier.
- Fighting revealed Song’s military vulnerability to its new ally.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The alliance followed a recognizable strategic logic: use Liao’s rising challenger to recover land that earlier Song campaigns had failed to regain. Explain the intended outcome before describing the failure. Students should understand why the plan appealed to Song rulers, rather than assuming they knowingly invited their own defeat. Removing Liao did not ensure that Jin would accept Song’s preferred balance of power.

### Slide 12 | Jin’s Attack on Kaifeng Ended Northern Song in 1127

**Time:** 20:00–22:00  
**Format:** Timeline centered on 1115 and 1127, with a consequence callout  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 129, 138–139

**Student-facing bullets**

- After defeating Liao, Jin attacked Song.
- Kaifeng was besieged; the court made enormous payments.
- Members of the imperial family, including Huizong, were taken north as captives.
- The fall of the northern court in 1127 ended Northern Song.

**Callout:** “Northern Song ended; the Song Dynasty continued.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Huizong’s cultural patronage deserves one brief contextual sentence, but do not make artistic interests the sole cause of defeat. The important sequence is Jin’s rise, the anti-Liao alliance, the exposure of Song’s weakness, and Jin’s attack. Captivity disrupted the court and removed members of its ruling family. The phrase Northern Song describes a phase of the dynasty, not a dynasty unrelated to Southern Song.

### Slide 13 | Jin Adopted Chinese Institutions to Govern Northern China

**Time:** 22:00–24:00  
**Format:** Institutional borrowing panel with three bullets  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 138  
**Review alignment:** Q7, governing the conquered population

**Student-facing bullets**

- Jin initially continued elements of Liao’s dual administration.
- It employed former Liao officials, both Chinese and Khitan.
- Governing a much larger Chinese population encouraged wider use of Chinese institutions and officials.
- Hereditary succession strengthened rulers in competition with their own nobles.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Conquest and administration are separate problems. Victory did not eliminate the need for officials and institutions capable of governing a large settled population. Returning to Liao’s dual system makes adaptation intelligible rather than introducing another unexplained structure. Chinese institutions could serve Jurchen rulers’ political goals; borrowing them did not mean handing sovereignty to the Chinese officials who operated them.

### Slide 14 | Jin Rulers Combined Institutional Borrowing with Jurchen Authority

**Time:** 24:00–26:00  
**Format:** Two-column panel: “Institutions adopted” and “Authority and identity maintained”  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 138

**Student-facing bullets**

- Chinese language, customs, and institutions spread among Jurchens.
- Some rulers promoted Jurchen-language schools, translations, and examinations.
- Jin emperors asserted authority over officials, including through corporal punishment.
- Later rulers claimed the Chinese classics and the “Central Kingdom” as their own.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Adoption of Chinese practices remained contested and changed over time. Jurchen-language education was one response to concerns about cultural loss. The chapter’s example of officials being flogged shows that using a Chinese bureaucracy did not require adopting every Confucian norm concerning officials’ treatment. Close with the claim to the Central Kingdom: Jin rulers did not necessarily accept the Song view that only Song represented legitimate civilization. Do not make the uncertain particulars of the 1161 assassination central to this slide.

### Slide 15 | Song Survived by Reestablishing Its Court in the South

**Time:** 26:00–28:00  
**Format:** Southern Song map with a compact capital comparison  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 139; Map 8.2, p. 139

**Student-facing bullets**

- A son of Huizong escaped capture and became Emperor Gaozong in 1127.
- The court retreated south under continuing Jin pressure.
- Hangzhou became the Southern Song capital.
- A peace treaty with Jin in 1141 required further payments of silk and silver.

**Capital comparison:** Northern Song: Kaifeng | Southern Song: Hangzhou

**Presenter-note guidance**

Use Map 8.2 as a view of the later, ca. 1200 arrangement, not a snapshot of the moment Gaozong became emperor. Point out the Yangzi River and Hangzhou’s location south of it. Jin pursued Song forces across the Yangzi, so the river was not an automatic guarantee of safety. Political relocation, continuing defense, and negotiation all mattered. Briefly establish that the economic center had already shifted south; Lecture 2 will explain the process and its fiscal consequences.

### Slide 16 | Rival Empires Shared a Region Claimed by Multiple Rulers

**Time:** 28:00–30:00  
**Format:** Three synthesis callouts  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 130–131, 138–139, 147

**Student-facing bullets**

- Civilian control stabilized Song internally without eliminating external rivals.
- Liao and Jin used Chinese institutions while preserving their own ruling authority.
- Song’s survival depended on adapting to a changing balance of power.

**Closing callout:** “More than one ruler claimed imperial legitimacy.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Reconnect political events to the chapter’s title. “Among equals” does not mean every state had identical strength, culture, or diplomatic status. The central issue is that Song could not impose a single political order on all its neighbors. The chapter’s conclusion notes that multiple rulers called themselves Son of Heaven, complicating the ideal of one universally legitimate emperor. End with the bridge to Lecture 2: the court lost northern territory, yet retained much of the population and productive southern land. Do not provide the completed Q7 answer before students attempt it.

### Slide 17 | Pause & Process: Explain Jin’s Conquest and Government

**Time:** 30:00–35:00  
**Format:** Final classroom activity slide  
**Source:** Reading guide, Review Question 7; textbook, p. 138

**Original review question**

> Explain the collapse of the Northern Song and the subsequent rise of the Jurchen Jin Dynasty. How did the Jin adapt to ruling a large Chinese population while asserting their own political authority?

**Chronology clarification in the task:** Jin was founded in 1115, before Northern Song fell in 1127.

**Student-facing task**

- Write a response of 3–5 sentences.
- Explain how the Song–Jin alliance against Liao contributed to Northern Song’s collapse.
- Identify one institution or administrative practice Jin adopted.
- Identify one way Jin maintained its own authority or identity.

**Five-minute routine**

1. **Two minutes:** Write individually from lecture evidence.
2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; check both chronology and the distinction between conquest and government.
3. **One minute:** Share an example and make a final correction.

Keep instructor response criteria in the appendix. No presenter notes are needed for the activity. If the activity is later used as an engagement submission, this final slide can carry the submission instruction without adding another task.

## Instructor-only activity response criteria

### Activity 1 | Q1

A satisfactory response should explain that Taizu retired or rotated generals and placed them under civilian supervision to reduce the danger of military takeovers. Song’s northern rivals presented a different problem: they were independent powers beyond its domestic chain of command. After unsuccessful warfare and a threatening Liao invasion, Song negotiated peace through annual payments. Stronger responses explain the competing considerations of security, cost, lost territory, and prestige.

**Useful debrief correction:** The policy restrained commanders; it did not eliminate the army. Peace payments did not mean that Liao directly governed Song.

### Activity 2 | Q7

A satisfactory response should place Jin’s founding before the fall of Northern Song and connect the anti-Liao alliance with Jin’s discovery of Song’s military vulnerability. It should give an administrative example, such as retaining Liao officials, using Chinese-style institutions, or employing more Chinese officials. It should also give an authority or identity example, such as hereditary succession, Jurchen-language education, distinct treatment of officials, or claims to the Central Kingdom.

**Useful debrief correction:** Jin’s conquest did not end the entire Song Dynasty. Using Chinese institutions did not make Jin rulers subjects of Song.

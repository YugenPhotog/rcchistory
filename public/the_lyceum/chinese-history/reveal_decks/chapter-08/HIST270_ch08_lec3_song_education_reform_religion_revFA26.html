Educated culture · Review Question 5

Printing Expanded Access to Books and Educated Culture

Cheaper books
Printing lowered the cost of books.
→
More readers
More readers could obtain classics, poetry, histories, and works on other subjects.
→
Exam demand
Demand for books grew with preparation for government examinations.
The educated elite became larger, better educated, and less aristocratic in its habits.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 134.

Lecture 2 showed papermaking expanding to supply books. Here is the cultural result.

Textbook anchor: "The spread of printing aided the expansion of the educated class." (p. 134)

Breaking it down: in China, as in Europe centuries later, printing brought down the price of books. Song scholars could afford many more books than their predecessors. Publishers printed the classics in huge editions, and readers bought works on philosophy, science, medicine, and Buddhism, along with Han and Tang poetry and histories.

How it connects: books and examinations reinforced each other. Preparing for the exams created demand for texts, and cheaper texts made preparation easier. Compared with the Tang, the Song scholar-official class was larger, better educated, and less aristocratic in its habits.

Keep the terms straight. A scholar-official was an educated man serving in government. The wider educated class also included many men who never held office. And more books did not make literacy universal.

The examinations · recruitment and fairness

More degrees

Song recruited more holders of the highest examination degree (jinshi) than Tang had.

Numbers, not names

Candidate names were replaced with numbers.

Recopied papers

Clerks recopied papers so examiners could not recognize handwriting.

Growing competition made success difficult and often required repeated attempts.
Anonymous evaluation reduced opportunities for favoritism.
×

Jinshi

"Presented scholar": the highest examination degree identified in this chapter. It brought great prestige, but not a guaranteed career or rapid promotion.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 135.

The Song examination system grew larger and tried to become fairer.

Two points of view: early Song emperors wanted the exams to identify capable men; if successful candidates were literate, intelligent, and hard-working, rulers were satisfied even if some able men were missed. Candidates cared about fairness. Did everyone get an equal chance? Did examiners favor people they knew?

Fairer procedures: to build confidence in examiners' objectivity, candidates' names were replaced with numbers, and clerks recopied every paper so handwriting could not be recognized. Recopying concealed identity; it did not change what candidates wrote.

More competition: the Song recruited four to five times more jinshi per year than the Tang. Rather than cheapening the degree, this drew many more men into the competition. Because the number of posts did not grow, each candidate's chances fell sharply. Men often took the exams several times and were, on average, a little over thirty when they passed. Remember the trend, not every total.

Keep four things separate: passing an exam, holding a degree, being appointed, and being promoted. Success at one step did not guarantee the next.

Opportunity and privilege

Family Resources Continued to Shape Access to Office

Expanded opportunities

  • Examinations could identify capable men beyond established official families
  • Anonymous papers reduced examiner favoritism
  • Successful candidates gained prestige

Continuing advantages

  • Sustained education required family resources
  • Prominent families supplied teachers, contacts, and support
  • Senior officials could nominate sons and grandsons for appointments

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 135–136.

A fairer test is not the same as equal access to the test.

The privilege route: sons and grandsons of high officials did not have to take the examinations at all. Their fathers or grandfathers could nominate them for appointment, and the chapter estimates that around 40 percent or more of posts were filled this way. These appointees usually started at the very bottom, as sheriffs in remote places, and might spend whole careers in county posts, which is why most officials' sons still tried the exams.

How historians see it: in the 1950s and 1960s, Western historians stressed the system's meritocracy; lists of graduates showed only about half had fathers, grandfathers, or great-grandfathers who had been officials. More recent historians stress the advantages of official families, including uncles and maternal grandfathers who had served. The textbook's balance: compared with other premodern societies, including Korea and Japan, Song China offered exceptional opportunities to talented men without powerful relatives. But mobility was nowhere near modern levels.

Family resources: families able to educate their sons were generally landholders. In one county of twenty thousand households, a dozen or so family lines might produce nearly everyone who gained national notice.

Why this matters: state recruitment became more open without becoming socially equal.

The new elite did more than prepare for tests. This slide completes Review Question 5's question about culture.

Networks: scholar-officials rarely spent their lives at home. They traveled to study with famous teachers, went to the capital for exams, and moved from post to post across the empire, calling on colleagues along the way. When the poet Lu You took up a post in Sichuan in 1170, he spent 157 days on the road.

Scholar-officials · the art of the cultivated mind

Su Shi (Su Dongpo, 1037–1101)

Exiled to Huangzhou after the 1079 poetry trial, he wrote the Cold Food poems shown here.
Painting should express the artist's mind and ideas, not mere outward likeness: a founding idea of literati (scholar-amateur) painting.
Expressive calligraphy: heavy, deliberately uneven strokes; characters grow and shift as feeling rises.
Poetry, painting, and calligraphy as one art of the cultivated mind.

Let's look closely at this scroll. Su Shi, Su Dongpo, wrote it in Huangzhou, where he was exiled after the 1079 poetry trial. These are his two poems on the Cold Food festival of 1082, in his own hand.

Start at the right and read down the columns. The opening characters are fairly small and steady. Now move left. The strokes get heavier, the characters swell, some lean, some crowd together. That unevenness is deliberate. As the poem turns to rain, poverty, and homesickness, the brush seems to carry the feeling.

This is the heart of his style. Su argued, and I'm paraphrasing, that judging a painting by how closely it resembles its subject is a child's way of seeing. What matters is the mind and ideas of the person behind the brush. That view helped found the ideal of literati painting, made by scholar-amateurs rather than professionals.

For Su, poetry, painting, and calligraphy were one art. Each one showed a cultivated mind at work.

Use the arrows to walk through the gallery. After the Cold Food scroll comes Withered Tree and Strange Rock, attributed to him: a twisting tree and a swirling rock done in quick, rough strokes, a painting of ideas rather than likeness. Last is his own copy of the Former Ode on the Red Cliff, written in 1083; compare its calm, even characters with the agitated Cold Food scroll.

You may notice the gallery is mostly calligraphy. No painting survives that scholars all agree is by Su Shi; even Withered Tree and Strange Rock is only attributed. His calligraphy, though, was prized and collected from his own day onward, so it is our best surviving evidence of how his brush worked.

Scholar-officials · curiosity about the natural world

Shen Gua (1031–1095)

Dream Pool Essays (c. 1088, written in retirement near Zhenjiang): hundreds of short notes on astronomy, math, fossils, medicine, music, technology, and court life.
Earliest clear description of the magnetic compass needle and of magnetic declination.
His book is our only source for Bi Sheng, a commoner who invented movable type (c. 1040s) using baked-clay characters.
He reasoned about fossils and worked on the calendar and maps.
×

Magnetic declination

A compass needle doesn't point exactly to true (geographic) north. It points to magnetic north, slightly east or west of true north. Shen Gua noticed this gap, which navigators must correct for.

Now meet Shen Gua, a very different kind of scholar-official. He served under Wang Anshi, ran the court's astronomical bureau, and worked on calendar reform and maps.

Around 1088, in retirement at his Dream Brook garden near Zhenjiang, he wrote the Dream Pool Essays. Think of it as a notebook: several hundred short entries on astronomy and the calendar, mathematics, geology and fossils, medicine, music, engineering and technology, plus anecdotes about court and government. What you see is a Ming printing, made centuries later. No portrait of him from his own time survives.

Two entries matter most. First, the earliest clear description of a magnetized needle compass. He also noticed that the needle does not point exactly north or south. Click the term to see what that means.

Second, be careful here: Shen Gua did not invent movable type. His book is our only source for Bi Sheng, a commoner who printed with baked-clay type in the 1040s.

And he saw fossil shells far inland and reasoned that the land was once under water. Remember that this kind of curiosity was rarer among the elite than poetry and collecting.

Reform · the 1040s

Reformers Wanted Officials to Serve the State More Effectively

The problem: officials disagreed about recruitment, public service, military policy, and revenue.
Fan Zhongyan (989–1052), a poor orphan, rose through the exams and commanded troops on the Xia frontier.
His Qingli reforms (1043–1045) proposed promotion by merit, not seniority, and limits on officials' sons' privileges. See all ten points
Officials whose privileges were threatened ended them within about two years, but Fan inspired later reformers, including Wang Anshi.
×

Fan Zhongyan's Ten-Point Memorial (1043)

  1. Promote and demote officials by merit and performance, not seniority.
  2. Curb yin, the privilege that let high officials obtain posts for their sons.
  3. Reform the exams toward policy essays and practical learning, and build schools.
  4. Choose capable local officials.
  5. Equalize the stipend lands that supported officials, so local officials had enough income.
  6. Encourage farming and water-control works.
  7. Strengthen local militia and defense.
  8. Reduce labor-service burdens.
  9. Make sure imperial acts of grace actually reach the people.
  10. Enforce laws and edicts faithfully.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 136–137.

Passing the exams did not make officials agree, or even make them good at their jobs. Younger, idealistic officials saw the average official as morally lazy and blamed the exam system. Military and fiscal policy caused tension too.

Enter Fan Zhongyan. He lost his father as a small child and grew up poor, studied hard, and passed the exams. He became prefect of Kaifeng and led the frontier defense against the Tangut Xia. In his essay on Yueyang Tower he set out the ideal he is remembered for: the scholar should be first to worry about the realm's troubles and last to enjoy its pleasures.

As a chief minister in 1043, he sent the emperor a ten-point memorial. Open the pop-up to see all ten. At its heart: promote by merit instead of seniority, stop high officials from simply placing their sons, make the exams more practical, and raise local officials' incomes to discourage corruption.

Officials comfortable with the old rules pushed back, and by 1045 the program was dropped and Fan sent out of the capital. His example, though, inspired Wang Anshi.

Reform · the man behind the New Policies

Wang Anshi (1021–1086)

Born in Jiangxi; passed the jinshi exam in 1042.
Spent years as a local official; as magistrate of Yin county he tried state loans to farmers.
1058: his "Myriad Word Memorial" to Emperor Renzong urged sweeping reform.
1069: young Emperor Shenzong summoned him to lead reform. He was also a celebrated poet and prose writer.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 137.

Before we look at his program, let's meet the man. The picture on the right is an imagined likeness, made with AI; no reliable portrait from his lifetime survives.

Wang Anshi was born in 1021 in Jiangxi, in the south. He passed the highest exam in 1042 and then, unusually, chose years of service in the provinces rather than a fast track in the capital. As magistrate of Yin county, near Ningbo, he lent grain to farmers in the lean season and collected it back with modest interest. Keep that in mind: it is a small test of an idea he later took nationwide.

In 1058 he sent Emperor Renzong a very long memorial, the "Myriad Word Memorial," criticizing how the state recruited and trained officials and calling for sweeping change. Nothing much happened. Then in 1069 the young Emperor Shenzong summoned him to court and backed him.

And remember, he was not only a policymaker. He is counted among the great poets and prose writers of the Song.

Exclusive control over a line of trade. Wang expanded state monopolies on tea, salt, and wine.

Baojia (保甲)

Introduced in 1070, baojia grouped rural households into units of 10, 50, and 500 families: the bao, the large bao, and the superior bao. Members were responsible for one another, policing and reporting crime in their unit. Some men were also trained as a part-time local militia, so the state could rely less on its expensive professional standing army.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 137.

Wang Anshi managed to do what Fan Zhongyan could not: put a reform program into action. Shenzong, who had come to the throne at nineteen, made him chancellor and backed his program, the New Policies.

Textbook anchor: "Realizing that government income was ultimately linked to the prosperity of farming families, he instituted measures he thought would help them, such as low-cost loans and replacing labor service with a tax." (p. 137)

Read the table as policy, then intention. The Green Sprouts loans of 1069 scaled up his Yin county experiment. Hired service swapped labor duty for a tax. State trade and monopolies on tea, salt, and wine raised revenue, and a land survey aimed at fairer land taxes. The baojia system grouped households for mutual security and militia service, to cut the cost of a standing army. These are intentions; the table does not prove each worked.

Wang also dropped poetry from the civil service exam, hoping for more practical men, and made his own commentaries on the classics required reading.

Shenzong Kept the New Policies Going, With or Without Wang

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 137–138.

Now follow the reform years as a story.

In 1069 Shenzong puts his trust in Wang, and the New Policies begin to roll out, starting with the Green Sprouts loans. Over the next few years come hired service, state trade, the land survey, and the baojia militia.

The opposition is fierce, and it includes some of the most admired men of the age: the historian Sima Guang, and Su Shi, whom you have already met. In 1074, under pressure, Wang resigns. A year later, in 1075, Shenzong calls him back as chancellor. But it does not last. In 1076 Wang retires for good to Nanjing, where he spends his last decade writing.

Here is the surprise: the policies do not leave with him. Shenzong keeps them running for nearly another ten years, until his own death in 1085.

The portrait on the right is Shenzong himself, an official court portrait in red robe and winged cap, painted for the imperial family and now in the National Palace Museum in Taipei.

Factions · why reform divided officials

Disagreements over Reform Became Moral and Political Conflicts

Interests

Reforms affected existing arrangements and people's interests.

Philosophy

Officials disagreed philosophically about good government.

Rivalry

Competition, alliances, and personal antagonisms intensified disagreement.

Both sides portrayed themselves as principled and their opponents as morally inferior.
Disagreement about policy became a judgment about character.

Key term: faction

×

Faction

A group of officials allied around shared political positions and personal relationships.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 136–137.

Why did the New Policies provoke such bitter fighting? The textbook points to several causes working together.

Competition: even after passing the exams, officials kept competing. Promotions were not automatic, there were more qualified men than posts, and choice assignments depended on recommendations from high officials. To push a proposal, an official had to get the emperor's ear, line up allies, and malign opponents.

Interests and ideas: the strength of the resistance has led historians to suspect that interests were at stake. Wang and many reformers came from the south, but the split was not simply north versus south, or old elite versus new. Personal antagonisms and philosophical differences also mattered.

Moral language: in the vocabulary of the time, the struggle was between "men of principle" who cared about the common good and "inferior men" who could not see the larger picture. Each side, of course, saw itself as the men of principle. When each side believes it is protecting the common good, compromise is hard.

Why this matters: this is the "conflict" half of Review Question 6.

×

Regent

Someone who governs for a ruler unable to exercise power independently, often a child. Here the regent was the boy emperor Zhezong's grandmother, Empress Dowager Gao.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 137–138.

Whoever had the throne's backing could put policies into effect, and punish the other side.

Under Shenzong, opposition looked like obstruction. Critics were sent to posts outside the capital, and when the court wanted to be harsh, to the far south, where malaria was sometimes fatal to northerners. Yet dismissing every critic made it hard to learn of unforeseen problems.

In 1085 Shenzong dies in his mid-thirties. His heir, Zhezong, is only nine, so his grandmother, Empress Dowager Gao, becomes regent. She never approved of the reforms. She brings in Sima Guang, and in this Yuanyou period they cancel the New Policies almost wholesale, even popular ones like hired service. Wang Anshi dies in Nanjing in 1086, watching his work undone.

In 1093 the grandmother dies. Zhezong takes power, and from 1094 brings the reformers back and punishes their opponents. His successor Huizong also sides with the reformers. Each swing purged the other side, and the bitterness drained the Northern Song of talent and trust.

On the right is Zhezong, in the same formal court-portrait style as his father; he was the boy emperor at the center of these reversals.

Households · Review Question 10

Marriage Joined Families and Reflected Social Status

Parents arranged marriages through matchmakers or personal connections.
Families usually sought partners of comparable status.
Examination success could make a young man an attractive marriage prospect.
A man had one legal wife, but wealthy men could also take concubines: often purchased, ranked below the wife, yet their sons could be heirs.

The marriage agreement

Agreements identified family backgrounds and the gifts and dowry to be exchanged.

  • Birth dates of bride and groom
  • Parents' and grandparents' names
  • Gifts to be exchanged
  • The bride's dowry

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 141–142.

Marriage connects this part of the lecture to the educated elite you just studied.

Textbook anchor: "Before a wedding took place, written agreements were exchanged that listed the prospective bride's and groom's birth dates, parents' and grandparents' names, and the gifts that would be exchanged, as well as the dowry the bride would bring." (p. 141)

Breaking it down: women usually married between sixteen and twenty, to husbands a couple of years older. Parents arranged the match, often through professional matchmakers (usually older women) or with suggestions from friends and relatives. The aim was to match families of roughly equal status, but a young man who had just passed the civil service exams was a good prospect even if his family had little wealth or rank.

After the wedding: the bride moved into her husband's family. Her first priority was winning over her mother-in-law. In a patrilineal system, which traces the family line through men, a woman fully secured her place by becoming the mother of a son.

Concubines: taking concubines was common among officials and wealthy merchants but rare in ordinary farm families. Whoever gave birth, the wife was the legal mother of all of the man's children.

Arranged marriage does not tell us how every couple felt. The chapter also gives examples of real companionship, as you will see with Li Qingzhao.

Property · the dowry

Dowries Transferred Resources to Daughters

A dowry was property a bride's family provided when she married.
Clothes and bedding were common; wealthier dowries could include jewelry or land deeds.
Women could draw on dowry resources in family life.
Huang Sheng's burial goods illustrate the wealth transferred to one elite daughter.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 141, 146.

The dowry links the commercial wealth of Lecture 2 to family life.

Breaking it down: a few days before the wedding, the bride's family sent her dowry. At minimum it held boxes of clothes and bedding; in better-off families it included items of real value, such as gold jewelry or deeds for land. Song records show women using these resources: women who drew on their dowries to help their husbands' sisters marry well, and childless widows who accused their nephews of seizing their property. That shows women were involved with property. It does not show they were equal in every legal or household relationship.

Look at the vest. In 1242, at fourteen or fifteen, Huang Sheng married a distant member of the imperial clan. Her father, a high official, had been superintendent of foreign trade at Quanzhou. She died the following year, possibly in childbirth. Buried with her were 201 pieces of women's clothing and 153 lengths of cloth, all finely made and probably her dowry. This vest is one of them.

Read the evidence critically. One wealthy burial cannot represent every woman's circumstances. It shows how an elite family could pass property to a daughter.

Women's lives · work, learning, circumstance

Women's Work and Literacy Extended Their Household Roles

Work

Textile production and other work contributed to household livelihoods.

Some women worked as innkeepers, midwives, or producers of goods for sale.

Learning

Expanded education enabled some women to teach children and participate in literary culture.

Circumstances

Widowhood, motherhood, wealth, and household position shaped women's options.

Girls could not attend schools or take exams, but elite daughters often learned to read at home, valued because a literate mother could teach her sons.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 141–143.

The old ideal said men belong outside the house and women inside it. Song records show a much more varied reality.

Work: stories, documents, and legal cases show widows running inns, midwives delivering babies, farmers' daughters weaving mats for sale, and nuns calling on women at home. Families that could afford it kept wives and daughters at home, where spinning, weaving, and sewing took a great deal of time. In silk-producing families, women fed and warmed the silkworms through the growing season. This was economically important work, not leisure.

Learning: as the educated class grew, more women learned to read, and in scholar-official families many were literate enough to be their children's first teachers. Li Qingzhao (1084–ca. 1151), one of the finest Song poets, came from a scholar-official family; she wrote of evenings spent with her husband studying his newly bought paintings and bronzes.

How girls learned: girls could not attend schools or sit the exams, so learning happened at home, taught by a parent or a brother. Typical texts included Ban Zhao's Admonitions for Women, the Classic of Filial Piety for Women, poetry, and Buddhist sutras. Li Qingzhao is the outstanding example of how far that home education could go. Most girls outside the elite instead learned spinning, weaving, and how to manage a household.

Circumstances: a woman's options depended on her stage of life. Widows could stay with their husbands' families, return home, or remarry. One Song teacher, Cheng Yi, said a widow should rather starve than remarry, but in Song times widows commonly remarried; that strict ideal became influential later. Wives outranked concubines, though the children of both counted as family members.

What is ahead: families also sought health, protection, and good fortune through religion.

Women · rights and resources

Song Women Had Real Economic Agency

A woman's dowry stayed legally hers, and she could take it with her if widowed or divorced.
Unmarried daughters could inherit a share of family property, in some rulings half a son's share.
Wives could leave a marriage by mutual consent or if deserted, and divorced women and widows often remarried.
Scholars now see Song women as having real economic agency within a patriarchal system.
×

Dowry

The property, such as land, cash, cloth, and furnishings, that a bride brought into her marriage from her own family. In the Song it was often large, and it stayed identified as hers.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 141–143; Ebrey, The Inner Quarters (1993); Birge, Women, Property, and Confucian Reaction in Sung and Yüan China (2002).

Let's look at what Song women actually controlled.

Start with the dowry. A bride brought land, cash, cloth, and furnishings from her own family, and Song law treated that property as hers. If she was widowed or divorced, she could take it with her. Bettine Birge's research on women and property shows how seriously courts took this.

Daughters could inherit too. When a family divided its property, unmarried daughters could receive a share, and some court rulings set it at half a son's share. Patricia Ebrey's The Inner Quarters shows how families used dowries and inheritance in practice.

Marriage could also end. A couple could separate by mutual consent, called heli. A wife whose husband deserted her for three years could leave. And for serious offenses the state could force a separation, called yijue. Divorced women and widows often remarried.

So scholars have moved away from telling a story of steady decline. Song women had real economic agency, within a patriarchal system.

For the instructor: tighter limits, such as the widow-chastity ideal and widespread foot binding, hardened mostly in the Ming and Qing, and will be discussed in another lecture.

A source · Review Question 11

Hong Mai's Stories Reveal Everyday Religious Concerns

The source

The Record of the Listener

Compiled by Hong Mai (1123–1202) · twelfth century · more than two hundred chapters

Hong Mai compiled The Record of the Listener in the twelfth century.
His stories described gods, ghosts, ancestors, illness, and unexpected events.
Tales of retribution linked wrongdoing with supernatural punishment.
The collection reveals beliefs and concerns through an educated author's account.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 144–146.

How do historians know what ordinary Song people believed? Much of it comes from one remarkable collection.

The source: Hong Mai came from a prominent official family in Jiangxi. His huge book, The Record of the Listener, recorded events he learned about himself or from friends, relatives, and colleagues, and many deal with the spirit realm. Because printing helped more Song works survive, historians can see parts of life poorly documented in earlier periods.

Tales of retribution: the chapter reprints two of Hong Mai's stories in which people who wronged family members, including an unfilial son, are punished by ghosts or struck by lightning. They link religious imagination to family obligations.

Read the record critically. These stories are evidence of how events were told and interpreted. They do not prove that the supernatural events happened, and an author from an official family may not represent every commoner's view.

People made sense of the unseen world by comparing it to the world they knew.

Textbook anchor: "Gods were seen as parts of complex hierarchies, much like those in the human world." (p. 144)

Breaking it down: blessings and misfortunes could come from many gods and spirits: the national gods of Buddhism and Daoism, gods and demons of a particular locality, and ancestors, who might cause illness if dissatisfied. Like people, they had emotions. Demons might extort offerings like local bullies. Some gods were local kings and lords; others held posts in an otherworldly government and passed paperwork up the chain. Gods were not all-powerful: one might tell a petitioner he needed a higher god's approval.

How it connects: you already know a bureaucracy: offices with assigned duties and superiors above them. That is the model people used for the gods.

Religion in Song life was practical: people turned to it to solve problems.

Breaking it down: to learn whether a spirit was behind a problem, people used divination, or watched for dreams and visions. Once they knew the source, they could act themselves, through offerings and prayers. Often they turned to experts: ordained Buddhist and Daoist clergy, unordained practitioners, professional fortunetellers, and wardens of local temples who served as mediums or exorcists. An exorcism is a ritual meant to drive out or subdue a troubling spirit.

An example from the chapter: when medical doctors failed, people regularly called on religious experts. In one case a medium had a spirit descend into a boy, who then spoke in the voice of a maid who had died years earlier. The medium wrote out a dispatch to the City God, who had the maid's spirit sent to purgatory, much like a government official handling paperwork.

People did not always pick one religion and use only its specialists. This describes historical belief and practice, not advice.

This short case is evidence for Review Question 11.

How to read it: a man pestered by a ghost first hired a local exorcist. When that failed, he asked a visiting Daoist priest to perform an offering ceremony. Finally, Buddhist monks from the local monastery recited incantations and performed an exorcism, which, according to the account, worked.

What it shows: the man's search for help crossed three kinds of specialist and two religious traditions. When one source of help seemed to fail, he simply tried another. The story does not show that one religion was generally more effective, and it does not prove a ghost was present; it shows what people did and how the event was told.

The state and local gods

The Song State Recognized and Regulated Local Gods

Regulating

  • The state claimed authority to approve or disapprove local shrines.
  • Some shrines judged illicit or excessive were destroyed.

Recognizing

  • More often, the government awarded titles to local gods.
  • Local supporters petitioned for recognition of gods credited with miracles.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 146.

Government reached into religious life too.

Breaking it down: the Song state claimed the power to approve and disapprove local shrines. Occasionally the court ordered the destruction of illicit or excessive shrines, such as those whose gods made extortionate demands on people. Much more common was rewarding gods: local supporters regularly petitioned the government to grant their god the title of king, duke, or lord because of miracles it had performed.

How it connects: state involvement was not only suppression. Recognition placed a local god within an official hierarchy, while worshippers sought prestige for their god by petitioning. A skeptical official could pay a price in Hong Mai's stories: one who ignored a prominent shrine later begged the god's forgiveness after deaths in his family.

Pulling it together: officials, households, and religious life all involved claims about authority, obligation, and security.

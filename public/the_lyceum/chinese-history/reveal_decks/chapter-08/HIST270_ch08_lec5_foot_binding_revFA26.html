Foot Binding Linked Beauty, Family Status, and Bodily Constraint

How can we explain a valued practice that harmed and constrained the body?

Origins · Development · Reform · Cultural comparison

This lecture follows one practice across many centuries: foot binding. Its guiding question is a hard one: how can we explain a practice that people valued, recognize the harm it did, and compare it responsibly with body practices we know today?

You will move from origin stories, to evidence from the Song, to wider spread under the Ming and Qing, to reform, and finally to a careful comparison with modern practices.

Start here · a definition

What was foot binding?

Foot binding was the practice of tightly wrapping young girls’ feet to keep them small.
  • When: about 1,000 years, ending in the 1900s
  • How: toes folded under, feet wrapped tightly from about age 5
  • Goal: a three-inch “golden lotus” foot
  • Why: seen as beautiful, and it helped a daughter marry well

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella’s Sisters: A Revisionist History of Footbinding (2005); Patricia Buckley Ebrey, A History of China; McClung Museum, Bound to Be Beautiful.

If you have never heard of foot binding before, you are not alone. Start here.

Where it came from: the practice began among elite women around the 10th–12th centuries and later spread widely among Han Chinese families of many classes. It faded in the early-to-mid 1900s.

The basic idea: for about a thousand years, many Chinese families wrapped their daughters’ feet tightly to keep them small. A mother or another female relative usually did the binding when the girl was usually between about 5 and 8 years old. She folded the four smaller toes under the sole and wound long cloth bandages around the foot. Over the next several years the wrapping was tightened again and again. That pulled the toes toward the heel and bent the foot into a high arch, often breaking bones. The ideal was a foot about three inches long, called a “golden lotus.”

Two things to keep straight: binding was not a one-time event. After the first painful years of reshaping, women kept wrapping their feet for the rest of their lives. And the three-inch “golden lotus” was an extreme ideal from later centuries, not the actual size of every bound foot, and not a standard we can prove for Song women.

Why it matters: it hurt, and many women lived with pain and limited mobility. Yet families did it because they believed it made their daughters beautiful, respectable, and more likely to marry well. Keep both of those facts in mind for the whole lecture. Our job is to understand why people valued the practice and to recognize the harm it caused.

Where did foot binding come from? Nobody knows for certain, but people told stories about it. Here are the three you will hear most often.

The fox fairy. In one folk tale, Daji, the beautiful and cruel consort of the last king of the Shang dynasty (about 1046 BCE), was actually a fox spirit who had taken human form. Her transformation was not complete: her feet were still fox feet. To hide them, she wrapped them tightly in cloth. The king’s court admired her, and other women began binding their feet to look like her.

Yang Guifei. Yang Guifei was the beloved consort of the Tang emperor Xuanzong and the most famous beauty of the Tang dynasty. She died in 756 during the An Lushan Rebellion. Centuries later, writers imagined her with tiny feet, and stories spread about a small stocking or slipper she left behind. But Tang paintings and figurines show court women with natural, unbound feet.

Yao Niang. This is the most famous story. Li Yu, the last ruler of the Southern Tang kingdom (r. 961–975), was a poet who loved art and dance. He had a golden lotus built, six feet tall and decorated with jewels, pearls, and colored ribbons. His dancer Yao Niang wrapped her feet in white silk so they curved like a new moon, then danced on top of the lotus so lightly that she seemed to rise like a cloud. That is where the phrase “golden lotus” for a bound foot is said to come from. The oldest version we have was written down in the Song dynasty, more than a hundred years after Li Yu’s kingdom fell.

Read the stories critically. A legend tells us what later people thought the practice meant: beauty, dance, refinement, the glamour of a court. It does not prove who first did it or when. Real textual and material evidence exists for the later practice, as the next slides show.

The Song · what the evidence shows

Song Evidence Shows a Gradually Expanding Beauty Practice

Early associationsSong writers linked early binding with entertainers and with beauty.
Gradual spreadIt spread gradually and probably remained largely elite during Song.
EvidenceSong texts and elite burials supply evidence beyond the origin stories.
Later mass prevalence should not be projected backward.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144; bioarchaeological study, “Foot binding in a Ming dynasty cemetery near Xi'an, China.”

By the Song, foot binding clearly existed. But it was not yet the widespread practice of later centuries.

What historians know: foot binding began during the Song Dynasty, but Confucian teachers did not recommend it. It was associated with the pleasure quarters and with women's efforts to beautify themselves. It spread gradually during the Song but probably remained largely an elite practice (p. 143). Notice the word "probably": historians are careful here, and so should we be.

Pleasure quarters were entertainment districts that could include performers and sex workers. Where a practice started does not tell us who took it up later.

A common mistake: in later centuries foot binding became extremely common in north and central China and eventually spread to all classes. Do not read that later picture back into the Song. No source gives percentages for Song women, and proving that a practice existed is different from measuring how widespread it was.

Class and family · who bound, and how tightly

Class and Family Resources Shaped Bodily Constraints

Resources

  • Song evidence links binding especially with elite settings.
  • Servants and other resources could help households accommodate reduced mobility.
  • Especially tight binding was linked with the ability to afford servants.

Family

  • Mothers participated in transmitting the bodily standard.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144.

Who bound feet, and how tightly, depended on what a family could afford.

What historians know: women with bound feet were less mobile than women with natural feet, but only those who could afford servants bound their feet so tightly that walking was difficult (p. 144). A household with servants could absorb a woman's reduced mobility; a poorer household needed her to move and work.

Family: mothers did the binding, passing the standard from one generation to the next. That does not make it the child's choice. A girl of five to eight was not choosing as an adult would.

Two cautions. A link to wealth in the Song does not mean only affluent women bound their feet in every period. And reduced mobility is not the same as the most severe binding. Bound feet did not always mean an idle life; many rural women with bound feet did productive handwork at home.

Social meanings · beauty, eroticism, marriage

Erotic Meanings and Marriage Expectations Gave Bound Feet Social Value

Bound feet and their shoes could acquire erotic meanings.
Beauty, respectability, status, and marriage expectations could overlap.
Preferences were culturally shaped and varied over time.
Expected marriage advantages were not guaranteed outcomes.

Sources: instructor's existing lecture; Yuan-Ling Chao, “Poetry and Footbinding,” World History Connected (2009); Brown and Satterthwaite-Phillips, PLOS ONE (2018).

Why would families do this to their daughters? Several meanings overlapped.

Erotic meanings: bound feet and the decorated shoes that covered them could carry erotic meaning. A valued body part was hidden by shoes, yet socially displayed through their decoration. Historians stress that this erotic meaning grew stronger over time and that not all men or households shared the same preferences. The next slide shows how powerful it became.

Respectability and marriage: beauty, respectability, status, and marriage prospects could reinforce one another. A later saying, reported by Yuan-Ling Chao from Ida Pruitt's A Daughter of Han, puts it this way: "A plain face is given by heaven but poorly bound feet are a sign of laziness." That is a later reported judgment, not a Song proverb, and not every woman's view.

A caution: families may have expected binding to improve marriage chances, but later research challenges the idea that it reliably produced upward marriage. That later finding cannot tell us every Song family's motives either.

Social meanings · desire, secrecy, marriage

Hidden from View, the Bound Foot Became a National Fetish

Fetish: an object or body part that becomes necessary to desire, a fixation.
Concealment: bindings and slippers stayed on, even in bed. Baring the foot was as shocking as public nudity in the West.
The shoe: lotus shoes became love tokens in the Ming novel Jin Ping Mei and wine cups in drinking games.
Connoisseurs: a Qing writer ranked bound feet the way gardeners ranked blossoms. Meet Fang Xun
Why did it last a thousand years? Because it was tied to marriage and male desire. Men wanted small feet, so matchmakers demanded them, and a mother who didn't bind risked her daughter's whole future.
×

Fang Xun, Grading Fragrant Lotuses

In the Qing dynasty a writer called Fang Xun 方絢 (possibly a pen name) wrote Xianglian pinzao 香蓮品藻, a connoisseur's guide to bound feet, which he called “fragrant lotuses.”

He copied the form of a Song-dynasty guide to plum blossoms. He prized three qualities, plumpness, softness, and fineness, sorted feet into five shapes and eighteen types named after lotus varieties, and ranked them in nine grades, from “divine” down to “false.”

Howard Levy, The Lotus Lovers, discusses Fang Xun (as “Fang Hsun”) as a leading voice of the foot-binding admirers. Chinese text: Xianglian pinzao, Chinese Text Project.

Sources: instructor's existing lecture; Howard S. Levy, The Lotus Lovers (1966/1992); Wang Ping, Aching for Beauty (2000); Fang Xun, Xianglian pinzao.

On the last slide you saw that bound feet carried several meanings. Here's the one that kept it going: desire.

What a fetish is: a fetish is an object or body part that becomes necessary to someone's desire, a fixation. For many Han men, the tiny bound foot became that.

Why hiding mattered: the bound foot was almost never shown unwrapped, even to a husband. Women wore their bindings and slippers to bed. Because men rarely saw the real foot, they could imagine it as perfect. Showing it bare would have felt as shocking as public nudity in the West.

The shoe becomes the object: since the foot stayed hidden, the embroidered shoe took its place. In the Ming novel Jin Ping Mei, shoes act as love tokens and props in flirtation. Howard Levy describes drinking games, from the Yuan through the Qing, in which a wine cup was set inside a courtesan's shoe. Some admirers also believed binding changed women's bodies in sexual ways; Levy shows these were false beliefs men held.

The link to marriage: men wanted small feet, so families seeking good matches demanded them, and mothers kept binding daughters.

The big answer: this slide answers the lecture's hardest question. How could mothers keep doing this to their daughters for a thousand years? Because bound feet were tied to marriage, and marriage was tied to what men desired. A girl's marriage decided her livelihood and status, so a mother who refused to bind was gambling with her daughter's future. Keep this idea in mind, because it also explains why reformers and the Republic found binding so hard to stop.

Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1911) · wider reach

Ming and Qing Diffusion Changed the Practice's Social Reach

Binding spread beyond the largely elite settings of the Song.
During Ming and Qing it appeared across wider regions and social groups.
Shoes could express handwork, taste, and claims to refinement.
Expansion did not make prevalence or severity uniform.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144; Chao, “Poetry and Footbinding”.

After the Song, foot binding spread far beyond its elite beginnings.

The big picture: in later centuries foot binding became extremely common in north and central China, eventually spreading to all classes (p. 144). The Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1911) are the broad periods of that spread.

Look at the shoe. Surviving lotus shoes like these mostly date from the last centuries of the practice, long after the Song. Notice how the styles differ: shapes, heels, and decoration varied by region and fashion. Shoes like this could show off a woman's needlework, her taste, and claims to refinement. Treat it as later evidence, not a Song object.

A caution: wider spread did not mean the same practice everywhere. How common binding was, and how severe, still varied by region, family, and period.

Variation · Han, Hakka, and Manchu

Han and Manchu Differences Show That Foot Binding Was Not Universal

Binding became associated with many Han Chinese communities.
Manchu women generally maintained unbound feet and distinctive footwear.
Hakka communities illustrate important variation within Han society.
Ethnic and regional labels alone do not explain every case.

Sources: Hsieh, “Origin and Migrations of the Hakkas” (1929), ed. David K. Jordan; John R. Shepherd (2016); The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 45.108.3.

Foot binding was never universal in China.

Breaking it down: binding became associated with many Han Chinese communities. Manchu women, from the group that ruled the Qing, generally kept their feet unbound and wore distinctive shoes. The Hakka are a Han Chinese population, not a non-Han minority, and their traditions of not binding show variation even within Han society.

Look at the shoe. This Manchu woman's shoe stands on a tall central pedestal, very different from the small pointed shoe on the previous slide. You may read that Manchu women designed these shoes to imitate the gait of women with bound feet. That claim is contested, so do not treat it as settled.

Why this matters: labels like "Han" or "Manchu" help, but they do not explain every family's choice.

The Qing · two ethnic standards side by side

Under Qing Rule, Feet Marked the Line Between Han and Manchu Women

Qing rulers forbade Manchu women to bind, while Han women kept binding.

Sources: John R. Shepherd, “The Qing, the Manchus, and Footbinding,” Frontiers of History in China 11, no. 2 (2016); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); photograph, Leiden University Libraries.

Look at this group of Peking women. Under the Qing dynasty, a woman's feet told you which group she belonged to.

The Han women around the edges have bound feet, and they let them show. For many Han families a small, well-bound foot was a point of pride: proof of discipline, good upbringing, and a respectable household.

The Manchu lady in the center does not bind. The Manchus who ruled China after 1644 repeatedly told their own women not to bind their feet, to keep them distinct from the Han majority. Her long robe falls to the ground, so her feet stay out of sight. Manchu women often wore tall platform shoes under robes like this, which gave some of the swaying walk that admirers linked with bound feet, without the binding.

A scholarly correction: you may hear that the Qing banned foot binding for everyone and the Han resisted. The historian John Shepherd argues there was never an enforced conquest-era ban on Han women; a 1664 proposal was withdrawn. The firm rules applied to Manchu and banner women. So binding became one of the clearest everyday markers separating Han from Manchu.

Late Qing reform · 1870s–1890s

Chinese Reformers and Foreign Activists Challenged Foot Binding

Late-Qing reformers connected the practice with national and social reform.
Chinese activists, Christian missionaries, and secular foreign women organized campaigns.
Anti-foot-binding societies: Amoy (Xiamen), 1874–75, missionary John MacGowan's Heavenly Foot Society · Nanhai, near Canton, 1883, Kang Youwei's Unbound Feet Society · Shanghai, 1895, Alicia Little's Natural Feet Society · Shanghai, 1897, Unbound Feet Society founded by Liang Qichao, Tan Sitong, and others

Sources: Yen-Wei Miao, “The Social Participation of the Memsahibs in Late-Qing China: The Shanghai Natural Feet Society (1895–1906)” (2011); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella’s Sisters (2005); Chao, “Poetry and Footbinding.”

In the late nineteenth century, foot binding became a public cause.

Breaking it down: late-Qing Chinese reformers linked ending foot binding to strengthening the nation and reforming society. They were participants in their own right, not just followers of foreign missionaries. Christian missionaries and secular foreign women also organized campaigns.

The societies: the missionary John MacGowan started a Heavenly Foot Society among Christian families in Amoy (Xiamen) in the mid-1870s. In 1883 the young Kang Youwei started an Unbound Feet Society in his home county of Nanhai, near Canton. In 1895 Alicia Little and other foreign women in Shanghai founded the Natural Feet Society; the historian Yen-Wei Miao shows how its members both cooperated and competed with Chinese reform networks. In 1897 Liang Qichao, Tan Sitong, and other reformers launched the Unbound Feet Society in Shanghai and publicized it in their newspaper.

Different goals: these groups overlapped but did not want exactly the same things. Some stressed national strength, some China's image abroad, some Christian faith, and some women's health.

Late Qing reform · the program

What the Reformers Actually Called For

The pledge

  • Members would not bind their daughters.
  • Their sons would not marry women with bound feet.
  • Together, families made unbound daughters marriageable.

  • Shame before foreigners

  • 1902: a Qing edict under Cixi urged families to stop.

Shanghai Unbound Feet Society, 1897

From its “Trial Rules” (Shiban buchanzu hui jianming zhangcheng), printed in the reform newspaper Shiwu bao:

“Daughters born to members may not have their feet bound.”

“Sons born to members may not marry women with bound feet.”

Our translation of the Chinese text (凡入會人所生女子，不得纏足；凡入會人所生男子，不得娶纏足之女). Drafted by Wu Qiao and Liang Qichao.

Sources: Liang Qichao and Wu Qiao, “Shiban buchanzu hui jianming zhangcheng,” Shiwu bao (1897); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella’s Sisters (2005); Kang Youwei, memorial requesting a ban on foot binding (1898); Qing edict of 1902.

What did the anti-foot-binding societies actually ask people to do?

The pledge: members promised not to bind their daughters, and not to let their sons marry women with bound feet. Why the second promise? Parents bound daughters partly so they could marry well. One family alone that stopped binding risked leaving a daughter unmarriageable. If many families promised together, unbound girls had husbands waiting. That is collective action: a risk shared becomes a risk you can take.

The arguments: reformers said weak, housebound mothers raised weak children, so binding weakened the nation. They said it made China look backward to foreigners. They also pointed to pain and poor health. Their slogan was tianzu, “heavenly” or natural feet.

The state: in 1898 Kang Youwei sent a memorial to the Guangxu Emperor asking him to ban binding. The reform failed that year. In 1902 a Qing edict issued under Empress Dowager Cixi urged families to give up the practice, but it encouraged rather than punished.

The Republic · the law meets the family

The Republic Banned Foot Binding, but the Ban Was Nearly Impossible to Enforce

1912 · The ban: Sun Yat-sen's new Republican government ordered an end to foot binding.
1910s–1930s · Enforcement: Provinces and later the Nationalist government sent inspectors and threatened fines, with uneven results.
A law could not easily reach inside the home. As long as small feet meant beauty, desirability, and a good marriage, many mothers kept binding.

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); Melissa J. Brown and Damian Satterthwaite-Phillips, “Economic correlates of footbinding,” PLOS ONE 13, no. 9 (2018).

After the Qing fell, the new Republic of China tried to end foot binding by law.

The ban: in 1912 Sun Yat-sen's provisional government ordered that foot binding stop. Later, provincial leaders and the Nationalist government in the late 1920s renewed the ban, sent inspectors into villages, and threatened families with fines.

Why it was so hard to enforce: binding happened at home, done by mothers to young daughters, often out of sight. Officials could not watch every household. And the reasons families bound had not gone away. Small feet still stood for beauty, sexual appeal, and respectability, and many families believed an unbound daughter would be hard to marry. A mother who unbound her daughter took a real risk with that girl's future. Some women unbound when inspectors came and rebound when they left.

Look at the photo. It was taken in Beiping (Beijing) in 1934, more than twenty years after the first ban, and it shows young girls whose feet had been bound. The lower photo, from a Japanese photo series published in the 1920s and 1930s, shows an adult woman rewrapping her feet with a long cotton cloth, a daily routine for women who had been bound as girls.

The takeaway: the same force that kept foot binding alive for a thousand years, its link to marriage and male desire, is what made it so hard to end. A law can change what is allowed, but it cannot by itself change what people find beautiful or what makes a good marriage. Binding faded as those ideas changed, generation by generation.

Cultural comparison · a framework

Cultural Comparison Helps Us Examine How Bodies Acquire Social Value

Practices to compare

  • Corsets, cosmetic surgery, and high heels.
  • The wider history also includes tattoos, piercings, cranial shaping, and lip plates.

How to compare

  • Compare the age of participants, pressures, intended meanings, and consequences.
Understanding a practice's context does not require approving it.

Sources: instructor's existing lecture; instructor's analytical framework.

Every society shapes bodies to fit its ideas of beauty and status. Comparison can help us see that, if we do it carefully.

Cultural relativism as a method: here it means understanding a practice's meaning in its own context. That is different from the ethical claim that anything customary must be acceptable. You can explain why families bound their daughters' feet and still judge the practice harmful.

The examples: corsets, cosmetic surgery, high heels, tattoos, piercings, cranial shaping, and lip plates are prompts for comparison. They do not all have the same effects, and each needs its own evidence and context. The same is true of practices such as circumcision and female genital cutting, which differ in procedure, age, setting, and harm and should not be equated with footwear.

Four questions: how old were the people involved? What pressures acted on them? What did the practice mean? What were the consequences?

Think about it: what can comparison reveal about beauty, status, and social pressure? What must be established before we claim two practices are equivalent?

×

Bunion (hallux valgus)

A deformity at the joint of the big toe, in which the toe angles toward the other toes. It is more than a simple bony growth.

Source: Maxwell S. Barnish and Jean Barnish, “High-heeled shoes and musculoskeletal injuries: a narrative systematic review,” BMJ Open 6, no. 1 (2016).

High heels are a useful comparison because they share something with foot binding, and because they differ in important ways.

What the research says: a 2016 systematic review by Maxwell and Jean Barnish found that high-heel use is associated with bunions (hallux valgus), musculoskeletal pain, and injury to the wearer. It did not find an association with osteoarthritis. "Associated with" does not mean heels inevitably cause each condition.

Read the table row by row. Both practices connect appearance, social expectations, and movement. But foot binding began in childhood, was decided by adults, and reshaped a growing foot permanently. High heels are usually worn by older people and can be taken off, though earlier injury may remain. Removable is not the same as free of pressure: jobs and social expectations can shape adult shoe choices too.

A caution: do not say the two are "only different by degree." Some differences, such as age, permanence, and who decides, may change the nature of the comparison.

High heelsMain worked comparison, with verified health associations and clear differences in age, reversibility, and social context.
CorsetsA short example of historically valued bodily presentation; do not assert identical mechanisms or consequences.
Cosmetic breast surgeryA comparison prompt about beauty, risk, access, and social pressure, not a medical advice section.
Tattoos and piercingsUseful for asking how meaning and choice vary; specific practices require their own context.
Lip plates and cranial shapingRetain in the wider comparison list, without an anonymous exoticizing image montage or one universal explanation.
Circumcision and female genital cuttingMay remain in the instructor's broader comparison bank. Distinguish procedures, ages, contexts, and harms; do not equate them automatically with footwear or reproduce the unsourced old prevalence statistic.
Cultural relativismExplain cultural understanding as a method and distinguish it from automatic ethical approval.
"Only different by degree"Replace with a question about which differences are matters of degree and which change the nature of the comparison.

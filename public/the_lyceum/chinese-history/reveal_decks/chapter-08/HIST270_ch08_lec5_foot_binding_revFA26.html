Foot Binding Linked Beauty, Family Status, and Bodily Constraint

This lecture follows one practice across many centuries: foot binding. Its guiding question is a hard one: how can we explain a practice that people valued, recognize the harm it did, and compare it responsibly with body practices we know today?

You will move from origin stories, to evidence from the Song, to wider spread under the Ming and Qing, to reform, and finally to a careful comparison with modern practices.

Start here · a definition

What was foot binding?

Foot binding was the practice of tightly wrapping young girls’ feet to keep them small.
  • When: about 1,000 years, ending in the 1900s
  • How: toes folded under, feet wrapped tightly from about age 5
  • Goal: a three-inch “golden lotus” foot
  • Why: seen as beautiful, and it helped a daughter marry well

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella’s Sisters: A Revisionist History of Footbinding (2005); Patricia Buckley Ebrey, A History of China; McClung Museum, Bound to Be Beautiful.

If you have never heard of foot binding before, you are not alone. Start here.

Where it came from: the practice began among elite women around the 10th–12th centuries and later spread widely among Han Chinese families of many classes. It faded in the early-to-mid 1900s.

The basic idea: for about a thousand years, many Chinese families wrapped their daughters’ feet tightly to keep them small. A mother or another female relative usually did the binding when the girl was usually between about 5 and 8 years old. She folded the four smaller toes under the sole and wound long cloth bandages around the foot. Over the next several years the wrapping was tightened again and again. That pulled the toes toward the heel and bent the foot into a high arch, often breaking bones. The ideal was a foot about three inches long, called a “golden lotus.”

Two things to keep straight: binding was not a one-time event. After the first painful years of reshaping, women kept wrapping their feet for the rest of their lives. And the three-inch “golden lotus” was an extreme ideal from later centuries, not the actual size of every bound foot, and not a standard we can prove for Song women.

Why it matters: it hurt, and many women lived with pain and limited mobility. Yet families did it because they believed it made their daughters beautiful, respectable, and more likely to marry well. Keep both of those facts in mind for the whole lecture. Our job is to understand why people valued the practice and to recognize the harm it caused.

Where did foot binding come from? Nobody knows for certain, but people told stories about it. Here are the three you will hear most often.

The fox fairy. In one folk tale, Daji, the beautiful and cruel consort of the last king of the Shang dynasty (about 1046 BCE), was actually a fox spirit who had taken human form. Her transformation was not complete: her feet were still fox feet. To hide them, she wrapped them tightly in cloth. The king’s court admired her, and other women began binding their feet to look like her.

Yang Guifei. Yang Guifei was the beloved consort of the Tang emperor Xuanzong and the most famous beauty of the Tang dynasty. She died in 756 during the An Lushan Rebellion. Centuries later, writers imagined her with tiny feet, and stories spread about a small stocking or slipper she left behind. But Tang paintings and figurines show court women with natural, unbound feet.

Yao Niang. This is the most famous story. Li Yu, the last ruler of the Southern Tang kingdom (r. 961–975), was a poet who loved art and dance. He had a golden lotus built, six feet tall and decorated with jewels, pearls, and colored ribbons. His dancer Yao Niang wrapped her feet in white silk so they curved like a new moon, then danced on top of the lotus so lightly that she seemed to rise like a cloud. That is where the phrase “golden lotus” for a bound foot is said to come from. The oldest version we have was written down in the Song dynasty, more than a hundred years after Li Yu’s kingdom fell.

Read the stories critically. A legend tells us what later people thought the practice meant: beauty, dance, refinement, the glamour of a court. It does not prove who first did it or when. Real textual and material evidence exists for the later practice, as the next slides show.

The Song · what the evidence shows

Song Evidence Shows a Gradually Expanding Beauty Practice

Early associationsSong writers linked early binding with entertainers and with beauty.
Gradual spreadIt spread gradually and probably remained largely elite during Song.
EvidenceSong texts and elite burials supply evidence beyond the origin stories.
Later mass prevalence should not be projected backward.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144; bioarchaeological study, “Foot binding in a Ming dynasty cemetery near Xi'an, China.”

By the Song, foot binding clearly existed. But it was not yet the widespread practice of later centuries.

What historians know: foot binding began during the Song Dynasty, but Confucian teachers did not recommend it. It was associated with the pleasure quarters and with women's efforts to beautify themselves. It spread gradually during the Song but probably remained largely an elite practice (p. 143). Notice the word "probably": historians are careful here, and so should we be.

Pleasure quarters were entertainment districts that could include performers and sex workers. Where a practice started does not tell us who took it up later.

A common mistake: in later centuries foot binding became extremely common in north and central China and eventually spread to all classes. Do not read that later picture back into the Song. No source gives percentages for Song women, and proving that a practice existed is different from measuring how widespread it was.

Class and family · who bound, and how tightly

Class and Family Resources Shaped Bodily Constraints

Resources

  • Song evidence links binding especially with elite settings.
  • Servants and other resources could help households accommodate reduced mobility.
  • Especially tight binding was linked with the ability to afford servants.

Family

  • Mothers participated in transmitting the bodily standard.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144.

Who bound feet, and how tightly, depended on what a family could afford.

What historians know: women with bound feet were less mobile than women with natural feet, but only those who could afford servants bound their feet so tightly that walking was difficult (p. 144). A household with servants could absorb a woman's reduced mobility; a poorer household needed her to move and work.

Family: mothers did the binding, passing the standard from one generation to the next. That does not make it the child's choice. A girl of five to eight was not choosing as an adult would.

Two cautions. A link to wealth in the Song does not mean only affluent women bound their feet in every period. And reduced mobility is not the same as the most severe binding. Bound feet did not always mean an idle life; many rural women with bound feet did productive handwork at home.

Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1911) · wider reach

Ming and Qing Diffusion Changed the Practice's Social Reach

Binding spread beyond the largely elite settings of the Song.
During Ming and Qing it appeared across wider regions and social groups.
Shoes could express handwork, taste, and claims to refinement.
Expansion did not make prevalence or severity uniform.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, pp. 143–144; Chao, “Poetry and Footbinding”.

After the Song, foot binding spread far beyond its elite beginnings.

The big picture: in later centuries foot binding became extremely common in north and central China, eventually spreading to all classes (p. 144). The Ming (1368–1644) and Qing (1644–1911) are the broad periods of that spread.

Look at the shoe. Surviving lotus shoes like these mostly date from the last centuries of the practice, long after the Song. Notice how the styles differ: shapes, heels, and decoration varied by region and fashion. Shoes like this could show off a woman's needlework, her taste, and claims to refinement. Treat it as later evidence, not a Song object.

A caution: wider spread did not mean the same practice everywhere. How common binding was, and how severe, still varied by region, family, and period.

Variation · Han, Hakka, and Manchu

Han and Manchu Differences Show That Foot Binding Was Not Universal

Binding became associated with many Han Chinese communities.
Manchu women generally maintained unbound feet and distinctive footwear.
Hakka communities illustrate important variation within Han society.
Ethnic and regional labels alone do not explain every case.

Sources: Hsieh, “Origin and Migrations of the Hakkas” (1929), ed. David K. Jordan; John R. Shepherd (2016); The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 45.108.3.

Foot binding was never universal in China.

Breaking it down: binding became associated with many Han Chinese communities. Manchu women, from the group that ruled the Qing, generally kept their feet unbound and wore distinctive shoes. The Hakka are a Han Chinese population, not a non-Han minority, and their traditions of not binding show variation even within Han society.

Look at the shoe. This Manchu woman's shoe stands on a tall central pedestal, very different from the small pointed shoe on the previous slide. You may read that Manchu women designed these shoes to imitate the gait of women with bound feet. That claim is contested, so do not treat it as settled.

Why this matters: labels like "Han" or "Manchu" help, but they do not explain every family's choice.

The Qing · two ethnic standards side by side

Under Qing Rule, Feet Marked the Line Between Han and Manchu Women

Qing rulers forbade Manchu women to bind, while Han women kept binding.

Sources: John R. Shepherd, “The Qing, the Manchus, and Footbinding,” Frontiers of History in China 11, no. 2 (2016); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); photograph, Leiden University Libraries.

Look at this group of Peking women. Under the Qing dynasty, a woman's feet told you which group she belonged to.

The Han women around the edges have bound feet, and they let them show. For many Han families a small, well-bound foot was a point of pride: proof of discipline, good upbringing, and a respectable household.

The Manchu lady in the center does not bind. The Manchus who ruled China after 1644 repeatedly told their own women not to bind their feet, to keep them distinct from the Han majority. Her long robe falls to the ground, so her feet stay out of sight. Manchu women often wore tall platform shoes under robes like this, which gave some of the swaying walk that admirers linked with bound feet, without the binding.

A scholarly correction: you may hear that the Qing banned foot binding for everyone and the Han resisted. The historian John Shepherd argues there was never an enforced conquest-era ban on Han women; a 1664 proposal was withdrawn. The firm rules applied to Manchu and banner women. So binding became one of the clearest everyday markers separating Han from Manchu.

Late Qing reform · 1870s–1890s

Chinese Reformers and Foreign Activists Challenged Foot Binding

Late-Qing reformers connected the practice with national and social reform.
Chinese activists, Christian missionaries, and secular foreign women organized campaigns.
Anti-foot-binding societies: Amoy (Xiamen), 1874–75, missionary John MacGowan's Heavenly Foot Society · Nanhai, near Canton, 1883, Kang Youwei's Unbound Feet Society · Shanghai, 1895, Alicia Little's Natural Feet Society · Shanghai, 1897, Unbound Feet Society founded by Liang Qichao, Tan Sitong, and others

Sources: Yen-Wei Miao, “The Social Participation of the Memsahibs in Late-Qing China: The Shanghai Natural Feet Society (1895–1906)” (2011); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella’s Sisters (2005); Chao, “Poetry and Footbinding.”

In the late nineteenth century, foot binding became a public cause.

Breaking it down: late-Qing Chinese reformers linked ending foot binding to strengthening the nation and reforming society. They were participants in their own right, not just followers of foreign missionaries. Christian missionaries and secular foreign women also organized campaigns.

The societies: the missionary John MacGowan started a Heavenly Foot Society among Christian families in Amoy (Xiamen) in the mid-1870s. In 1883 the young Kang Youwei started an Unbound Feet Society in his home county of Nanhai, near Canton. In 1895 Alicia Little and other foreign women in Shanghai founded the Natural Feet Society; the historian Yen-Wei Miao shows how its members both cooperated and competed with Chinese reform networks. In 1897 Liang Qichao, Tan Sitong, and other reformers launched the Unbound Feet Society in Shanghai and publicized it in their newspaper.

Different goals: these groups overlapped but did not want exactly the same things. Some stressed national strength, some China's image abroad, some Christian faith, and some women's health.

What did the anti-foot-binding societies actually ask people to do?

The pledge: members promised not to bind their daughters, and not to let their sons marry women with bound feet. Why the second promise? Parents bound daughters partly so they could marry well. One family alone that stopped binding risked leaving a daughter unmarriageable. If many families promised together, unbound girls had husbands waiting. That is collective action: a risk shared becomes a risk you can take.

The arguments: reformers said weak, housebound mothers raised weak children, so binding weakened the nation. They said it made China look backward to foreigners. They also pointed to pain and poor health. Their slogan was tianzu, “heavenly” or natural feet.

The state: in 1898 Kang Youwei sent a memorial to the Guangxu Emperor asking him to ban binding. The reform failed that year. In 1902 a Qing edict issued under Empress Dowager Cixi urged families to give up the practice, but it encouraged rather than punished.

The Republic · the law meets the family

The Republic Banned Foot Binding, but the Ban Was Nearly Impossible to Enforce

1912 · The ban: Sun Yat-sen's new Republican government ordered an end to foot binding.
1910s–1930s · Enforcement: Provinces and later the Nationalist government sent inspectors and threatened fines, with uneven results.
A law could not easily reach inside the home. As long as small feet meant beauty, desirability, and a good marriage, many mothers kept binding.

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); Melissa J. Brown and Damian Satterthwaite-Phillips, “Economic correlates of footbinding,” PLOS ONE 13, no. 9 (2018).

After the Qing fell, the new Republic of China tried to end foot binding by law.

The ban: in 1912 Sun Yat-sen's provisional government ordered that foot binding stop. Later, provincial leaders and the Nationalist government in the late 1920s renewed the ban, sent inspectors into villages, and threatened families with fines.

Why it was so hard to enforce: binding happened at home, done by mothers to young daughters, often out of sight. Officials could not watch every household. And the reasons families bound had not gone away. Small feet still stood for beauty, sexual appeal, and respectability, and many families believed an unbound daughter would be hard to marry. A mother who unbound her daughter took a real risk with that girl's future. Some women unbound when inspectors came and rebound when they left.

Look at the photo. It was taken in Beiping (Beijing) in 1934, more than twenty years after the first ban, and it shows young girls whose feet had been bound. The lower photo, from a Japanese photo series published in the 1920s and 1930s, shows an adult woman rewrapping her feet with a long cotton cloth, a daily routine for women who had been bound as girls.

The takeaway: binding was tied to marriage and desire, and that is what made it so hard to end. You will look at that link more closely in the next section. A law can change what is allowed, but it cannot by itself change what people find beautiful or what makes a good marriage. Binding faded as those ideas changed, generation by generation.

Explaining the practice

How Could They?

Why did this practice last for a thousand years?

You now know the story: where foot binding came from, how it spread, how reformers attacked it, and why the Republic's ban was so hard to enforce. This section steps back and asks the hardest question of all. How could mothers, for a thousand years, do something so painful to daughters they loved?

The answer is not that they were cruel. Beauty and Desire: Small feet became associated with feminine attractiveness and erotic appeal.
Marriage Expectations: Families believed bound feet could improve a daughter's marriage prospects.
Cultural Knowledge: Mothers acted on the customs and expectations of their communities.
Social Pressure: Fear of disadvantaging a daughter helped perpetuate the practice.
The belief that bound feet improved marriage prospects mattered, whether or not that advantage could be demonstrated statistically.

What Is Sexual Capital?

Sexual capital means the social advantages a person can gain from being seen as attractive or sexually desirable. Like money, it can be "spent": on a better marriage, more status, or more security.

The idea grows out of sociologist Pierre Bourdieu's point that people hold several kinds of capital, not just money: economic (wealth), social (connections), and cultural (education, taste, manners). Later sociologists, such as John Levi Martin and Matt George (2006) and Adam Isaiah Green (2008), added desirability to the list. Catherine Hakim's similar term is "erotic capital."

The key insight is that a community decides what counts as desirable. A trait has value only because people agree it does. In late imperial China, many people agreed that small, bound feet were beautiful, so bound feet became a kind of capital a family could invest in for a daughter.

The term is a modern tool for analysis. Qing families did not use it, but it helps us see why they acted as they did.

Sources: Patricia Buckley Ebrey, A History of China; Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); Brown et al., Journal of Asian Studies (2012); Brown and Satterthwaite-Phillips, PLOS ONE (2018).

A useful term, sexual capital: sociologists use this term for the social advantages a person can gain from being seen as physically attractive or sexually desirable. Think of it like money in the bank: it can be "spent" to open doors. In imperial China, bound feet took on this kind of cultural value.

See it through a mother's eyes: imagine a mother in Qing China who wants her daughter to marry well. She knows small feet are admired, and that a future husband and his family may value them. She may believe that leaving her daughter unbound would put the girl at a disadvantage for life.

She has no statistics: this mother cannot look up modern research. She decides based on the customs, advice, and expectations of the people around her.

A loop that feeds itself: mothers bind partly because they believe the marriage market demands it. Each bound daughter then strengthens the expectation that a respectable woman should have bound feet.

Belief versus outcome: modern historians have questioned whether binding actually helped women marry into wealthier families. But whether it worked is a different question from whether families believed it would. The belief alone could drive what people did.

Keep it nuanced: not every family believed the same things, not all men preferred bound feet, and male desire was not the only motive. Beauty, respectability, family status, work, and marriage hopes all worked together.

As historians, we do not have to approve of foot binding to understand why families practiced it. Nor must we prove that it produced better marriages to recognize that the expectation of a better marriage could be a powerful motive.

Social meanings · desire, secrecy, marriage

Hidden from View, the Bound Foot Became a National Fetish

Fetish: an object or body part that becomes necessary to desire, a fixation.
Concealment: bindings and slippers stayed on, even in bed. Baring the foot was as shocking as public nudity in the West.
The shoe: lotus shoes became love tokens in the Ming novel Jin Ping Mei and wine cups in drinking games.
Connoisseurs: a Qing writer ranked bound feet the way gardeners ranked blossoms. Meet Fang Xun
Why did it last a thousand years? Because it was tied to marriage and male desire. Men wanted small feet, so matchmakers demanded them, and a mother who didn't bind risked her daughter's whole future.

Fang Xun, Grading Fragrant Lotuses

In the Qing dynasty a writer called Fang Xun 方絢 (possibly a pen name) wrote Xianglian pinzao 香蓮品藻, a connoisseur's guide to bound feet, which he called “fragrant lotuses.”

He copied the form of a Song-dynasty guide to plum blossoms. He prized three qualities, plumpness, softness, and fineness, sorted feet into five shapes and eighteen types named after lotus varieties, and ranked them in nine grades, from “divine” down to “false.”

Howard Levy, The Lotus Lovers, discusses Fang Xun (as “Fang Hsun”) as a leading voice of the foot-binding admirers. Chinese text: Xianglian pinzao, Chinese Text Project.

Sources: instructor's existing lecture; Howard S. Levy, The Lotus Lovers (1966/1992); Wang Ping, Aching for Beauty (2000); Fang Xun, Xianglian pinzao.

On the last slide you saw that bound feet carried several meanings. Here's the one that kept it going: desire.

What a fetish is: a fetish is an object or body part that becomes necessary to someone's desire, a fixation. For many Han men, the tiny bound foot became that.

Why hiding mattered: the bound foot was almost never shown unwrapped, even to a husband. Women wore their bindings and slippers to bed. Because men rarely saw the real foot, they could imagine it as perfect. Showing it bare would have felt as shocking as public nudity in the West.

The shoe becomes the object: since the foot stayed hidden, the embroidered shoe took its place. In the Ming novel Jin Ping Mei, shoes act as love tokens and props in flirtation. Howard Levy describes drinking games, from the Yuan through the Qing, in which a wine cup was set inside a courtesan's shoe. Some admirers also believed binding changed women's bodies in sexual ways; Levy shows these were false beliefs men held.

The link to marriage: men wanted small feet, so families seeking good matches demanded them, and mothers kept binding daughters.

The big answer: this slide answers the lecture's hardest question. How could mothers keep doing this to their daughters for a thousand years? Because bound feet were tied to marriage, and marriage was tied to what men desired. A girl's marriage decided her livelihood and status, so a mother who refused to bind was gambling with her daughter's future. Think back to the reformers and the Republic: this same link is why their pledges and bans had such a hard time.

Body modification · beyond foot binding

Body Modification Reflects Cultural Ideals of Beauty, Identity, and Status

Body modification: The deliberate alteration of the human body for aesthetic, cultural, religious, or social purposes.
  • Practiced across cultures and throughout history.
  • Expresses beauty, identity, belonging, or social status.
  • Varies in permanence, physical consequences, and consent.
  • Reflects what societies value about the human body.

Image credits appear on the five example slides that follow.

Foot binding was not the only way people have reshaped the human body to match what their community valued. Across the world and throughout history, people have changed their bodies on purpose: by marking the skin, by enlarging pierced ears, by shaping the waist, or by wearing objects that change how the body looks and moves. This short mini-lecture steps back from China to look at that wider human practice, and then returns to foot binding with sharper questions.

The term body modification is used in more than one way. Some writers reserve it for physical alterations of the body itself, such as tattooing or ear stretching, which change the skin or other tissue. Others also include practices that mainly change appearance while something is being worn, such as corseting or high heels. Here the term covers both, and it covers permanent as well as temporary changes. Keep in mind that definitions vary, and that the line between modifying the body and simply decorating it is not always sharp.

People modify their bodies for many reasons: beauty, sexual attractiveness, membership in a group, religious meaning, personal identity, and social status. These motives usually overlap. A single tattoo can be beautiful, sacred, and a sign of belonging all at once, and a fashionable waist can signal both attractiveness and respectability.

One caution matters for everything that follows. Practices that look similar from the outside do not necessarily share the same meaning, the same pain, the same permanence, or the same degree of choice. A comparison is useful only if we ask, for each practice, who does it, at what age, with what physical consequences, and what it means to the people involved.

Victorian Corsets Shaped the Body to Meet Fashionable Ideals

Europe and North America | Nineteenth century

Corsets shaped the waist and torso to create fashionable silhouettes associated with beauty, refinement, and respectability.

Corset, about 1885, Worcester Corset Company (American); cotton, metal, bone. The Metropolitan Museum of Art, Brooklyn Museum Costume Collection, 2009.300.3112a-d. Public domain (Met Open Access).

This corset was made in the United States around 1885. Like most corsets of its time, it is built from layers of cotton stiffened with many narrow strips of bone or metal, called boning, and it laces closed. Pulled snug, it shaped the torso into the smooth, curved line that fashionable dresses were cut to fit, and it also helped support the weight of the skirts and bodice worn over it. The ideal silhouette was not fixed. Waistlines rose and fell and the emphasis moved between bust, waist, and hips across the nineteenth century, so corsets changed shape along with fashion.

A well-shaped figure carried social meaning. A neatly corseted body suggested refinement, femininity, self-control, and respectability, while a loose, uncorseted figure could look careless. Dressing well also took money and knowledge: a good corset, the right dress, and an understanding of current style were markers of class as much as of attractiveness.

Corsets could restrict movement and breathing during exertion, and they could cause discomfort, especially when laced very tightly. Tight lacing drew sharp criticism from doctors and dress reformers at the time. Historians such as Valerie Steele, however, caution against treating extreme tight lacing as the ordinary experience. Most women wore corsets more moderately, many wore them while working, and corsets also gave support, somewhat like a modern bra.

The connection to our larger theme is this: corsets shaped the body to meet a valued standard, but not every woman experienced that shaping in the same way. Wealth, age, work, and personal choice all made a difference.

Ear Stretching Expressed Beauty and Cultural Identity

Maasai communities | East Africa

Gradually enlarged earlobes and decorative ornaments could communicate beauty, identity, and cultural belonging.

Maasai woman, Serengeti National Park, Tanzania, 2008. Photograph by William Warby, via Wikimedia Commons. CC BY 2.0.

The Maasai are pastoral peoples of southern Kenya and northern Tanzania. In many Maasai communities, the earlobe is pierced and then enlarged gradually over time by inserting larger and larger plugs or ornaments. Once stretched, the lobe can hold beadwork, metal ornaments, and other decorations, like those worn by the woman in this photograph. The National Museums of Kenya note that ear piercing and stretching were once common among many Kenyan peoples, were often tied to rites of passage from childhood to adulthood, and that the process could be painful.

The practice has never been uniform. It varies among communities, between generations, and from person to person. Today many younger Maasai, especially men, do not stretch their ears, while others continue the tradition or wear it for ceremonies. Ornaments themselves carry meaning: anthropologists Donna Klumpp and Corinne Kratz have shown that Maasai beadwork can signal age, gender, and status, and that people judge it with real aesthetic expertise.

Ear stretching is a useful counterexample. Not every body modification is mainly about sexual attractiveness. Stretched earlobes and ornaments can certainly be beautiful, but they also express identity, tradition, maturity, and membership in a community. Here the emphasis falls on collective belonging as much as on individual appeal.

The connection to our theme: the meaning of a body practice depends on its cultural setting. Stretched lobes on a Maasai elder and stretched lobes on a college student in the United States may look alike, but they belong to different histories and say different things.

Check yourself: Why is ear stretching a useful counterexample to the idea that body modification is always about sexual attractiveness?

Traditional Tattooing Made Identity and Belonging Visible

Polynesia and Japan | Different historical traditions

Permanent tattoos could communicate cultural identity, social responsibilities, artistic expression, and belonging.

Left: Samoan man with pe'a, about 1890s, photograph by Thomas Andrew. National Library of New Zealand, via Wikimedia Commons. Public domain.

Right: tattooed man, about 1870 to 1890, hand-colored albumen print by Kusakabe Kimbei or Raimund von Stillfried. J. Paul Getty Museum, via Wikimedia Commons. Public domain.

Tattooing places pigment beneath the skin so that a design becomes a permanent part of the body. These two photographs come from two very different traditions, and it is important not to treat them as interchangeable.

Samoa. The man on the left wears the pe'a, the traditional Samoan male tattoo, which covers the body from the waist to the knees. It is applied by a master tattooist, the tufuga ta tatau, using combs and a mallet, and it takes many painful sessions to complete. Receiving the pe'a has long been a serious undertaking. It is often linked to adulthood, to commitment, and to readiness to serve one's family, village, and chiefs. Samoan women have their own tattoo, the malu, applied mainly to the thighs. Te Papa curator Sean Mallon and other scholars show that tatau has continued from the nineteenth century to today, in Samoa and across the Samoan diaspora, and is widely honored as cultural heritage.

Japan. The photograph on the right shows irezumi, the large pictorial tattoos that flourished in the Edo period and peaked in the nineteenth century. Designs drew on woodblock prints and popular stories: dragons, flowers, waves, and heroes from tales such as the Water Margin. Irezumi were especially popular among urban workers such as firefighters, couriers, and laborers. Their reception changed sharply. In 1872 the new Meiji government banned tattooing, partly because it feared Westerners would see it as a sign of backwardness, and the ban lasted until 1948. Yet foreign visitors bought photographs like this one as souvenirs. Today many Japanese still associate large tattoos with organized crime, and some public baths and gyms refuse tattooed customers, even as irezumi is admired as an art form around the world.

So a tattoo can be celebrated in one setting and stigmatized in another. The connection to our theme: a permanent trait can gain or lose social value as attitudes change, even though the mark on the skin stays exactly the same.

Check yourself: How can a tattoo change its social meaning even though the tattoo itself never changes?

Kayan Neck Coils Created a Culturally Admired Silhouette

Kayan communities | Myanmar

Brass coils create the appearance of an elongated neck and can express feminine beauty, identity, and cultural continuity.

Kayan woman weaving in a village near Inle Lake, Myanmar. Photograph by Thomas Schoch, via Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0.

The Kayan are a people of eastern Myanmar, often grouped with the wider Karenni, or Karen, peoples. Outsiders have often called them Padaung, an outside name that refers to the long-necked look of the women. Some Kayan women wear brass coils around the neck. Traditionally a girl might receive her first coil at around five years old, and longer coils replaced it as she grew. Each coil is actually one long spiral of brass rather than a stack of separate rings, and many women also wear coils on their arms and legs.

The coils create the visual impression of a very long neck, but they do not stretch the neck bones. Their weight presses down on the collarbones and upper ribs over many years, which lowers the shoulders and makes the neck look longer. X-rays in a 2019 medical study by Pattawan Lapo and colleagues showed unusually low collarbones in older coil wearers, and the same study found lower lung function in women who wore coils than in Kayan women who did not. So, like other practices in this lecture, the coils have real physical consequences.

For many Kayan, the coils have expressed feminine beauty, identity, and continuity with ancestors and community, and several origin stories explain them. The practice is not universal, though. Many Kayan women have never worn coils, it had been declining, and attitudes toward it differ among Kayan themselves. Since the civil conflicts of the later twentieth century, many Kayan have lived as refugees in northern Thailand, where villages of coil-wearing women became tourist destinations. Museums such as the Pitt Rivers Museum point out that tourism has helped keep the practice visible while raising serious concerns about exploitation. Even this photograph, taken in a weaving workshop near Inle Lake that welcomes visitors, comes from that world of tourism.

Because coils are often first fitted in childhood, the practice raises questions of consent similar to those raised by foot binding. A young child cannot meaningfully agree to a lifelong change, even if, as an adult, she later chooses to keep or remove the coils.

The connection to our theme: cultural expectations can be built into a developing body, so that a standard of beauty literally shapes how a person grows.

Check yourself: Why does the age at which a practice begins matter when we think about consent?

Modern Beauty Practices Continue to Alter Bodily Appearance

Contemporary societies

High heels and cosmetic procedures alter appearance in pursuit of personal preferences, socially valued beauty, or perceived attractiveness.

Left: pumps, Corbeau shoe shop, Rotterdam, 2008 to 2009. Museum Rotterdam, object 87057-1-2, via Wikimedia Commons. CC BY-SA 3.0. Right: silicone gel-filled breast implant. Photograph by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2019, via Wikimedia Commons. Public domain.

Body modification is not only something that happened long ago or far away. Our own societies also reshape bodies to fit valued looks.

High heels. A high heel raises the heel well above the toes and shifts body weight forward onto the ball of the foot. That changes posture and the way a person walks, and it makes the legs look longer, which many people find attractive. Heels are temporary in the sense that they come off, but long or frequent wear is associated with foot pain, bunions, and injury. You will compare high heels with foot binding in detail in a few slides, so for now the point is simply that a fashionable shoe changes how the body looks and moves.

Cosmetic procedures. The photograph on the right shows a breast implant: a flexible silicone shell filled with silicone gel. In breast augmentation, a surgeon places an implant, filled with either silicone gel or saline, behind the breast tissue or partly beneath the chest muscle to enlarge or reshape the breast. Implants were first developed for reconstruction after surgery such as mastectomy, and only later used widely for cosmetic enlargement. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration restricted silicone gel implants for cosmetic use in 1992 over safety concerns, then approved two silicone implants again in 2006 with requirements for long-term follow-up. The procedure permanently changes the body, carries surgical risks, and implants are not lifetime devices, so many people need further surgery later. Other procedures, such as injectable treatments, wear off within months. Procedures differ widely in permanence, cost, and medical risk.

People choose these practices for many reasons: they like how they look, they feel more confident, they enjoy fashion, or they want to meet expectations at work or in dating. Advertising, the fashion industry, celebrities, and social media all shape what looks desirable. That social influence is real, but it does not erase personal agency. Adults weigh these influences and make their own decisions, sometimes embracing a standard and sometimes rejecting it.

The connection to our theme: modern societies, including our own, also value certain appearances and offer ways to reshape the body to reach them.

Body modification · sexual capital

Body Modification Can Transform Attractiveness into Sexual Capital

Sexual capital: Social advantages associated with being considered attractive or sexually desirable.
  • Societies establish culturally specific standards of beauty.
  • Body modifications may increase perceived attractiveness.
  • Attractiveness can influence desirability, status, and marriage expectations.
  • Belief in these advantages can shape behavior, even when benefits are uncertain.
A cultural beauty standard↓ A body practice to meet it↓ Perceived attractiveness↓ A believed social advantage

Sources: Catherine Hakim, “Erotic Capital,” European Sociological Review 26, no. 5 (2010); Brown, Bossen, Gates, and Satterthwaite-Phillips, Journal of Asian Studies 71, no. 4 (2012); Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005).

Earlier, on the slide about how bound feet became sexual capital, you met this term. As a quick reminder, sexual capital means the social advantages that come from being seen as attractive or sexually desirable. The sociologist Catherine Hakim developed a closely related idea she called erotic capital, in a 2010 article in the European Sociological Review. She argued that attractiveness works as a personal asset alongside money, education, and social connections. The plain idea is simple: in many societies, looks can open doors.

Here is how that works with body modification. Every society sets its own standards of beauty. Body practices can move a person closer to those standards, which can raise how attractive others think that person is. Perceived attractiveness, in turn, can affect who is seen as desirable, who gains respect or status, and who is considered a good marriage partner. Think back to the examples. A fashionable corseted waist was read as attractive and refined. High heels change posture and make legs look longer in ways many people find appealing. Cosmetic procedures try to bring features closer to a valued ideal.

Sexual capital does not explain everything, though. Samoan tatau, Maasai ear stretching, and Kayan neck coils carry meanings of identity, heritage, adulthood, and belonging. Beauty may be part of those practices, but treating them only as attempts to attract partners would flatten their meaning. Sexual capital is one useful lens, not a master key.

Now return to foot binding. Many families believed that small feet made a daughter more desirable as a wife and could help her marry well. In 2012, Melissa Brown, Laurel Bossen, Hill Gates, and Damian Satterthwaite-Phillips examined the marriages of more than seven thousand rural women from several regions of China in the first half of the twentieth century. In most regions they found no statistically significant difference between bound and unbound girls in their chances of marrying into a wealthier household. Sichuan was an exception. So modern research cannot consistently show that binding brought an economic marriage advantage. But families did not have that research. They acted on what their communities knew and expected, and that belief was enough to shape what they did.

One more distinction matters. Being seen as attractive is not the same as having autonomy or power. A woman admired for her bound feet could still have limited mobility, limited work outside the home, and little say over her own marriage, which her family usually arranged. Whatever "capital" her feet represented was often spent by others.

The main insight: a culturally valued trait can influence behavior because people believe it brings advantages, whether or not those advantages can be proven.

Check yourself: Why can belief in an advantage shape behavior even when research cannot show that the advantage exists?

Body modification · choice and pressure

Beauty Practices Reveal a Tension Between Personal Choice and Social Pressure

  • People may genuinely enjoy modifying their bodies.
  • Personal preferences develop within cultural expectations.
  • Social approval and disapproval can influence individual choices.
  • Age, consent, and physical consequences distinguish different practices.

Class discussion · about 2 minutes

If rejecting a beauty standard carries social consequences, how voluntary is conformity?

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); Barnish and Barnish, BMJ Open (2016); instructor synthesis.

It is tempting to sort beauty practices into two boxes: free choices and forced ones. Real life rarely works that way. Choice and pressure are not opposites; they usually operate together.

Start with enjoyment, because it is real. Many people genuinely like wearing heels, enjoy fashion, love their tattoos, or are glad they had a cosmetic procedure. Those feelings are not fake. At the same time, nobody develops tastes in a vacuum. What we find beautiful is shaped by advertising, fashion, celebrities, social media, friends, family, and the hope of romance. Social approval rewards people who meet a standard, and disapproval, teasing, or lost opportunities can follow those who do not. That is the central paradox: a choice can be freely made and still be shaped by social pressure.

That paradox does not make all practices equal. Foot binding is a clear example of why. Girls were usually bound in early childhood, usually between about five and eight years old, by mothers or other female relatives, and they had little authority to refuse. The process was painful and reshaped the growing foot for life. It would be misleading to equate that with an adult choosing a pair of heels she can take off at the end of the day. To compare fairly, weigh several factors together: the person's age, whether they could consent, whether they were coerced, how much physical harm resulted, and whether the change can be reversed.

Social pressure can shape decisions without necessarily eliminating agency. Adults can and do push back against beauty standards, and even within strong traditions individuals make their own decisions. The point is to see both forces at once.

Take about two minutes for a quick whole-class discussion of the question on the slide. A few voices are enough; the aim is to surface different views, not to reach agreement.

Check yourself: Can a choice be both freely made and shaped by social pressure? Give one example from your own experience or from this lecture.

Body modification · cultural construction

Beauty Standards Are Culturally Constructed and Historically Changeable

  • Societies differ in what they consider physically attractive.
  • Standards of beauty change across time and place.
  • Cultural expectations influence bodily practices and social behavior.
  • Historians examine beliefs within their cultural context.

Late imperial China

In many Han communities, bound feet signaled beauty, desirability, and respectability.

→

Reform era, 1890s to 1930s

Reformers linked natural feet to health, modernization, and national strength.

Who determines what makes a body beautiful, and who bears the costs of meeting that standard?

Sources: Dorothy Ko, Cinderella's Sisters (2005); Patricia Buckley Ebrey, A History of China.

To say that beauty standards are culturally constructed does not mean that attraction is imaginary or that biology plays no part. People everywhere notice bodies and respond to them. What culture does is attach meanings to particular traits, and decide which traits count as beautiful, respectable, or desirable. Those decisions differ from one society to another and change over time.

You have seen this throughout the mini-lecture. A corseted waist, stretched earlobes, a pe'a, a full-back irezumi, a column of brass coils, and a pair of stilettos each made sense within a particular setting. Some of those meanings stayed stable for generations; others changed quickly.

Foot binding shows how dramatic that change can be. In many Han communities in late imperial China, bound feet meant beauty, erotic desirability, and respectability. By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, reformers described binding as a cause of weakness and natural feet as a sign of health, modernization, and national progress. Within a few generations, the same trait went from admired to embarrassing. A trait can lose its value when the culture around it changes, even though the body itself has not.

This is why historians use cultural relativism as a method. It means trying to understand a practice within its own cultural context: what it meant, who valued it, and why. That is different from moral approval. A historian can explain why loving mothers bound their daughters' feet without endorsing the practice or ignoring the pain it caused. Understanding comes first; judgment can follow, and it is better informed when it does.

Here is the conclusion to carry forward: societies create standards of beauty, and those standards can shape human behavior, social opportunities, and bodily autonomy. With that in mind, the next slide returns to foot binding for a close comparison with high heels.

Bunion (hallux valgus)

A deformity at the joint of the big toe, in which the toe angles toward the other toes. It is more than a simple bony growth.

Source: Maxwell S. Barnish and Jean Barnish, “High-heeled shoes and musculoskeletal injuries: a narrative systematic review,” BMJ Open 6, no. 1 (2016).

You met high heels a few slides ago as one modern beauty practice. Now look at them closely, side by side with foot binding. High heels are a useful comparison because they share something with foot binding, and because they differ in important ways.

What the research says: a 2016 systematic review by Maxwell and Jean Barnish found that high-heel use is associated with bunions (hallux valgus), musculoskeletal pain, and injury to the wearer. It did not find an association with osteoarthritis. "Associated with" does not mean heels inevitably cause each condition.

Read the table row by row. Both practices connect appearance, social expectations, and movement. But foot binding began in childhood, was decided by adults, and reshaped a growing foot permanently. High heels are usually worn by older people and can be taken off, though earlier injury may remain. Removable is not the same as free of pressure: jobs and social expectations can shape adult shoe choices too.

A caution: do not say the two are "only different by degree." Some differences, such as age, permanence, and who decides, may change the nature of the comparison.

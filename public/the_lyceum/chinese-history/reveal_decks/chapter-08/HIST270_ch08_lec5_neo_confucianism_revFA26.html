HIST 270 · Chapter 8 · Lecture 5

Neo-Confucianism Connected Cosmic Order to Moral Cultivation

How could an account of the universe explain human goodness and guide moral learning?

The Learning of the Way · Zhou Dunyi · Zhu Xi

This whole lecture is about one intellectual movement: the Learning of the Way, usually called neo-Confucianism in English. Keep the title's question in mind: how could an account of the universe explain human goodness and guide moral learning?

You will meet three stages: Zhou Dunyi's picture of the cosmos, the Cheng brothers' account of principle and vital energy, and Zhu Xi's synthesis, which turned those ideas into a program of study.

A Confucian revival · new questions

Confucian Thinkers Renewed Earlier Traditions through New Questions

Reality and morality

Song thinkers developed explanations of both reality and moral life.

Rival traditions

Buddhist and Daoist ideas supplied challenges and intellectual resources.

Human responsibilities

Confucian cultivation remained directed toward persons, families, and society.

Key terms: metaphysics · cosmology

×

Metaphysics

An account of reality and how it works: what things fundamentally are and why they exist as they do.

×

Cosmology

An account of the ordered world and its processes: how the cosmos came to be arranged and how it keeps changing.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 140; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Song-Ming Confucianism.”

Earlier Confucians mostly told people to be good. Song thinkers asked bigger questions: what is reality, and why is human nature what it is?

The context: eleventh-century scholar-statesmen such as Fan Zhongyan, Wang Anshi, and Sima Guang pursued Confucian goals by advising rulers and aiding the people. Other teachers, notably the Cheng brothers, were more interested in metaphysics and ethics, and argued that moral self-cultivation was more fundamental than service to the ruler. The textbook calls their explanations a response to the sophisticated metaphysics of Buddhism.

Breaking it down: Buddhism and Daoism were both rivals and resources. Neo-Confucians argued against them while learning from the questions they raised. Their own goal stayed practical: good persons, ordered families, and a well-governed society. The movement also had real spiritual depth; it was not only social ethics.

Be careful about causes: the revival was creative reinterpretation, not simple restoration of the past. It began well before 1127. The loss of the north later pushed Southern Song thinkers toward local and educational reform, but military defeat was not its single origin.

Before the Song · Tang precursors

Tang Precursors Argued for Renewing the Confucian Way

TangHan Yu and Li Ao were important Tang precursors.
TangThey defended Confucian learning amid the influence of Buddhism.
Into the SongQuestions about human nature and recovering authoritative teaching continued into Song.

Source: Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhou Dunyi.”

The Song movement had roots in the Tang.

Breaking it down: Han Yu and Li Ao lived in the Tang, well before the Northern Song masters. In an age when Buddhism was highly influential, they defended Confucian learning and raised questions, about human nature and about recovering authoritative ancient teaching, that Song thinkers took up.

How it connects: claiming to recover ancient teaching could actually support new ideas. A thinker could present an innovation as a return to what the sages had really meant.

Two cautions. Criticizing Buddhism did not mean later thinkers rejected every Buddhist idea. And it is not true that neo-Confucians had generally been monks.

Check yourself: How could a claim to "recover" ancient teaching make room for new ideas?

Zhou Dunyi · the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate

Zhou Dunyi's Diagram Connected Cosmic Processes with Human Cultivation

1017–1073Zhou Dunyi lived before the fall of Northern Song.
Supreme Ultimate (Taiji): activity and stillness generate yang and yin.
Yin and yang combine through five phases: water, fire, wood, metal, and earth.
The account proceeds to living beings and the sage's moral cultivation.
Human cultivation belongs within the order of the cosmos.
×

Taiji

The Supreme Ultimate, also translated "Supreme Polarity." Taiji is not the same as qi (vital energy and substance); the old spelling "Tai-qi" confuses the two.

×

Five phases

Water, fire, wood, metal, and earth, understood as patterned modes of transformation. They are not chemical elements or a periodic table.

Sources: Joseph A. Adler, Zhou Dunyi: The Metaphysics and Practice of Sagehood; Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhou Dunyi.”

Zhou Dunyi drew the universe as a diagram, and then explained how human beings fit into it.

Look at the diagram. This is the full historical vertical diagram, not just the familiar circular yin–yang symbol. Read it from top to bottom: the Supreme Ultimate; activity and stillness, which generate yang and yin; yin and yang combining through the five phases; further differentiation; and finally the myriad things.

Two things with similar names: the Taijitu is the diagram. The Taijitu shuo is Zhou's explanation of it, and it is the explanation that turns from the cosmos to human beings and to becoming a sage.

A hard opening: Zhou's explanation begins by pairing Wuji and Taiji. Scholars interpret that relationship in different ways, so do not read it as a simple creation story in which an empty universe produces a creator.

The context: Zhou drew on resources associated with Daoism and the Book of Changes (Yijing), then gave the cosmology a Confucian moral use. Taiji is not qi; you will meet qi with the Cheng brothers.

Think about it: where in the sequence does cosmic explanation become moral explanation?

The Cheng brothers · principle and vital energy

The Cheng Brothers Explained Principle and Concrete Existence

Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi emphasized moral cultivation.

Li 理

Principle or intelligible order, including physical and moral dimensions.

Qi 氣

The vital energy and substance constituting actual things.

These related concepts explain an ordered world in its concrete diversity.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 140; Adler, Zhou Dunyi.

Two words, li and qi, carry the rest of this lecture.

Breaking it down: according to the textbook, the principle for something could be moral or physical. The principle for wives, for example, is moral; the principle for trees is physical. But for either to exist, there must also be the energy and substance that make up actual things: qi. Principle (li) is broader than a rule; it is the intelligible order of things. Qi is broader than a modern unit of energy; it is what things are actually made of.

The name: the Cheng brothers' followers called their school the Learning of the Way (Daoxue). In English it is often called neo-Confucianism, to stress how different it was from early Confucianism.

The connection to Zhou: the Chengs briefly studied with Zhou Dunyi, but how much they owed him philosophically is uncertain. Later accounts made the connection prominent. Do not picture a finished system passed unchanged from teacher to student.

Check yourself: In your own words, what is the difference between li and qi?

Human nature · goodness and wrongdoing

Underlying Human Goodness Did Not Guarantee Good Conduct

Good nature
The Cheng brothers defended Mencius's claim that human nature is good. The principle underlying human beings is good.
→
Uneven qi
Uneven or impure endowments of qi could give rise to selfish impulses.
→
Cultivation
Moral cultivation addressed the gap between underlying goodness and conduct.
Goodness is a foundation for cultivation, not a substitute for it.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 140.

If human nature is good, why do people do wrong? The li–qi theory gave an answer.

From the textbook: the theory let Song thinkers validate Mencius's claim that human nature is good and still explain wrongdoing. The principle underlying human beings is good, but each person's endowment of vital energy is more or less impure, which gives rise to selfish impulses (p. 140).

An illustration (not a historical case): you know you owe a friend help moving this weekend. Your underlying sense of duty is real, but you take a better offer instead. The goodness was there; the conduct fell short. Cultivation is the work of closing that gap.

Two cautions. Qi is not evil in itself. And li and qi are not a good soul trapped in a bad body. They are two aspects of everything that exists.

How it connects: Zhou and the Chengs share a concern: grounding moral cultivation in an account of how the world works. Their doctrines are not identical.

Check yourself: Explain how a person with a good nature can still act selfishly, using the words li and qi.

Zhu Xi · 1130–1200

Zhu Xi Brought Earlier Ideas into a Comprehensive Synthesis

Inherited strands

  • Zhu Xi (1130–1200) developed the Cheng brothers' account of principle.
  • He integrated ideas from Zhou Dunyi and Zhang Zai.

Zhu Xi's synthesis

  • For Zhu Xi, Taiji expressed the ultimate natural and moral order.
  • He connected explanations of reality with a program of learning.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 140, 147; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhu Xi.”

The textbook calls Zhu Xi "the great synthesizer" (p. 147). This slide shows what he brought together.

The context: after the loss of the north, the elite lost confidence in reform from above and looked for ways to build a better society from the bottom: reforming families and communities, founding schools and academies, publishing for many audiences. Zhu Xi was the greatest of these Southern Song masters. He passed the jinshi examination at eighteen but spent very little of the next fifty-two years in government service.

Breaking it down: Zhu extended the Cheng brothers' account of principle and brought in Zhou Dunyi's cosmology and Zhang Zai's ideas about qi. For Zhu, Taiji expressed the ultimate order of nature and morality. Li and qi can be distinguished in thought, but in actual reality they are always together; they are not two independent substances.

Why this matters: a synthesis is more than a collection of quotations. Zhu linked cosmology, an account of human psychology, and a practice of education into one system.

The curriculum · the Four Books

The Four Books Organized Zhu Xi's Moral Curriculum

Analects
Mencius
Great Learning
Doctrine of the Mean
Zhu Xi's commentaries connected inherited texts to moral development.
He selected and interpreted these works; he did not author them.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 140; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhu Xi.”

Zhu Xi's most lasting contribution was a reading list, and the commentaries that told students how to read it.

Textbook anchor: "He encouraged his students to master the Four Books—the Analects, Mencius, Doctrine of the Mean, and the Great Learning." (p. 140)

Breaking it down: Zhu's contemporaries wanted a path toward goodness, with steps to follow and ways to judge their progress. Confucius and Mencius had simply said to be good. The last two of the Four Books, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean, were each a chapter of the classic Book of Rites. Both stress that improving the world starts with improving the mind, which made them ideal for Zhu's program.

What Zhu did: he selected these texts, arranged them, edited them, and wrote commentaries interpreting them. He did not write the books, and the finished Four Books curriculum was not simply handed to him by the Cheng brothers.

Study and order · the Great Learning

Careful Study Linked Knowledge with Family and Political Order

Investigation of things→Knowledge→Sincere thoughts→Rectified mind→Cultivated person→Ordered family→Governed state→Virtue throughout the world

Paraphrase of the Great Learning, read from the starting point outward.

Investigation of things connected the extension of knowledge with cultivation.
Zhu Xi urged thorough study rather than hurried reading of many books.
The Great Learning traced public order back through family, person, and mind.
Learning was meant to shape conduct and responsibilities.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 140–141.

The Great Learning gave Zhu Xi a map from private study to public order.

The passage: the textbook quotes it (pp. 140–141). It begins with the ancients who wished to illuminate virtue throughout the world, and works backward: they first governed their states; to govern states, they first ordered their families; to order families, they cultivated themselves; to cultivate themselves, they rectified their minds; to rectify their minds, they made their thoughts sincere; to make thoughts sincere, they extended their knowledge. "The extension of knowledge lies in the investigation of things." The chain on the slide reads that passage from its starting point outward. Treat it as a moral ideal, not an automatic formula.

How to study: Zhu argued that study should be intensive rather than extensive. When a student said he wanted to survey many books, Zhu told him to read one book thoroughly before starting another, comparing it to archery: if you are strong enough for a five-pint bow, use a four-pint one, so you can draw it fully with strength to spare. Investigation meant engaging texts and wider questions, not rote reading.

Why this matters: learning was supposed to shape conduct and responsibilities. That does not mean individual virtue could guarantee military or fiscal success.

Check yourself: According to the Great Learning, what must come before an ordered family?

Institutions · private academies

Academies Made Moral Learning a Sustained Collective Practice

Zhu Xi taught disciples and helped establish private academies.

Academies gave teachers and learners places for concentrated study.

Teaching, discussion, and publication carried the movement into communities.

Moral influence could be pursued beyond an active court career.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 140.

Ideas need institutions to last. For neo-Confucianism, that institution was the private academy.

Breaking it down: Zhu Xi taught groups of disciples and led the way in establishing private academies as the institutional base for the revived Confucianism. These gathering places for teachers and disciples were often on mountains, as monasteries were, and like monasteries they offered a retreat from the world. That resemblance does not mean the teachers were monks.

Support: the government in effect supported Zhu's teaching by regularly appointing him to sinecures, official posts with few or no duties.

How it connects: this fits the Southern Song search for improvement from the local level. The movement stayed tied to institutions, patrons, and society even when its teachers worked away from court.

Check yourself: How could a teacher with almost no government career still have great influence?

Different emphases · within the movement

Neo-Confucians Disagreed about How Best to Cultivate Understanding

Learning of Principle

Associated with Cheng–Zhu approaches to study and investigation.

Learning of Heart/Mind

Associated with Lu Jiuyuan's emphasis on reflective moral awareness.

These were differences of emphasis within shared concerns about goodness and cultivation.
×

Xin · heart/mind

The Chinese word xin covers both thinking and feeling. "Mind" here does not mean intellect separated from emotion.

Source: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Metaphysics in Chinese Philosophy.”

Neo-Confucians agreed on many goals but argued about the best road.

Breaking it down: the Learning of Principle (Li Xue) is associated with Cheng–Zhu approaches that stress study and the investigation of things. The Learning of Heart/Mind (Xin Xue) is associated with Lu Jiuyuan, a contemporary of Zhu Xi, who emphasized reflective moral awareness.

This was not a split between people who reasoned and people who did not, or between people who used books and people who rejected them. Both shared concerns about goodness and cultivation; they differed in emphasis. (A better-known Heart/Mind philosopher, Wang Yangming, lived much later, under the Ming.)

A case from the chapter · Zhu Xi and Cai Yuanding

The Cai Case Shows the Breadth of Zhu Xi's Intellectual Interests

The case

Cai Yuanding

Fujian geomancer and scholar · fellow teacher to Zhu Xi

Cai Yuanding collaborated with Zhu Xi as a fellow teacher.
Geomancy used ideas about landscape and qi in choosing burial sites.
Zhu Xi drew on this expertise; some educated contemporaries were skeptical.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 141–142.

Zhu Xi's interests went well beyond the Four Books.

The case: Cai Yuanding learned geomancy, the science of "wind and water," from his father, Cai Fa, along with Confucian philosophy. Geomancy held that the energy (qi) in the veins of the earth, together with wind and water, made some burial sites far better than others for the descendants of the person buried there. Cai never passed the civil service exams or held office. When he first visited Zhu Xi at twenty-five, Zhu treated him as a fellow teacher rather than a student, and one contemporary said Zhu learned geomancy, yin and yang, and music theory from him.

The memorial: in 1194 Zhu Xi used Cai's ideas to propose a new burial site for a recently deceased emperor. He admitted that many educated men dismissed geomancy and might ridicule him for trusting it.

Why this matters: cultivation and learning lived inside a wider intellectual world. Treat geomancy as historical reasoning, not modern science, and notice that intellectual authority did not require holding office. The chapter's discussion questions ask whether Zhu's confidence in geomancy fits his philosophy; think about it.

Check yourself: What does the Cai case show about where intellectual authority could come from?

Transmission of the Way · daotong

Claims about Transmission Established Intellectual Authority

Ancient sages→Zhou Dunyi→Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi→Zhu Xi

A claimed lineage, not a record of uninterrupted personal teaching.

Daotong meant a claimed transmission or recovery of the Confucian Way.
Zhu Xi gave Zhou Dunyi a prominent place in the recovered lineage.
The lineage connected ancient sages with Song teachers and their interpretations.

Sources: Adler, Zhou Dunyi; Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhou Dunyi.”

Who had the authority to say what Confucianism really meant? Neo-Confucians answered with a lineage.

Breaking it down: daotong, often translated "transmission of the Way," was a claim made by historical thinkers: that the authentic teaching of the ancient sages had been recovered and passed on through particular Song teachers. Zhu Xi gave Zhou Dunyi a prominent place in that lineage, which helped make Zhou's cosmology central to later Confucian identity.

Read the claim critically. The lineage was not an unbroken chain of personal teachers. Remember the earlier slide: the Cheng brothers had only brief contact with Zhou. Later lineage-building made that connection look larger. You do not need to settle scholarly debates about it; just distinguish historical contact from a constructed lineage.

Check yourself: Why would a lineage reaching back to the ancient sages give Zhu Xi's interpretation authority?

Controversy and influence · 1190s and after

Contested Teachings Later Shaped Education across East Asia

Late 1190sZhu Xi's teachings were condemned near the end of his life.
1241The court honored him and government students studied his commentaries.
LaterHis framework influenced later education and intellectual debate.
Beyond ChinaKorean and Japanese thinkers later adapted it to their own circumstances.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 141; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhu Xi.”

Zhu Xi died under a cloud. Within decades his teachings became orthodox.

From the textbook: many contemporaries found Zhu offensively self-righteous. Near the end of his life his teachings were condemned as "spurious learning," and exam candidates were forbidden to cite them; his friend Cai Yuanding was banished in the same campaign. Within decades the verdict was reversed. In 1241 an emperor credited Zhu Xi with "illuminating the Way," and government students had to study his commentaries on the Four Books (p. 141).

Wider influence: through texts, schools, and continued debate, his framework shaped later education. Korean and Japanese thinkers later adapted it to their own circumstances. Recognition was not immediate or universal, and spreading across East Asia did not make the regional traditions identical.

Now step back · the Learning of the Way

Neo-Confucianism Joined Cosmic Explanation with Moral Learning

1

Zhou Dunyi

Zhou connected cosmic processes with sagehood.

2

The Cheng brothers

The Cheng brothers explained goodness and imperfect conduct through li and qi.

3

Zhu Xi

Zhu Xi organized inherited ideas and texts into a program of cultivation.

Sources: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 140–141, 147; Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Zhu Xi.”

This slide reconnects the two halves of the lecture: the cosmos and the classroom.

Breaking it down: Zhou Dunyi set human cultivation inside the order of the cosmos. The Cheng brothers used li and qi to explain why a good human nature can still produce imperfect conduct. Zhu Xi brought these ideas together with the Four Books, the investigation of things, and academy teaching into one program.

This was a synthesis made by particular thinkers, followed by real differences and debates. It was not a timeless list of identical doctrines.

## Historical argument

Neo-Confucian thinkers connected explanations of cosmic order and human nature with moral cultivation. Zhou Dunyi’s cosmology, the Cheng brothers’ li–qi account, and Zhu Xi’s teaching framework reveal complementary developments rather than an unchanged doctrine handed down intact. Zhu’s synthesis brought inherited ideas into educational and social practice.

- Recognize transmission claims and differences among neo-Confucian approaches.

### Slide 4 | Zhou Dunyi’s Diagram Connected Cosmic Processes with Human Cultivation

**Time:** 2:30–4:30  
**Format:** diagram  
**Sources:** A, I (source key below)  

**Student-facing bullets**

- Zhou Dunyi lived from 1017 to 1073, before the fall of Northern Song.
- Supreme Ultimate (Taiji): activity and stillness generate yang and yin.
- Yin and yang combine through five phases: water, fire, wood, metal, and earth.
- The account proceeds to living beings and the sage’s moral cultivation.

**Callout:** Human cultivation belongs within the order of the cosmos.

**Diagram discussion:** Use the full historical vertical diagram, not just the familiar circular yin–yang symbol. Follow Taiji/Wuji, activity and stillness, yin and yang, the five phases, generative differentiation, and the myriad things. Zhou’s accompanying explanation then turns to human beings and sagehood. Ask orally where cosmic explanation becomes moral explanation, and explain the transition; this is discussion within instruction, not a third activity.

**Historical diagram:** [Taijitu reproduced with Adler’s translation](https://www2.kenyon.edu/Depts/Religion/Fac/Adler/Writings/taijitu.jpg). Keep Wuji/Taiji’s interpretive complexity in the notes rather than inventing a literal creation timeline.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Use Zhou’s historical diagram and read its levels from top toward bottom. Distinguish the diagram, Taijitu, from its explanatory text, Taijitu shuo. The opening juxtaposes Wuji and Taiji; present that relationship as interpretively difficult, not a simple sequence in which an empty universe becomes a creator. The five phases describe patterned transformations rather than modern chemical elements. Zhou uses resources associated with Daoism and the Yijing, then gives the cosmology a Confucian moral application. Taiji is distinct from qi; correct the old slide’s spelling “Tai-qi.”

### Slide 6 | The Cheng Brothers Explained Principle and Concrete Existence

**Time:** 7:30–9:30  
**Format:** comparison  
**Sources:** T, A (source key below)  

**Student-facing bullets**

- Cheng Hao and Cheng Yi emphasized moral cultivation.
- Li: principle or intelligible order, including physical and moral dimensions.
- Qi: the vital energy and substance constituting actual things.
- These related concepts explain an ordered world in its concrete diversity.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define the concepts together to conserve time while keeping the distinction clear. Principle is broader than rules; qi is broader than a modern unit of energy. The textbook discusses physical principles and good human nature. The Chengs briefly studied with Zhou, but the extent of their direct philosophical debt is uncertain. Later accounts of transmission made the connection prominent; do not imply that a fully formed system passed unchanged through a simple teacher-to-student chain.

### Slide 7 | Underlying Human Goodness Did Not Guarantee Good Conduct

**Time:** 9:30–12:00  
**Format:** bullets  
**Sources:** T (source key below)  

**Student-facing bullets**

- The Cheng brothers defended Mencius’s claim that human nature is good.
- The principle underlying human beings is good.
- Uneven or impure endowments of qi could give rise to selfish impulses.
- Moral cultivation addressed the gap between underlying goodness and conduct.

**Callout:** Goodness is a foundation for cultivation, not a substitute for it.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Separate a claim about human nature from a claim about every action. The chapter’s account preserves underlying goodness while explaining selfishness. Use a brief teaching example of recognizing a duty but acting for personal advantage; label it as an illustration. Qi is not inherently evil, and li and qi are not a good soul opposed to a bad body. Connect back to Zhou only at the level of a shared concern: cosmological explanations help ground moral cultivation. Their accounts should not be collapsed into identical doctrines.

### Slide 9 | Zhu Xi Brought Earlier Ideas into a Comprehensive Synthesis

**Time:** 17:00–18:30  
**Format:** comparison  
**Sources:** T, Z (source key below)  

**Student-facing bullets**

- Zhu Xi (1130–1200) developed the Cheng brothers’ account of principle.
- He integrated ideas from Zhou Dunyi and Zhang Zai.
- For Zhu Xi, Taiji expressed the ultimate natural and moral order.

**Presenter-note guidance**

This restores the existing lecture’s emphasis on synthesis and makes its components explicit. Zhang Zai supplies another strand concerning qi; Zhu’s system is not merely a compilation of quotations. The old term “monistic-dualism” is replaced with a teachable claim: li and qi can be distinguished conceptually while actual reality remains interconnected. Do not label them two independent substances. Explain synthesis through the links among cosmology, moral psychology, and educational practice.

**Time:** 22:30–24:00  
**Format:** bullets  
**Sources:** T (source key below)  

**Student-facing bullets**

- Zhu Xi taught disciples and helped establish private academies.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The movement remained connected to institutions, patrons, and society even when teachers worked away from court.

## Instructor-only activity response criteria

### Activity 1 | Characterize the Learning of the Way

A satisfactory response explains the movement’s renewed account of reality and morality; distinguishes principle from concrete vital energy and substance; and explains how underlying goodness can coexist with selfish conduct. It connects that gap to cultivation. Zhou’s diagram is an optional supporting example linking cosmology and sagehood, not a substitute for explaining li and qi.

Useful corrections: Taiji and qi are different terms. The movement developed before the loss of northern China. Qi is not inherently evil. Zhou’s diagram is not a modern scientific account or a simple picture of a creator god.

### Activity 2 | Explain Zhu Xi’s synthesis

A satisfactory response connects an earlier idea to Zhu’s program of study and moral development, with an example from the Four Books, investigation, or academies. It explains the connection to family or political responsibilities. Strong responses link philosophical explanation, practice, and social purpose rather than listing thinkers. Recognition of disagreement and later influence can add nuance but is not required in a short answer.

Useful corrections: Zhu did not write the Four Books. Claims about transmission do not prove an uninterrupted succession of personal teaching. Neo-Confucian approaches differed. Official recognition came after controversy.

## Key distinctions for teaching

| Term or relationship | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Taiji | Supreme Ultimate; also translated Supreme Polarity. In Zhu’s interpretation, the ultimate natural/moral order. |
| Qi | Vital energy and substance; not an alternate spelling of Taiji. |
| Li | Principle or pattern; not a separate physical substance. |
| Wuji and Taiji | A difficult opening relationship in Zhou’s explanation; avoid an overconfident linear creation story. |
| Five phases | Water, fire, wood, metal, earth as modes of transformation; not a periodic table. |
| Diagram and explanation | Taijitu is the diagram; Taijitu shuo is the explanatory text that develops its ethical application. |
| Daotong | A claimed recovery/transmission of authoritative teaching, historically constructed and contested. |
| Principle and Heart/Mind approaches | Broad differences of emphasis in cultivation, not two wholly unrelated traditions. |

# HIST 270 | Chapter 8 | Lecture 3
# Education, Reform, and Religion Shaped Song Social Life

**Format:** Detailed slide outline for a future Reveal.js deck  
**Duration:** 35 minutes, including two five-minute classroom activities  
**Audience:** First-year community college students  
**Assigned source:** Ebrey, *A History of China*, Chapter 8, especially pp. 134–138 and 141–146  
**Sequence:** Lecture 3 of five; follows “Agriculture and Trade Sustained Song Prosperity” and precedes the dedicated neo-Confucianism lecture

## Historical argument

Song economic and educational expansion enlarged the scholar-official elite, but access to education remained unequal and shared learning did not produce political agreement. Family status, marriage, and property shaped women’s opportunities, while people across society sought practical help from a diverse spirit realm. Government, family life, and religious practices were connected parts of Song society, each organized through relationships of authority and obligation.

**Framing question:** How did people seek advancement, order, and security in Song society?

The lecture moves through three settings: educated officials and government, women and households, and local religious life. Give each setting its own explanation. Do not imply that printing caused every family practice or religious belief described here.

## Learning targets

By the end of the lecture, students should be able to:

- Explain how printing and examinations changed the size and culture of the educated elite.
- Distinguish fairer examination procedures from equal access to preparation and office.
- Explain Wang Anshi’s goals and the sources of opposition to the New Policies.
- Describe how arranged marriage, dowries, household work, and literacy shaped women’s lives.
- Explain the bureaucratic model of the spirit realm and identify ways people sought religious assistance.
- Use Hong Mai’s stories as evidence of beliefs and practices while recognizing the author’s perspective.

### Slide 2 | Printing Expanded Access to Books and Educated Culture

**Time:** 0:30–2:00  
**Format:** Short causal sequence with three supporting bullets  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 134  
**Review alignment:** Q5

**Student-facing bullets**

- Printing lowered the cost of books.
- More readers could obtain classics, poetry, histories, and works on other subjects.
- Demand for books grew with preparation for government examinations.
- The educated elite became larger, better educated, and less aristocratic in its habits.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Connect to the papermaking industry in Lecture 2, then explain the cultural consequence: more available books supported longer and broader study. A scholar-official was an educated man participating in government service; the wider educated class also included men who never obtained office. Examinations and books reinforced one another: preparation created demand for texts, while cheaper texts made preparation more accessible. Expanded access did not make literacy universal.

### Slide 3 | Examinations Expanded Recruitment and Sought Fairer Evaluation

**Time:** 2:00–3:30  
**Format:** Three examination features with a fairness callout  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 135

**Student-facing bullets**

- Song recruited more holders of the highest examination degree than Tang had.
- Candidate names were replaced with numbers.
- Clerks recopied papers so examiners could not recognize handwriting.
- Growing competition made success difficult and often required repeated attempts.

**Callout:** “Anonymous evaluation reduced opportunities for favoritism.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define jinshi as the highest examination degree identified in this chapter. Passing it brought prestige, but it did not guarantee an easy career or rapid promotion. Explain recopying as an effort to conceal identity, not as punishment or rewriting candidates’ arguments. The chapter notes that candidate numbers rose dramatically; use the trend rather than requiring students to memorize several totals. Avoid making examination passing, possession of a degree, appointment, and promotion interchangeable.

### Slide 4 | Family Resources Continued to Shape Access to Office

**Time:** 3:30–5:00  
**Format:** Two-column comparison of procedures and preparation  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 135–136

**Student-facing comparison**

| Expanded opportunities | Continuing advantages |
|---|---|
| Examinations could identify capable men beyond established official families | Sustained education required family resources |
| Anonymous papers reduced examiner favoritism | Prominent families supplied teachers, contacts, and support |
| Successful candidates gained prestige | Senior officials could nominate sons and grandsons for appointments |

**Presenter-note guidance**

The chapter treats Song’s opportunities as unusual by premodern standards while warning against assuming modern levels of mobility. Families supporting extensive education were generally landholders. Appointment through relatives’ privilege remained substantial; the chapter estimates around 40 percent or more of posts, but the proportion need not be displayed. Such appointees often began in low-ranking local positions. State recruitment became more open without ceasing to be socially unequal.

### Slide 5 | Scholar-Officials Participated in a Broad Cultural World

**Time:** 5:00–6:00  
**Format:** Three compact cultural examples  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 136

**Student-facing bullets**

- Study, examinations, and officeholding brought educated men into extensive networks.
- Poetry, painting, and collecting expressed cultivated identities.
- Su Shi valued painting as an expression of a scholar’s ideas.
- Shen Gua pursued subjects including mathematics, engineering, and medicine.

**Presenter-note guidance**

This slide completes Q5’s culture component. The new elite did more than prepare for tests: travel, correspondence, art, and intellectual interests helped connect its members. Use Su Shi and Shen Gua as two brief examples rather than beginning separate biographies. Shen Gua’s breadth was exceptional, and the chapter says artistic interests were more widespread than his kind of scientific inquiry. Shared culture did not prevent the disagreements examined next.

### Slide 6 | Reformers Wanted Officials to Serve the State More Effectively

**Time:** 6:00–7:00  
**Format:** One governmental problem and one early reform example  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 136–137

**Student-facing bullets**

- Officials disagreed about recruitment, public service, military policy, and revenue.
- Fan Zhongyan proposed changes to recruitment, pay, and promotion in the 1040s.
- Resistance ended his program quickly.
- His example encouraged later reformers, including Wang Anshi.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Explain the underlying problem: an examination-certified official might still perform poorly, and defending the state required resources that officials did not agree how to raise. Fan wanted more capable and conscientious government. His higher-pay proposal aimed to discourage corruption, and proposed recruitment or promotion changes threatened people accustomed to existing arrangements. Keep Fan’s example under one minute so Wang’s program receives the required explanation.

### Slide 7 | Wang Anshi Linked Stronger Government to Rural Prosperity

**Time:** 7:00–9:00  
**Format:** Policy-to-purpose table  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 137  
**Review alignment:** Q6

**Student-facing comparison**

| Representative New Policy | Intended purpose |
|---|---|
| Low-cost loans to farming families | Support rural prosperity |
| Replace labor service with a tax | Change how households met state obligations |
| Expand monopolies and resurvey land | Raise revenue and make land taxes more equitable |
| Organize a local militia | Reduce the expense of the standing army |

**Student-facing callout:** “A stronger productive base could support a stronger state.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Emperor Shenzong supported Wang’s program. Explain labor service as work households owed the government, a monopoly as exclusive control over a line of trade, and militia as locally organized military service. Present purposes as intentions, not proof that each policy worked. Wang also changed examination content by dropping poetry and requiring his own classical commentaries, hoping to recruit officials with a more practical orientation. Include this last example orally to connect education and government without crowding the table.

### Slide 8 | Disagreements over Reform Became Moral and Political Conflicts

**Time:** 9:00–10:30  
**Format:** Three sources of conflict plus a short interpretive callout  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 136–137

**Student-facing bullets**

- Reforms affected existing arrangements and people’s interests.
- Officials disagreed philosophically about good government.
- Competition, alliances, and personal antagonisms intensified disagreement.
- Both sides portrayed themselves as principled and their opponents as morally inferior.

**Callout:** “Disagreement about policy became a judgment about character.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define a faction as a group of officials allied around political positions and relationships. The chapter cautions against reducing the conflict to a simple regional or class division. Explain how a moral language of public service could make compromise difficult: each side believed it protected the common good. Present opposition as a combination of interests, convictions, and political competition. Do not invent particular policy abuses or economic outcomes not described in the assigned chapter.

### Slide 9 | Changes at Court Reversed Policies and Punished Opponents

**Time:** 10:30–12:00  
**Format:** Three-stage court-policy sequence  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 137–138

**Student-facing bullets**

- Under Shenzong, reform supporters held imperial backing.
- After his death in 1085, a regency brought opponents led by Sima Guang into power.
- When Zhezong ruled independently in 1093, reform supporters returned.
- Successive factions reversed policies and removed opponents from influential posts.

**Presenter-note guidance**

A regent governs for a ruler unable to exercise power independently, often a child. The regent here was Zhezong’s grandmother. Transfers away from the capital reduced officials’ influence; some punitive assignments were especially harsh. Explain the cycle rather than listing every official: court access changed who could implement policies and who suffered retaliation. Huizong later supported the reform side, but do not repeat his reign or the conquest narrative from Lecture 1. Students now have both reform goals and an explanation of severe factional conflict for Q6.

> What were the primary goals of Wang Anshi’s “New Policies,” and why did they provoke such severe factional strife within the Song bureaucracy?

**Student-facing task**

- Write a response of 3–5 sentences.
- Identify a main goal and explain one policy intended to achieve it.
- Explain a source of opposition.

**Five-minute routine**

2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; check that the answer explains both the program and the conflict.

### Slide 11 | Marriage Joined Families and Reflected Social Status

**Time:** 17:00–18:30  
**Format:** Marriage agreement panel with three explanatory bullets  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 141–142  
**Review alignment:** Q10, marriage practices

**Student-facing bullets**

- Parents arranged marriages through matchmakers or personal connections.
- Families usually sought partners of comparable status.
- Agreements identified family backgrounds and the gifts and dowry to be exchanged.
- Examination success could make a young man an attractive marriage prospect.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Use the final point to connect marriage to the educated elite just discussed. The bride moved into her husband’s family, where relations with her mother-in-law and expectations of bearing sons mattered. Define patrilineal as tracing the family line through men. Family arrangement does not tell us the feelings of every couple; the chapter also offers examples of emotional and intellectual companionship. Omit the detailed wedding procession to preserve time for property and women’s varied activities.

### Slide 12 | Dowries Transferred Resources to Daughters

**Time:** 18:30–20:00  
**Format:** Material-evidence panel with a concise definition  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 141, 146  
**Review alignment:** Q10, property

**Student-facing bullets**

- A dowry was property a bride’s family provided when she married.
- Clothes and bedding were common; wealthier dowries could include jewelry or land deeds.
- Women could draw on dowry resources in family life.
- Huang Sheng’s burial goods illustrate the wealth transferred to one elite daughter.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Dowry connects commerce and family resources without requiring an unsupported claim about universal property rights. The chapter describes women using their dowries and contesting property seizures; this demonstrates women’s involvement in property, not equality in every legal or household relationship. Huang Sheng’s 201 pieces of clothing and 153 lengths of cloth provide a vivid optional oral detail from p. 146. Use her garments here to discuss resources; reserve her bound-foot shoes and bodily beauty standards for Lecture 5. One affluent burial cannot represent every woman’s circumstances.

### Slide 13 | Women’s Work and Literacy Extended Their Household Roles

**Time:** 20:00–22:00  
**Format:** Three example panels: work, learning, and life circumstances  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 141–143  
**Review alignment:** Q10, social and economic realities

**Student-facing bullets**

- Textile production and other work contributed to household livelihoods.
- Some women worked as innkeepers, midwives, or producers of goods for sale.
- Expanded education enabled some women to teach children and participate in literary culture.
- Widowhood, motherhood, wealth, and household position shaped women’s options.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The chapter distinguishes prescriptive ideals about women’s domestic place from records of their varied activities. Domestic textile production was economically significant, not simply leisure. Li Qingzhao provides a brief example of an accomplished woman poet from an educated family; do not add a separate literary analysis. Song widows commonly remarried despite moral objections, so later ideals should not be treated as universal Song practice. Wives outranked concubines within households, while the children of both were recognized as family members. Mention this distinction only as needed to clarify household hierarchy.

**Transition:** “Families also sought health, protection, and good fortune through religious practices.”

### Slide 14 | Hong Mai’s Stories Reveal Everyday Religious Concerns

**Time:** 22:00–23:30  
**Format:** Historical-source panel  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 144–146  
**Review alignment:** Q11, the source and religious landscape

**Student-facing bullets**

- Hong Mai compiled *The Record of the Listener* in the twelfth century.
- His stories described gods, ghosts, ancestors, illness, and unexpected events.
- Tales of retribution linked wrongdoing with supernatural punishment.
- The collection reveals beliefs and concerns through an educated author’s account.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Hong Mai came from a prominent official family and recorded accounts learned through experience and social contacts. Printing helped more Song works survive, giving historians access to aspects of life poorly documented for earlier periods. His stories provide evidence of how events were narrated and interpreted; they do not independently prove supernatural causation or represent every commoner’s beliefs. Briefly identify the chapter’s retribution stories as examples connecting religious imagination with family obligations, without retelling their violence at length.

### Slide 15 | People Imagined the Spirit Realm through Human Hierarchies

**Time:** 23:30–25:00  
**Format:** Two-column comparison of human and spirit institutions  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 144–145

**Student-facing bullets**

- Gods, ancestors, and other spirits could bring blessings or misfortunes.
- Local gods could resemble territorial rulers.
- Some gods held offices, transmitted documents, and referred matters to higher authorities.
- Spirits were understood to have emotions and interests resembling those of people.

**Presenter-note guidance**

This is an analogy within the historical beliefs, not a claim that every spirit belonged to one uniform system. Some spirits were imagined as dangerous extortionists, while others were protectors or officials. Gods were not universally understood as omnipotent; a petitioner might need the approval of a higher power. Explain bureaucracy in familiar terms: offices with assigned responsibilities and superior authorities. The model connects everyday religion to institutions students have already encountered.

### Slide 16 | People Used Several Religious Traditions to Seek Practical Help

**Time:** 25:00–27:00  
**Format:** Concern–response–specialist table  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 144–145

**Student-facing comparison**

| Religious concern | Ways of responding |
|---|---|
| Illness or unexplained misfortune | Divination, dreams, or consultation to identify a cause |
| A spirit’s displeasure or a need for protection | Prayer and offerings |
| A troubling ghost | Rituals conducted by specialists |

**Student-facing callout:** “Buddhist and Daoist clergy, mediums, diviners, and local temple specialists offered different forms of assistance.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

People did not always select one exclusive religious identity and use only its specialists. An individual could consult several kinds of practitioner in addressing a problem. Define a medium as someone understood to permit communication with spirits, divination as a way of seeking otherwise hidden information, and exorcism as a ritual intended to remove or subdue a troubling spirit. The chapter describes religious consultation after medical treatment failed; keep this clearly within historical belief and practice rather than presenting it as current advice.

### Slide 17 | One Ghost Story Shows People Consulting Different Specialists

**Time:** 27:00–28:30  
**Format:** Three-step case sequence  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 145

**Student-facing sequence**

1. A man troubled by a ghost sought help from a local exorcist.
2. When that failed, he asked a visiting Daoist priest to perform a ceremony.
3. Buddhist monks then performed incantations and an exorcism, which the account described as successful.

**Callout:** “The case shows how religious assistance could cross traditions.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Make the attribution explicit: the account reports that the final ritual worked. The historical point is that the man’s search for help passed through multiple specialists. This is a short teaching example supplying evidence for Q11, not an additional classroom activity or a claim that one religion was generally more effective. The sequence also shows that people responded to perceived failures by seeking another source of assistance.

### Slide 18 | The Song State Recognized and Regulated Local Gods

**Time:** 28:30–30:00  
**Format:** State–local shrine relationship panel  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 146

**Student-facing bullets**

- The state claimed authority to approve or disapprove local shrines.
- Some shrines judged illicit or excessive were destroyed.
- More often, the government awarded titles to local gods.
- Local supporters petitioned for recognition of gods credited with miracles.

**Presenter-note guidance**

State involvement did not consist only of suppressing religious practice. Recognition could place a local cult within official hierarchies, while worshippers sought prestige for their gods through petitions. The chapter gives titles such as king, duke, or lord. Use the last twenty seconds to reconnect the lecture’s settings: educated officials, household relationships, and religious life all involved claims about authority, obligation, and security. Preview that Lecture 4 examines a systematic philosophical account of moral cultivation; do not begin teaching it before the final activity.

**Student-facing task**

**Five-minute routine**

2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; check for both a conception of the spirit realm and examples of interaction.

## Instructor-only activity response criteria

### Activity 1 | Q6

A satisfactory response should explain Wang’s effort to strengthen government while supporting farming households, with an example such as low-cost loans, revised service obligations, taxation, or militia organization. It should identify opposition through affected interests, philosophical differences, or personal and career competition. It should explain escalation through moral accusations, removal from influential posts, and reversals when court backing changed. Strong responses distinguish a policy’s intended benefit from demonstrated success.

**Useful debrief corrections:** Sharing an examination background did not make officials politically unanimous. Opposition cannot be reduced to a single north–south division. Policies changed through imperial and regency decisions as well as officials’ arguments.

### Activity 2 | Q11

A satisfactory response should identify a populated spirit realm including gods, ancestors, or troublesome ghosts, and explain a feature such as emotional motives or bureaucratic hierarchy. It should provide two practices, such as divination, offerings, prayer, consultation with specialists, or exorcism. It may use the multi-specialist ghost story to explain overlapping traditions. Strong responses attribute accounts to Hong Mai or the chapter and use them as evidence of religious ideas and actions rather than proof of supernatural events.

**Useful debrief corrections:** The religious landscape included local practices as well as Buddhist and Daoist institutions. People could seek help across traditions. Hong Mai’s official-family background matters when interpreting his accounts of ordinary people.

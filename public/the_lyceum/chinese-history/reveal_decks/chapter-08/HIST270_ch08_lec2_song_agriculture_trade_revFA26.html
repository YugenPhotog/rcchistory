Lecture 1 ended with a puzzle. Song lost its northern capital in 1127, yet it survived for another century and a half. This lecture asks: How could Song lose its northern capital and still sustain a prosperous economy?

The answer is not a list of inventions. Watch how each change, from rice fields to ships to paper money, solved a problem and made the next change possible.

From Lecture 1 · after 1127

Song's Economic Strength Survived the Loss of Its Northern Court

Jin captured the northern court at Kaifeng in 1127.
Song continued in the south, eventually based at Hangzhou.
China's economic center had already shifted toward the Yangzi region.
Explaining Song's survival requires looking beyond the imperial capital.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 132–133, 139; Map 8.2, p. 139.

This slide recalls only the political facts you need for today's question.

Read the map. By about 1200, Kaifeng lies inside Jin territory, and the Song court sits at Hangzhou, south of the Yangzi River.

Two different "centers": a capital is where the court lives. An economic center is where production and exchange are concentrated. In Song China these were not the same place for most of the Northern Song. The court was at Kaifeng in the north, while the economy's center of gravity was already moving south toward the Yangzi.

Why this matters: the court's move in 1127 followed an economic shift that was already underway. It did not create southern prosperity from nothing. The full explanation of Southern Song survival comes on slide 17, once you have seen the processes underneath it.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 132.

The economic story begins with food.

Read the numbers carefully. In 742, under the Tang, China's population was about 50 million, a little lower than it had been in 2 C.E. By 1102 the Song population had reached about 100 million. China was certainly the largest country in the world, and its population exceeded that of all of Europe. These two estimates describe change across more than three centuries, from Tang into Song. They are not a sudden doubling within one reign, and the chapter gives only these two points, not a year-by-year series.

How it connects: the textbook links the growth to the expansion of rice cultivation in central and south China, which steadily increased the food supply. More food supported more people. Denser populations, in turn, created more opportunities to buy and sell.

Why this matters: treat this as the start of an interacting process, not a single cause. Each part of the economy fed the others, as the next slides show.

Geography · the Yangzi region

Southern Agriculture and Waterways Shifted the Economic Center

Rice could yield more calories per unit of land than wheat or millet.
Milder southern conditions often permitted two successive crops on the same land.
Rivers and streams reduced transport costs.
Productive farming and accessible shipping favored the Yangzi region.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 132–133.

Why did the economic center move south? The textbook gives three geographic reasons.

Breaking it down: first, rice, which grew in the south, provides more calories per unit of land than wheat or millet, so it supports denser settlement. Second, the south's milder temperatures often allowed two crops to be grown on the same plot, one after the other. Be careful here: the chapter describes successive cropping, not every farm producing two rice harvests every year. Third, the south's many rivers and streams made shipping easy, which lowered the cost of transportation.

How it connects: cheap transport is the key economic point. When moving goods costs less, a household can sell its products elsewhere and buy what it does not produce. That made regional specialization practical.

Read the map. Find the Yangzi River, and Hangzhou near the coast to its southeast. Compare their location with Kaifeng farther north.

The shift was gradual and uneven. Northern agriculture and industry did not disappear.

Why this matters: this is the first part of the answer to Review Question 8. The south was already economically important before the court arrived.

The countryside · market participation

Farmers Increasingly Produced for Markets

Selling surplus

Some farmers sold surpluses and purchased goods such as tea, oil, or charcoal.

Growing for sale

Others grew commercial products such as tea, cotton, sugar, or oranges.

Buying food

Some households purchased grain instead of growing all their own food.

Commercialization meant greater participation in buying and selling.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 132.

Here the countryside joins the market.

Textbook anchor: "Agricultural prosperity and denser settlement patterns fostered commercialization of the economy." (p. 132)

Breaking it down: in many regions, farmers found that producing for the market made a better life possible, so they no longer aimed at self-sufficiency. Peasants in densely populated areas with many markets sold their surpluses and bought charcoal, tea, oil, and wine. In many places, farmers bought grain and grew commercial crops such as sugar, oranges, cotton, silk, and tea.

An illustration (not a real person): imagine a household on a hillside that grows tea. It sells the tea and uses the money to buy rice grown in a lowland valley. Neither household has to produce everything it needs. That is the process the chapter describes.

Avoid two exaggerations. Not every rural household was self-sufficient before the Song, and not every Song farmer became a specialized commercial producer. The chapter describes increasing market participation, especially in densely settled regions.

Why this matters: commercialization is the heart of Review Question 3's "economic revolution."

Consumers
Transport linked rural production with urban consumption.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 132–133.

Specialized farming only works if goods can reach buyers.

How it connects: the relationship ran in both directions. Commercial production increased the need to move goods, which stimulated inland and coastal shipping and created jobs for shipbuilders and sailors. At the same time, available shipping made specialization more practical. Markets could not keep growing if goods could not reach buyers at a reasonable cost.

A witness: Marco Polo, the Venetian merchant who described his visit to China in the late thirteenth century, was astounded by the boat traffic on the Yangzi. He claimed to have seen no fewer than fifteen thousand vessels docked at a single city on the river. Treat that number as testimony to how enormous the traffic seemed to him, not as a verified count.

Why this matters: producers, transport workers, and consumers formed one connected system. Remove the transport link, and specialization stalls.

Money and business · late Tang to Song

Growing Trade Encouraged New Money and Business Organizations

Moving value

  • More buying and selling increased demand for currency.
  • Deposit receipts offered an alternative to carrying heavy copper coins.
  • Paper money developed through merchant practices and government involvement.

Organizing exchange

  • Partnerships and merchant guilds organized increasingly specialized trade.
×

Deposit receipt

A document representing money or goods left with a business (a "deposit shop"). Merchants traded the receipts instead of hauling heavy coins.

×

Guild

An association of merchants in a particular line of trade. In large Song cities, guilds were organized by the type of product sold.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 132.

As more goods changed hands, the demand for money grew enormously.

Moving value: the late Tang government had stopped using bolts of silk as a supplementary currency, which increased the demand for copper coins. By 1085 coin output had increased tenfold since Tang times, to more than 6 billion coins a year. Copper coins are heavy, so for large transactions merchants in late Tang times began trading receipts from deposit shops where they had left money or goods. Early Song authorities gave a small set of shops a monopoly on issuing these certificates, and later the government took over the system, producing the world's first government-issued paper money. Notice the sequence: merchant practice first, then government involvement.

Organizing exchange: merchants formed partnerships and joint stock companies, separating owners (shareholders) from managers. In large cities they were organized into guilds by product. Guilds periodically set prices and arranged sales from wholesalers to shop owners, and when the government requisitioned goods or assessed taxes, it dealt with the guild heads.

Do not equate these organizations with modern corporations, and do not picture paper money as a bank balance on a screen. It was a printed certificate of value.

Armies required armor, swords, and other manufactured equipment.
Papermaking expanded to supply books, documents, money, and packaging.
×

Coke

A fuel made from coal and used for high-temperature production such as iron smelting. In Song China, bituminous coke largely replaced charcoal by the end of the eleventh century.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 132.

Commercial growth was matched by industrial growth.

Read the numbers carefully. With advances in metallurgy, iron production reached around 125,000 tons a year in 1078, about six times the output of the year 800.

Fuel and resources: ironmakers first used charcoal, which led to deforestation in parts of north China. By the end of the eleventh century, coke, a fuel made from coal, had largely taken its place. The change shows how industrial growth put pressure on resources.

Military demand: much of the iron went to the army. Armor was mass-produced in small, medium, and large sizes, and high-quality steel was made for swords. Engineers also experimented with gunpowder; in the twelfth-century wars against the Jurchens, defenders of a besieged city used it to propel projectiles. Economic strength and military manufacturing mattered, but, as Lecture 1 showed, they did not guarantee victory.

Paper: papermaking flourished to meet demand for books, documents, money, and wrapping paper. Lecture 3 picks up what printed books did to education.

Urban life · Kaifeng

Commercial Growth Changed City Life

Kaifeng grew around commerce near the northern end of the Grand Canal.
Its urban life differed from Chang'an's planned, separated wards.
The abolition of Kaifeng's curfew in 1063 allowed businesses to remain open at night.
Urban markets supported numerous specialized goods and services.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 132–133.

The quickening economy fueled great cities.

The context: Song broke with precedent and chose neither Chang'an nor Luoyang as its capital. It chose Kaifeng, a city that had prospered because of its location near the northern end of the Grand Canal. Recall Tang Chang'an from Chapter 5: a planned grid with walled wards. Kaifeng instead grew over time as its economy developed, without the clearly separated wards, so officials were in frequent contact with ordinary residents. That does not mean Kaifeng had no planning or regulation. The curfew was abolished in 1063, and from then on many businesses in the entertainment quarters stayed open all night.

Look at the painting. Song court painters sometimes portrayed ordinary people. Here a woman and several children crowd around an itinerant peddler who carries hundreds of items on a shoulder pole. The painting shows how commercial life was portrayed: many small goods, eager customers. It is not a statistical record of what people bought.

Why this matters: the city is a consequence of the wider changes you have just seen: more people, more markets, more goods moving.

Key term: tariff

×

Tariff

A tax on goods crossing a designated trading boundary. The Song government collected tariffs on trade at its supervised border markets.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 130, 133–134.

Remember the treaty payments from Lecture 1? Here is where much of that silver went.

How it connects: the indemnities Song paid gave Liao, Xia, and later Jin the means to buy Song products. Song set up supervised markets along the border to encourage this trade and collected tariffs on it. Song exports normally exceeded imports by a large margin, so much of the silver sent north found its way back into China as payment for Chinese goods.

What moved: going north, in large quantities, went tea, silk, copper coins (widely used as currency outside China), paper and printed books, porcelain, lacquerware, jewelry, grain, and ginger and other spices. Coming back were some of the silver, the horses Song badly needed for its armies, camels and sheep, and goods that had crossed the Silk Road, such as fine Indian and Persian cotton cloth, gems, incense, and perfumes.

Do not overstate it: not every payment returned to Song, and trade did not end conflict. Military rivalry and economic exchange simply coexisted.

The sea routes · Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean

Overseas Commerce Expanded Across the Indian Ocean

Maritime foreign trade exceeded overland foreign trade during Song times.
Sea networks linked China with Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean world.
Song officials encouraged foreign traders to visit Chinese ports.
Major exportPorcelain
Important importsSpices and aromatics

Key term: South Seas

×

South Seas

The maritime world of Southeast Asia, linked to the wider commerce of the Indian Ocean.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 133–134.

For the first time, more of China's foreign trade went by sea than by land.

Breaking it down: the Song government sent missions to Southeast Asian countries to encourage their traders to come to China. Chinese junks were seen throughout the Indian Ocean and began to displace Indian and Arab merchants in the South Seas. Shards of Song porcelain have been found as far away as East Africa. An early twelfth-century writer reported that ceramics were the most common product the ships carried.

A customs officer's view: in 1225 Zhao Rukua, superintendent of customs at the port of Quanzhou, wrote an account of the countries Chinese merchants traded with and their goods: pearls from the Persian Gulf, ivory from Aden, myrrh from Somalia, pepper from Java and Sumatra, cotton from India. His account shows official interest in these societies; it does not show that he visited them all. Likewise, a product's place of origin does not prove a Chinese ship sailed there; goods passed through interconnected markets.

Song expanded older ocean networks. It did not invent ocean trade.

Bulkhead

A wall that divides a ship's interior into compartments. Watertight bulkheads keep water that enters one compartment from flooding the whole ship.

×

Sounding line

A weighted line lowered into the water to measure its depth.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 134.

What made Song ships able to carry so much trade? Read the table as feature → what it did.

Textbook anchor: "Chinese junks were larger than the ships of most of their competitors, such as the Indians and Arabs, and had many technical advances, including waterproofing with tung oil, watertight bulkheads, sounding lines to determine depth, and stern-mounted rudders for improved steering." (p. 134)

Breaking it down: bulkheads divide a ship into compartments. A sounding line measures water depth. Some ships used both oars and sails and could hold several hundred men.

The compass: people had known for centuries that a magnetic needle points north. What Song added was making it practical at sea: the needle was made smaller and attached to a fixed stem instead of floating in water, and sometimes placed in a small protective case with a glass top. The first reports of a compass used this way date to 1119. So the Song achievement was improved use of the compass, not the discovery of magnetism.

Why this matters: these features made long voyages more practical, but none of them removed storms, shipwreck, or pirates.

Money at sea · profit and risk

Investors Pursued Profits While Spreading Maritime Risk

Incentives

  • Demand for traded goods offered opportunities for substantial profits.
  • Large cargoes helped cover the expenses of long-distance trade.

Risks

  • Pirates, shipwreck, and port payments made voyages costly and risky.
Spreading the risk: investors spread their money among several ships; ships had multiple investors.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 134.

Technology explains how merchants could sail. Incentives explain why they did.

Breaking it down: an early twelfth-century writer gave two reasons ships had to be large and carry several hundred sailors. First, they had to be ready to fight off pirates. Second, they needed high volume so there would still be a profit after giving substantial "gifts" to the authorities at every port.

Spreading risk: much money could be made, but the risks were great, so investors usually divided their money among many ships, and each ship had many investors. An illustration (not a documented case): if you put all your money in one ship and it sinks, you lose everything; if you spread it across five ships and one sinks, you still have most of your stake.

A witness: one observer worried that eagerness to invest in overseas trade was draining copper cash from the country. He wrote that coastal people gave merchants money to buy foreign goods and "regularly make profits of several hundred per cent." Read that as evidence of what people expected and believed, not as a guaranteed return for every investor.

Why this matters: Review Question 4 asks about economic incentives as well as technology.

Check yourself: Why would an investor prefer small shares in several ships to owning one ship outright?

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 134.

Written sources tell us what people said about trade. A shipwreck shows us what a ship actually carried.

The evidence: in 1973 a Song ship that had sunk in 1277 was excavated off the south China coast. It was 78 feet long and 29 feet wide and had twelve bulkheads, the watertight compartments from slide 13. Inside were the luxury objects Song imported: more than 5,000 pounds of fragrant wood from Southeast Asia, pepper, betel nut, cowries, tortoiseshell, cinnabar, and ambergris from Somalia.

Read the evidence critically. The wreck dates to the very end of the Song, so it is not evidence about Northern Song alone. Cargo from Somalia does not prove the ship sailed to Somalia; goods changed hands through connected markets. And one wreck cannot tell us the exact mix of all maritime trade.

Why this matters: the wreck brings together the ship design, the demand, and the traded goods you have just studied.

Southern Song · Hangzhou

Hangzhou Became a Major Center of Commerce and Urban Services

Trade

Hangzhou's position at the southern end of the Grand Canal favored trade.

The city grew to approximately one million or more residents.

Consumers

Restaurants, tea houses, entertainment, and catering served urban consumers.

City problems

Fire stations, clinics, and relief institutions addressed problems of city life.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 139–140.

The Southern Song capital became one of the great cities of the world.

Breaking it down: Hangzhou grew to 1 million or more residents. At the southern end of the Grand Canal, it was a natural center for trade. Its entertainment quarters had fortunetellers, acrobats, puppeteers, storytellers, tea houses, and restaurants. Catering companies supplied food, tents, tables, chairs, and decorations for banquets. Better-off residents formed clubs; a 1235 text lists a West Lake Poetry Club, a Buddhist Tea Society, an Anglers' Club, an Antique Collectors' Club, and more.

Government services: to fight fire, the government stationed two thousand soldiers at fourteen fire stations in the city. Poverty was worse in crowded cities than in the countryside, so the government distributed alms and ran public clinics, old-age homes, and paupers' graveyards. City growth created responsibilities as well as opportunities.

A limit to the picture: prosperity was uneven. Brokers offered girls and young women for purchase or hire as maids, concubines, singers, or prostitutes. Lecture 3 takes up gender and family life more fully.

Inflation

Now you can answer the question this lecture opened with.

Textbook anchor: "The government still had to devote a large part of its revenues to defense, but it was able to raise much of its revenue through taxes on commerce." (p. 139)

The full answer to Review Question 8, in three parts:

1. Geography and production. Because the economic center of the country had already shifted south, losing the north did not ruin the Song economy. The south's productive rice agriculture and cheap water transport were already in place.

2. People and commerce. Sixty percent of the population remained under Song control, along with much of the most productive farmland and active urban and maritime markets.

3. Revenue and its limits. The government still spent heavily on defense, but it raised much of its revenue through commercial taxes. Over time, however, its monetary policies produced rampant inflation. The chapter does not explain every mechanism, so do not conclude that paper money by itself causes inflation.

Put simply: the dynasty survived politically because its court reestablished itself in the south (Lecture 1), and economically because that court kept a productive, commercially connected region. Prosperity did not end Song's military vulnerability.

# HIST 270 | Chapter 8 | Lecture 2
# Agriculture and Trade Sustained Song Prosperity

**Format:** Detailed slide outline for a future Reveal.js deck  
**Duration:** 35 minutes, including two five-minute classroom activities  
**Audience:** First-year community college students  
**Assigned source:** Ebrey, *A History of China*, Chapter 8, especially pp. 132–134 and 139–140  
**Sequence:** Lecture 2 of five; follows “Song China Shared Power with Strong Northern States” and precedes “Education, Reform, and Religion Shaped Song Social Life”

## Historical argument

Song prosperity grew from the interaction of productive agriculture, denser settlement, market-oriented production, inexpensive water transport, expanding industry, and international trade. These changes shifted China’s economic center south before the court lost northern China in 1127. Southern Song therefore retained a strong productive and commercial base, even while military expenses and monetary problems continued.

**Framing question:** How could Song lose its northern capital and still sustain a prosperous economy?

The lecture answers this question by explaining economic change before returning to Southern Song survival. It is not a catalogue of inventions: each technology or institution should be connected to the problem it helped people solve.

## Learning targets

By the end of the lecture, students should be able to:

- Explain connections among rice cultivation, population growth, commercialization, and urban growth.
- Explain why China’s economic center shifted toward the Yangzi River region.
- Describe how increased exchange encouraged new forms of money and business organization.
- Connect maritime technologies and economic incentives to the expansion of overseas trade.
- Use the excavated ship and its cargo as evidence of trade connections.
- Explain how agriculture, trade, and commercial taxes sustained Southern Song after 1127.

## Detailed slide outline

### Slide 1 | Agriculture and Trade Sustained Song Prosperity

**Time:** 0:00–0:30  
**Format:** Title slide with a verified river-commerce image or a simple typographic treatment

**Student-facing text**

- HIST 270 | Chapter 8 | Lecture 2
- Farms, markets, cities, and maritime trade
- Framing line: “The economic transformation that sustained a politically vulnerable dynasty.”

No presenter notes are needed for the title slide. Introduce the framing question orally and move to the connection with Lecture 1.

### Slide 2 | Song’s Economic Strength Survived the Loss of Its Northern Court

**Time:** 0:30–1:30  
**Format:** Brief political recap beside a regional map  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 132–133, 139

**Student-facing bullets**

- Jin captured the northern court at Kaifeng in 1127.
- Song continued in the south, eventually based at Hangzhou.
- China’s economic center had already shifted toward the Yangzi region.
- Explaining Song’s survival requires looking beyond the imperial capital.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Recall only the political facts needed for today’s question. The court’s move followed an economic shift already underway; it did not create southern prosperity from nothing. Distinguish a capital, where the court resides, from an economic center, where productive activity and exchange are concentrated. Save the completed survival explanation for slide 17, after students have learned the underlying processes.

### Slide 3 | Expanding Rice Cultivation Helped Support Population Growth

**Time:** 1:30–3:00  
**Format:** Two population estimates plus three causal bullets  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 132

**Student-facing bullets**

- Rice cultivation expanded in central and southern China.
- Increased food supplies helped support a growing population.
- Population rose from about 50 million in 742 to about 100 million in 1102.
- More densely settled regions supported larger markets.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The estimates describe a change across several centuries, from Tang into Song, not a sudden doubling within one reign. More food supported more people, while denser populations also increased opportunities for exchange. Use this as the beginning of an interacting process rather than a single-cause explanation. The assigned chapter does not identify a particular introduced rice variety here; do not make an unsupported variety or introduction date essential to the lecture.

### Slide 4 | Southern Agriculture and Waterways Shifted the Economic Center

**Time:** 3:00–4:30  
**Format:** Yangzi-region map with three geographical explanations  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 132–133

**Student-facing bullets**

- Rice could yield more calories per unit of land than wheat or millet.
- Milder southern conditions often permitted two successive crops on the same land.
- Rivers and streams reduced transport costs.
- Productive farming and accessible shipping favored the Yangzi region.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The chapter refers to successive cropping; do not imply that every farm produced two rice harvests each year. Explain why geography mattered economically: lower transport costs made it easier to sell products elsewhere and buy what a household did not produce. The economic shift was gradual and uneven, not the disappearance of northern agriculture or industry. This slide begins the geographical explanation needed for Q8.

### Slide 5 | Farmers Increasingly Produced for Markets

**Time:** 4:30–6:00  
**Format:** Household-production comparison with a short definition  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 132

**Student-facing bullets**

- Some farmers sold surpluses and purchased goods such as tea, oil, or charcoal.
- Others grew commercial products such as tea, cotton, sugar, or oranges.
- Some households purchased grain instead of growing all their own food.
- Commercialization meant greater participation in buying and selling.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Avoid portraying all rural households as self-sufficient before Song or all Song farmers as specialized commercial producers. The chapter describes increasing market participation in many regions, especially densely settled ones. Use a simple teaching illustration: a household selling tea can purchase grain grown elsewhere. Label this as an illustration of the chapter’s process, not a documented individual farmer’s biography.

### Slide 6 | Shipping Connected Specialized Producers and Consumers

**Time:** 6:00–7:30  
**Format:** A compact production–transport–market diagram  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 132–133

**Student-facing bullets**

- Inland and coastal shipping moved commercial crops and other goods.
- Shipping demand created work for shipbuilders and sailors.
- Regional specialization depended on connections to buyers.
- Transport linked rural production with urban consumption.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Explain the relationship in both directions: commercial production increased demand for shipping, while available shipping made commercial specialization more practical. Markets could not expand indefinitely if goods could not reach buyers economically. Marco Polo’s striking vessel totals can be mentioned as testimony to perceived scale, but should not be treated as an independently verified count. The main point is the connection among producers, transport workers, and consumers.

### Slide 7 | Growing Trade Encouraged New Money and Business Organizations

**Time:** 7:30–9:00  
**Format:** Two short panels: “Moving value” and “Organizing exchange”  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 132

**Student-facing bullets**

- More buying and selling increased demand for currency.
- Deposit receipts offered an alternative to carrying heavy copper coins.
- Paper money developed through merchant practices and government involvement.
- Partnerships and merchant guilds organized increasingly specialized trade.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define a deposit receipt as a document representing value left with a business, and a guild as an association of merchants in a particular line of trade. The chapter describes the evolution from receipts to government paper currency; the outline does not add an exact issuance date. Merchant organizations coordinated transactions and gave government officials identifiable contacts for taxation and procurement. Do not equate these organizations automatically with modern corporations or present paper money as an electronic bank balance.

### Slide 8 | Industrial Growth Supplied Expanding Civilian and Military Demand

**Time:** 9:00–10:30  
**Format:** Iron-production callout beside two short industry examples  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 132

**Student-facing bullets**

- Iron production reached about 125,000 tons annually in 1078, according to the chapter.
- Coke increasingly replaced charcoal as a fuel for iron production.
- Armies required armor, swords, and other manufactured equipment.
- Papermaking expanded to supply books, documents, money, and packaging.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define coke as a fuel made from coal and used in high-temperature production. Charcoal consumption contributed to deforestation in parts of the north; the change of fuel illustrates a connection between industrial growth and resource pressures. Briefly mention the chapter’s gunpowder weapons in the wars against Jin, without claiming that military innovation ensured victory. Economic strength and military manufacture mattered, but did not erase the strategic difficulties discussed in Lecture 1. Reserve printing’s effects on education for Lecture 3.

### Slide 9 | Commercial Growth Changed City Life

**Time:** 10:30–12:00  
**Format:** City comparison with the chapter’s peddler image as an optional supporting visual  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 132–133

**Student-facing bullets**

- Kaifeng grew around commerce near the northern end of the Grand Canal.
- Its urban life differed from Chang’an’s planned, separated wards.
- The abolition of Kaifeng’s curfew in 1063 allowed businesses to remain open at night.
- Urban markets supported numerous specialized goods and services.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Recall Chang’an only long enough to clarify the contrast in urban organization. Do not describe Kaifeng as having no planning or regulation. If using the peddler illustration on p. 133, draw attention to the many small goods and the represented customers; it is evidence of how commercial life was portrayed, not a statistical census of purchasing behavior. The city is one consequence of the wider economic changes students will explain in the next activity.

### Slide 10 | Pause & Process: Explain the Economic Revolution

**Time:** 12:00–17:00  
**Format:** Classroom activity slide  
**Source:** Reading guide, Review Question 3; textbook, pp. 132–133

**Original review question**

> What were the primary drivers of the medieval Chinese economic revolution during the Song period, and how did these changes affect the demographic and commercial landscape?

**Student-facing task**

- Write a brief response of 3–5 sentences.
- Connect at least three developments from the lecture rather than listing them.
- Include one effect on population or settlement and one effect on commerce.
- Use one concrete example, such as rice cultivation, shipping, commercial crops, paper money, or city growth.

**Five-minute routine**

1. **Two minutes:** Write individually from lecture evidence.
2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; underline the words that explain a connection, such as “because” or “therefore.”
3. **One minute:** Share a connection and revise the explanation if necessary.

Do not require students to recopy the reading guide’s evidence sentence. Keep response criteria in the instructor appendix. No presenter notes are needed for this activity.

### Slide 11 | Trade Connected Song with Its Military Rivals

**Time:** 17:00–18:30  
**Format:** Border-market exchange diagram  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 130, 133–134

**Student-facing bullets**

- Treaty payments helped neighboring states purchase Song goods.
- Supervised border markets connected rival governments and populations.
- Song exported goods including tea, silk, porcelain, and printed books.
- Imports included horses, other animals, and goods arriving through Central Asia.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Return briefly to the peace payments from Lecture 1. Some silver flowed back through purchases of Song products, while Song also collected tariffs on border trade. A tariff is a tax on goods crossing a designated trading boundary. Military rivalry and economic exchange could coexist. Do not claim that every payment returned to Song, or that trade eliminated conflict; the chapter describes a substantial flow of commerce within a politically divided region.

### Slide 12 | Overseas Commerce Expanded Across the Indian Ocean

**Time:** 18:30–20:00  
**Format:** Regional trade map with a small exports/imports key  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 133–134

**Student-facing bullets**

- Maritime foreign trade exceeded overland foreign trade during Song times.
- Sea networks linked China with Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean world.
- Porcelain was a major export; spices and aromatics were important imports.
- Song officials encouraged foreign traders to visit Chinese ports.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Define the South Seas in the review question as the maritime world of Southeast Asia, linked to wider Indian Ocean commerce. Overseas trade expanded older networks; Song did not invent ocean trade. Distinguish goods traveling through interconnected markets from evidence that one Chinese ship visited every place where a product originated. Zhao Rukua’s 1225 account shows official interest in trading societies and their products; his account does not establish that he personally visited every place described.

### Slide 13 | Shipbuilding and Navigation Supported Maritime Expansion

**Time:** 20:00–22:00  
**Format:** Clearly labeled schematic or technology-to-function table  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 134

**Student-facing comparison**

| Feature | Contribution to seafaring |
|---|---|
| Large hulls | Greater carrying capacity |
| Waterproofing and watertight bulkheads | Protection against water intrusion |
| Stern-mounted rudders | Improved steering |
| Sounding lines and improved compasses | Depth measurement and navigation |

**Presenter-note guidance**

Bulkheads divide a ship’s interior into compartments. A sounding line measures water depth, while a compass supplies directional information. Explain functions in plain language rather than asking students to memorize unfamiliar engineering terms. The chapter describes improvements that made magnetic compasses more suitable for use at sea; it does not present magnetism itself as a Song discovery. These features supported trade, but none removed storms, shipwreck, or piracy. Any diagram should be an explanatory schematic, not labeled as a reconstruction of one excavated ship unless verified.

### Slide 14 | Investors Pursued Profits While Spreading Maritime Risk

**Time:** 22:00–23:30  
**Format:** Risk-and-incentive comparison  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 134

**Student-facing bullets**

- Demand for traded goods offered opportunities for substantial profits.
- Pirates, shipwreck, and port payments made voyages costly and risky.
- Large cargoes helped cover the expenses of long-distance trade.
- Investors spread their money among several ships; ships had multiple investors.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Technology alone does not explain why merchants sailed. Demand and prospective profit encouraged voyages, while pooled investment and investments in several ships spread exposure to losses. Explain this through a simple illustrative case: losing one ship need not destroy an investor’s entire investment if it is distributed among several voyages. Identify that case as a teaching illustration. Contemporary reports of very high profits are evidence of expectations and perceptions, not a guaranteed return for every investor.

### Slide 15 | A Shipwreck Reveals the Goods Carried by Maritime Trade

**Time:** 23:30–25:00  
**Format:** Material-evidence panel with three cargo examples  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 134

**Student-facing bullets**

- A ship wrecked in 1277 was excavated off China’s southern coast in 1973.
- Its hull contained twelve bulkheads.
- Cargo included fragrant wood, pepper, betel nut, and other valuable goods.
- The wreck provides physical evidence of maritime technology and imported commodities.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The wreck belongs to the late Song-era transition and should not be treated as evidence confined to Northern Song. The chapter describes fragrant wood from Southeast Asia and ambergris from Somalia, illustrating the breadth of the exchange network. A cargo’s geographical origins do not prove the ship sailed directly to each origin. One wreck also cannot establish the exact composition of all maritime trade. Use the archaeological evidence to reinforce the already-taught relationship between ship design, demand, and traded goods; do not add a third activity.

### Slide 16 | Hangzhou Became a Major Center of Commerce and Urban Services

**Time:** 25:00–27:00  
**Format:** City-life panel with services grouped into three categories  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 139–140

**Student-facing bullets**

- Hangzhou’s position at the southern end of the Grand Canal favored trade.
- The city grew to approximately one million or more residents.
- Restaurants, tea houses, entertainment, and catering served urban consumers.
- Fire stations, clinics, and relief institutions addressed problems of city life.

**Presenter-note guidance**

The chapter’s entertainment quarters, clubs, and specialist services make the scale of urban life concrete. Prosperity was uneven: poverty remained a serious problem, and some markets included the purchase or hiring of women as servants, performers, concubines, or prostitutes. Mention this as a limit to an uncomplicated picture of urban flourishing; broader gender and family analysis belongs to Lecture 3. Government services show that city growth created administrative responsibilities as well as commercial opportunities.

### Slide 17 | Southern Song Retained Productive Land and Taxable Commerce

**Time:** 27:00–29:00  
**Format:** Three foundations of survival with a fiscal-pressure callout  
**Source:** Textbook, p. 139  
**Review alignment:** Q8

**Student-facing bullets**

- The economic center had shifted south before the loss of northern China.
- Song retained about 60 percent of the population and much productive agricultural land.
- Commercial taxes supplied substantial government revenue.
- Defense remained expensive, and monetary policies eventually produced severe inflation.

**Callout:** “A reduced territory could still contain a strong economic base.”

**Presenter-note guidance**

Give the full Q8 explanation explicitly here. The dynasty survived politically because its court reestablished itself, and it survived economically because that court retained a productive and commercially connected region. Explain that commercial taxes are levies on exchange and business activity. Define inflation as a sustained rise in prices that reduces money’s purchasing power. Do not say that prosperity ended military vulnerability or that paper money inherently causes inflation; the chapter connects later monetary policies to inflation without explaining every mechanism.

### Slide 18 | Connected Economic Changes Sustained Song Prosperity

**Time:** 29:00–30:00  
**Format:** Three synthesis callouts  
**Source:** Textbook, pp. 132–134, 139–140

**Student-facing bullets**

- Agricultural productivity, settlement, and markets expanded together.
- Transport, money, industry, and investment connected producers and consumers.
- Southern resources and commerce sustained Song after the loss of the north.

**Presenter-note guidance**

Use this minute to reconnect the evidence to the lecture’s argument, without reciting a complete answer to Q4. Economic revolution means a substantial transformation over time, not a single event or an overnight industrial revolution. Give the next-lecture bridge orally: expanding production of books and the resources available for education also helped reshape the scholar-official class. Then begin the final activity on time.

### Slide 19 | Pause & Process: Explain Why Maritime Trade Expanded

**Format:** Final classroom activity slide  
**Source:** Reading guide, Review Question 4; textbook, p. 134

> Evaluate the expansion of international maritime trade during the Song Dynasty. What technological innovations and economic incentives allowed Song Chinese merchants to thrive in the South Seas?

**Student-facing task**

- Write a response of 3–5 sentences.
- Explain how one shipbuilding or navigation feature helped trade.
- Explain one economic incentive and one way merchants or investors managed risk.
- Support the explanation with an example of goods or a trading connection.

**Five-minute routine**

1. **Two minutes:** Write individually using lecture evidence.
2. **Two minutes:** Compare with a partner; check that the response explains both technology and economic motivation.
3. **One minute:** Share an example and make a final revision.

## Instructor-only activity response criteria

### Activity 1 | Q3

A satisfactory response should connect expanding rice cultivation and increased food supply with population growth or denser settlement. It should explain how market participation, specialization, or shipping increased commercial exchange. Money, industry, or city growth can provide an additional consequence or example. Strong responses show interaction among developments rather than presenting an unconnected list of inventions.

**Useful debrief corrections:** Population growth occurred over centuries. Commercial farming did not replace all subsistence production. The economic shift south was underway before the court relocated after 1127.

### Activity 2 | Q4

A satisfactory response should link a specific feature to its function: for example, rudders improved steering, compasses helped navigation, or watertight compartments limited water intrusion. It should identify demand and expected profit as incentives, with pooled or distributed investment as a response to risk. A cargo example such as porcelain, pepper, or fragrant wood should illustrate the trading network. Strong responses distinguish technological capacity, reasons to trade, and financial arrangements that helped merchants undertake risky voyages.

**Useful debrief corrections:** Improved compass use was not the discovery of magnetism. Overseas trade was not new to Song. Cargo origins do not establish a ship’s complete itinerary. Reported large profits were not guaranteed returns.

## Q8 teaching checkpoint

By slide 17, students have received all three parts of the answer:

1. **Geography and production:** The south’s productive agriculture and transport networks had already made it economically important.
2. **People and commerce:** Southern Song retained a majority of the population, productive land, and active urban and maritime markets.
3. **Government revenue and limits:** Commercial taxation helped finance the state, while military spending and inflation remained problems.

Do not replace this explanation with a statement that Hangzhou was beautiful or wealthy. Q8 asks how continued economic strength was possible.

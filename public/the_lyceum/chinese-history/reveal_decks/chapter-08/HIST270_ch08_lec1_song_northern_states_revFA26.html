Song China Shared Power with Strong Northern States

Military rivals, negotiated peace, and a dynasty that survived the loss of its northern capital.

Northern Song · Southern Song

Welcome to Chapter 8. This lecture is built around one framing question: How could a powerful Chinese dynasty survive without controlling all of the territory it claimed?

Keep that question in mind as you meet Song's neighbors: the Liao and Xia states that already existed when Song was founded, and the Jin state that rose later and took northern China.

This slide sets the stage. Song did not dominate East Asia the way the Tang had, and it did not even rule every area where Chinese speakers lived.

Read the map. Map 8.1 shows the region around 1050. Song (shaded) holds the center and south, with its capital at Kaifeng. Liao stretches across the north and includes the Beijing region. Xia sits in the northwest. Northeast of Liao you can see the label "Jurchens"; remember it, because they will matter later in this lecture. Jin does not appear on this map because in 1050 it did not yet exist.

Keep the names straight. Khitan is the people and Liao is their dynasty. Tangut is the people and Xia is their dynasty. "Northern Song" means the phase of the Song Dynasty when its court was at Kaifeng (960–1127).

Why this matters: "Shared power" in this lecture's title means several states coexisted and negotiated with one another. It does not mean they ran one government together, or that they had equal resources.

In the south, about ten smaller kingdoms ruled at the same time.
Whoever commanded the troops could seize the throne.
×

Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms

The name historians give to 907–960. "Five Dynasties" means the five regimes that followed one another in the north (Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, Later Zhou). "Ten Kingdoms" means the regional states, mostly in the south, that existed alongside them.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130. Map: Wikimedia Commons, "Five Dynasties Ten Kingdoms 923 CE.png," CC BY 3.0.

Picture north China in the year 907. The Tang dynasty, which had lasted almost three hundred years, is gone. And what replaces it is not one new dynasty but a revolving door. In the next fifty-three years, five dynasties rule the north, one after another. Later Liang, Later Tang, Later Jin, Later Han, Later Zhou. You do not need to memorize the names. What you need to notice is who founded each one: a general. Every time, a commander with loyal troops pushed out the ruler and declared himself emperor. Meanwhile, in the south, about ten smaller kingdoms ran their own affairs, some of them rich and peaceful. Historians call this the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period. So put yourself in the place of any ruler in the north. Your biggest danger is not a foreign army. It is your own general. Hold on to that idea, because the man who founds Song was exactly that kind of general.

The Chenqiao mutiny · 960

Soldiers Wrapped Their General in a Yellow Robe and Made Him Emperor

In 960 the Later Zhou throne passed to a seven-year-old boy.
At Chenqiao, the army draped a yellow imperial robe on its commander, Zhao Kuangyin.
He became Taizu; he and his brother Taizong reunited the south by 979.
They expanded the civil service exams to staff the new state with scholars.

Pierian Spring: the "cup of wine" banquet →

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130; Song shi (History of Song, 1345), Taizu annals.

Now meet that general. It is early in 960. The Later Zhou emperor has died, and his heir is a boy of seven. Zhao Kuangyin, commander of the palace army, marches north with his troops and camps at a post station called Chenqiao, not far from the capital, Kaifeng. That night, so the official story goes, his officers burst into his tent and throw a yellow robe over his shoulders. Yellow was the emperor's color. They shout that he is now the Son of Heaven. Zhao, the record insists, was reluctant. Be a little skeptical here: this story was written by Song's own historians, and a founder who had to be forced onto the throne looks much better than one who grabbed it. Either way, he becomes Emperor Taizu. Over the next two decades, he and his younger brother Taizong conquer the southern kingdoms one by one and finish reunifying China by 979. And they expand the civil service examinations, so that educated men, not soldiers, fill the government. [Brief · banquet story in Pierian Spring]

Key term: warlord

×

Warlord

A commander whose military base lets him exercise political power with limited central control. After the Tang, rule by such commanders had fragmented China.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130.

Song began with a military takeover. Zhao Kuangyin was a general whose troops put him on the throne when their previous ruler was succeeded by a child. As emperor he is known as Taizu (r. 960–976).

The context: after the Tang, China had suffered a long period of military fragmentation, when commanders with their own armies could make and unmake rulers. Taizu understood that danger better than anyone, because his own rise proved it.

How it worked: the textbook says he "retired or rotated his own generals and assigned civil officials to supervise them, thus subordinating the armed forces to the civil bureaucracy" (p. 130). Notice what this did and did not do. It did not abolish Song's army. It changed who held authority over the army's commanders.

Be careful not to stretch this policy too far. It helped end warlordism inside Song, but it is not, by itself, the explanation for every later Song military defeat.

Why this matters: this is the first half of Review Question 1. Song's internal military problem got an internal, institutional solution.

Check yourself: Why was Taizu's own path to the throne a warning to him once he became emperor?

The northern frontier · Five Dynasties to early Song

Liao Held the Northern Defenses Song Wanted to Recover

Liao had acquired the Sixteen Prefectures during the Five Dynasties period.
The region included mountains, passes, and the line of the Great Wall.
Taizu and Taizong tried unsuccessfully to recover it.
A large population did not guarantee victory over Liao's mounted forces.
×

Sixteen Prefectures

A strip of land in north China, the northern parts of modern Shanxi and Hebei, that had long been considered part of China proper. Liao gained control of it during the Five Dynasties period. A prefecture was an administrative territory.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130.

Curbing his generals solved Taizu's problem at home. It did not solve the problem of the Khitan Liao to the north.

The context: during the Five Dynasties, Liao gained control of a strip of north China, the northern parts of Shanxi and Hebei, which Song called the Sixteen Prefectures. A prefecture was simply an administrative territory. These particular prefectures mattered because they contained the line of the Great Wall and the mountains and passes that had been central to Chinese defense against northerners since before the Han. Whoever held that defensive terrain could affect military access to the plains farther south.

Do not picture the Great Wall here as the modern tourist monument, or as a wall no army could cross. The point is the defensive line of mountains and passes.

Read the map. Beijing sits inside Liao territory, north of Song's frontier and its capital at Kaifeng. The textbook map does not outline the Sixteen Prefectures, but they lay in that borderland.

Taizu and his younger brother Taizong made every effort to reclaim the region. Their claim mixed strategy with the conviction that the land belonged within their realm. Yet although Liao ruled a population tiny by Chinese standards, its horsemen were more than a match for Song armies.

Key term: sinification

×

Sinification

Adopting Chinese cultural practices and institutions. Adoption could be selective: a ruling group could borrow some Chinese methods while keeping its own language, customs, and organization.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 130–131.

This slide answers Review Question 2: how Liao governed two very different populations.

The context: in the early tenth century, Abaoji (d. 926) of the Yelü clan united eight to ten Khitan tribes. He set aside the Khitan custom of tribal councils electing chiefs for limited terms and adopted hereditary succession on the Chinese model, so that his son would succeed him. His successors did not have to erase Khitan practices to use Chinese administrative methods.

The south: Liao governed its Chinese territories by Chinese traditions, with a civil bureaucracy modeled on the Tang, complete with civil service examinations. Its Southern Capital stood at modern Beijing, the beginning of that city's history as a capital, and the south produced most of Liao's tax revenue. It was not a complete copy of Song government, though: counties and even prefectures were granted to imperial relatives and high officials as fiefs under their own jurisdiction.

The north: the northern section was huge but thinly settled. Its government was mobile, with the ruler and his officials moving from place to place with the seasons. A Khitan script was created to keep records.

Textbook anchor: "This dual form of administration allowed the Khitans to maintain their tribal organization and resist sinification." (p. 131)

Why this matters: Liao shows that borrowing Chinese institutions could be selective. The ruling elite became adept in both Khitan and Chinese ways, while ordinary Khitans in the north kept their traditional social and military organization.

Bolt

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130.

This slide gives the second half of Review Question 1: how Song managed Liao once war had failed.

Textbook anchor: "After a Liao invasion of 1004 came within a hundred miles of the capital Kaifeng, the Song settled with Liao, agreeing to pay tribute to Liao in exchange for Liao's maintaining the peace." (p. 130)

Read the sequence carefully. The invasion of 1004 led to the settlement. The chapter does not give a separate formal treaty date, so do not invent one. Each year Song sent Liao 100,000 ounces of silver and 200,000 bolts of silk; a bolt is a length of cloth. In 1042 the sum was raised to 500,000 units.

How to think about it: the payments were a negotiated way to manage a military rival that Song could not defeat. The reading guide calls this strategy "unprecedented." Read that as "a striking new arrangement for Song," not as a claim that no Chinese ruler had ever negotiated with or paid a neighbor before.

Why this matters: the treaty kept Song rule secure, but it also meant accepting that the Sixteen Prefectures stayed in Liao hands. Song accommodated a rival it could not dominate.

The cost debate · early 1000s

Treaty Payments Cost Less Than Maintaining the Army

¾

By 1022, the military consumed about three-quarters of state tax revenues.

2–3%

Peaceful relations with Liao cost no more than about 2–3 percent of annual revenues.

Advocates of peace emphasized expense and stability.
Advocates of war emphasized lost territory and humiliation.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 130.

Was paying Liao a smart bargain or a disgrace? Song officials argued about exactly that.

Read the numbers carefully. During the reigns of the first three Song emperors, the armed forces grew rapidly, to almost 1 million soldiers by 1022. By then the military was consuming three-quarters of tax revenues. By contrast, even counting the expense of exchanging embassies, maintaining peaceful relations with Liao cost no more than 2 or 3 percent of the state's annual revenues. These figures compare the whole military budget with the cost of keeping peace with Liao. They are not a comparison of one campaign with one treaty payment.

The two sides: the pro-peace "accommodationists" could point out that tribute was far cheaper than war. The pro-war "irredentists," who wanted the lost territory back, felt humiliated, and thought it common sense that payments helped Liao and hurt Song.

The chapter adds that much of the silver flowed back into China as Liao bought Chinese goods. Lecture 2 develops that trade story.

Why this matters: the financial case for peace looks strong, but keep the moral and political objection in view. Loss of territory and prestige were real concerns to people at the time.

Check yourself: Which side do you find more persuasive, and what would the other side say back?

The northwest · Xia, 1044

Xia Also Forced Song to Negotiate

Tangut rulers consolidated Xia in the northwest.
Xia controlled important horse-trading connections and developed its own script.
After unsuccessful fighting, Song accepted a treaty with Xia in 1044 and made payments.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 131.

Liao was not Song's only organized neighbor.

The context: the Tanguts spoke a language related to Tibetan. Under pressure from the expanding Tibetan kingdom in Tang times, they moved north and east into northern Shaanxi and Gansu. In 881 the Tang court appointed a Tangut chief as military governor of the region, and the office became essentially hereditary. After the Tibetan and Uighur empires collapsed, the Tanguts gained control of the important horse trade with the Chinese.

Xia becomes a dynasty: under Yuanhao (r. 1032–1048), the Tanguts adopted a script for their language and the dynastic name Xia. When Yuanhao demanded that Song recognize Xia as a sovereign state, Song sent an army. The fighting went poorly for Song, and in 1044 a treaty committed Song to payments to Xia, smaller than Liao's (200,000 units altogether).

Xia's institutions drew on Tang, Song, Liao, Tibetan, and Uighur models, and Buddhism was its state religion. Xia sometimes fought Liao and sometimes made treaties recognizing Liao as the superior party. So Liao and Xia did not have identical relationships with Song or with each other.

Why this matters: Xia reinforces the geography from the opening map. Song faced more than one organized neighbor.

The Jurchens · 1115

The Jurchens Founded Jin Before Northern Song Fell

1115Wanyan Aguda proclaimed the Jin Dynasty.
Jurchen communities lived northeast of Song and challenged Liao authority.
Their economy combined hunting, fishing, animal husbandry, and farming.
Jin's rise changed the balance among Song, Liao, and Xia.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 138.

A new power now enters the story, and the balance among Song, Liao, and Xia starts to change.

Who were the Jurchens? They are not another name for the Khitans of Liao, and they are not the Tanguts or the Mongols. They lived in villages and small walled towns, and their economy combined fishing, hunting, animal husbandry, and some farming. Jurchens living near Chinese, Khitan, or Goryeo cities adopted practices and technologies from those neighbors, which led to a distinction between "civilized" and "wild" Jurchens. Their lands were ideal for raising horses; by the mid-eleventh century they were selling the Khitans about ten thousand horses a year. Horse raising and regional contacts help explain Jurchen military strength, so avoid the stereotype of purely nomadic outsiders.

The rise: the Wanyan clan gradually became dominant among the Jurchens. Under Wanyan Aguda (1068–1123) they began challenging Liao authority, and in 1115 they made their rejection of Liao overlordship explicit by proclaiming their own dynasty, the Jin ("Golden").

Read the map. On the 1050 map the Jurchens appear northeast of Liao, beside the Manchurian Plain.

Why this matters: remember the order of events. Jin existed by 1115, before it conquered northern Song in 1127. The conquest did not create the dynasty.

Diplomacy · early 1100s

Song Allied with Jin to Recover Its Northern Frontier

The goal
Song still wanted to recover the Sixteen Prefectures.
→
The plan
Song and Jin agreed to cooperate against Liao; the proposed division of Liao territory promised Song its disputed frontier.
→
The result
Fighting revealed Song's military vulnerability to its new ally.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 138.

To understand the alliance, start with what Song hoped to gain, not with how it ended.

The context: Song learned what was happening in the northeast through rumors from Chinese defectors from Liao. Emperor Huizong's leading general urged a secret alliance with Jin. After a series of envoys, the two sides agreed to cooperate in defeating Liao and then divide its territory, with Song promised the recovery of the Sixteen Prefectures.

How it connects: the plan followed a recognizable strategic logic. Earlier Song campaigns had failed to win back the Sixteen Prefectures. Using Liao's rising challenger looked like a way to get them at last. Song's rulers were not knowingly inviting their own defeat.

What went wrong: in the process of defeating Liao, Jin discovered that Song was not much of a military threat. Removing Liao did not guarantee that Jin would accept the balance of power Song preferred.

Kaifeng · 1115–1127

Jin's Attack on Kaifeng Ended Northern Song in 1127

1115Aguda proclaims the Jin Dynasty.
Liao fallsAfter defeating Liao, Jin attacked Song.
SiegeKaifeng was besieged; the court made enormous payments.
1127Members of the imperial family, including Huizong, were taken north as captives. The fall of the northern court ended Northern Song.
Northern Song ended; the Song Dynasty continued.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 129, 138–139.

Here the alliance turns into catastrophe for Song.

The context: Emperor Huizong was deeply committed to the cultural side of rulership. He collected paintings, calligraphy, and antiquities on a huge scale and was himself a poet and painter. That is worth knowing, but artistic interests were not the sole cause of defeat. The important sequence is Jin's rise, the anti-Liao alliance, the exposure of Song's weakness, and Jin's attack.

What happened: after defeating Liao, Jin turned on Song. Kaifeng was besieged, and an enormous ransom was paid to escape slaughter. Thousands were taken captive, including Huizong, the imperial clan, craftsmen, and female entertainers. Captivity disrupted the court and removed members of the ruling family.

Keep the terms straight. "Northern Song" names a phase of the Song Dynasty, the years when the court was at Kaifeng. It is not a separate dynasty from Southern Song, which you will meet in a few slides.

Why this matters: 1127 marks the end of Song rule in the north, not the end of the dynasty.

Jin government · after 1127

Jin Adopted Chinese Institutions to Govern Northern China

Inherited

Jin initially continued elements of Liao's dual administration.

Staffed

It employed former Liao officials, both Chinese and Khitan.

Expanded

Governing a much larger Chinese population encouraged wider use of Chinese institutions and officials.

Strengthened

Hereditary succession strengthened rulers in competition with their own nobles.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 138.

Conquering northern China and governing it were two different problems.

The context: Jin went on to establish a stable government in north China and Manchuria. At first it continued Liao's dual government and employed former Liao officials, both Chinese and Khitan. You already know that system from the Liao slide; Jin built on it rather than starting from nothing.

Breaking it down: Jin ruled a much larger Chinese population than Liao had, and it had to distribute Jurchens throughout north China to maintain control. Gradually it adopted more Chinese political institutions and employed more Chinese officials. Jin moved its capital from central Manchuria to Beijing in 1153 and to Kaifeng in 1161.

Why rulers liked it: like other non-Chinese rulers before them, the Jurchen rulers found that Chinese institutions such as hereditary succession were a potent weapon in competition with their own nobles. Chinese institutions could serve Jurchen rulers' goals. Borrowing them did not hand sovereignty to the Chinese officials who operated them.

Why this matters: this is the first half of the second part of Review Question 7: how Jin adapted to ruling a large Chinese population.

Jin rulers · borrowing and identity

Jin Rulers Combined Institutional Borrowing with Jurchen Authority

Institutions adopted

  • Chinese language, customs, and institutions spread among Jurchens.
  • Later rulers claimed the Chinese classics and the “Central Kingdom” as their own.

Authority and identity maintained

  • Some rulers promoted Jurchen-language schools, translations, and examinations.
  • Jin emperors asserted authority over officials, including through corporal punishment.
×

Central Kingdom

The common Chinese term for China. When Jin rulers claimed it, they were saying their state need not be seen as a "border" people's country, but as a legitimate center of civilization.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 138.

Borrowing Chinese ways was contested inside Jin, and it changed over time.

Breaking it down: because they lived surrounded by Chinese, many Jurchens adopted Chinese language, dress, and rituals. Some Jurchen leaders worried about this. One emperor worked to raise the prestige of written Jurchen: he ordered Jurchens to attend Jurchen-language schools, had Chinese texts translated into Jurchen, and created Jurchen-language civil service examinations.

Authority over officials: Jin rulers used a Chinese-style bureaucracy without adopting every Confucian norm about how officials should be treated.

Textbook anchor: "Jin emperors had high officials flogged in open court, a brutal violation of the Confucian dictum that officials are to be treated according to ritual and not subjected to corporal punishments." (p. 138)

A claim to legitimacy: later Jin emperors largely accepted sinification and viewed the Chinese classics as universal texts, not exclusively Chinese ones. In 1191 an emperor even outlawed calling the Jurchens "border people," seeing no reason their country should not be regarded as the Central Kingdom. Jin rulers did not accept the Song view that only Song represented legitimate civilization.

Why this matters: this completes Review Question 7. Jin borrowed institutions and asserted its own authority and identity at the same time.

Check yourself: Give one example of Jin borrowing from Chinese practice and one example of Jin keeping its own authority or identity.

Southern Song · 1127–1141

Song Survived by Reestablishing Its Court in the South

A son of Huizong escaped capture and became Emperor Gaozong in 1127.
The court retreated south under continuing Jin pressure.
Hangzhou became the Southern Song capital.
A peace treaty with Jin in 1141 required further payments of silk and silver.
Northern SongKaifeng
Southern SongHangzhou

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, p. 139; Map 8.2, p. 139.

The Song Dynasty survived the loss of its northern capital. Here is how.

The context: one of Huizong's sons was away from Kaifeng when the Jurchens occupied it. After his father and brothers were taken north, Song forces rallied around him and installed him as emperor: Gaozong (r. 1127–1162). The south had never been held by forces from the steppe, and Gaozong wisely retreated there. Even so, the situation stayed precarious. The Jurchens pursued him across the Yangzi River and even out into the ocean, so the river was no automatic guarantee of safety. To get far from them, Song made its capital at Hangzhou, a beautiful city well south of the Yangzi.

Read the map. Map 8.2 shows the arrangement around 1200, not the moment Gaozong became emperor. Find the Yangzi and Hangzhou south of it. Compare Kaifeng: on Map 8.1 it was the Song capital; on Map 8.2 it lies inside Jin.

Peace again: efforts to drive the Jurchens out of the north were largely abandoned in 1141, when a peace treaty with Jin required heavy payments of silk and silver, much as Northern Song had paid Liao. Relocation, continued defense, and negotiation all mattered.

What is ahead: the economic center of China had already shifted south before 1127. Lecture 2 explains that shift and why it let Southern Song survive.

Source: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 8, pp. 130–131, 138–139, 147.

Now connect the political events to the chapter's title, "China Among Equals."

Breaking it down: "among equals" does not mean every state had the same strength, culture, or diplomatic standing. Song was far larger and richer than its neighbors. The point is that Song could not impose a single political order on all of them. It had to coexist, negotiate, and pay.

The bigger idea: the chapter's conclusion notes that the Mandate of Heaven, the idea that heaven recognizes a single Son of Heaven ruling the civilized world, became harder and harder to sustain. During Song times, more than one ruler called himself "Son of Heaven."

What is ahead: Song lost its northern territory but kept much of the population and the productive southern land. Lecture 2, "Agriculture and Trade Sustained Song Prosperity," explains why that mattered so much.

Pierian Spring · 961

Taizu Persuaded His Generals Over Wine to Give Up Their Commands

In 961, Taizu invited his top generals to a palace banquet.
He asked: what if your own troops put a yellow robe on you?
He offered land, wealth, and comfort if they gave up their commands. They agreed.
Historians call this bei jiu shi bing quan, and treat it as partly legendary.

Bei jiu shi bing quan 杯酒釋兵權

"A cup of wine to release military power." The famous phrase for Taizu's peaceful removal of his generals. The full scene first appears in accounts written decades later, so historians doubt the details while accepting that Taizu did retire his senior commanders.

Source: Li Tao, Xu Zizhi tongjian changbian (12th c.); Sima Guang, Sushui jiwen (11th c.); cf. Ebrey, ch. 8, p. 130. ← Back to Taizu's policy

Here is the most famous story about how Taizu tamed his generals. It is the year 961, a year after the yellow robe. Taizu throws a banquet for the commanders who helped him take the throne. The wine is flowing. Then the emperor turns gloomy and says he cannot sleep at night. Why, they ask. He answers: who wouldn't want my seat? You are loyal, but what if your own soldiers wrap a yellow robe around you some night? You would have no choice. The generals understand instantly, because that is exactly what happened to him. The next morning they all report sick and ask to resign. Taizu rewards them with estates, money, and marriage ties to the imperial family. You should know that historians treat this scene as partly legendary. It appears in full only in sources written decades later, and it is a little too neat. But the outcome is real: Taizu did retire his senior commanders. That is the policy you saw on the Taizu slide.

HIST 270: History of China

Chapter 4, Lecture 3

Buddhism's Conquest of China

Religious Transformation in an Age of Division

ca. 200–580 CE

📚 Context

Final lecture of 3 on Period of Division. Today: Why Buddhism spread, how it was adapted, impact on Chinese civilization. Header color = imperial gold (cultural chapter).

🎯 Goals

Explain why Buddhism succeeded. Describe adaptations. Analyze relationship with state. Understand impact on art, thought, society. How Daoism responded.

The Big Question

How did Buddhism—an imported religion—become compelling to Chinese elites and ordinary people during political fragmentation?

By 580 CE, there were:

  • Over 6,000 Buddhist temples in north alone
  • More than 77,000 monks and nuns
  • Massive cave temple complexes carved into cliffs
  • Buddhism integrated into daily life and state power

🎯 Frame

Buddhism spread as toolkit: rituals, communities, texts, images, specialists. Not just ideas—entire institutional system. Puzzle: how did foreign religion become so Chinese? Numbers from 477 CE census (northern dynasties).

Review Question Roadmap

  • Q7: Why did Buddhism spread despite conflicts with lineage, filial duty, and ancestor-centered practice?
  • Q8: What new social and spiritual options did Buddhist institutions offer women?
  • Q9: How could rulers patronize Buddhism while also regulating clergy and monasteries?
  • Q10: How did organized Daoism change during the Period of Division?

Follow the chain: crisis and salvation → adaptation → institutions → state power and competition.

The Impossible Success

Buddhism should have failed in China.

Confucian Values Buddhist Practice
Marry and produce heirs Celibacy
Serve your parents Leave your family
Venerate ancestors Reject attachments to deceased
This world matters This world is illusion
Social hierarchy natural All beings can achieve enlightenment

Yet Buddhism conquered China. How?

🎯 Hook

Contradiction is real—should trouble students. Don't resolve too quickly; tension drives lecture. But avoid caricature: frame as "elite expectations" vs. "Buddhist possibilities" rather than absolute opposition. Huiyuan's argument: "A monk does not bow down before a king" (monks and kings occupy different moral orders).

Buddhism Before China

Core Concepts (Very Brief)

  • The Buddha: Siddhartha Gautama, Indian prince (c. 500 BCE) who achieved enlightenment
  • Karma: Actions have consequences across lives
  • Samsara: Cycle of rebirth
  • Nirvana: Liberation from rebirth cycle
  • Bodhisattva: Beings who delay nirvana to help others
  • Merit: Moral "capital" that can be transferred to others

Mahayana traditions became especially influential in China

Emphasized bodhisattvas, merit transfer, compassion. Could be reconciled with Confucian ancestor veneration more easily than earlier forms.

×

The Buddha

Siddhartha Gautama (c. 563–483 BCE), Indian prince who renounced wealth, achieved enlightenment through meditation, taught path to liberation.

×

Karma

Actions have moral consequences that determine future rebirths. Good deeds → better rebirth. Bad deeds → worse rebirth. Provides explanation for suffering and inequality.

×

Samsara

The repeating cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Driven by karma and desire. Goal is to escape this cycle, not to be reborn better.

×

Nirvana

Liberation from cycle of rebirth. Not "nothingness" but release from suffering, desire, and attachments. Ultimate goal of Buddhist practice.

×

Bodhisattva

Enlightened being who delays final nirvana to help others achieve salvation. Embodies compassion and active engagement with suffering world. Key to Mahayana Buddhism.

×

Merit (功德)

Positive karma accumulated through good deeds, donations, prayers. Can be dedicated/transferred to benefit others, especially deceased relatives. Merit transfer helped reconcile Buddhist practice with Chinese family and ancestor obligations.

×

Mahayana Buddhism

"Greater Vehicle" traditions became especially influential in East Asia. Key features included the bodhisattva ideal, merit transfer, compassion, and the possibility of universal salvation.

📚 Keep Brief

Not world religions class. Focus on aspects that matter for Chinese reception. Merit transfer was one important way Buddhist practice could be reconciled with Chinese family and ancestor obligations. Emphasize ritual practice as much as philosophy: Buddhism spreads as ritual + charisma + community, not just ideas.

Buddhism enters as part of entire package of transregional exchange. Not "China receives religion"—it's "China joins religious network." Initial Han contact had minimal impact. Why? Han Confucian ideology confident, government strong. Period of Division changed everything—Confucianism seemed inadequate, non-Chinese rulers had no loyalty to Confucian tradition, suffering population sought spiritual consolation.

The Missionary Miracles

Fotudeng (d. 349): Wonder-Worker

  • Central Asian monk who arrived in 310
  • Converted violent Shi Le (non-Chinese ruler from Lecture 2!)
  • Method: Performing magic—filled prayer bowl with water, made blue lotus emerge
  • Advised Shi Le on military and political matters

Why Miracles Mattered:

  • In age of suffering, people wanted protection
  • Buddhism offered supernatural power accessible to any believer
  • Contrast with Confucianism's focus on ethics and study

"In a rough and tumultuous age, Buddhism offered appealing emphasis on kindness, charity, preservation of life, and prospect of salvation."

×

Fotudeng (佛圖澄, d. 349)

Central Asian monk, master of "miracle tales." Converted Later Zhao ruler Shi Le through demonstrations of Buddhist efficacy.

Teaching point: Don't teach miracles as true/false. Teach as historical signals: what counted as persuasive, what anxieties addressed, how charisma worked at court. In age when governments collapse, religion promising protection/healing/cosmic order won't struggle for audience.

🎯 Miracles as Evidence

Don't dismiss as "superstition"—take seriously what people found compelling. Fotudeng converting Shi Le shows religion crossing ethnic lines. Buddhism offered: explanation for suffering (karma), hope for escape (nirvana), community (sangha). Beyond miracles: compassion vs. military culture.

Multi-group appeal explains Buddhism's reach.

Making Buddhism Chinese

The Translation Problem

How do you explain Indian concepts in Chinese?

  • Early translators used Daoist vocabulary
  • Nirvana → "non-action" (wu wei)
  • Dharma → "the Way" (dao)
  • Geyi (格義): Matching concepts—helpful but created confusion

The Core Cast of Translators

  • Dao'an (312–385): Institutional builder, scripture cataloging
  • Kumārajīva (344–413): Translation revolution in Chang'an, elegant Chinese style
  • Huiyuan (334–416): Southern elite Buddhism, argued for sangha autonomy
  • Faxian (travel 399–414): Pilgrimage to India for texts
×

Geyi (格義) - "Matching Concepts"

Interpretive strategy used by Chinese intellectuals to explain Buddhist ideas via existing categories (often Daoist terms).

Modern scholarship caution: Not just "Daoist translation method." Better understood as broader strategy in elite discussion. Sometimes in translation, often in commentary. Helpful but created distortions—nirvana isn't exactly wu wei!

📚 Translation is Worldview Problem

Not dictionary problem—had to build new Chinese religious vocabulary. Early translation used familiar terms (helpful but misleading). Core cast non-optional for this period. Translation revolution: Kumārajīva's crisp style shapes what educated readers think Buddhism sounds like.

The Ancestor Solution

One important adaptation to Chinese family and ancestor obligations:

The Problem

  • Original Buddhism: Attachments (including to deceased) cause suffering
  • Chinese question: How can monks who left families be filial?
  • Confucian critique: You abandoned parents!

The Solution

  • Merit transfer: Monks' prayers benefit families
  • Donations to monasteries generate merit for deceased
  • New funerary rituals
  • Moral reframing: leaving home helps family across lifetimes

Yan Zhitui (educated Confucian official): "wanted Buddhist services after his death and told his sons to omit meat from traditional ancestral offerings."

An educated Chinese official who honored BOTH traditions!

🎯 Key Adaptation

Merit transfer was one important mechanism for reconciling Buddhism with ancestor veneration, but it was not the single explanation for Buddhist success. Missionary activity, miracles and charismatic specialists, salvation, compassion and charity, royal patronage, lay devotional practices, monastic institutions, opportunities for women, pilgrimage, and religious art all contributed. The Yan Zhitui example shows how family obligations and Buddhist practice could be combined.

Most Chinese Buddhists Never Became Monks

  • Laypeople could practice Buddhism while remaining inside ordinary households
  • Donations to temples and monasteries allowed people to make religious merit
  • Devotional groups formed around important Buddhist scriptures
  • Chinese communities increasingly adapted Buddhist teachings and practices to local needs

Buddhism succeeded because participation did not require abandoning ordinary family life.

🎯 Teaching Point

Students may hear about monks and monasteries and assume that becoming Buddhist meant leaving one's family. For most converts, it did not.

Lay Buddhists could worship, donate to monasteries, sponsor images, participate in devotional associations, and support Buddhist institutions while continuing ordinary family and economic life.

The textbook specifically mentions devotional groups centered on scriptures such as the Lotus Sutra, Pure Land Sutra, and Vimalakirti.

Buddhist adaptation increasingly occurred inside China itself. Chinese Buddhists produced new texts and practices, including some scriptures presented as translations even though they had been composed in China.

Do not turn this into a detailed discussion of the Buddhist canon. The central point is: Buddhism became a mass religion because it offered many levels of participation.

Women and New Religious Choices

Buddhist institutions widened the range of possible lives for women.

  • A nunnery could provide an alternative to marriage or continued residence with a husband's family in widowhood
  • Women could join religious communities, pursue learning and discipline, and seek salvation directly
  • Patronage and merit-making gave elite women public religious roles beyond the ancestral lineage
  • These options remained constrained by family, class, and monastic authority

Q8: Buddhism did not erase patriarchy, but it created institutions in which some women could pursue a different social and spiritual path.

Chinese Buddhist Schools

These traditions began developing during and after the Period of Division and would become much more important in later centuries.

Pure Land Buddhism

  • Devotion to Amitabha Buddha
  • Rebirth in Western Paradise
  • Most accessible: even illiterate could practice
  • "Call on Buddha as many times as possible"

Chan Buddhism (later Zen)

  • Meditation and sudden enlightenment
  • Combined Buddhist practice with Daoist spontaneity
  • A Chinese Buddhist tradition that would become especially influential in later centuries
×

Pure Land Buddhism (淨土宗)

Devotional school focusing on Amitabha Buddha and rebirth in Western Paradise (Pure Land).

Why it succeeded: Simple practice (chanting Buddha's name), accessible to everyone (not just educated/monks), promises concrete reward (rebirth in paradise). It would become one of the most popular forms among ordinary Chinese.

×

Chan Buddhism (禪宗)

Meditation school emphasizing sudden enlightenment and direct experience. Becomes Zen in Japan.

Hybrid nature: Combined Indian Buddhist meditation with Chinese emphasis on spontaneity and naturalness. It would become especially influential in later centuries.

📚 Indigenous Developments

These traditions show Buddhism adapting to Chinese contexts, but students should not imagine fully developed later institutions already in place throughout 200–580. Pure Land devotional practices expanded the possibilities for lay participation. Chan remained a developing tradition that would become especially influential in later centuries.

🎯 Cave Temples as Statecraft

Buddhism is BUILT, not just studied. Cave temples = both devotion and statecraft. What does state gain by carving dharma into rock? Public proof of virtue + order, permanent monuments, control of sacred space. Images standardize devotion. Architecture organizes public space—monasteries become urban anchors. Donor inscriptions reveal who's buying in: elite women, rulers, military patrons, merchants.

💡 Teaching

Emperor Wu controversial—later historians blamed Buddhist devotion for military weakness. "Ransom" story appears twice (527 and later)—shows pattern. This is religious AND political—don't force students to choose one interpretation. Shows Buddhism's power over even emperors.

Buddhism and State Power

Royal Patronage

  • Non-Chinese rulers found Buddhism useful (not tied to Confucian tradition)
  • Could claim legitimacy as Buddhist monarchs
  • Monastic institutions could be organized and counted

Northern Wei Example:

  • Empress Dowager Hu built Monastery of Eternal Tranquility (516)
  • 900-foot wooden pagoda, visible from 100 li away
  • Over 1,000 Buddhist temples in Luoyang by 534
  • "Luoyang became magnificent city with half-million residents, vast palaces, elegant mansions, and more than thousand Buddhist temples"

The Dangers

  • Monasteries accumulated enormous wealth (tax-exempt land)
  • Monks didn't pay taxes or perform labor service
  • By 477: 6,478 temples, 77,258 monks/nuns in north alone
  • State response: Rulers twice ordered closing of monasteries, return of monks to lay life
  • But suppression never lasted—Buddhism too popular

📚 Push-Pull Dynamic

Patronage not whole story. Major suppressions: Northern Wei (446) under Taiwu, Northern Zhou (574-577) under Wu. Economic issue real—monasteries became major landholders. State attempts to regulate show Buddhism never simply tool of rulers. "Buddhism becomes indispensable to rulers—and therefore frightening."

×

Faxian (法顯, c. 337–422)

Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled to India seeking authentic texts, especially monastic discipline rules (Vinaya).

Significance: His journey shows Chinese weren't just passively receiving Buddhism—they were actively seeking authentic sources, willing to risk death for texts. "Pilgrimage logic: we need the Vinaya texts." Shows China plugged into Indian Buddhist centers as equals.

🎯 Active Participation

Faxian's journey shows Chinese weren't passive recipients. At 60+, walked to India, risked death for texts. Returned by sea (dangerous route). Spent rest of life translating. Shows Buddhism's pull on educated Chinese. By early 5th century, Chinese fully integrated into Buddhist world.

Daoism Was Also Becoming a Religious Tradition

  • Popular Daoist communities practiced healing and communal ritual
  • Elite practitioners sought bodily immortality
  • Practices included diet, breathing exercises, meditation, alchemy, and talismans
  • Ge Hong wrote extensively about methods for extending life and achieving immortality

Daoism during the Period of Division was much more than the philosophy of Laozi and Zhuangzi.

×

Ge Hong (283–343)

Daoist scholar and practitioner who collected and explained techniques for bodily cultivation, medicines, alchemy, and the pursuit of immortality.

🎯 Teaching Point

Students already know philosophical Daoism from earlier chapters. Here they need to distinguish that intellectual tradition from the expanding religious Daoist movements of the Period of Division.

Elite religious practitioners pursued transformation of the body through diet, exercise, meditation, visualization, alchemy, medicines, talismans, and ritual.

Use Ge Hong (283–343) as the concrete example, but do not turn him into a separate biography lecture.

The main point is that organized religious Daoism was developing rapidly at the same time Buddhism was expanding.

Migration Transformed Daoism Too

  • Northern religious specialists migrated south during the political upheavals
  • They encountered established southern traditions of ritual and esoteric practice
  • New revelations produced large collections of sacred scriptures
  • Traditions such as Supreme Purity and Numinous Treasure emerged

Political division moved religious ideas and specialists as well as armies and refugees.

🎯 Teaching Point

The fall of northern political centers pushed elite families, monks, officials, and Daoist specialists southward. These migrants encountered local southern religious traditions.

The result was not simple preservation of an old religion. New revelations and new texts transformed Daoism.

Students do not need to memorize the names Supreme Purity and Numinous Treasure for detailed testing unless already included in the course review structure. Their purpose is to show that new organized Daoist traditions emerged during this era.

Competition with Buddhism Changed Daoism

  • Daoists assembled increasingly organized bodies of sacred scripture
  • Some Daoist communities adopted celibate clergy and monastic institutions
  • Buddhist and Daoist leaders competed for court patronage
  • Each tradition criticized the religious claims of the other

Buddhism did not replace Daoism. Competition helped transform both traditions.

🎯 Teaching Point

Buddhism provided organizational models that Daoists could borrow without becoming Buddhist.

By the end of the Period of Division, Daoism possessed extensive scriptures and increasingly institutionalized religious organizations.

Competition could become intense. Buddhist and Daoist clergy competed for political favor and produced polemics against each other.

Avoid framing this as Buddhism simply "creating" Daoism. Daoism had older indigenous roots, including popular religious movements and immortality traditions. The correct causal claim is that Buddhist success accelerated and influenced Daoist institutional development.

A Transformed World

Political division transformed Chinese civilization culturally, ethnically, geographically, and religiously.

  • Lecture 1: Political unity collapsed, but new institutions and elite cultures emerged
  • Lecture 2: Non-Chinese rule transformed both conquerors and Chinese society
  • Lecture 3: Buddhism became deeply rooted in China while Daoism also transformed in response

The Bottom Line:

Political division produced cultural integration and religious transformation.

🎯 Full Arc Synthesis

Bring back all three lectures. This wasn't "dark age" of decline—it was transformation. Chinese civilization didn't just survive conquest and division—it incorporated conquerors and new religions to become larger, more complex, ultimately more resilient. "What is Chinese?" has no simple answer. Period of Division showed Chinese civilization was not fixed thing but living process—could absorb foreign rulers and religions while remaining recognizably itself.

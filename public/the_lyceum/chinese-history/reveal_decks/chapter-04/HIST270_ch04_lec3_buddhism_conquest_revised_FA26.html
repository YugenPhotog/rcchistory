The Impossible Success

Buddhism should have failed in China.

Confucian Values Buddhist Practice
Marry and produce heirs Celibacy
Serve your parents Leave your family
Maintain ancestral lineage and offerings Monastic renunciation leaves the household
Fulfill duties within family and society Liberation requires overcoming attachment
Social hierarchy natural All beings can achieve enlightenment

Yet Buddhism conquered China. How?

This slide sets up the lecture’s big puzzle. Read the table row by row: each row puts a core Chinese family value next to a Buddhist monastic practice that seemed to contradict it.

Here’s the background. Chinese families, shaped by Confucian teaching, put huge weight on filial piety, the duty children owe their parents: obedience, care in old age, and proper rites after death. A son was also expected to marry and have sons so that offerings to the ancestors would continue. Buddhist monks and nuns did the opposite. They were celibate (they did not marry or have children), and they “left the household” to live in monasteries. To a Confucian critic, that looked like abandoning your parents and ending your family line.

Notice the last row too: Buddhism taught that all beings could reach enlightenment, which sat uneasily beside the idea that social hierarchy was natural.

So why did Buddhism succeed anyway? The next several slides give you the answer in pieces: adaptation, lay participation, royal support, and new institutions.

Mahayana Buddhism: A Brief Introduction

Watch on YouTube (9:29)

Before you can see how Buddhism changed in China, you need a few basic ideas it brought with it. Click each dotted term for a short popup.

The Buddha (“awakened one”) was Siddhartha Gautama, a teacher from northern India. Karma is the idea that your actions have moral consequences that carry into future lives. Samsara is the endless cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Nirvana is release from that cycle and the suffering that comes with it.

Mahayana (“Great Vehicle”) is the broad branch of Buddhism that became dominant in China. It honors bodhisattvas, beings who vow to reach enlightenment for the sake of all living things and are often described as putting off their own final nirvana to help others. It also stresses merit, spiritual credit earned through good deeds, gifts, and prayers, which you can dedicate to someone else, even a dead parent. Watch for that idea: it becomes the key on slide 9.

One more term: the sangha is the community of Buddhist monks and nuns. Buddhism reached China not just as ideas but as a sangha with rituals, texts, and institutions. For more on Mahayana, watch the short video (9:29).

The Silk Road Gateway

  • Buddhism spread along Silk Road trade routes
  • Oasis cities became Buddhist centers
  • Merchants and missionaries traveled together
  • Buddhism arrives as transregional system: images, relics, ritual objects, languages

This slide answers a simple question: how did Buddhism physically get to China? The answer is along the Silk Road, the network of overland trade routes linking China with Central Asia, India, and beyond.

Buddhism first reached China during the Han dynasty, around the 1st century CE, but for a long time it had only limited impact. Along the routes, oasis cities (towns built around water sources in the deserts of Central Asia) became Buddhist centers. Merchants and monks often traveled together, so religion moved with trade. Notice the last bullet: Buddhism arrived as a whole package of images, relics (sacred remains of holy figures), ritual objects, and texts in foreign languages.

Why did it take off during the Period of Division? After the Han fell, war and disorder made people hungry for protection, charity, and hope. Some non-Chinese rulers in the north also liked a tradition that was not tied to Confucian ideas about how to rule.

Takeaway: trade contact → networks of monks and texts → political breakdown → a new opening for Buddhism. That doesn’t mean Confucianism disappeared; changed conditions simply gave Buddhism room to grow. The next slide shows the routes on a map.

Silk Road Buddhist transmission map: India through Central Asia and the Tarim Basin to northern China

Map: Overland routes by which Buddhist texts, monks, and artistic traditions traveled from India through Central Asia into China.

Now trace the route yourself. Start in India, follow the path north and east through Central Asia, and then along the chain of oasis towns around the Tarim Basin, a large desert basin in what is now western China. The routes come together at Dunhuang, the gateway town at China’s western edge, and then continue east to Chang’an and Luoyang, the great capitals of northern China.

What to notice: Buddhism did not arrive in a single trip or on a single date. Monks, texts, statues, and ritual objects moved step by step through many connected communities, and each stop could add its own languages and artistic styles. That is one reason early Chinese Buddhist art shows Indian and Central Asian influences.

Fotudeng (佛圖澄, d. 349)

Central Asian monk, master of "miracle tales." Converted Later Zhao ruler Shi Le through demonstrations of Buddhist efficacy.

Why the stories matter: Historians read miracle tales not as simply true or false, but as clues: they show what people found persuasive, what fears they lived with, and how a holy man's personal power worked at court. In an age when governments collapsed, a religion promising protection, healing, and cosmic order easily found an audience.

This slide introduces one of the first famous Buddhist missionaries in China. Fotudeng was a monk from Central Asia who arrived in 310 and became an advisor at the court of Shi Le, the non-Chinese ruler of the Later Zhao state in north China (you met these northern conquest states in Lecture 2).

Stories say Fotudeng won Shi Le over with wonders, such as making a blue lotus rise from a bowl of water, and that he healed, predicted events, and advised on war. Click his name for the popup. Historians don’t try to prove each miracle. They ask what the stories reveal: in a violent age, people wanted protection, and a monk who seemed to hold real spiritual power was persuasive. Notice the contrast in the gold box: Confucianism focused on ethics and study, while Buddhism promised supernatural help, kindness, and salvation to anyone.

Fotudeng also shows Buddhism crossing ethnic lines. According to the Biographies of Eminent Monks, when an official urged Shi Le’s successor, Shi Hu, to forbid Chinese subjects from worshiping a foreign god, Shi Hu replied that he himself came from the frontier, so the Buddha suited him well.

The Ancestor Solution

One important adaptation to Chinese family and ancestor obligations:

The Problem

  • Original Buddhism: Attachments (including to deceased) cause suffering
  • Chinese question: How can monks who left families be filial?
  • Confucian critique: You abandoned parents!

The Solution

  • Merit transfer: Monks' prayers benefit families
  • Donations to monasteries generate merit for deceased
  • New funerary rituals
  • Moral reframing: leaving home helps family across lifetimes

Yan Zhitui (educated Confucian official): "wanted Buddhist services after his death and told his sons to omit meat from traditional ancestral offerings."

An educated Chinese official who honored BOTH traditions!

Remember the puzzle from slide 4? This slide shows one of the most important ways Buddhism solved it.

Read the red box first. Early Buddhist teaching said attachment, including attachment to family, causes suffering. So Chinese critics asked: how can a monk who left his parents be a good son?

Now read the teal box. The answer was merit transfer. A monk’s prayers, a family’s gifts to a monastery, and new Buddhist funeral rites all created merit, and that merit could be dedicated to dead parents and ancestors to help them toward a better rebirth. Buddhists could even argue that leaving home helped your family more, across many lifetimes, than staying would.

The primary source at the bottom makes this concrete. Yan Zhitui was an educated official raised in the Confucian tradition, yet he asked his sons to hold Buddhist services after his death and to leave meat out of the usual ancestral offerings (Buddhists avoided killing animals). He honored both traditions at once.

Most Chinese Buddhists Never Became Monks

  • Laypeople could practice Buddhism while remaining inside ordinary households
  • Donations to temples and monasteries allowed people to make religious merit
  • Devotional groups formed around important Buddhist scriptures
  • Chinese communities increasingly adapted Buddhist teachings and practices to local needs

Buddhism succeeded because participation did not require abandoning ordinary family life.

Here’s a fact that surprises many students: most Chinese Buddhists never became monks or nuns. They were laypeople, ordinary believers who stayed at home, married, farmed, or ran businesses.

Buddhism offered two levels of participation. Monastic Buddhism meant leaving the household and living by strict rules in a monastery or nunnery. Lay Buddhism let you practice without giving up family life. Lay believers worshiped, gave gifts to temples, paid for statues and copies of scriptures, and joined devotional groups built around favorite sutras (Buddhist scriptures). Each of these acts earned merit.

This is a big part of the answer to the lecture’s puzzle. The conflict with family duty you saw on slide 4 applied mainly to monks and nuns. For everyone else, Buddhism fit alongside ordinary family life instead of replacing it. That is how it became a mass religion.

Women and New Religious Choices

Buddhist institutions widened the range of possible lives for women.

  • A nunnery could provide an alternative to marriage or continued residence with a husband's family in widowhood
  • Women could join religious communities, pursue learning and discipline, and seek salvation directly
  • Patronage and merit-making gave elite women public religious roles beyond the ancestral lineage
  • These options remained constrained by family, class, and monastic authority

This slide begins Review Question 8: what did Buddhism offer women? The short answer is more choices than before, but not unlimited ones.

In traditional Chinese families, a woman’s life was usually defined by marriage. She moved into her husband’s family, and if he died, she was often expected to stay with his family as a widow. A Buddhist nunnery (a community of nuns, also called a convent) offered a different path. Women there could study, meditate, follow religious discipline, and seek salvation directly. Wealthy laywomen could also sponsor temples and images, which gave them a public religious role.

Now read the last bullet carefully, because it matters just as much. These options had real limits. Families still held great authority, most women whose lives we know about came from elite families, and nunneries had their own hierarchies. Buddhism did not end patriarchy (a social system in which men hold most of the authority), but it opened some new doors within it.

The next slide gives you a real example: a nun named Tanhui.

What Could Buddhism Offer a Woman? The Story of Tanhui

Watch on YouTube (7:20)

This slide gives you one real woman’s story, so you can see the choices from slide 12 in action. Read the bullets first, then click the video link (7:20) to hear the story in more detail. The painting on the right is AI-generated: it imagines what a nun of this era might have looked like, and it is not a portrait of Tanhui.

Where the story comes from: Tanhui’s life is known from her biography in the Biqiuni zhuan (Lives of the Nuns), compiled by the monk Baochang around 516 (Taishō vol. 50, no. 2063, juan 4). To read it in English, see Kathryn Ann Tsai, Lives of the Nuns: Biographies of Chinese Buddhist Nuns from the Fourth to Sixth Centuries (Honolulu: University of Hawai‘i Press, 1994), biography no. 54, pp. 92–95.

Her story in more detail: Tanhui (family name Qingyang, personal name Baiyu) came from Chengdu in Shu (modern Sichuan) and later lived at the Changle (“Enduring Joy”) Convent there. When she was eleven, the foreign meditation master Kālayaśas came to Shu. He praised her talent, told her to practice meditation, and asked a nun named Fayu to look after her. But her mother had already promised her in marriage to the son of her father’s sister (her cousin), and the wedding date was set, so Fayu secretly took her to the convent. Tanhui vowed: “If I cannot carry out my intentions to lead the religious life but instead am compelled to marry, then I shall burn myself to death” (Tsai, p. 93; Chinese: 若我道心不遂，遂致逼迫者，當以火自焚耳).

The regional governor, Zhen Fachong, called her in and questioned her in front of officials, local leaders, monks, and nuns. When she told him this had long been her wish, he approved and told her aunt to release her from the engagement. She became a nun at thirteen.

It didn’t end there. When Tanhui was sixteen, her former fiancé and some companions tried to seize her, but her servants protected her. When the case went back to the governor, Kālayaśas advised him not to oppose her and offered to help cover the cost of dissolving the engagement, and the matter was settled.

Her later life: The biography says Tanhui came to understand Mahayana teachings, such as the permanence of Buddha-nature (the capacity for enlightenment present in every being), through her own meditation, and that famous teachers could not defeat her in debate. In 442, at age twenty-one, the Prince of Linchuan (Liu Yiqing) invited her to his post. Later about 1,200 followers, both lay and monastic, gathered around her in a region the biography calls “southern Chu” (its exact location is uncertain). Missing her mother, she returned to Chengdu, built a pagoda and temple and then three more convents, and died in 504 at age eighty-three.

Read the source like a historian: The Lives of the Nuns is a collection of inspiring biographies, not a neutral record; it also credits Tanhui with visions and almost miraculous resources. Its dates don’t always match other sources, either: it places Kālayaśas in Shu in 432, while the Gaoseng zhuan (Biographies of Eminent Monks) dates his journey to Shu to 442. Tsai calls Tanhui “the most dramatic example, perhaps, of a woman fleeing marriage” in the collection (Tsai, p. 7).

Why this matters: Look at the gold box. The convent gave Tanhui a real alternative to marriage, but she still needed the governor’s approval to take it, and her fiancé’s side fought back. That is the “new choices, real limits” pattern from slide 12.

Emperor Wu of Liang Made Buddhism Royal Policy

r. 502-549

  • Emperor Wu studied Daoism as a young man, but later became a major Buddhist patron
  • In 504, he urged his family and officials to give up Daoism
  • He removed meat and wine from palace banquets as acts of Buddhist devotion
  • Beginning in 527, he gave himself to a monastery; in 529 he refused to return until officials paid the monastery a large ransom

Buddhism had become powerful enough that an emperor could use monastic devotion as public politics.

Now you move to southern China. Emperor Wu of Liang ruled the southern Liang dynasty for almost fifty years (502–549) and became one of the most devoted Buddhist rulers in Chinese history.

Read the bullets as a story of growing devotion. As a young man he studied Daoism, but later he committed himself to Buddhism. Buddhist sources say that in 504 he urged his family and officials to give up Daoism, though that account was recorded by later Buddhist writers and some historians doubt its date or accuracy. He also removed meat and wine from palace banquets, since Buddhists avoid killing living things.

The most famous episode: beginning in 527, he “gave himself” to the Tongtai Monastery in his capital and lived there like a monk. On his second stay, in 529, he refused to come back until his officials paid the monastery a huge ransom, reported as 100 million cash. He did it again in 546 and 547.

Why it matters: read the gold box. Was this sincere faith or political theater? Probably both. The ransom moved enormous wealth from the court to a monastery, which shows how important monasteries had become.

Rulers Patronized Buddhism to Strengthen Their Rule

  • Rulers could claim legitimacy as Buddhist monarchs
  • Non-Chinese rulers in the north valued a tradition not tied to Confucian political ideals
  • Monumental temples displayed both piety and power

Northern Wei Example:

Empress Dowager Hu built the Monastery of Eternal Tranquility (516). Before 534, Luoyang had 1,367 Buddhist temples.

×

Monastery of Eternal Tranquility (永寧寺, Yongning)

Founded in Luoyang in 516 by Empress Dowager Hu. Its nine-story wooden pagoda towered over the Northern Wei capital until fire destroyed it in 534.

How tall was it? Yang Xuanzhi's Record of Buddhist Monasteries in Luoyang (547) claims 90 zhang (900 Chinese feet) plus a 10-zhang spire, visible from 100 li (roughly 40–50 km) away. Historians consider this an exaggeration. Other early sources give about 40–49 zhang, and modern reconstructions based on the excavated foundation (about 38 m square) put the pagoda at roughly 110–150 m, still among the tallest structures in the premodern world.

Why it matters: Yang counted 1,367 Buddhist temples in a capital of about 109,000 households, often estimated at over half a million people. Royal building on this scale turned Buddhist devotion into a public display of dynastic power.

Slide 16 showed one emperor in the south. This slide explains the general pattern: why rulers across China gave so much to Buddhism. Patronage means support from a powerful sponsor, usually money, land, or buildings.

Rulers had good reasons. Supporting Buddhism let them present themselves as pious Buddhist monarchs, which boosted their legitimacy (their claim to have the right to rule). Non-Chinese rulers in the north especially valued a tradition that was not tied to the Confucian political ideals of earlier Chinese dynasties. And huge temples were a visible way to show both devotion and power.

The northern example: Empress Dowager Hu, who ran the Northern Wei court as regent for her young son, founded the Monastery of Eternal Tranquility in Luoyang in 516. Click its name for the popup, and read about its enormous wooden pagoda (a many-storied Buddhist tower) and the debate over its height. Before 534, Luoyang had 1,367 temples.

  • Monks and nuns paid no taxes and owed no labor service
  • Clergy numbers kept growing

Where the figures come from: The northern counts appear in the official Book of Wei (Wei shu); the southern count comes from a later tally by the Tang monk Falin. Premodern totals are approximate, but they show how quickly monastic communities, and the land and labor they controlled, were growing.

Here’s the other side of patronage: the more land and people monasteries had, the bigger a problem they became for the state.

Read the red box. Monastery land was usually tax-exempt, and monks and nuns paid no taxes and owed no labor service (the unpaid work, such as building roads and walls, that commoners owed the state). Every new monk meant one fewer taxpayer and worker. Click “Clergy numbers kept growing” to see how fast: 6,478 temples in the north in 477, and 13,727 by about 512–515.

Now the teal box. Rulers counted and regulated monasteries and clergy, and twice in the north they banned Buddhism outright. In 446, Emperor Taiwu of the Northern Wei ordered Buddhist images and scriptures destroyed and monks killed (many escaped because the crown prince delayed announcing the order). In 574, Emperor Wu of the Northern Zhou (not the same person as Emperor Wu of Liang) abolished both Buddhism and Daoism, destroyed images, and sent monks and Daoist priests back to ordinary life, extending the ban to newly conquered territory in 577. Both policies were reversed within a few years of each emperor’s death.

Takeaway: rulers both valued and feared Buddhism, and its return after each ban shows how deep its roots had grown.

Faxian (法顯, c. 337–c. 422)

Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled to India because Chinese monasteries lacked complete copies of the Vinaya, the rules of monastic discipline. He set out overland from Chang'an in 399 CE, collected texts in India and Sri Lanka, and returned by sea, landing in China in 412.

Why it matters: His journey shows that Chinese Buddhists were not just passively receiving Buddhism; they were actively seeking authoritative sources and risking death to obtain them. His travel record, the Foguoji (A Record of Buddhist Kingdoms), is a key source for Buddhism in Central Asia, India, and Sri Lanka around 400 CE.

This slide shows Chinese Buddhists going out into the Buddhist world instead of waiting for teachers to come to them. Faxian was a Chinese monk who, already in his sixties, set out from Chang’an in 399 on a journey to India.

Why go? Chinese monasteries lacked complete copies of the Vinaya, the rules of monastic discipline that tell monks and nuns how to live, dress, eat, and run their communities. Without reliable rules, the fast-growing sangha had trouble organizing itself.

Follow the bullets as a route: overland through Dunhuang, Khotan (an oasis kingdom in the Tarim Basin), and the Pamir Mountains into India, then home by sea by way of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. He landed in China in 412 and by 414 was in Nanjing (then called Jiankang, the southern capital), translating the texts he had brought back. Click his name for the popup, which introduces his travel record, A Record of Buddhist Kingdoms.

Why it matters: read the gold line. Chinese Buddhists had become active participants, willing to risk their lives to find authoritative texts.

This is the lecture’s final big takeaway. People sometimes call the centuries after the Han a “dark age,” as if China simply fell apart. This slide argues that the label misses what actually happened.

Yes, there was war and political breakup. But in these same centuries China absorbed new religious communities, sacred images, monasteries and nunneries, and new ideas about salvation. And Buddhism was not simply imposed from outside. Chinese monks, nuns, rulers, donors, artists, families, and lay believers chose what to adopt, paid for it, translated it, and reshaped it.

Now look at the quote in red: “The conquest of Buddhism was also the making of Chinese Buddhism.” It means two things happened together. Buddhism spread across China, and Chinese people turned it into something new that fit Chinese family and political life. Merit transfer for ancestors is a good example of both. The Translation Problem

How do you explain Indian concepts in Chinese?

  • Early translators used Daoist vocabulary
  • Nirvana → "non-action" (wu wei)
  • Dharma → "the Way" (dao)

The Core Cast of Translators

  • Dao'an (312–385): Institutional builder, scripture cataloging
  • Kumārajīva (344–413): Translation revolution in Chang'an, elegant Chinese style
  • Huiyuan (334–416): Southern elite Buddhism, argued for sangha autonomy
  • Faxian (travel 399–414): Pilgrimage to India for texts
×

Geyi (格義) - "Matching Concepts"

Interpretive strategy used by Chinese intellectuals to explain Buddhist ideas via existing categories (often Daoist terms).

What historians now think: Geyi was once described simply as a Daoist method of translation. Scholars now see it as a broader strategy in elite discussion of Buddhism, appearing sometimes in translations but more often in commentary and teaching. Matching concepts made Buddhist ideas easier to grasp, but it also created distortions: nirvana, for example, is not the same thing as wu wei.

Imagine trying to explain a brand-new religion in a language that has no words for its key ideas. That was the translators’ problem. Buddhist texts came in Indian and Central Asian languages such as Sanskrit, and ideas like nirvana had no Chinese equivalent.

Early translators often borrowed Daoist words. Nirvana was sometimes rendered as wu wei (“non-action”), and dao (“the Way”) was used for Buddhist ideas such as the dharma (the Buddha’s teaching). Click geyi (“matching concepts”) for the popup. Matching terms made Buddhism feel familiar, but it also distorted it: nirvana is not the same as wu wei.

Meet the key figures. Dao’an organized monastic life and cataloged scriptures. Kumārajīva, a monk from the oasis kingdom of Kucha, produced clear, elegant translations in Chang’an that are still widely read. Huiyuan led elite Buddhism in the south and argued for the sangha’s independence, including the right of monks not to bow to rulers. Faxian you already know from slide 20.

Chan Buddhism (禪宗)

Meditation school emphasizing sudden enlightenment and direct experience. Becomes Zen in Japan.

Historical development: Chan developed within Chinese Buddhist communities through changing approaches to meditation, monastic practice, scripture, and direct religious experience. Later interpretations often emphasized spontaneity and immediacy, but Chan should not be reduced to a simple mixture of Buddhism and Daoism.

This slide looks ahead. Two traditions that started taking shape during and after the Period of Division became hugely important later, so it’s worth meeting them now. Their mature forms belong mainly to the Tang dynasty and after, not to 220–589.

Pure Land Buddhism centers on devotion to Amitabha Buddha, who is believed to welcome the faithful into his Pure Land, a Western Paradise where reaching enlightenment is far easier. Its main practice, calling on Amitabha’s name, was simple enough for anyone, even people who could not read. That’s why it became one of the most popular forms of Buddhism among ordinary Chinese.

Chan Buddhism (called Zen in Japan) stresses meditation and direct experience of enlightenment, sometimes described as sudden. Click each name for a popup. Watch out for a common shortcut: Chan is often described as just “Buddhism plus Daoism,” but it grew out of changing Chinese Buddhist approaches to meditation, monastic life, and scripture.

Ge Hong (283–343)

Daoist scholar and practitioner who collected and explained techniques for bodily cultivation, medicines, alchemy, and the pursuit of immortality.

Buddhism wasn’t the only religion growing in this era. Daoism was changing too, and this slide shows that “Daoism” meant much more than the philosophy you studied in Chapter 2.

You may remember Laozi and Zhuangzi, the classic Daoist thinkers. By the Period of Division, Daoism had also become an organized religion. Popular Daoist communities practiced healing and group rituals. Elite practitioners pursued immortality (living forever in a perfected body) through special diets, breathing exercises, meditation, alchemy (making elixirs from substances such as cinnabar), and talismans (written charms believed to protect or command spirits).

Enrichment: Migration Transformed Daoism Too

  • Northern religious specialists migrated south during the political upheavals
  • They encountered established southern traditions of ritual and esoteric practice
  • New revelations produced large collections of sacred scriptures
  • Traditions such as Supreme Purity and Numinous Treasure emerged

Political division moved religious ideas and specialists as well as armies and refugees.

Earlier in this chapter you saw that war drove many northern families south. This slide shows that religious experts moved too, and that the move changed Daoism.

As the north fell into chaos in the 300s, Daoist specialists and elite families fled south. There they met older southern traditions of ritual and esoteric practice (secret teachings passed only to insiders). The mix produced something new: fresh revelations, texts believed to come directly from gods and immortals, which grew into large collections of scripture.

Two major traditions came out of this: Supreme Purity (Shangqing), based on revelations received in the 360s, and Numinous Treasure (Lingbao), whose scriptures appeared around 400 and borrowed heavily from Buddhist ideas.

Follow the chain: political upheaval → migration of religious specialists → contact with southern traditions → new texts and revelations → new religious institutions.

Enrichment: Competition with Buddhism Changed Daoism

  • Daoists assembled increasingly organized bodies of sacred scripture
  • Some Daoist communities adopted celibate clergy and monastic institutions
  • Buddhist and Daoist leaders competed for court patronage
  • Each tradition criticized the religious claims of the other

Buddhism did not replace Daoism. Competition helped transform both traditions.

This slide finishes the Daoism story by showing what happened when Buddhism and Daoism competed.

Competition pushed both religions to get more organized. Daoists gathered their scriptures into larger, more organized collections, much as Buddhists had. Some Daoist communities even adopted Buddhist-style features such as celibate clergy and monasteries. Meanwhile, Buddhist and Daoist leaders competed for the court’s favor and funding, and each side wrote attacks on the other’s claims. Rulers could switch sides: Buddhist sources say Emperor Wu of Liang gave up Daoism for Buddhism (slide 16), and the Northern Zhou ban of 574 (slide 18) hit both religions.

Read the gold box carefully: Buddhism did not replace Daoism, and it did not create Daoism either. Daoism had deep Chinese roots in philosophy, popular movements, ritual, and immortality practices. What Buddhism did was speed up and shape Daoism’s growth as an organized religion.

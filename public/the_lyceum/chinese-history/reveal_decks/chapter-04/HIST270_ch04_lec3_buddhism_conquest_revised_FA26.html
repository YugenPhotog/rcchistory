HIST 270: History of China

Chapter 4, Lecture 3

Buddhism's Conquest of China

Religious Transformation in an Age of Division

ca. 200–580 CE

This lecture explains how Buddhism became deeply rooted in China during the Period of Division. You will follow how transmission, adaptation, lay practice, religious institutions, art, pilgrimage, and state power turned an imported tradition into a lasting part of Chinese civilization.

The Big Question

How did Buddhism—an imported religion—become compelling to Chinese elites and ordinary people during political fragmentation?

Already by 477 CE in the north, there were:

  • Over 6,000 Buddhist temples in north alone
  • More than 77,000 monks and nuns
  • Massive cave temple complexes carved into cliffs
  • Buddhism integrated into daily life and state power

The 477 CE figures show the scale of Buddhist growth in northern China: thousands of temples, tens of thousands of monks and nuns, and monumental cave complexes. Buddhism was therefore more than a set of ideas. Temples, clergy, rituals, images, and organized communities show that it had penetrated social life and state politics. The lecture’s central question is how this imported tradition became so deeply established.

The Impossible Success

Buddhism should have failed in China.

Confucian Values Buddhist Practice
Marry and produce heirs Celibacy
Serve your parents Leave your family
Maintain ancestral lineage and offerings Monastic renunciation leaves the household
Fulfill duties within family and society Liberation requires overcoming attachment
Social hierarchy natural All beings can achieve enlightenment

Yet Buddhism conquered China. How?

Chinese lineage life placed great importance on marriage, descendants, care for parents, and ancestral rites. Buddhist monasticism could conflict with those expectations because monks and nuns practiced celibacy and left the household, apparently giving up the duty to produce heirs and maintain the family line.

This tension is one of the lecture’s major historical puzzles. Buddhism became successful even though its monastic ideal challenged practices that many Chinese families considered essential. The following slides explain how adaptation, lay participation, patronage, and new institutions helped bridge that gap.

Buddhism Before China

Core Concepts (Very Brief)

  • The Buddha: Siddhartha Gautama, Indian prince (c. 500 BCE) who achieved enlightenment
  • Karma: Actions have consequences across lives
  • Samsara: Cycle of rebirth
  • Nirvana: Liberation from rebirth cycle
  • Bodhisattva: Beings who delay nirvana to help others
  • Merit: Moral "capital" that can be transferred to others

Mahayana traditions became especially influential in China

Emphasized bodhisattvas, merit transfer, compassion. Could be reconciled with Confucian ancestor veneration more easily than earlier forms.

×

The Buddha

Siddhartha Gautama (c. 563–483 BCE), Indian prince who renounced wealth, achieved enlightenment through meditation, taught path to liberation.

×

Karma

Actions have moral consequences that determine future rebirths. Good deeds → better rebirth. Bad deeds → worse rebirth. Provides explanation for suffering and inequality.

×

Samsara

The repeating cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Driven by karma and desire. Goal is to escape this cycle, not to be reborn better.

×

Nirvana

Liberation from cycle of rebirth. Not "nothingness" but release from suffering, desire, and attachments. Ultimate goal of Buddhist practice.

×

Bodhisattva

Enlightened being who delays final nirvana to help others achieve salvation. Embodies compassion and active engagement with suffering world. Key to Mahayana Buddhism.

×

Merit (功德)

Positive karma accumulated through good deeds, donations, prayers. Can be dedicated/transferred to benefit others, especially deceased relatives. Merit transfer helped reconcile Buddhist practice with Chinese family and ancestor obligations.

×

Mahayana Buddhism

"Greater Vehicle" traditions became especially influential in East Asia. Key features included the bodhisattva ideal, merit transfer, compassion, and the possibility of universal salvation.

Karma means that actions have moral consequences across lives, while samsara is the continuing cycle of birth, death, and rebirth. Nirvana is liberation from that cycle and its suffering.

In Mahayana traditions, a bodhisattva delays final nirvana to help others. Good actions, donations, and prayers create merit, and that merit can be dedicated to another person through merit transfer. Buddhism reached China not only as a belief system but also through rituals, specialists, texts, communities, and institutions.

The Silk Road Gateway

  • Buddhism spread along Silk Road trade routes
  • Oasis cities became Buddhist centers
  • Merchants and missionaries traveled together
  • Buddhism arrives as transregional system: images, relics, ritual objects, languages

Buddhism had reached China before the Period of Division, but those earlier contacts had relatively limited impact. It traveled through Central Asian trade and oasis networks, where merchants, missionaries, and monks carried texts, images, relics, ritual objects, and languages from one community to another.

Political fragmentation after the Han changed the setting in which Buddhism was received. Warfare and social disruption increased the appeal of communities that offered ritual support, charity, moral authority, and hope, while some non-Chinese rulers found Buddhist institutions useful because they were not tied exclusively to older Confucian political traditions.

The causal chain is: Silk Road contact → transmission networks → political fragmentation → new religious opportunity. This does not mean Confucianism simply failed; it means that changed political and social conditions created more room for Buddhist institutions to grow.

Silk Road Buddhist transmission map: India through Central Asia and the Tarim Basin to northern China

Map: Overland routes by which Buddhist texts, monks, and artistic traditions traveled from India through Central Asia into China.

Read the route from India through Central Asia and the oasis corridors around the Tarim Basin, then east through Dunhuang toward Chang’an and Luoyang. The map shows that transmission was not a single journey or event: people, texts, images, and ritual objects moved through a chain of connected communities before reaching major Chinese centers.

The Missionary Miracles

Fotudeng (d. 349): Wonder-Worker

  • Central Asian monk who arrived in 310
  • Converted Shi Le, ruler of Later Zhao and a non-Chinese ruler from Lecture 2
  • Method: Performing magic—filled prayer bowl with water, made blue lotus emerge
  • Advised Shi Le on military and political matters

Why Miracles Mattered:

  • In age of suffering, people wanted protection
  • Buddhism offered supernatural power accessible to any believer
  • Contrast with Confucianism's focus on ethics and study

"In a rough and tumultuous age, Buddhism offered appealing emphasis on kindness, charity, preservation of life, and prospect of salvation."

×

Fotudeng (佛圖澄, d. 349)

Central Asian monk, master of "miracle tales." Converted Later Zhao ruler Shi Le through demonstrations of Buddhist efficacy.

Teaching point: Don't teach miracles as true/false. Teach as historical signals: what counted as persuasive, what anxieties addressed, how charisma worked at court. In age when governments collapse, religion promising protection/healing/cosmic order won't struggle for audience.

Fotudeng was a Central Asian monk who worked at the Later Zhao court of Shi Le, a non-Chinese ruler in northern China. Stories credited Fotudeng with miracles, healing, prediction, and ritual power. Their historical importance does not depend on proving whether each miracle literally occurred; the stories reveal what rulers and communities accepted as evidence of religious authority.

During a period of warfare and uncertainty, Buddhism could offer protection, effective ritual, charity, salvation, and moral prestige. Fotudeng’s influence at Shi Le’s court also shows Buddhism crossing ethnic boundaries and becoming useful within the new political order of the north.

The Ancestor Solution

One important adaptation to Chinese family and ancestor obligations:

The Problem

  • Original Buddhism: Attachments (including to deceased) cause suffering
  • Chinese question: How can monks who left families be filial?
  • Confucian critique: You abandoned parents!

The Solution

  • Merit transfer: Monks' prayers benefit families
  • Donations to monasteries generate merit for deceased
  • New funerary rituals
  • Moral reframing: leaving home helps family across lifetimes

Yan Zhitui (educated Confucian official): "wanted Buddhist services after his death and told his sons to omit meat from traditional ancestral offerings."

An educated Chinese official who honored BOTH traditions!

Merit transfer was one important way Buddhist practice could coexist with family and ancestor obligations. Donations, prayers, funerary rites, and support for monasteries could generate merit that believers dedicated to deceased relatives, allowing religious action to benefit the family across lifetimes.

This was not the single explanation for Buddhist success, but it addressed a serious concern about filial duty. Yan Zhitui provides a concrete example: an educated Confucian official could request Buddhist services after death while still maintaining family traditions. Religious accommodation allowed people to participate in both worlds.

Most Chinese Buddhists Never Became Monks

  • Laypeople could practice Buddhism while remaining inside ordinary households
  • Donations to temples and monasteries allowed people to make religious merit
  • Devotional groups formed around important Buddhist scriptures
  • Chinese communities increasingly adapted Buddhist teachings and practices to local needs

Buddhism succeeded because participation did not require abandoning ordinary family life.

Monastic Buddhism required monks and nuns to leave ordinary household life, but lay Buddhism did not. Lay believers could remain with their families while worshiping, donating to monasteries, making merit, sponsoring temples and images, and joining devotional groups centered on important scriptures.

These different levels of participation allowed Buddhist practices to be adapted within Chinese communities. Buddhism became a mass religion because most believers did not have to abandon ordinary family and economic life in order to participate.

Women and New Religious Choices

Buddhist institutions widened the range of possible lives for women.

  • A nunnery could provide an alternative to marriage or continued residence with a husband's family in widowhood
  • Women could join religious communities, pursue learning and discipline, and seek salvation directly
  • Tanhui shows how a convent could become an alternative to an unwanted marriage
  • Patronage and merit-making gave elite women public religious roles beyond the ancestral lineage
  • These options remained constrained by family, class, and monastic authority

Q8: Buddhism did not erase patriarchy, but it created institutions in which some women could pursue a different social and spiritual path.

×

Tanhui

The textbook describes Tanhui as a young woman who studied with a foreign meditation master beginning at age eleven. When faced with an unwanted marriage, she threatened suicide rather than marry her fiance.

Why she matters: Her story shows that Buddhist institutions could offer some women an alternative social path. That option was real, but it remained shaped by family power, class, religious authority, and negotiation with male relatives.

Nunneries could give some women an alternative to marriage or to continued residence in a husband’s family after widowhood. Within Buddhist institutions, women could pursue religious discipline, learning, merit-making, and salvation more directly. Tanhui’s resistance to an unwanted marriage provides a concrete example of a convent becoming an alternative social path.

These possibilities had real limits. Most surviving nun biographies concern elite women, family authority remained powerful, and Buddhist institutions were themselves hierarchical. Buddhism did not erase patriarchy, but it created some new religious and social choices within it.

Material Culture: Cave Temples

Buddhism brought new forms of religious expression:

Major Sites:

  • Yungang (near Datong): Northern Wei patronage, mid-5th century
  • Longmen (Luoyang): After capital move, late 5th century onward
  • Dunhuang/Mogao: Long-run devotional archive, 4th century onward
  • Cave 285 at Dunhuang: textbook case study of elite and local patronage

"Earlier Chinese rarely depicted gods in human form, but now Buddhist temples were furnished with profusion of images."

Yungang, Longmen, and Dunhuang Buddhist cave temples

Yungang, Longmen, and Dunhuang show how Buddhism reshaped China’s sacred landscape and visual culture.

×

Cave 285 at Dunhuang

In 523, Prince Dongyang, a Northern Wei royal, and wealthy local families sponsored a new cave at Dunhuang. Its central image was the historical Buddha, with meditating monks, temple guardians, and heavenly beings arranged around the sacred space.

Why it matters: Cave 285 shows Buddhism as material culture. It required patrons, artists, wealth, devotion, and a community that understood images and caves as sacred power.

At Yungang, Longmen, and Dunhuang, communities carved cliffs, created monumental Buddha images, painted cave interiors, and organized sacred spaces. Building these sites required wealth, patrons, artists, laborers, and religious communities; Buddhism was being constructed physically, not only discussed as a set of ideas.

Cave 285 makes the support network visible because a Northern Wei prince and wealthy local families helped sponsor it. These sites show Buddhism becoming embedded in the Chinese landscape and changing visual and sacred culture through stone, plaster, paint, ritual, and patronage.

Emperor Wu of Liang Made Buddhism Royal Policy

r. 502-549

  • Emperor Wu studied Daoism as a young man, but later became a major Buddhist patron
  • In 504, he urged his family and officials to give up Daoism
  • He removed meat and wine from palace banquets as acts of Buddhist devotion
  • In 527, he entered a monastery and refused to return until officials paid a large ransom to the monastery

The history first: Buddhism had become powerful enough that an emperor could use monastic devotion as public politics.

Emperor Wu of Liang became a major Buddhist patron in the south. He promoted Buddhist practice at court, removed meat and wine from palace banquets, and in 527 entered a monastery and refused to return until his officials made a large payment to the institution.

The episode shows that monasteries had become politically visible and economically important. Emperor Wu’s actions could express sincere devotion and function as public political performance at the same time; personal faith, royal patronage, court finance, and monastic wealth overlapped.

Buddhism and State Power

Royal Patronage

  • Non-Chinese rulers found Buddhism useful (not tied to Confucian tradition)
  • Could claim legitimacy as Buddhist monarchs
  • Monastic institutions could be organized and counted

Northern Wei Example:

  • Empress Dowager Hu built Monastery of Eternal Tranquility (516)
  • 900-foot wooden pagoda, visible from 100 li away
  • Over 1,000 Buddhist temples in Luoyang by 534
  • "Luoyang became magnificent city with half-million residents, vast palaces, elegant mansions, and more than thousand Buddhist temples"

The Dangers

  • Monasteries accumulated enormous wealth (tax-exempt land)
  • Monks didn't pay taxes or perform labor service
  • By 477: 6,478 temples, 77,258 monks and nuns in the north
  • Some decades later: 2,846 temples and 82,700 clerics in the south
  • State response: Rulers twice ordered closing of monasteries, return of monks to lay life
  • But suppression never lasted—Buddhism too popular

Rulers patronized Buddhism because monasteries, rituals, sacred images, public piety, and religious prestige could help legitimize their rule. Patronage then helped Buddhist institutions accumulate land, wealth, clergy, and influence, making them increasingly important to both courts and communities.

That growth also created problems for governments. Monasteries often held tax-exempt land, while monks and nuns could be exempt from taxation, labor service, and military obligations. Large institutions outside ordinary state obligations could reduce revenue, manpower, and administrative control, so some rulers imposed regulation or suppression.

This is a push-pull relationship: rulers valued Buddhist institutions but also feared their autonomy and resources. Suppression did not necessarily mean rejection of Buddhist belief, and Buddhism’s repeated return after suppression shows how deeply rooted it had become.

Faxian's Pilgrimage

399–414 CE

  • Chinese monk, already over 60 years old
  • Walked overland to India through Kucha, Khotan, Kashgar
  • Sought Vinaya (discipline) texts
  • Returned by sea through Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia
  • By 414 back in Nanjing, spent years translating sutras

Shows Chinese becoming active participants, not just recipients!

×

Faxian (法顯, c. 337–422)

Chinese Buddhist monk who traveled to India seeking authentic texts, especially monastic discipline rules (Vinaya).

Significance: His journey shows Chinese weren't just passively receiving Buddhism—they were actively seeking authentic sources, willing to risk death for texts. "Pilgrimage logic: we need the Vinaya texts." Shows China plugged into Indian Buddhist centers as equals.

Faxian was a Chinese monk, already more than sixty years old, who left China in 399 to seek authoritative Buddhist texts in India. He especially wanted the Vinaya, the rules governing monastic discipline, because Chinese Buddhist communities needed reliable guidance for organizing the lives of monks and nuns.

He traveled west overland through the Central Asian oasis routes to India, then returned by sea through Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. After reaching China, he spent years translating the texts he had collected.

By the early fifth century, Chinese Buddhists were no longer merely receiving Buddhism from outsiders. They were actively seeking texts, traveling internationally, translating scriptures, and participating in the wider Buddhist world.

A Transformed World

Political division transformed Chinese civilization culturally, ethnically, geographically, and religiously.

  • Lecture 1: Political unity collapsed, but new institutions and elite cultures emerged
  • Lecture 2: Non-Chinese rule transformed both conquerors and Chinese society
  • Lecture 3: Buddhism became deeply rooted in China through adaptation, lay participation, patronage, art, pilgrimage, and state regulation

The Bottom Line:

Political division produced cultural integration and Buddhist transformation.

Across the three lectures, political unity first broke down, then northern conquest and migration reshaped both conquerors and Chinese society. Buddhism became deeply rooted through adaptation, lay participation, monastic institutions, art, pilgrimage, royal patronage, and conflict with the state.

The larger point is that the Period of Division was not simply an age of political decline. Fragmentation also produced institutional, ethnic, geographic, cultural, and religious transformation.

The Period of Division Mattered

This wasn't a "dark age" of decline.
It was an era of transformation.

Chinese civilization didn't just survive conquest and division—
it incorporated new religious communities,
sacred images, monastic institutions,
and practices of salvation into Chinese life.

"The conquest of Buddhism was also the making of Chinese Buddhism."

Calling the Period of Division a “dark age” is inadequate because political fragmentation coexisted with major social, institutional, artistic, and religious innovation. Buddhism was not simply imposed on China. Chinese monks, nuns, rulers, donors, artists, families, and lay believers selected, supported, translated, and reshaped Buddhist practices.

“The conquest of Buddhism was also the making of Chinese Buddhism” means two things happened together: Buddhism spread widely across China, and Chinese communities transformed the imported religion into forms that worked within Chinese social and political life.

Enrichment: Making Buddhism Chinese

The Translation Problem

How do you explain Indian concepts in Chinese?

  • Early translators used Daoist vocabulary
  • Nirvana → "non-action" (wu wei)
  • Dharma → "the Way" (dao)
  • Geyi (格義): Matching concepts—helpful but created confusion

The Core Cast of Translators

  • Dao'an (312–385): Institutional builder, scripture cataloging
  • Kumārajīva (344–413): Translation revolution in Chang'an, elegant Chinese style
  • Huiyuan (334–416): Southern elite Buddhism, argued for sangha autonomy
  • Faxian (travel 399–414): Pilgrimage to India for texts
×

Geyi (格義) - "Matching Concepts"

Interpretive strategy used by Chinese intellectuals to explain Buddhist ideas via existing categories (often Daoist terms).

Modern scholarship caution: Not just "Daoist translation method." Better understood as broader strategy in elite discussion. Sometimes in translation, often in commentary. Helpful but created distortions—nirvana isn't exactly wu wei!

Translation was not simply replacing one word with another. Translators had to explain unfamiliar Indian religious concepts through a Chinese intellectual vocabulary. Familiar terms such as dao or wu wei could make Buddhism easier to understand, but they could also distort concepts that were not exact equivalents.

Dao’an helped organize Buddhist institutions and catalog scriptures; Kumārajīva produced influential Chinese translations; Huiyuan defended monastic autonomy and cultivated elite Buddhism in the south; and Faxian traveled to obtain texts. Together, they represent different parts of the process by which Buddhism became intellectually established in China.

Forward-Looking Buddhist Traditions

These traditions began developing during and after the Period of Division and would become much more important in later centuries.

Pure Land Buddhism

  • Devotion to Amitabha Buddha
  • Rebirth in Western Paradise
  • Most accessible: even illiterate could practice
  • "Call on Buddha as many times as possible"

Chan Buddhism (later Zen)

  • Meditation and sudden enlightenment
  • Developed distinctive Chinese styles of Buddhist meditation and teaching
  • A Chinese Buddhist tradition that would become especially influential in later centuries
×

Pure Land Buddhism (淨土宗)

Devotional school focusing on Amitabha Buddha and rebirth in Western Paradise (Pure Land).

Why it succeeded: Simple practice (chanting Buddha's name), accessible to everyone (not just educated/monks), promises concrete reward (rebirth in paradise). It would become one of the most popular forms among ordinary Chinese.

×

Chan Buddhism (禪宗)

Meditation school emphasizing sudden enlightenment and direct experience. Becomes Zen in Japan.

Historical development: Chan developed within Chinese Buddhist communities through changing approaches to meditation, monastic practice, scripture, and direct religious experience. Later interpretations often emphasized spontaneity and immediacy, but Chan should not be reduced to a simple mixture of Buddhism and Daoism.

Pure Land devotional practices and early Chan-related traditions developed during and after the Period of Division, but their mature institutions belong mainly to later centuries. These slides are forward-looking: they show directions that Chinese Buddhism was beginning to take, not fully developed schools already operating throughout 200–580.

Pure Land devotion widened access through practices centered on Amitabha and rebirth in a paradise. Chan developed through changing approaches to meditation, monastic life, scripture, and direct religious experience. It should not be reduced to a simple formula of “Buddhism plus Daoism.”

Enrichment: Daoism Was Also Becoming a Religious Tradition

  • Popular Daoist communities practiced healing and communal ritual
  • Elite practitioners sought bodily immortality
  • Practices included diet, breathing exercises, meditation, alchemy, and talismans
  • Ge Hong wrote extensively about methods for extending life and achieving immortality

Daoism during the Period of Division was much more than the philosophy of Laozi and Zhuangzi.

×

Ge Hong (283–343)

Daoist scholar and practitioner who collected and explained techniques for bodily cultivation, medicines, alchemy, and the pursuit of immortality.

The philosophical ideas associated with Laozi and Zhuangzi are only part of Daoist history. During the Period of Division, organized religious Daoist traditions expanded through healing, communal ritual, meditation, bodily cultivation, medicines, alchemy, and talismans.

Ge Hong provides a concrete example of an elite practitioner who collected and explained methods for longevity and immortality. Organized religious Daoism was therefore developing rapidly at the same time Buddhism was expanding.

Enrichment: Migration Transformed Daoism Too

  • Northern religious specialists migrated south during the political upheavals
  • They encountered established southern traditions of ritual and esoteric practice
  • New revelations produced large collections of sacred scriptures
  • Traditions such as Supreme Purity and Numinous Treasure emerged

Political division moved religious ideas and specialists as well as armies and refugees.

Political upheaval pushed religious specialists and elite families from the north into southern China. There they interacted with established southern ritual and esoteric traditions, so migration did more than preserve older beliefs: it created new combinations.

The causal chain is: political upheaval → migration of religious specialists → interaction with southern traditions → new texts and revelations → new religious institutions. Supreme Purity and Numinous Treasure are examples of organized traditions that emerged from this changing environment.

Enrichment: Competition with Buddhism Changed Daoism

  • Daoists assembled increasingly organized bodies of sacred scripture
  • Some Daoist communities adopted celibate clergy and monastic institutions
  • Buddhist and Daoist leaders competed for court patronage
  • Each tradition criticized the religious claims of the other

Buddhism did not replace Daoism. Competition helped transform both traditions.

Daoists assembled more organized bodies of scripture, and some communities adopted new clerical or monastic forms. Buddhist and Daoist leaders competed for court patronage and criticized one another’s religious claims, so competition encouraged both traditions to define and strengthen their institutions.

Daoism had older Chinese roots in philosophical traditions, popular religious movements, ritual, and immortality practices. Buddhism did not create Daoism; its success accelerated and influenced Daoist institutional development.

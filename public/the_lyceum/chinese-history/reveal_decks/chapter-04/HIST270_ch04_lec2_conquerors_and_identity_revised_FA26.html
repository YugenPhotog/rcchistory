HIST 270: History of China

Chapter 4, Lecture 2

Conquerors and the Question of Identity

Non-Chinese Rule in the North

316–580 CE

📚 Context

Lecture 2 of 3 on Period of Division. Today: ethnic complexity, sinification debates, Northern Wei transformation.

🎯 Goals

Identify major non-Chinese groups. Analyze sinification vs. hybridity. Explain Xiaowen's program. Evaluate what "Chinese" meant.

The Big Question

When "barbarians" rule China,
who gets to define what "Chinese" means?

After 316 CE, north China was ruled by non-Chinese dynasties for nearly 300 years.

Chinese elite who remained had to decide:

  • Can we serve these rulers?
  • Are they legitimate?
  • What does our identity mean now?

🎯 Frame

Most ethnically complex period in Chinese history. "Chinese" identity revealed as process, not fixed essence. Modern relevance but focus on historical understanding.

Connection to Lecture Arc

  • Lecture 1: Han ended in 220; Three Kingdoms stalemate; Western Jin collapsed in 316.
  • Lecture 2 (Today): Nearly three centuries when various non-Chinese dynasties ruled northern China—transforming both conquerors AND Chinese identity.
  • Lecture 3: How Buddhism—another "foreign" import—conquered Chinese hearts/minds during same period.

📚 Arc Logic

L1=political collapse. L2=identity complexity. L3=religious transformation. Together show complete restructuring of Chinese civilization.

Review Question Roadmap

  • Q2: Which military, technological, and demographic changes helped non-Chinese regimes dominate the north?
  • Q3: How did Northern Wei combine Chinese-style institutions with Xiaowen's integration program?
  • Q4: Why did the northern garrisons revolt in 523, and how did the aftermath help produce the fubing system?
  • Q5: Why did southern military founders repeatedly fail to create stable successions?

Follow the chain: conquest advantage → state-building → military fracture → new hybrid institutions.

Who Were the "Barbarians"?

Not a single group—diverse peoples with different histories.

The Five Barbarian Groups (Wu Hu)

  • Xiongnu — Remember from Han Dynasty; many settled inside China by 300 CE
  • Xianbei — Northern nomads; would become dominant (Northern Wei)
  • Jie — Small group; brutal conflicts both ways
  • Di — Northwestern peoples, some agricultural
  • Qiang — Western peoples, pastoral and agricultural

Important: These weren't racial categories—they were political and cultural identities that shifted over time.

×

Five Barbarians (Wu Hu 五胡)

Chinese classificatory label, not stable ethnographic map. These identities were fluid—families could shift between categories through political alliance, marriage, or relocation.

Key point: "Hu" often means "people from north/west" or "non-Han" in documents, but same person could be labeled differently depending on context (office, marriage, residence).

🎯 Key Points

Don't memorize all five. Focus: Xiongnu (continuity from Han), Xianbei (Northern Wei). Challenge assumption that conquest was simple "barbarians vs. civilization." These are administrative/political labels as much as ethnic ones.

Why Northern Conquest Succeeded

  • Stirrup and armored cavalry: mounted warriors gained greater stability and striking power
  • Military organization: steppe leaders mobilized experienced cavalry networks
  • Demographic change: many northern peoples had lived inside or along China's frontiers for generations
  • Political fragmentation: Jin collapse left rival states competing for soldiers, grain, and legitimacy

Technology mattered because institutions and political conditions made it usable at scale.

The Xiongnu Legacy

  • Remember them from Han Dynasty (heqin marriage alliances)
  • After confederation collapsed, many groups settled inside China
  • By 300 CE: Xiongnu had lived in border regions for generations
  • Some spoke Chinese, knew Chinese classics, used Chinese names

Case Study

Liu Yuan founded a Xiongnu-led state called Han and claimed a connection to the earlier Han imperial house; forces serving his regime, including Shi Le's army, captured Luoyang in 311.

×

Liu Yuan and the Disaster of Yongjia (311)

Liu Yuan founded the Han-Zhao regime and claimed Han dynastic legitimacy. He died in 310. In 311, armies of the regime, including forces led by Shi Le, captured and plundered Luoyang.

Identity complexity: Liu Yuan claimed Han imperial descent, used Chinese dynastic name, but led Xiongnu confederation. Shows how blurred lines already were.

💡 Key Point

Liu Yuan exemplifies complexity: Xiongnu leader claiming Han legitimacy. Identity was already negotiable, not fixed. The 311 Luoyang sack (Disaster of Yongjia) is major trauma marker—like Roman sack of 410.

The Sixteen Kingdoms Era

304–439 CE: Chaos Before Consolidation

  • Name "Sixteen Kingdoms" is traditional but misleading—there were more
  • Dynasties rose and fell quickly; average reign very short
  • Violence endemic: cities sacked, populations massacred or enslaved
  • New rulers rapidly learned: Cannot rule North China without Chinese clerks, tax systems, and Mandate of Heaven ideology
Map of China during the Sixteen Kingdoms period, c. 350 CE

China during the Sixteen Kingdoms period, c. 350 CE

📚 Context

304-439 = fragmented northern regimes before Northern Wei unifies (439). Emphasize institutions travel: even conqueror states need clerks, taxes, ritual legitimacy. Meanwhile Han elites learn: need local patronage, negotiation, flexible identity performance.

Shi Le: Complexity in Action

His Story

  • Born Jie tribesman
  • Sold into slavery as young man
  • Rose to lead mounted bandits
  • Founded Later Zhao (319–351)
  • Could not read Chinese

His Strategy

  • Employed Chinese advisors
  • Used Chinese administration
  • Patronized Buddhism
  • Governed without reading Chinese himself

Pattern: Non-Chinese rulers needed Chinese administrative expertise.
Chinese officials needed non-Chinese patrons to survive.

×

Shi Le (274–333 CE)

Jie leader, former slave, founder of Later Zhao. One of most fascinating figures of era.

Significance: Shows you could govern North China without reading Chinese, BUT only if you employed Chinese administrators. Also converted to Buddhism (Fotudeng as advisor)—we'll see why that mattered in Lecture 3.

Ethnic violence: His successor launched persecution of ethnic Chinese, which Chinese subjects carried out "with vengeance"—violence flowed multiple directions based on who held power.

🎯 Teaching Point

Shi Le shows: (1) Conquest didn't destroy Chinese institutions—new rulers needed them. (2) Literacy question significant—governed without reading Chinese himself. (3) Mutual dependence key. (4) Ethnic violence went both ways—don't simplify.

Review Question 2

Use your lecture notes and textbook

Review Question 2: Which military, technological, and demographic changes helped non-Chinese regimes dominate the north?
  • Identify one military change, one technological change, and one demographic change.
  • Use evidence from the textbook and your lecture notes, not memory alone.
  • Turn your evidence into a one-sentence answer.
  • Be ready to explain why no single factor is enough by itself.

Suggested source area: textbook Ch. 4, Non-Chinese Dominance in the North.

Answer Key: Review Question 2

Answer: Non-Chinese regimes prevailed through a combination of military organization, technology, demographic change, and Jin political collapse. Steppe leaders could mobilize experienced cavalry networks, while the stirrup made heavily armored mounted warfare more effective. Large communities of Xiongnu, Xianbei, and other northern peoples had already lived inside or along China's frontiers for generations, so conquerors drew on settled populations familiar with Chinese territory and institutions. The breakdown of Jin authority then created the opening in which these advantages could be converted into political power; no single factor was sufficient by itself.

Textbook evidence: Patricia Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 4, p. 65, identifies the invention of the stirrup around 300 CE as a development that made cavalry more crucial in warfare.

The Northern Wei: A Different Approach

Xianbei Unification (386–439)

  • Tuoba clan of Xianbei created most durable northern dynasty
  • Founded Northern Wei in 386 from base in northern Shanxi
  • Advantage: Access to steppe cavalry AND Chinese agricultural population
  • By 439: Unified all of north China; ruled until 534

Early Strategy: Separation Model

  • Capital at Pingcheng (northern Shanxi)—closer to steppe
  • Xianbei warriors settled nearby as herders, not farmers
  • Chinese ran bureaucracy; Xianbei ran military
  • Why? Fear of being "overwhelmed" by more numerous Chinese
×

Northern Wei (386–534)

Tuoba/Xianbei dynasty that unified north (439) and experimented with centralizing reforms.

Early phase (Pingcheng): Hybrid state—steppe military organization + Chinese bureaucratic practices. Military effectiveness depended on cavalry skills maintained through pastoral life. Identity preservation: remain Xianbei, not become Chinese.

📚 Northern Wei as Anchor

Most durable northern dynasty (386-534). Early strategy: keep Chinese and Xianbei separate. Why separation? Military effectiveness depends on cavalry skills; fear of being overwhelmed; identity preservation. This changes dramatically under Xiaowen...

Northern Wei Learned to Govern Chinese Farmers

  • Conquest gave the Xianbei military power, but ruling north China required taxes and agricultural production
  • Northern Wei employed educated Chinese officials to administer farming populations
  • The state adopted the equal-field system to distribute land and stabilize production
  • Land allotments were based largely on a household's available labor

Northern Wei became durable by combining steppe military power with Chinese-style agricultural administration.

×

Equal-Field System (Juntian)

Northern Wei land-allocation system designed to keep farmland productive and households registered for taxes and labor obligations.

Allotments were based largely on available household labor, with additional allocations shaped by rank and status. The system asserted the state's authority to organize land and taxpayers.

🎯 Teaching Point

The important issue is not simply that Xianbei rulers adopted Chinese customs. They also had to solve the practical problem of governing a large agricultural population.

Military conquest could seize territory, but armies alone could not produce tax revenue, maintain registers, or keep farmland productive. Northern Wei therefore relied increasingly on Chinese administrative knowledge.

The equal-field system asserted state authority over land distribution. Families received allotments largely according to their labor capacity, with additional allocations tied to rank and status.

Do not turn this into a detailed land-tenure lecture. Students need the larger institutional point: steppe military power + Chinese bureaucratic administration = a durable hybrid state.

This prepares students for Xiaowen's later reforms.

Emperor Xiaowen's Revolution

471–499 CE: Everything Changed

Born to Chinese mother (Empress Dowager Feng). Wanted to unite Chinese and Xianbei elites. Decided separation was unsustainable.

The Radical Program (493–499):

  • 1. Moved capital south from Pingcheng to Luoyang (300 miles closer to Chinese heartland)
  • 2. Banned Xianbei language and clothes at court (officials under 30 must speak Chinese)
  • 3. Encouraged intermarriage between highest Xianbei and Chinese families
  • 4. Changed surnames to Chinese ones (imperial house: Tuoba → Yuan)
  • 5. Expanded Chinese-style court and bureaucratic institutions
×

Emperor Xiaowen (r. 471–499)

Most significant figure in early medieval Chinese history, yet often overlooked.

Context: 494 capital move to Luoyang (old Han/Jin capital). 150,000 Xianbei warriors moved south. Court-driven reforms associated with "sinicization."

Modern scholarship caution: Don't frame as "they became Chinese." Frame as state centralization + elite competition. Reforms strengthened bureaucratic center, reoriented court culture, BUT produced backlash among frontier military elites.

🎯 Xiaowen's Program

One of most significant figures in Chinese history. Program controversial AMONG Xianbei—many resisted. Note Empress Dowager Feng's influence (women's political power). Modern framing: this is governing strategy, not simple cultural conversion. The equal-field system, adopted earlier under Northern Wei, was a state attempt to stabilize taxation by tying people to land/registers.

The Capital Move: Geography as Strategy

Map of the Northern Wei capital relocation from Pingcheng to Luoyang in 494 CE

Northern Wei capital relocation, 494 CE

Moving the capital was geopolitical statement: We are not steppe warriors anymore—we are Chinese emperors.

💡 Geography Matters

300-mile move = major geopolitical shift. Pingcheng close to steppe (military advantage, identity connection). Luoyang = traditional capital (Han/Jin), symbolic legitimacy, Chinese heartland. Cost: distance from steppe weakens cavalry recruitment. Benefit: access to agricultural resources, Chinese elites, legitimacy.

Review Question 3

Use your lecture notes and textbook

Review Question 3: How did Northern Wei combine Chinese-style institutions with Xiaowen's integration program?
  • List two Chinese-style institutions or governing tools Northern Wei used.
  • List two Xiaowen reforms that pushed elite integration.
  • Explain how these policies helped the state rule north China.
  • Add one complication: who might have resisted these changes, and why?

Suggested source area: textbook Ch. 4, The Northern Wei and Hybrid Xianbei-Chinese Culture.

Answer Key: Review Question 3

Answer: Northern Wei paired Xianbei military power with Chinese-style administration. Educated Chinese officials helped govern farming populations, and the equal-field system allocated land largely by household labor capacity to stabilize production, registration, taxation, and service. Emperor Xiaowen deepened elite integration by moving the capital to Luoyang, requiring younger Xianbei officials to speak Chinese at court, banning Xianbei court dress, adopting Chinese surnames, encouraging intermarriage, and expanding Chinese-style court institutions. These measures strengthened central rule and access to the agricultural heartland, but they also reduced the status of frontier Xianbei soldiers and helped provoke resistance. The result was a hybrid state, not simple cultural replacement.

Textbook evidence: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 4, p. 66, describes Xiaowen's court-language and clothing rules and his encouragement of intermarriage between leading Chinese and Xianbei families.

The Hybrid Reality

Despite Xiaowen's program, result was more complex than "becoming Chinese"

Xianbei Culture Persisted

  • Language in private/family contexts
  • Military culture retained steppe elements
  • Hunting/martial skills remained elite markers
  • Buddhism appealed to both groups

Chinese Culture Transformed

  • Northern Chinese adopted steppe elements
  • Cavalry warfare became central
  • Sitting on chairs spread (Central Asian?)
  • Diet, clothing, music showed hybrid influences

This wasn't "Chinese win" or "Xianbei win"—it was mutual transformation.

💡 Challenge Simple Narratives

Despite Xiaowen's reforms, Xianbei culture didn't disappear. Chinese culture also transformed—northern Chinese adopted steppe elements. Challenge: "Chinese absorbed barbarians" narrative. Reality: both changed. This hybrid culture will reunify China (Sui/Tang).

The Revolt of the Garrisons, 523

  • Northern frontier troops lost status after the Xianbei court and elite moved south to Luoyang
  • Worsening conditions and food shortages triggered rebellion in the northern garrisons
  • The revolt expanded into civil war and split Northern Wei into eastern and western successor states
  • Western commanders rebuilt military service around mixed Xianbei-Chinese units

Crisis in the old garrison order helped produce the hybrid fubing system.

Gao Huan: The Perfect Hybrid

  • Han Chinese by ancestry, but raised among Xianbei
  • Married into Xianbei family
  • Could appeal to both ethnic communities
  • Became powerful warlord in Eastern Wei sphere

His speeches to each group

To Xianbei: "The Han are your slaves... they till and weave for you."

To Chinese: "The Xianbei are your retainers... they fight bandits so you are safe."

Both statements were partially true. Both communities needed each other.

×

Gao Huan (496–547)

Han Chinese by ancestry but raised among Xianbei. Perfect example of hybrid identity in northern society.

Political genius: His speech to each group shows remarkable manipulation of ethnic tension. He tells each side they're superior, but frames superiority differently (military vs. economic). Shows he understood mutual dependence.

Significance: Represents new generation of elites who could operate in both worlds—neither purely Xianbei nor purely Chinese, but something new.

🎯 Gao Huan's Significance

Perfect hybrid—neither purely Chinese nor Xianbei. His speeches show political manipulation of ethnic tension but also reveal truth: mutual dependence. He represents new generation of elites who will reunify China. His Eastern Wei faction leads to Northern Qi.

Northern Wei Split into Two Different Hybrid Regimes

East: Gao Huan

  • Built power from the eastern garrison world
  • Drew heavily on Xianbei military communities
  • Balanced Chinese agricultural producers with Xianbei soldiers
  • His regime became the basis of Eastern Wei and Northern Qi

West: Yuwen Tai

  • Controlled the Chang'an region
  • Had fewer Xianbei soldiers available
  • Incorporated more Chinese into military service and elite networks
  • His regime became the basis of Western Wei and Northern Zhou

Both regimes were hybrid, but the western state had stronger incentives to integrate Chinese and Xianbei military elites.

🎯 Teaching Point

Students already understand Gao Huan as a hybrid political figure. Yuwen Tai supplies the missing western half of the story.

The important distinction is institutional. Gao Huan could rely on a larger Xianbei military population in the east. Yuwen Tai had fewer Xianbei available in the northwest and therefore had stronger incentives to incorporate Chinese families into military and political structures.

Yuwen Tai encouraged intermarriage and granted Xianbei surnames to leading Chinese officials. The result was not simply "sinification." In some cases Chinese elites were being incorporated into Xianbei-style military culture.

This is the immediate context for the creation of the fubing system on the next slide.

Do not overload students with the detailed succession of Eastern Wei, Western Wei, Northern Qi, and Northern Zhou. The two-column structure should make the lineage visible without requiring memorization of every date.

The Divisional Militia (Fubing)

  • Created in northwest where Xianbei and Chinese most mixed
  • Multiethnic military system
  • Soldiers registered separately from regular taxpayers—honorable status
  • 577: Northern Zhou defeated Northern Qi and reunified northern China

Why This Matters

The hybrid military institutions created during division became tools of reunification. Fubing later became a foundation of Sui and Tang military organization.

×

Fubing (府兵) - Divisional Militia

Military system associated with Western Wei/Northern Zhou reforms under Yuwen Tai. Matters for Sui/Tang military state.

Key features: Multiethnic; registered separately (honorable status); combined steppe and Chinese elements; emerged from hybrid northwestern society.

Bridge forward: This system will appear again in Tang lectures as foundation of early Tang military power.

💡 Fubing Key

Multiethnic military system from hybrid northwest. Important because: (1) Shows successful integration, (2) Will help reunify China, (3) Foundation for Sui/Tang. Don't get bogged in details—just note it exists and matters. Yuwen Tai's western regime developed the institutional foundation; Northern Zhou later used this military system to reunify the north in 577, creating an important bridge to Sui reunification.

Review Question 4

Use your lecture notes and textbook

Review Question 4: Why did the northern garrisons revolt in 523, and how did the aftermath help produce the fubing system?
  • Identify the immediate causes of the garrison revolt.
  • Explain how Xiaowen's reforms changed the status of frontier soldiers.
  • Trace the aftermath: Northern Wei split into eastern and western regimes.
  • Connect Yuwen Tai's western regime to the emergence of fubing.

Suggested source area: textbook Ch. 4, Revolt of the Garrisons and the Division of the North.

Answer Key: Review Question 4

Answer: After the Northern Wei court and elite moved from the frontier to Luoyang, soldiers in the northern garrisons lost prestige and endured worsening material conditions. Food shortages sparked rebellion in 523, and the revolt widened into civil war that divided Northern Wei into eastern and western successor regimes. In the west, Yuwen Tai had fewer Xianbei soldiers available and therefore incorporated more Chinese families into military service and elite networks. This reconstruction produced the fubing, a multiethnic divisional militia whose soldiers held a distinct, honorable status. The system helped Northern Zhou reunify the north and later influenced Sui and Tang military organization.

Textbook evidence: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 4, p. 67, explains that after the Xianbei elite transferred to Luoyang, the northern garrison forces saw their status fall to little better than hereditary military households.

The South Became an Economic Center as Well as a Refuge

  • Northern migrants opened new farmland throughout the Yangzi region
  • Agriculture and commercial networks expanded
  • Southern ports traded with Funan, Champa, India, and regions farther west
  • Han Chinese settlement placed increasing pressure on indigenous peoples outside the major river valleys

Political division accelerated a long-term shift of population and economic activity toward southern China.

🎯 Teaching Point

Do not let students interpret the Southern Dynasties merely as a politically weak refuge for displaced aristocrats.

The south was becoming more economically important. Migration brought labor, skills, elite families, texts, administrative practices, and capital. Agricultural settlement expanded, while maritime trade connected southern China more strongly to Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean world.

Funan and Champa are useful examples, but students do not need to memorize them as dynastic names. Their purpose is to show that southern China's economic world extended beyond the traditional North China heartland.

Also note the cost of this expansion. Han Chinese settlement increasingly pushed indigenous populations toward upland regions or encouraged assimilation.

This development matters beyond Chapter 4 because the demographic and economic importance of southern China will continue to grow in later Chinese history.

The Southern Perspective

Eastern Jin at Nanjing (317–420)

Northern Dynasties Southern Dynasties
Non-Chinese military elite Chinese refugee aristocracy
Hybrid culture emerging "Pure" Chinese culture claimed
More unified (Northern Wei) Constant dynastic change
Buddhist-Confucian-Daoist mix Buddhist flourishing + aristocratic culture

Southern Elite Culture:

  • Great families maintained genealogies obsessively
  • Married only within families of equivalent pedigree
  • Nine Rank System helped powerful families preserve access to high office
  • BUT: Couldn't control their own generals (four dynasties in two centuries)

Military strongmen could seize and govern a dynasty during their own lifetimes, but they could not reliably transfer power to heirs or subordinate other armed rivals.

📚 South Briefly

Eastern Jin = refugee government at Nanjing (Jiankang). Southern dynasties: Song, Qi, Liang, Chen (constant military coups). Elite culture: genealogies, pedigree, "pure" Chinese culture claimed. Irony: "purity" claim despite military weakness. Military problem: generals repeatedly seized power; military households near-slaves.

Review Question 5

Use your lecture notes and textbook

Review Question 5: Why did southern military founders repeatedly fail to create stable successions?
  • Use the Southern Perspective slide and textbook to identify the political problem.
  • Explain why a general could seize power but struggle to pass it securely to heirs.
  • Connect the problem to armed commanders, aristocratic families, and dynastic turnover.
  • Write a two-sentence answer: cause first, consequence second.

Suggested source area: textbook Ch. 4, The Southern Dynasties.

Answer Key: Review Question 5

Answer: Southern dynasties were repeatedly founded by generals who commanded enough armed force to seize the capital and hold a regime together during their own lifetimes. They did not, however, build institutions strong enough to subordinate rival commanders, control powerful aristocratic families, and transfer military and political authority securely to their heirs. Succession therefore reopened competition among armed strongmen, producing coups, short-lived dynasties, and repeated turnover at Jiankang.

Textbook evidence: Ebrey, A History of China, ch. 4, p. 68, states that these generals could hold their governments together during their lifetimes but could not concentrate power sufficiently to ensure successful transfers to their heirs.

Who Won the Identity War?

By 580 CE, north China had been ruled by non-Chinese dynasties for over 250 years. But something remarkable happened:

The lines between "Chinese" and "non-Chinese" had blurred beyond recognition.

  • The Xianbei who conquered were themselves transformed
  • The Chinese who remained incorporated steppe influences
  • New hybrid elites emerged who could operate in both worlds
  • A dynasty emerging from the hybrid northwest, the Sui, was founded in 581 and reunified China in 589.

Who counts as Chinese? The Period of Division showed Chinese identity was never simply about blood or birth. It was about culture, language, values—and these could be adopted, adapted, and transformed.

🎯 Synthesis

By 580: lines blurred beyond recognition. Xianbei transformed, Chinese transformed, hybrid elites emerged. Answer to opening question: Chinese identity is process, not fixed essence. Conquest dynasties didn't destroy civilization—they became part of it. Sui/Tang preview: these centuries mattered for what came next.

Grandfather and Grandson

Kangxi Won Over the Conquered; His Grandson Qianlong Showed a Different Face to Every People, and Hunted Disloyal Books

1661–1799 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome! This deck is your Study Buddy for the Qing at its height.

The story in one breath. A boy who had survived smallpox became the Kangxi emperor in 1661. He won over the Chinese elite, crushed the last great revolt, and closed the northern frontier for good. Sixty years later his grandson, the Qianlong emperor, ruled the largest empire in Chinese history. He showed a different face to each of its peoples, yet he hunted sorcerers who did not exist and destroyed thousands of books (Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 274–277).

The two portraits are the official court likenesses of the grandfather, left, painted late in his life, and the grandson, right, painted when he was young.

Q5 hook · Beijing · 1700s

The Qing Wrote Its Name in Four Languages

The Qing government issued documents in the four main languages of its realm.
On this plaque at a government-sponsored temple in Beijing, the name appears in Manchu, then Chinese, then Tibetan, then Mongolian.
One dynasty addressed many peoples, each in its own script.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274.

Start with a single object. This is the name plaque over a hall of a government-sponsored Buddhist temple in Beijing. Read it from right to left: the name is written first in Manchu, then Chinese, the three gold characters in the middle, then Tibetan, and finally Mongolian (Ebrey, p. 274).

The Qing government issued its documents in these four main languages of its realm. That is a clue to everything in this lecture. The Manchus were a small minority ruling a huge and varied empire, and they spoke to each people in its own terms.

The temple is the Yonghe Temple, often called the Lama Temple; that name comes from sources beyond Ebrey. Keep this plaque in mind when we reach the Qianlong emperor on slide o.

Q3 story · Beijing · 1661

A Boy Who Had Survived Smallpox Became Emperor

The Shunzhi emperor died of smallpox, a disease that struck many Manchus after they settled in Beijing.
One of his sons, who had already survived the disease, was chosen to succeed him.
As the Kangxi emperor (r. 1661–1722), he lived to see the Qing Empire firmly established.
He is the boy promised at the end of the conquest story · L1 w · next ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274.

Our first lecture ended with a promise: a boy who survived smallpox would make the conquest permanent. Here he is. The link on the slide takes you back to that slide.

The man in the portrait is his father, the Shunzhi emperor, the boy-emperor whose uncle Dorgon had taken Beijing in 1644. Shunzhi died of smallpox, which struck many Manchus once they settled in crowded Beijing. To choose a successor, the court picked one of his sons who had already survived the disease and so could not catch it again (Ebrey, p. 274).

That son reigned as the Kangxi emperor from 1661 to 1722, sixty-one years, and lived to see the Qing firmly established.

Q3 and Q5 names · Three emperors, three kinds of name

Names Box: Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong Are Reign Names

Reign Name · Personal Name · Temple Name

Kangxi r. 1661–1722 · personal name Xuanye · temple name Shengzu

Yongzheng r. 1723–1735, Kangxi's son · personal name Yinzhen · temple name Shizong

Qianlong r. 1736–1795, Yongzheng's son and Kangxi's grandson · personal name Hongli · temple name Gaozong

"Kangxi," "Yongzheng," and "Qianlong" are the names of reign periods, not the emperors' own names.
Each of these emperors kept one reign name for his whole reign, so the name came to stand for the man.
Other books may use their personal names, used before they took the throne, or their temple names, given after death.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 274, 276.

A quick word on names, because you will meet these three emperors under more than one.

"Kangxi" is not a personal name. It is the name of a reign period, a label for the years of his rule, used on calendars and documents. The textbook calls him "the Kangxi emperor," meaning "the emperor of the Kangxi era" (Ebrey, p. 274). Under the Qing each emperor kept one reign name for his whole reign, so the label came to stand for the man.

Kangxi's son ruled as the Yongzheng emperor, and his grandson as the Qianlong emperor (p. 276).

The personal names, Xuanye, Yinzhen, and Hongli, and the temple names, Shengzu, Shizong, and Gaozong, come from sources beyond Ebrey. You will see them in other books, and in museum labels.

Q3 story · The palace · 1660s

As a Teenager Kangxi Jailed His Regent and Ruled for Himself

Kangxi proved adept at meeting the expectations of both the Manchu and the Chinese elites.
In 1667, at thirteen, he announced that he would rule on his own; in 1669 he had his regent imprisoned.
He could speak, read, and write Chinese.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274.

A child emperor needed regents, senior Manchus who ruled in his name. In 1667, at thirteen, Kangxi announced that he would begin ruling on his own, and in 1669, at fifteen, he had his regent imprisoned (Ebrey, p. 274). Think about the nerve that took.

The regent was Oboi, a veteran banner general; his name, and the portrait identified as him, come from sources beyond Ebrey. Oboi kept power in his own hands even after the emperor took charge, so the arrest was the real start of Kangxi's rule.

From the start, Kangxi had to satisfy two audiences. The Manchu elite expected a warrior khan. The Chinese elite expected a scholar-ruler. Kangxi could do both. He could speak, read, and write Chinese, and he understood what educated Chinese needed to hear.

Q3 concept · Legitimacy

Concept: Legitimacy Meant Persuading People a Ruler Had the Right to Rule

Concept

Legitimacy

The belief, shared by the people who are ruled, that a ruler has the right to rule, so that they obey because they accept him and not only because they fear him.

Kangxi appreciated the value of persuading educated Chinese that the Manchus had a legitimate claim to the Mandate of Heaven.
The Mandate of Heaven was the old Zhou idea that Heaven gives the right to rule to a virtuous ruler and takes it from a bad one · Ch. 1 L3 · Mandate of Heaven ↗
A conquest won by force would last only if the conquered came to accept it.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274; Ch. 1 L3 for the Mandate of Heaven.

Before we look at Kangxi's strategies, we need one idea: legitimacy. A ruler is legitimate when the people he rules believe he has the right to rule. A legitimate ruler is obeyed because people accept him, not only because they fear his army.

In China the language of legitimacy was the Mandate of Heaven, the idea you met with the Zhou in Chapter 1. Heaven grants the right to rule to a virtuous ruler and withdraws it from a bad one. The link opens that slide.

The Manchus had won China by force. Kangxi understood that force alone was expensive and fragile. He saw the value of persuading educated Chinese that the Manchus had a legitimate claim to the Mandate of Heaven (Ebrey, p. 274). The next three slides show how he went about it.

Q3 story · Courting the scholars · 1660s–1700s

Kangxi Kept the Ming Exams and Courted Ming Loyalists

Most Ming political institutions were taken over relatively unchanged, including the examination system · Ch. 14 L3 · exam-video-recap ↗
Kangxi worked to attract Ming loyalists who had been unwilling to serve the Qing · L1 r · concept-loyalism ↗
He held a special examination to select men to compile the official history of the Ming dynasty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274; Ch. 14 L3 for the examinations.

How do you persuade a conquered elite that you deserve to rule? Kangxi's first answer was continuity. Most of the political institutions of the Ming were taken over relatively unchanged, including the examination system (Ebrey, p. 274). Educated men could still study the classics, sit the exams, and become officials, just as their fathers had. The link opens our exams recap from Chapter 14.

His second answer was to reach out to the hardest cases: Ming loyalists, the men you met in our first lecture who refused to serve the new dynasty.

His cleverest move was a special examination to choose scholars to compile the official history of the Ming. Writing the history of the fallen dynasty was a duty loyalists could accept with honor, and it quietly announced that the Ming was over: dynasties get official histories only after they end.

Q3 story · The southwest · 1673–1684

In 1673 Wu Sangui, the Man Who Opened the Gate, Rebelled, and Few Followed Him

Wu Sangui and two other Chinese generals had been given vast lands in the south for joining the Qing · L1 l · gate-opens ↗
When the Qing began to curb their power in 1673, Wu declared his own state, and the other two "feudatories" joined him.
A turncoat himself, Wu attracted few followers, and after eight years the Qing armies defeated the revolt.
In 1683 a Qing fleet took Taiwan, the loyalists' last refuge, and in 1684 it became part of Fujian province · L1 q · koxinga ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274.

Kangxi's great military test came from an old acquaintance. Wu Sangui was the Ming general who opened the gate in the Wall in 1644; the link takes you back. As rewards, Wu and two other Chinese generals had been given vast tracts of land in the south. Wu became, in effect, satrap of Yunnan and Guizhou, and his armies had chased the last Ming pretender into Burma (Ebrey, p. 274).

In 1673 the Qing began to curb these generals. Wu declared himself ruler of an independent state, and the other two "feudatories" joined him. Many books call this the Revolt of the Three Feudatories, a name from sources beyond Ebrey.

The south was not yet reconciled to the Qing, so why did it fail? Because Wu was a turncoat himself. Few Chinese would die for the man who let the Manchus in. It took eight years, but the Qing military held. In 1683 the admiral Shi Lang took Taiwan, where the last loyalists had held out, and in 1684 it was made part of Fujian.

The print, from sources beyond Ebrey, illustrates a novel of 1645 whose caption calls Wu a man of "solitary loyalty." Many loyalists then believed he had called in the Manchus only to crush Li Zicheng and avenge the last Ming emperor. By 1673 that hope was long gone, and that is part of why so few followed him.

Q3 story · Mongolia and the frontiers · 1680s–1720s

Guns, Grain, and Diplomacy Won Kangxi Mongolia

In 1696 Kangxi led eighty thousand men into Mongolia against the Zunghar leader Galdan, and within a few years Manchu supremacy was accepted there.
Qing armies had more and heavier cannon and muskets, and they could feed large armies far out on the steppe.
The guns came by way of the Ming: Chinese defectors brought muskets and Portuguese-style cannon, and the Jesuit Verbiest cast hundreds more · l · jesuits-court ↩
The eastern steppe was secure, though the Zunghars held out in the west until the 1750s · Ch. 14 L2 · great-wall ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 274; Ch. 14 L2 for the Ming Great Wall; Andrade, The Gunpowder Age (2016), for where the guns came from.

Now the frontiers. For centuries Chinese dynasties had worried about the steppe; the Ming built the Great Wall you studied in Chapter 14, and the link opens that slide. The textbook's verdict: "By annexing Mongolia, the Kangxi emperor made sure the Qing Dynasty would not have the northern border problems the Ming had faced" (Ebrey, p. 274).

His rival was Galdan, ruler of the Zunghar Mongols in the west, who attacked the eastern Mongols. In 1691 the eastern Mongol princes submitted to Kangxi at Dolon Nor. In 1696 Kangxi led eighty thousand men into Mongolia and crushed Galdan at Jao Modo. Galdan had muskets too; the Qing won with more and heavier guns and with grain convoys that fed a huge army far from home. The Zunghars stayed a threat until the 1750s.

Diplomacy helped: the 1689 Treaty of Nerchinsk fixed the border with Russia in Manchuria. In 1720 his armies took Lhasa in Tibet.

Where did the guns come from? Muskets reached the coast in the mid-1500s with Portuguese traders and Japanese pirates; the drawing is from a Ming encyclopedia of 1621. From 1622 the Ming bought Portuguese cannon at Macao. Then the know-how changed sides: helped by Ming defectors, Hong Taiji was casting European-style cannon by 1631. Under Kangxi the Jesuit Ferdinand Verbiest cast hundreds more; the 1689 gun on the left names him.

Why this matters: The Qing closed the steppe frontier that had troubled every dynasty before it.

Check yourself: What gave the Qing the edge over Galdan?

Instructor note: the textbook says the Mongols had "only bows and arrows," speaks of "two thousand years" of border problems, and dates Lhasa to 1718 (p. 274). Galdan used muskets; the 1718 force was destroyed, and Lhasa fell in 1720 (Perdue, 2005; Petech, 1972).

Before we move on to the grandson, a short side story that the Reading Guide asks about. Kangxi was curious. He took a personal interest in the European Jesuit priests who served at his court (Ebrey, p. 276).

The Jesuits were a Catholic religious order. In Beijing they served the emperor as astronomers, keeping the calendar accurate, and as cartographers, or mapmakers. They also translated many European works into Chinese.

Rites preview · Rome and Beijing · early 1700s

When the Pope Forbade Christians to Honor Their Ancestors, Kangxi Turned Against the Missionaries

Dominican and Franciscan missionaries opposed letting Chinese converts keep their ancestral altars.
The pope sided with them in what was called the rites controversy, and Kangxi objected strongly to a pope telling Chinese how to behave.
He let stay only missionaries who accepted the Jesuit view of the rites, later said none should preach, and kept the Jesuit scientists and painters · bb · ps-rites-dispute ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Here is how the Jesuit story ended. Not every missionary in China was a Jesuit. The Dominican and Franciscan orders opposed allowing Chinese converts to keep their ancestral altars, the household shrines where families honored their dead (Ebrey, p. 276).

The dispute went all the way to Rome. When the pope sided with the Dominicans and Franciscans, in what came to be called the rites controversy, Kangxi objected strongly. Who was a foreign priest to issue directives about how Chinese should behave?

So Kangxi turned against the missionaries. From 1706 only those who accepted the Jesuit view and took out a permit could stay. In 1721 he wrote that Europeans should no longer preach in China, and in 1724 his son banned Christianity outright. Jesuit scientists and painters stayed in Beijing, because the emperors valued their skills.

That is all you need for today. The independent study, from slide bb, explains the dispute in depth and ends with its Review Only question.

Q5 story · Central Asia · 1750s

Kangxi's Grandson Qianlong Conquered Xinjiang and Ruled It Lightly

Under Qianlong (r. 1736–1795) the Qing Empire reached its maximum extent with the addition of Chinese Turkestan, modern Xinjiang.
The Qing won the region in the 1750s in campaigns against Uighur and Dzungar Mongol forces.
Like Tibet, it was ruled lightly: its people kept their own religious leaders and did not have to wear the queue · L1 o · queue-order ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

Now jump forward a generation. Kangxi's grandson ruled as the Qianlong emperor from 1736 to 1795. Under him the empire reached its greatest size, with the addition of Chinese Turkestan, today's province of Xinjiang (Ebrey, p. 276).

Han and Tang troops had been stationed there, but neither the Song nor the Ming had tried to control the region. The Qing won it in the 1750s, in a series of campaigns against Uighur and Dzungar Mongol forces.

Then notice how it was ruled: lightly. The local population kept their own religious leaders, and men did not have to wear the queue. Compare that with 1645 in China proper, where refusing the queue could cost a man his life. The link takes you back.

The engraving shows one battle of that war, as Qianlong had it commemorated. He had his court artists design the scenes and sent them to Paris to be engraved; those details come from sources beyond Ebrey.

Q5 story · A many-faced emperor · 1740s–1790s

Qianlong Spoke to Each People in Its Own Language and Faith

Qianlong understood that holding the empire together meant speaking in the political and religious idioms of those he ruled · b · four-scripts ↩
Besides Manchu and Chinese, he learned to converse in Mongolian, Uighur, and Tibetan and addressed envoys in their own languages.
He was as much a patron of Tibetan Buddhism as of Confucianism, and had the Tibetan Buddhist canon translated into Mongolian and Manchu.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

Remember the four-language plaque on slide b? Here is the emperor behind it. Qianlong put much of his energy into impressing his subjects with his magnificence, and he understood that the Qing capacity to hold the empire together rested on speaking "in the political and religious idioms of those they ruled" (Ebrey, p. 276).

So he learned languages. Besides Manchu and Chinese, he could converse in Mongolian, Uighur, and Tibetan, and he addressed envoys in their own tongues.

And he patronized religions. He was as much a patron of Lamaist, that is Tibetan, Buddhism as of Chinese Confucianism. He launched a huge project to translate the Tibetan Buddhist canon into Mongolian and Manchu.

Look at the painting. On the left is the whole thangka, a Tibetan Buddhist religious painting, with Qianlong at the center of rows of Buddhist deities. On the right is a close-up of the center. To Tibetan and Mongol Buddhists, Qianlong appears here as a bodhisattva, Manjushri, the embodiment of wisdom. He wears a monk's cap and holds the wheel of the Buddhist law. His face was painted in a lifelike European style, probably by the Jesuit court painter Giuseppe Castiglione.

Instructor note: the textbook also says he had "the child Dalai Lamas raised and educated in Beijing." Specialists would not say this of the Dalai Lamas. The court did raise high incarnate lamas in Beijing, such as Changkya Rolpe Dorje (1717–1786) (Berger, 2003). It is left off the slide.

Q5 concept · Cakravartin

Concept: The Cakravartin Was the Buddhist "Wheel-Turning King"

Concept

Cakravartin

The Buddhist "wheel-turning king": a ruler whose conquests move the world toward the next stage in universal salvation.

Qianlong made much of the Buddhist idea of the cakravartin.
To Buddhist Tibetans and Mongols, his conquests could look like holy work, not mere conquest.
To Chinese scholars he showed a different face, the sage emperor of the next slide.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

One Buddhist idea did a great deal of work for Qianlong: the cakravartin, Sanskrit for "wheel-turning king." In Buddhist thought, the cakravartin is a ruler whose conquests move the world toward the next stage in universal salvation (Ebrey, p. 276).

Think about what that did for an emperor who had just conquered Xinjiang and controlled Tibet. To Buddhist Tibetans and Mongols, his wars could be understood not as conquest but as turning the wheel of the Buddhist law. The thangka on the last slide makes the same point visually.

Here is the key to Qianlong: he did not have one public face. He had one for each people he ruled. To Buddhists he was a wheel-turning king and a bodhisattva. To Chinese scholars, as the next slide shows, he was a poet, a collector, and a model son.

Q5 story · The palace and the south · 1740s–1780s

For Chinese Scholars He Played Poet, Collector, and Devoted Son

To show the scholar-officials he was a sage emperor, he worked on state affairs until early afternoon, then turned to reading, painting, and calligraphy.
He took credit for more than forty-two thousand poems and inscribed his own verses on hundreds of masterpieces in the palace collections.
He was ostentatiously devoted to his mother, visiting her daily, and toured the south partly to entertain her.
His southern tours cost ten times what Kangxi's had · h · southern-tours ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

Now the Chinese face. To demonstrate to the scholar-official elite that he was a sage emperor, Qianlong kept a scholar's schedule: state affairs from dawn to early afternoon, then reading, painting, and calligraphy (Ebrey, p. 276).

He took credit for more than forty-two thousand poems and ninety-two books of prose. He gathered masterpieces of painting and calligraphy into the palace and inscribed his own poems on hundreds of them. You can still see his inscriptions and seals crowding the margins of famous paintings.

He also played the perfect Chinese son, visiting his mother daily and tending to her comfort. He toured down the Grand Canal to Jiangnan partly to emulate his grandfather and partly to entertain her. The tours were extravagant: temporary palaces, triumphal arches, and ten times the cost of Kangxi's tours.

Now the other side of Qianlong. For all his displays of Chinese virtue, he still was not fully confident that the Chinese supported his rule, and he was quick to act on any suspicion of anti-Manchu thoughts or actions (Ebrey, p. 276).

Q5 concept · Literary inquisition

Concept: A Literary Inquisition Hunted Disloyal Words

Concept

Literary inquisition

A government campaign that searches writings for disloyal words and punishes the people who wrote them, or destroys the books.

Qianlong was quick to act on any suspicion of anti-Manchu thoughts or actions.
In a literary inquisition, words themselves were treated as evidence of disloyalty.
The danger was older than Qianlong: under Kangxi, the scholar Fang Bao spent two years in prison for writing a preface to a condemned book.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 276, 278.

One more idea before the climax: a literary inquisition. An inquisition is an official investigation hunting for disloyalty. In a literary inquisition the target is writing: books, poems, prefaces. Words themselves become evidence of crime.

Qianlong did not invent the danger. In 1711, under Kangxi, the scholar Fang Bao and his family were arrested because he had written a preface for a friend's collected works, and the friend's writings had just been condemned for language implying support for a Ming revival. Fang spent two years in prison before he was pardoned. He later wrote a vivid account of the prison, which the textbook prints (Ebrey, p. 278).

Qianlong took this kind of hunt and made it enormous, as the next slide shows.

Q5 story · The imperial library · 1770s

While Collecting Every Book in China, He Destroyed More Than Two Thousand

Qianlong sponsored the Complete Books of the Four Treasuries, an effort to catalogue nearly all books in China.
Suspecting governors of holding back seditious books, he ordered full searches for slurs on the Manchus or earlier alien conquerors.
Passages were cut or rewritten, and books judged wholly offensive were destroyed.
No copies survive of more than two thousand titles.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

A few years after the queue-clipping panic, Qianlong carried out a huge literary inquisition (Ebrey, p. 276).

It grew out of his grandest scholarly project, the Complete Books of the Four Treasuries, an effort to catalogue nearly all the books in China and copy the best of them. Provinces were ordered to send in books. Qianlong began to suspect that some governors were holding back books with seditious content.

So, in the textbook's words: "He ordered full searches for books with disparaging references to the Manchus or previous alien conquerors." Those earlier conquerors included the Jurchen Jin and the Mongol Yuan. Sometimes passages were omitted or rewritten. When an entire book was offensive, it was destroyed.

The result: no copies survive of more than two thousand titles. The same project that preserved China's books destroyed a part of them. The volumes in the photo are replicas of one of the original manuscript sets.

The Conquerors' Grandsons Lived Behind Garrison Walls on State Stipends

All three emperors used the banners to keep military control; bannermen lived in more than ninety walled garrisons · L1 y · ps-garrisons ↗

Two short slides on the bannermen, because they were the backbone of everything Kangxi and Qianlong did. You studied them in depth in the independent study of our first lecture; these slides only recap, and the links take you back.

The Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong emperors all used the banner system to maintain military control and preserve the Manchus' privileges. Bannermen lived in more than ninety walled garrisons. Banners recap · Garrison cities · 1700s

Forbidden Other Work, Many Bannermen Fell Idle and into Debt

The banner population grew faster than the need for soldiers, but bannermen could not take other jobs · L1 ee · ps-too-many ↗
Many lived in forced idleness on relatives' stipends; debts mounted and banner land was sold · L1 ff · ps-debt ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 277–278.

And the problem. Once the conquest was over, the banner population grew faster than the need for soldiers. Yet bannermen were not allowed to pursue any occupation other than soldier or official. Many led lives of forced idleness, surviving on stipends paid to relatives (Ebrey, p. 277).

Debts followed. The government forgave bannermen's debts from time to time, but many went bankrupt, and company commanders sometimes sold off banner land (p. 278).

By Qianlong's time this was serious enough that he had many Chinese bannermen, especially in the provincial garrisons, removed from the banners and reclassified as commoners. He also tried resettling Manchus in Manchuria, but most sneaked back to the cities.

Closing · The court · 1770s–1799

In Old Age Qianlong Trusted a Corrupt Favorite, Then Gave Up the Throne

In his sixties Qianlong began to favor Heshen, a handsome and intelligent young imperial bodyguard.
Heshen was rapidly promoted to posts over revenue and appointments, and the emperor did nothing to stop his blatant corruption.

Every reign ends. Qianlong lived into his eighties, but his judgment began to fail in his sixties, when he began to favor a handsome and intelligent young imperial bodyguard named Heshen (Ebrey, p. 277).

Heshen was rapidly promoted to posts normally held by experienced civil officials, including posts with power over revenue and civil-service appointments. When the emperor did nothing to stop his blatant corruption, officials began to worry that he was becoming senile.

Meanwhile uprisings in several parts of the country were proving hard to suppress. Heshen fed the emperor rosy reports of victory while pocketing much of the military budget himself.

In 1795 Qianlong announced that he would abdicate, so as not to rule longer than his grandfather, Kangxi; early in 1796 his son took the throne as the Jiaqing emperor. But Qianlong continued to dominate the court until he died in 1799, aged eighty-seven. Soon after, the new emperor forced Heshen to take his own life; that fact is from sources beyond Ebrey.

Pierian Spring · Q13 story · Missionaries in China · 1600s–1700s

Dominicans and Franciscans Accused the Jesuits of Allowing Ancestor Worship

From Matteo Ricci on, Jesuits dressed and studied like Chinese scholars and let converts keep honoring their ancestors.
The Dominican and Franciscan orders in China opposed allowing converts to maintain ancestral altars.
At stake was whether honoring ancestors was a family custom Christians could keep or a worship they must give up.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

To understand the quarrel, start with the Jesuits' method, which comes from sources beyond Ebrey. The first great Jesuit in China, Matteo Ricci, reached Beijing in 1601. He learned classical Chinese, dressed as a scholar, and won converts among officials, like Xu Guangqi, the man beside him in the engraving. Ricci decided that honoring one's ancestors was a family and civic custom, not a religion, so converts could keep doing it.

Other orders disagreed. The textbook tells us the Dominican and Franciscan orders in China opposed allowing converts to maintain ancestral altars (Ebrey, p. 276). To them, bowing and offering incense before the tablets of the dead was worship, forbidden to Christians.

So the question was simple to state and very hard to settle: was ancestral ritual a custom or a religion?

Here is the concept to carry into the review question. The rites controversy was the dispute among Catholic missionaries over whether Chinese converts could keep their ancestral altars. The textbook puts it in one long sentence, the one your Reading Guide points to: "However, when the pope sided with the Dominican and Franciscan orders in China who opposed allowing converts to maintain ancestral altars (known as the “rites controversy”), he objected strongly to the pope’s issuing directives about how Chinese should behave" (Ebrey, p. 276).

Two dates from sources beyond Ebrey help. In 1692 Kangxi had issued an edict tolerating Christianity in his empire. He was grateful to the Jesuits, and Ricci's approach made Christianity look compatible with Chinese custom.

Then Rome ruled. After years of argument, Pope Clement XI issued a bull, a formal papal decree, in 1715, forbidding Chinese Christians to take part in the rites for ancestors and for Confucius. Converts would have to choose between their church and their family duties.

Pierian Spring · Q13 story · Beijing · 1715–1724

Kangxi Would Not Let a Foreign Priest Tell Chinese How to Behave

Kangxi objected strongly to the pope's issuing directives about how Chinese should behave.
He ruled that Europeans should no longer preach in China; his son made the ban formal in 1724.
He still allowed Jesuit scientists and painters to remain in Beijing · m · rites-ban ↩
The quarrel was about authority: who ruled the conduct of the emperor's subjects?

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276.

Now put yourself in Kangxi's place. A foreign religious leader, who had never set foot in China, was telling the emperor's subjects how to honor their own parents. Kangxi objected strongly to the pope's "issuing directives about how Chinese should behave" (Ebrey, p. 276).

So he stopped the missionaries from preaching, and his son, the Yongzheng emperor, made the ban formal in 1724. Notice what he kept: Jesuit scientists and painters could remain in Beijing. He rejected the pope's authority, not European skills.

From sources beyond Ebrey: when a papal envoy brought the decree, Kangxi read a translation and wrote on it, in his own hand, that the Europeans did not understand Chinese ways and should no longer be allowed to preach. The portrait shows him with the brush he used for such comments.

That is the heart of the answer to Question 13. The rites controversy began as an argument among Catholics about ritual. It ended as a contest over sovereignty, and in China the emperor won.

Between grandfather and grandson came a father. Kangxi's heir ruled for twelve years as the Yongzheng emperor, from 1723 to 1735. He came to the throne late, at forty-four, after decades of watching his father govern (Ebrey, p. 276).

The textbook gives him just two sentences. The first is the one your Reading Guide points to: "A hard-working ruler, he tightened central control over the government." The next slides fill in what that meant, mostly from sources beyond Ebrey.

From sources beyond Ebrey: Yongzheng read officials' reports himself and wrote his replies on them in red ink, the "vermilion" brush reserved for the emperor. In a preface he wrote in 1732, he said he met officials by day and read reports by lamplight in the evening, often until late at night. By then more than ten thousand reports carrying his replies had piled up in the palace.

Pierian Spring · Q12 story · The Forbidden City · 1720s–1730s

Yongzheng Created a Small Council to Run Affairs from the Palace

Yongzheng tightened central control over the government.
In 1729, for a war against the Zunghar Mongols, he set up a small office of trusted ministers inside the palace; under his son it grew into the Grand Council.
He widened a system begun by Kangxi of secret reports sent straight to the emperor, to check on officials without going through the regular bureaucracy.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276; the council and secret reports (Bartlett; Academia Sinica).

What did "tightening central control" look like in practice? Two tools, both from sources beyond Ebrey.

The first was a small council inside the palace, in Chinese the Junjichu, which in English we call the Grand Council. It began in 1729, when Yongzheng went to war against the Zunghar Mongols in the northwest and wanted war business handled secretly, close to him, by a few trusted ministers. Under Yongzheng it stayed small and informal. The historian Beatrice Bartlett shows that it grew into the powerful Grand Council only under his son Qianlong, when it became the real center of decision, faster and more private than the great ministries outside the palace walls. The photo shows how modest its office was.

The second was the secret palace memorial. Kangxi had begun letting a few trusted officials send sealed reports straight to him. Yongzheng widened the privilege to far more officials, read and answered the reports himself, and had them returned to the palace for safekeeping. That let him check on officials without going through the regular bureaucracy.

Both tools put more power in the emperor's hands, which is what the textbook means by central control (Ebrey, p. 276).

Pierian Spring · Q12 story · County offices · 1720s

He Replaced a Patchwork of Fees with Regular Taxes

Yongzheng oversaw a rationalization of the tax structure, substituting new levies for a patchwork of taxes and fees.
Local officials had long added irregular surcharges, such as a "meltdown fee" for melting tax silver into ingots.
From 1724 he made the surcharge a fixed, official levy that paid officials allowances meant to keep them honest and covered local expenses.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 276. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

The other half of Question 12 is fiscal administration, the management of money. The textbook says Yongzheng "oversaw a rationalization of the tax structure, substituting new levies for a patchwork of taxes and fees" (Ebrey, p. 276). Rationalization means making a system regular and predictable.

What was the patchwork? From sources beyond Ebrey: official salaries were tiny, so county magistrates added their own surcharges to the land tax. The best known was the meltdown fee, charged supposedly to cover silver lost when small payments were melted into ingots. Rates varied from place to place, and much of it disappeared into private pockets.

Beginning in 1724, building on a proposal from officials in Shanxi province, Yongzheng made the surcharge a fixed, official levy collected by the provinces. It paid officials regular allowances "for nourishing honesty" and covered local public expenses. The historian Madeleine Zelin tells this story in The Magistrate's Tael (1984).

Here is the payoff of Yongzheng's reforms. His heir, the Qianlong emperor, "benefited from his father's fiscal reforms, and during his reign, the Qing government regularly ran surpluses" (Ebrey, p. 276).

Think back over the main lecture: the conquest of Xinjiang, the southern tours at ten times Kangxi's cost, the vast Four Treasuries project. Those were expensive. A treasury that regularly ran surpluses helped make them possible. That link is an inference, but a fair one from the text.

A Salt Merchant Hosted the Emperor in His Garden

Jiang Chun (1725–1793) was one of the head salt merchants of Yangzhou, licensed to sell the government's monopoly salt.

A last story, just for interest. Jiang Chun (1725–1793) was one of the head salt merchants of Yangzhou. The government claimed a monopoly on salt and licensed merchants to transport and sell it, and the twenty to thirty head merchants could each pocket 50,000 to 100,000 taels of silver a year (Ebrey, p. 275).

Qianlong visited Yangzhou six times on his southern tours, in 1751, 1757, 1762, 1765, 1780, and 1784. Jiang met him every time, and on the last four visits hosted the imperial party in his gardens. Qianlong himself named the Garden of Pure Fragrance, and once sat Jiang's seven-year-old son on his lap.

Then the money ran out. By 1771 Jiang was broke, and Qianlong personally lent him 500,000 taels, at 10 percent interest. A salt merchant, an emperor, and a garden: this is what the southern tours of slide q looked like from the hosts' side. The scroll shows the tour arriving in Suzhou, another Jiangnan city.

Four Treasuries

The Complete Books of the Four Treasuries was Qianlong's project to catalogue nearly all books in China. During it, he ordered searches for books that disparaged the Manchus or earlier alien conquerors (Ebrey, p. 276).

The "four treasuries" are the four traditional divisions of Chinese books.

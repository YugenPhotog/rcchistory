Inside the Garden, Outside the Law

Moralists Banned Novels, Yet Cao Xueqin Wrote the Greatest One; the Empire Prospered, Yet Brotherhoods and Sects Gathered Those It Left Behind

1700–1800 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome! This deck is your Study Buddy for the last part of Chapter 16, and the last lecture on the Qing at its height.

The story in one breath. After the Manchu conquest, many scholars blamed the Ming's fall on loose morals, and theaters were closed and novels banned. Yet in these same years a ruined bondservant's son, Cao Xueqin, wrote The Dream of Red Mansions, perhaps the greatest Chinese novel. Outside the garden walls, men with no land and no wives found brothers in secret societies, and believers in the White Lotus waited for the Eternal Mother to call them home. In 1796 they rose in revolt (Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281–286).

The painting shows the garden at the heart of the novel. It was painted in the 1800s by Sun Wen for a huge album illustrating the whole story; the painter and album are from sources beyond Ebrey.

Recap · Late Ming culture · 1500s–1600s

Recap: Late-Ming Readers Loved Novels and Plays

In late-Ming cities, publishers printed novels in the vernacular, the everyday written language · Ch. 14 L4 · vernacular ↗
Plays such as The Peony Pavilion celebrated love and feeling · Ch. 14 L4 · peony-pavilion ↗

We begin with something you already know. Context · After the conquest · 1650s–1700s

After the Conquest, Moralists Turned Against Plays and Novels

Many Confucian scholars concluded that the Ming fell because of moral laxity.
Literati called drama and fiction socially subversive, and the state banned many novels and plays.
One learned scholar even argued that the vernacular novel was the main threat to Confucian orthodoxy.
The court watched writers closely, too · L2 s · concept-inquisition ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 281; L2 for the literary inquisition.

After 1644 the mood changed. "Many Confucian scholars concluded that the Ming fell as a result of moral laxity" (Ebrey, p. 281). If loose morals had lost the empire, then the novels and plays that celebrated feeling were part of the problem.

So literati argued that drama and fiction were "socially subversive." Subversive means undermining the established order. The state issued ban after ban on novels and plays, but the bans never stuck; theater kept thriving. One highly learned scholar, Qian Daxin, "went so far as to argue that the vernacular novel was the main threat to Confucian orthodoxy." You will meet him again in the Pierian Spring.

Instructor note: the textbook prints the name as "Qian Daxian"; the scholar is Qian Daxin (1728–1804).

The emperors added their own pressure. In our second lecture you saw the literary inquisition, when writers were punished for words that seemed to insult the Manchus. The link takes you back.

This is the climate Review Question 9 asks about: a conservative age. And yet, as the next slides show, art and fiction kept growing.

Check yourself: Why did some scholars think novels were dangerous?

Instructor note: the textbook says "theaters were closed and novels banned" (p. 281). The bans were periodic and partial; in 1790 the Anhui troupes came to Beijing for Qianlong's eightieth birthday (Goldman, 2012).

Source beyond Ebrey: L2 for the literary inquisition.

Q9 story · Hangzhou and Nanjing · 1700s

Yet the Poet Yuan Mei Took Women as Students, and One Defended Him

Both the Manchu rulers and the Chinese elite left room for the less conventional.
The poet Yuan Mei risked censure by taking on women as poetry students.
In 1797 his student Luo Qilan defended him: if women's words had to stay indoors, Confucius would have cut women's poems from the Book of Poetry.
Yuan Mei had his women students painted on a scroll and wrote their names on it himself.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281–282.

Here is the surprise. "There are always those who resist calls for decorum and strenuous moral effort," the textbook says, "and in the eighteenth century, both the Manchu rulers and the Chinese intellectual elite provided room for the less conventional to contribute in creative ways" (Ebrey, p. 281).

Start with the poet Yuan Mei. He knew the great scholars of his day, yet he "was willing to risk their censure by taking on women as poetry students" (p. 282). Critics said it was improper for a man to teach young women, and for women to pass their poems around outside the family.

In 1797 one of those students, Luo Qilan, defended him with a clever argument: if Confucius had believed that "women's words spoken inside a chamber must stay indoors," he would have removed the poems by women from the Book of Poetry, the classic Confucius himself was said to have edited (p. 282). She beat the moralists with their own sage.

The scroll is from sources beyond Ebrey. In 1792 Yuan Mei had painters picture his women students gathered at a lakeside villa in Hangzhou, each one identified in his own inscription, which lists thirteen women. A few years later he had three more students added at the end. His note names the woman on the right, standing with an orchid at her sash, as Luo Qilan. So this is a picture of the woman whose argument you just heard, labeled by her teacher.

Q9 story · Yangzhou · 1700s

The Eight Eccentrics of Yangzhou Painted for Themselves, and Still Sold

Palace Museum, Beijing; Minneapolis Institute of Art; Honolulu Museum of Art (loan). Public domain, Wikimedia Commons, Wikimedia Commons, Wikimedia Commons.

The Eight Eccentrics of Yangzhou used ink painting as a means of self-expression.
They had no trouble finding patrons, even among social and cultural conservatives.
Jin Nong filled the space on his paintings with his own writing, in his own distinctive hand.
Zheng Xie was known for bamboo, and Luo Ping for ghosts.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281–282.

The best example of room for the unconventional is a group of painters. "Exploration of the potential of ink painting for self-expression reached a high point in the eighteenth century with a closely affiliated group of painters known as the Eight Eccentrics of Yangzhou." And the key point for our question: "These painters had no difficulty finding patrons, even among social and cultural conservatives" (Ebrey, pp. 281–282).

Why Yangzhou? It was the rich salt-merchant city you visited with Jiang Chun in our second lecture · L2 · ps-jiang-chun ↗. Salt merchants had money, wanted culture, and bought paintings.

On the left is Jin Nong (1687–1764), in a self-portrait he painted in 1759. Our textbook's feature shows another of his works, a Buddhist monk surrounded by his own writing. In it he says that when a monk friend told him his paintings had founded a new school that later generations would follow, "I roared with laughter" (p. 282).

From sources beyond Ebrey: in the middle is bamboo by Zheng Xie (1693–1765), also called Zheng Banqiao. He served as a county magistrate in Shandong, left office, and lived by selling his paintings and calligraphy in Yangzhou, even posting a price list. Bamboo, which bends in the wind but does not break, stood for the upright gentleman.

On the right is Luo Ping (1733–1799), Jin Nong's student, who became famous for painting ghosts. Look at the tall pale figure and the two little ghosts trotting after him: funny and eerie at once. "Eight" is a traditional label; lists of the Eccentrics name more than eight painters. Those details are from sources beyond Ebrey.

Q9 story · Nanjing and Beijing · 1700s

A Bondservant Family Rose with the Manchus, Then Went Bankrupt

Cao Xueqin (c. 1715–1763/64) wrote the first eighty chapters of The Dream of Red Mansions.
His family were bondservants of the ruling house who had risen with the Manchus.
They gained great wealth and power managing enterprises for the rulers; his grandfather hosted the Kangxi emperor in Nanjing · L2 h · southern-tours ↗
In the 1700s, however, the family lost favor and went bankrupt.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281–282.

Who wrote this novel? "The author of the first eighty chapters was Cao Xueqin (1715–1764)." He died before finishing, and another writer added forty chapters before it was published in 1791 (Ebrey, p. 281).

Cao Xueqin "came from a Chinese family that had risen with the Manchus. As bondservants of the ruling house, his family was in a position to gain great wealth and power managing enterprises for the rulers" (p. 282). A bondservant was a hereditary servant of the imperial household. That sounds lowly, but bondservants could become the emperor's trusted managers.

From sources beyond Ebrey: the Cao family ran the imperial silk works at Nanjing, shown here as a modern museum model, for three generations. Cao Xueqin's grandfather, Cao Yin, was a close friend of the Kangxi emperor and hosted him in Nanjing on his southern tours, which you saw in our second lecture.

Then the fall: "In the eighteenth century, however, the family lost favor and went bankrupt." From sources beyond Ebrey: under the Yongzheng emperor, in 1728, the family's property was confiscated. Cao Xueqin grew up remembering wealth and lived his adult life in poverty near Beijing. Many readers see that loss in the novel he wrote.

Check yourself: What did it mean to be a bondservant of the Qing ruling house?

Instructor note: the textbook gives 1715–1764. Cao's birth year is uncertain, and he died in 1763 or early 1764 (Spence, 1966).

Q9 names · One novel, two titles

Names Box: Dream of Red Mansions = Story of the Stone

One Novel, Several Names

The Dream of Red Mansions The title our textbook uses; in Chinese, Hongloumeng; also translated Dream of the Red Chamber

The Story of the Stone The novel's other title; the story begins with a magic stone that is born into the world as the boy Baoyu

120 chapters Eighty by Cao Xueqin, forty added by another writer; published in 1791

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281, 283.

You will see this novel under different names, so let us sort them out. Our textbook calls it The Dream of Red Mansions, "also called Story of the Stone," and calls it, in the view of many, "the most successful of all works of Chinese fiction" (Ebrey, p. 281). The textbook's quotation comes from David Hawkes's English translation, which uses the title The Story of the Stone (p. 283).

From sources beyond Ebrey: the Chinese title is Hongloumeng, often translated Dream of the Red Chamber. The other title comes from the novel's opening. A magic stone, left over when a goddess repaired the sky, longs to see the human world, and is born as a boy with a piece of jade in his mouth. That boy is Baoyu, our hero.

The print shows that stone under a pine. It is the first picture in an early printed edition; Wikimedia Commons dates it to 1791, the year the textbook gives for the first printing.

The novel has 120 chapters: eighty by Cao Xueqin, and forty added by "another writer" to complete it.

Q9 story · The Jia family mansion · the novel

The Jia Family Built a Garden for an Imperial Consort's Visit

Dream portrays the affairs of the wealthy Jia family in magnificent detail.
A magnificent garden was built in the family compound to receive a visit from Baoyu's sister, who had become an imperial consort.
After the visit, Baoyu, his cousins, and their servants moved into the garden, an idyllic world of youth and beauty.
Around them swirl officials, monks and nuns, gamblers, actors, and plots of every kind.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 283.

Now the story. "The Dream portrays in magnificent detail the affairs of the comparably wealthy Jia family," a family much like Cao Xueqin's own before its fall (Ebrey, pp. 282–283).

The great event of the early chapters: Baoyu's sister has become an imperial consort, one of the emperor's wives, and she is allowed to visit home. To receive her, the family builds "a magnificent garden" in the compound. From sources beyond Ebrey: the sister's name is Yuanchun, the woman in this print, and the garden is called the Grand View Garden, the one on our title slide.

After her visit, "Baoyu, his cousins, and their personal servants move into the garden, an idyllic world of youth and beauty." Idyllic means peaceful and perfect, like a dream. That is the "inside the garden" of our title.

Much of the novel's power comes from what happens around the garden: "officials, aristocrats, monks and nuns, pageboys, gardeners, country relatives, princes, gamblers, prostitutes, actors, and innkeepers," abuse of power, and family members competing for advantage (p. 283).

Q9 story · Three adolescents · the novel

Baoyu Lived Among Cousins: Sickly Daiyu and Cheerful Baochai

The central characters are three adolescents: Jia Baoyu and two female cousins of other surnames.
Lin Daiyu is sickly and difficult.
Xue Baochai is capable and cheerful.
Women with poetic talents figure prominently in the novel.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281, 283.

Meet the three young people at the center. "The central characters of the novel are three adolescents: Jia Baoyu and his two female cousins of other surnames who come to live with his family" (Ebrey, p. 283).

"One of the cousins, Lin Daiyu, is sickly and difficult; the other, Xue Baochai, is capable and cheerful." From beyond Ebrey, in the novel itself: Daiyu is an orphan, proud, quick to take offense, and the best poet of them all. Baochai is sensible, kind, and everything a family could want in a daughter-in-law. Baoyu loves Daiyu.

Notice how that connects back to Yuan Mei's women students on slide d.

The prints are by Gai Qi, who drew the novel's characters in the early 1800s; they were printed in 1879. The artist is from sources beyond Ebrey.

Q9 story · The end of the garden · the novel

Baoyu Was Tricked into Marrying Baochai as Daiyu Lay Dying

In the last forty chapters, added by another writer, the magical period in the garden ends.
Baoyu is tricked into marrying Baochai, thinking he is marrying Daiyu.
While the wedding is taking place, Daiyu is on her sickbed, dying of consumption.
The novel is about love and desire, money and power, life and death, and truth and illusion.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281, 283. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Every garden in this novel is too good to last. "This magical period comes to an end when Baoyu is tricked into marrying Baochai (thinking he is marrying Daiyu). While the wedding is taking place, Daiyu is on her sickbed, dying of consumption" (Ebrey, p. 283). Consumption is the old name for tuberculosis. This part of the story comes from the last forty chapters, added after Cao Xueqin's death (Levy, 1999).

Why the trick? From beyond Ebrey, in the novel's plot: the family elders judge Baochai the better match, and they hide the bride's face under the red wedding veil so that Baoyu, who is ill and confused, will go through with it.

This is why the textbook calls the novel a story of "love and desire, money and power, life and death, and truth and illusion," and also "a psychologically sensitive novel of manners" (p. 281). A novel of manners studies how people behave inside a society's rules.

Q9 story · The last chapter · the novel

Baoyu Passed the Provincial Exam, Then Walked Away

Near the end, Baoyu passes the provincial examination, placing seventh.
Then he leaves his wife and family to pursue religious goals.
Dream is a story about truth and illusion, and the ending turns the whole garden into a dream.
Readers still argue over the last forty chapters, added by another writer.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 281, 283.

How does it end? In chapter 119 Baoyu sits the provincial examination and passes, placing seventh. Then he walks out of the examination hall and disappears, leaving his wife and family "to pursue religious goals" (Ebrey, p. 283). The provincial degree was the step before the jinshi, the highest degree.

Think of what that means. Baoyu does exactly what his strict father always wanted, and then gives it all up. The family's glory, the garden, the marriage: all of it, the novel suggests, was a kind of dream. That is why the textbook lists "truth and illusion" among the novel's grand themes (p. 281).

From sources beyond Ebrey: the last forty chapters were added after Cao Xueqin's death, traditionally credited to an editor named Gao E, and readers have argued ever since about whether this is the ending Cao intended.

And notice the connection to Review Question 9: in an age when scholars said novels threatened Confucian order, the greatest novel ends with its hero rejecting the examination life at the very moment he wins it.

Check yourself: Why is it surprising that Baoyu leaves right after passing the examination?

Instructor note: the textbook says he passes "the jinshi examinations" (p. 283). In the 120-chapter novel he passes the provincial examination in chapter 119 (Levy, 1999).

Q9 primary text · The Story of the Stone · the novel

Baochai Remembered Reading Plays in Secret, and the Book Burnings

Baochai notices that Daiyu has unconsciously quoted a line from a play.
She confesses that the children in her family read plays in secret, boys and girls alike.
When the grown-ups found out, there were beatings, lectures, and burnings of books.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 283, quoting Cao Xueqin, The Story of the Stone, trans. David Hawkes, vol. 2, p. 333.

One small scene shows the whole age. Daiyu, without thinking, quotes a line from a play. Baochai notices, and later confesses that she too read plays as a child, from the age of seven or eight (Ebrey, p. 283).

Here are her words, in David Hawkes's translation: "All of us younger people hated serious books but liked reading poetry and plays. The boys had got lots and lots of plays: The Western Chamber, The Lute-Player, A Hundred Yuan Plays—just about everything you could think of. They used to read them behind our backs, and we girls used to read them behind theirs. Eventually the grown-ups got to know about it and then there were beatings and lectures and burnings of books—and that was the end of that."

Put that beside slide c. Moralists outside the novel said plays and fiction were dangerous; inside the novel, children read them in secret anyway, and a novel tells us so. That is Review Question 9 in a single scene.

The painting shows Daiyu in the garden, painted in the early 1800s by Fei Danxu. In the novel she carries a hoe and a bag to bury fallen petals, so that they will not be trampled. The painter is from sources beyond Ebrey.

Population growth and partible inheritance left farms too small to support a family · L3 m · concept-partible ↗

Q10 concept · Secret societies and sects

Concept: Secret Societies and Sects

Concept

Secret society · Sect

A secret society is a brotherhood whose members swear loyalty to one another and keep their rituals and signs hidden from outsiders. A sect is a religious group that follows its own teachers and scriptures outside the approved clergy.

Secret societies gave single men security, mutual aid, and empowerment.
Sects such as the White Lotus offered salvation, healing, and a community of believers.
Both drew on ideas from Daoism, Buddhism, and popular religion.
The Qing government tried, from time to time, to suppress them, because they were often anti-establishment.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 285, 287.

Two kinds of group matter for the rest of this lecture, and Review Question 10 asks about both.

A secret society is a brotherhood. Its members swear to help one another and keep their rituals hidden. For marginalized men, "the attraction was security, mutual aid, and empowerment" (Ebrey, p. 285). Marginalized means pushed to the edges of society.

A sect is a religious group outside the approved clergy, with its own teachers and scriptures. The White Lotus is our example.

Both made the government nervous. The textbook's summary: "The marginalized and disaffected were particularly likely to join secret societies such as the Heaven and Earth Society (Triads) or religious sects such as the White Lotus Society. The Qing government from time to time tried to suppress these societies, which were often anti-establishment" (p. 287).

Q10 story · Fujian, Taiwan, and the south · from the 1760s

The Heaven and Earth Society Offered Brothers, Loans, and Passwords

The Heaven and Earth Society, the best-known brotherhood, appeared in the 1760s and spread across the south.
Government functionaries and urban workers were among its members.
Lodge brothers helped each other with loans, funeral costs, and lawsuits, and could find brothers anywhere through secret signals or passwords.
But their morality was not Confucian: many lodges controlled gambling, narcotics, prostitution, and smuggling.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Here is the sentence your Reading Guide asks you to copy for Question 10: "To marginalized men, the attraction was security, mutual aid, and empowerment" (Ebrey, p. 285).

The best-known brotherhood "was called the Heaven and Earth Society, but other names were also used, such as the Three Harmonies Society or the Three Dots Society. (In English they are often called the Triads.)" It appeared "first in the 1760s" and "spread quickly through Taiwan, Fujian, Jiangxi, Guangdong, and Guangxi." Its members included "government functionaries and urban workers."

What did a brother get? "Members away from home could get in touch with members elsewhere through secret signals or passwords. Lodge brothers helped each other with loans, funeral costs, and lawsuits." Lodges settled disputes and punished cheating at gambling. Drawing on Daoism and popular religion, they also "promised access to supernatural powers through rituals and cultivation."

From sources beyond Ebrey: new members were initiated at night in a ceremony that included an oath, often sealed by drinking wine mixed with a drop of blood. That is the scene imagined here.

The dark side: "their morality was not Confucian. Many lodges controlled gambling, narcotics, prostitution, and smuggling in their region."

Q10 story · Taiwan and the south · 1760s–1780s

Its Slogan Was "Overthrow the Qing and Restore the Ming"

The societies were explicitly anti-establishment.
They used the rhetoric of "overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming" · L1 r · concept-loyalism ↗
The first uprising led by the Heaven and Earth Society broke out in Taiwan in the 1780s.
Afterward the Qing government tried, without much success, to suppress the society.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285.

A brotherhood of poor men might have been only a mutual-aid club. But the textbook stresses that "the societies were explicitly anti-establishment in that they espoused a rhetoric of 'overthrow the Qing and restore the Ming'" (Ebrey, p. 285). Rhetoric means the language a group uses to persuade.

Think back to our first lecture and the concept of loyalism: scholars who stayed loyal to the fallen Ming. A century later, the Ming had become a slogan for men who had never known it. The link takes you back.

And it could become a rebellion: "The first uprising led by the Heaven and Earth Society occurred in the 1780s in Taiwan, after which the Qing government tried without much success to suppress the society."

Recap · From the Yuan to the Qing

Recap: White Lotus Believers Had Helped Topple the Mongols

The White Lotus Society drew on folk Buddhist teachings that dated back to the Song.
It had been an important element in the rebellions at the end of the Yuan · Ch. 14 L1 · red-turbans ↗
Regular Buddhist clergy did not approve of its mixed teachings, married clergy, and unofficial scriptures.
It taught that the future Buddha, Maitreya, would usher in an era of peace.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285; Ch. 14 L1 for the Red Turbans.

The government was even more wary of a second kind of group. "The Qing government was also wary of the White Lotus Society, which drew on folk Buddhist teachings that dated back to the Song period. This society had been an important element in the rebellions at the end of the Yuan Dynasty" (Ebrey, p. 285).

You met those rebels in Chapter 14: the Red Turbans, whose rising helped bring down the Mongols and brought the Ming founder to power. The link takes you back.

Regular Buddhist clergy did not approve of the White Lotus "because of its syncretic teachings, married clergy, and noncanonical scriptures." Syncretic means mixing ideas from different religions; noncanonical means not part of the approved scriptures. It also taught a millenarian hope, drawn from Manichaeism and from the Buddhist idea of the future Buddha Maitreya, "who would usher in an era of peace."

Q10 story · The White Lotus faith

The Eternal Mother Called Her Lost Children Home

The sect's central deity was the Eternal Mother, the original ancestor of all humankind.
Grieving that her children had lost their way, she sent Buddhas to earth to lead them back to the Original Home.
When the end was near, floods, fires, and winds would destroy everyone who lacked faith in her.
Believers chanted a mantra for protection, and learned martial arts and breathing exercises for health.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

What did White Lotus believers believe? "Its central deity was the Eternal Mother, the original progenitor of all humankind. Grieving that her earthly children had lost their way, she wanted to bring them back to the Original Home, identified with nirvana. She sent Buddhas to earth to teach people the true way to salvation" (Ebrey, p. 285).

Then came the warning. "When the end was near, floods, fires, and winds would destroy everyone who lacked faith in the Eternal Mother." Believers were taught that repeating the mantra "Eternal Progenitor in Our Original Home in the World of True Emptiness" would bring blessings and protection. A mantra is a sacred phrase repeated in prayer.

And there were practical gifts: "martial arts exercises and breathing techniques that circulated qi were also taught as other ways to cure illnesses and promote health."

Imagine hearing this as a poor farmer or a boatman far from home. You are not lost; a mother is waiting for you; and the end of this hard world is coming.

Q10 story · Congregations in central China · 1790s

Women Joined on Equal Terms, and Some Led

White Lotus teachings had particular appeal to women, who were welcomed on the same terms as men.
When leaders were arrested or executed, often their wives took over.
The sect survived repeated suppression, probably because local congregations did not depend on a central establishment.
It was especially strong in the central provinces, from Sichuan and Shaanxi to Shandong.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285, quoting a Qing official cited in Kwang-ching Liu, "Religion and Politics in the White Lotus Rebellion of 1796 in Hubei." Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Here is something you will not find in the examination halls. "White Lotus teachings had particular appeal to women, who were welcomed as members on the same terms as men. When leaders were arrested or executed, often their wives took over" (Ebrey, p. 285).

A Qing official whose city was seized by White Lotus forces described the woman who led the local group: "When she emerges in a home, five or six young women will hold tobacco bags and towels for her, and she will be seated at the center of the hall. Both men and women make obeisance." Then they kowtowed. To make obeisance is to bow in respect. The picture imagines that scene.

Why could the government not stamp it out? "Probably because local congregations did not depend on a central establishment." Arrest one teacher, and another congregation carried on. "Itinerant prophets and teachers carried the message to new places." Itinerant means traveling from place to place.

Some leaders "combined talk of salvation with martial arts and herbal healing." In 1774 a White Lotus leader in Shandong convinced his followers that he was the Future Buddha and that his techniques made them invulnerable; armed only with spears, they captured several small towns before massive Qing armies crushed them. From beyond Ebrey, that leader's name was Wang Lun.

Q10 story · Hubei, Sichuan, and Shaanxi · 1793–1804

A Crackdown in 1793 Set Off the White Lotus Revolt of 1796

In 1793 the Qing government began a major investigation of White Lotus congregations.
The congregations soon took up arms to protect themselves from predatory local officials.
In 1796 open revolt began in Hubei and spread to Sichuan and Shaanxi; martial arts groups and bandits joined.
It took the government more than eight years to fully crush the White Lotus forces.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 286.

Now the explosion. "In 1793 the Qing government initiated a major investigation of White Lotus congregations, which soon had to take up arms to protect themselves from predatory elements in the local government" (Ebrey, p. 286). Predatory means preying on others. From sources beyond Ebrey: local clerks and runners used the hunt for sectarians as a chance to extort money from anyone they could accuse.

"In 1796 open revolt began in Hubei and soon spread to Sichuan and Shaanxi," the three provinces in red. White Lotus forces held fortified villages and towns and used them as bases to raid larger cities. "Armed bands often joined them, martial arts groups and bandits alike."

"It took the government more than eight years to fully annihilate White Lotus forces." From sources beyond Ebrey: the last bands were crushed in 1804. The same year the revolt began, 1796, the old Qianlong emperor handed the throne to his son, the Jiaqing emperor, who inherited the war.

Remember too the Miao revolt of 1795 from the Pierian Spring of our last lecture. The textbook says the Qing found the Miao uprisings "as difficult to suppress as the concurrent White Lotus ones" · L3 x · ps-1795 ↗.

Pierian Spring · Q8 concept · Orthodoxy

Concept: They Returned to Zhu Xi's "Orthodox" Teaching

Concept

Orthodoxy

The officially approved, "correct" form of a teaching. In Qing China, Confucian orthodoxy meant Zhu Xi's interpretation of the classics, which was also the basis of the civil service examinations.

Zhu Xi emphasized objective standards outside the individual, rather than inner feeling · Ch. 8 L5 · zhu-synthesis ↗
Since the Ming, his commentaries had been the official standard for the examinations · Ch. 14 L3 · zhu-xi-orthodoxy ↗
This conservative turn showed itself in several ways, in society as well as in scholarship.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 281; Ch. 8 L5 and Ch. 14 L3 for Zhu Xi.

The word you need here is orthodoxy: the officially approved, correct form of a teaching. The opposite is heterodoxy, a teaching the authorities consider wrong.

For Qing scholars, orthodoxy meant Zhu Xi. You met the great Song philosopher in Chapter 8, where he pulled Confucian learning together into a single system; the first link takes you back. In Chapter 14 you saw that the Ming made his commentaries the standard for the examinations; the second link takes you there.

Why Zhu Xi rather than Wang Yangming? Because Zhu Xi stressed "objective standards outside the individual" (Ebrey, p. 281): rules, rituals, and the careful study of the classics, rather than trusting one's own heart.

"This conservative turn was manifested in several ways." The next slides show three of them: in the lives of widows, in the law, and in scholarship.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · Villages and local histories · 1700s

Cult of Widow Chastity

The cult of widow chastity was an intense devotion to the ideal that a widow should stay faithful to her dead husband and never remarry.
It reached new heights, as local histories recorded more and more widows who refused to remarry.
Families and officials honored such women with titles and stone memorial arches.
A widow's chastity was praised as loyalty to her husband's family, like a minister's loyalty to his ruler.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 281.

The first sign of the conservative turn is in women's lives. "The cult of widow chastity reached new heights, with local histories recording more and more widows who refused to remarry" (Ebrey, p. 281). A cult here means an intense devotion to an ideal; chastity means sexual purity, and for a widow it meant never remarrying.

From sources beyond Ebrey: the state and local elites rewarded such women. Their names were entered in local histories, the government granted honors, and wealthy families built stone memorial arches like these at Tangyue in Anhui, home of a rich merchant lineage. Two of the seven arches honor widows of the Bao family.

Also from sources beyond Ebrey: moralists compared the chaste widow to the loyal minister. Just as a loyal official did not serve a second dynasty, a loyal wife did not serve a second husband. After the conquest, you can see why that comparison carried a special charge.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · The extreme case · 1700s

Some Girls Stayed "Widows" of Fiancés They Had Never Met

Some girls whose fiancés died before the wedding vowed to stay chaste for life · L1 t · gu-yanwu ↗
They lived as the celibate "widows" of men they had never even met.

The textbook gives one case that shows how far the ideal could go. Local histories recorded widows who refused to remarry, "including those who lived their entire lives as the celibate 'widows' of men to whom they had been engaged but who had died before they had even met" (Ebrey, p. 281). Celibate means never having sexual relations.

The chapter summary returns to it: "The cult of widow chastity reached such extremes that girls whose fiancés had died would commit themselves to stay chaste their entire lives as 'widows' of men they had not yet married" (p. 286).

How could a girl be engaged to a man she had never met? From sources beyond Ebrey: marriages were arranged by parents, often when the children were young, so a betrothal could be fixed years before a wedding, and the fiancé might die in the meantime.

Pierian Spring · Q8 concept · Evidential scholarship

Concept: Evidential Scholars Tested the Classics Like Detectives

Concept

Evidential scholarship

Rigorous study of the classics that relies on verifiable facts: comparing texts, checking words, dates, and place names, and asking which versions are genuine. In Chinese it is called kaozheng, "searching for evidence".

The conservative turn in scholarship fostered a new interest in rigorous textual analysis.
Some scholars went back to Han commentaries, hoping to free the classics from Buddhist and Daoist ideas.
Others relied solely on the classics themselves and concentrated on verifiable facts · L1 t · gu-yanwu ↗
This research needed large libraries, so it thrived in Jiangnan, with its many books and scholars.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 281.

The conservative turn also changed how scholars worked, and here it produced something new. "The conservative turn in scholarship fostered a new interest in rigorous textual analysis" (Ebrey, p. 281).

Some scholars "turned back to the Han commentaries on the classics, hoping that they could free their understandings of the texts from the contamination of Buddhist and Daoist ideas that had infiltrated Tang and Song commentaries." Others "wanted to rely solely on the classics themselves and to concentrate on verifiable facts."

Historians call this evidential scholarship, in Chinese kaozheng; the term is from sources beyond Ebrey. Think of these scholars as detectives of old books. You met one of the founders in our first lecture: Gu Yanwu, the Ming loyalist who traveled the north checking books against inscriptions and places. The link takes you back.

"Research of this sort required access to large libraries, and it thrived primarily in Jiangnan, with its high densities of both books and scholars." The next slide shows its most famous discovery.

Pierian Spring · Q8 story · Jiangnan · late 1600s

Yan Ruoju Proved That a Revered Classic Was Not Genuine

Yan Ruoju compiled a guide to the place names in the Four Books.
He proved that the "old text" version of the Book of Documents could not be genuine.
Those chapters had been studied for over a thousand years, and some were part of the examination curriculum.
Careful evidence could overturn even a sacred text.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 281. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

The great detective story of evidential scholarship. "Yan Ruoju compiled a guide to the place names in the Four Books and proved that the 'old text' version of the Book of Documents could not be genuine" (Ebrey, p. 281).

Why was that shocking? The Book of Documents was one of the Five Classics, speeches and records said to come from the sage kings and the early Zhou. From sources beyond Ebrey: the "old text" chapters had been accepted for more than a thousand years, and passages from them were studied for the examinations.

Also from sources beyond Ebrey: Yan Ruoju (1636–1704) spent decades on the problem. He showed that the suspect chapters borrowed phrases from later books, contained mistakes about places and dates, and could not have existed in the form claimed. They had been put together long after the Zhou, probably in the 300s CE.

Here is the irony. The conservative turn began as a return to authority, yet its careful methods ended by showing that part of a classic was a forgery.

Pierian Spring · More to explore · Suzhou and Hangzhou · 1700s

A Sect Ran Hostels for Old Canal Boatmen, and the Qing Destroyed Them

Many White Lotus believers were pious vegetarians who tried to live good lives.
A related sect, the Luo teaching, set up hostels in Suzhou and Hangzhou for canal boatmen far from home.
Boatmen stayed free in winter, ate on credit, and grew old there, since many had never married.
Deeply suspicious, the Qing government had all the hostels destroyed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 285.

One more story about the sects, and it is not about rebellion at all. "Many White Lotus adherents were pious vegetarians who tried to live good lives" (Ebrey, p. 285).

Remember the boatmen who hauled grain up the Grand Canal in our last lecture? Many had no work and no pay for several months each winter. A related sect, the Luo teaching, which officials lumped with the White Lotus, "established a mission to help canal boatmen far from their homes." It "set up hostels in Suzhou and Hangzhou where the boatmen could stay for free and could get meals on credit, to be repaid when they were paid in advance for the next year's work." Because many of these men never married, "the hostels also served as retirement homes for elderly boatmen" · L3 o · rootless ↗.

And the ending: "The Qing government, however, was deeply suspicious and had all the hostels destroyed." From sources beyond Ebrey: the main crackdown came in 1768.

Ask yourself: why would a government destroy a charity? Because to the state, any organization of rootless men with its own leaders and beliefs looked like the seed of a rebellion.

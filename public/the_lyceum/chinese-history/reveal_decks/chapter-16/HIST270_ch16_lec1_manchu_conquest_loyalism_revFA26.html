The Gate in the Wall

A Scattered People Built a Banner State, a Ming General Opened the Gate, and China Had to Choose

1583–1683 · Study Buddy edition

Background essay ↗

Welcome! This deck is your Study Buddy for the Manchu conquest of China.

The story in one breath. In the 1580s the Jurchens lived in small, quarrelling villages beyond the Ming border. A chief named Nurhaci united them and organized them into banners. In 1644, after rebels took Beijing, a Ming general opened a gate in the Great Wall and let the Manchu armies in. Within weeks they held the capital, and over the next forty years every Chinese had to decide: fight, surrender, die, or withdraw and write (Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 270–273).

The painting shows the eastern end of the Wall, where it runs into the sea; the gate in our title stood near here. The name Shanhai Pass comes from sources beyond Ebrey, which says only "the eastern end of the Great Wall."

Recap: In 1644 Rebels Took Beijing and the Last Ming Emperor Died

In the 1630s and 1640s the Ming was bankrupt, divided, and hit by famine and rebellion.
In 1644 the rebel Li Zicheng captured Beijing, and the last Ming emperor took his own life.
Recap of the Ming collapse · Ch. 14 L4 · beijing-1644 ↗ rc-collapse ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 270, 272; Ch. 14 L4 for the Ming collapse.

We start where Chapter 14 ended. You already know how the Ming fell apart: an empty treasury, bitter factions at court, cold years and famine, and rebel armies sweeping across the north. If you need a refresher, the links on the slide open the Chapter 14 slides in a new tab.

In 1644 the rebel Li Zicheng captured Beijing. The last Ming emperor, in despair, took his own life on the hill behind the palace you see in the photo.

Notice who brought down the Ming. It was Chinese rebels, not foreign invaders. Yet the dynasty that replaced the Ming was not Chinese at all. The textbook explains that the Manchus were "called in to help suppress peasant rebellions" and then "took the throne themselves" (Ebrey, p. 270).

Why this matters: the Manchus did not knock down a strong Ming. They walked into a vacuum.

Q1 story · Beyond the Ming border · 1100s–1500s

The Manchus Descended from the Jurchens Who Once Ruled North China

The Manchus were descendants of the Jurchens, who had ruled north China as the Jin dynasty (1127–1234) · Ch. 8 L1 · jurchens ↗
They had lost the Jin written language but kept the Jin hairstyle: the front of the head shaved, the rest in a long braid called a queue.
They spoke a Tungus language, related to languages of Siberia and distantly to Korean and Japanese.
They were hunters, fishers, and farmers, not nomads, living in what is loosely called Manchuria.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 271; Ch. 8 L1 for the Jurchen Jin.

Meet the people of this story. The Manchus were descended from the Jurchens, the people who had conquered north China in the 1120s and ruled it as the Jin dynasty until the Mongols destroyed them in 1234. You met them in Chapter 8; the link opens that slide (Ebrey, p. 271).

Five centuries later, the Jurchens no longer wrote their old language, but they kept the hairstyle. A man shaved the front of his head and wore the rest of his hair in a long braid, the queue. Remember that haircut. It will matter a great deal by slide o.

They lived in scattered communities in Manchuria, today's Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang. In the south they lived alongside Mongols, Koreans, and Chinese. They were not nomads: they hunted, fished, and farmed, and like the Mongols they were superb horsemen and archers. Their society had elites and slaves, often captured Koreans or Chinese.

Q1 story · Nurhaci's headquarters · 1595–1596

In 1595 a Korean Visitor Found Small, Armed, Quarrelling Villages

A Korean who traveled to Nurhaci's headquarters found Jurchen settlements of twenty households or fewer.
People lived by fishing, hunting for pelts, gathering pine nuts and ginseng, and growing wheat, millet, and barley.
Villages quarrelled over resources, and no man left his village without a bow or a sword.
Nurhaci already employed men from Ming Liaodong who spoke Chinese and Manchu and could write Chinese.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 271. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Let a visitor show us the Jurchen world. In 1595 and 1596 a Korean traveled to the headquarters of a rising chief named Nurhaci and wrote down what he saw (Ebrey, p. 271).

He passed many small settlements, most no larger than twenty households. People fished, hunted animals for their furs, gathered pine nuts and ginseng, and grew wheat, millet, and barley. Among them, in the open country, lived nomadic Mongols in yurts.

It was not a peaceful world. Villages quarrelled over resources, and men did not step outside their village without bows and arrows or a sword.

One detail points to the future. Nurhaci had men in his service from the Ming territory of Liaodong who spoke both Chinese and Manchu and could write Chinese. A chief of small villages was already hiring the skills a state would need.

Why this matters: keep this picture in mind. Within fifty years these quarrelling villages would conquer China.

Q1 story · Manchuria and Beijing · 1583–1618

Nurhaci Rose from One Chief Among Many to Master of the Manchus

In 1583, at twenty-four, Nurhaci became leader of one group of Manchus and spent decades expanding his lands.
He knew the Ming firsthand: he led an embassy to Beijing in 1590 and offered to help fight Japan in Korea in 1592.
He and his children married Mongols as well as Manchus, sealing alliances.
In 1616 he called himself khan of a revived Jin dynasty; in 1618 he declared war on the Ming.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 271.

The Manchus themselves credited their rise to one man. Nurhaci (1559–1626) became leader of one group of Manchus in 1583, when he was twenty-four. Over the next few decades he expanded his territory and united the Manchus (Ebrey, p. 271).

He was no stranger to China. In 1590 he led an embassy to Beijing, and in 1592, when Japan invaded Korea, he offered to join the Ming effort to drive the Japanese out of Korea. The Ming and Korea both welcomed Manchu missions, because they saw the Manchus as a counterweight to the Mongols.

Marriage was a tool of power. Nurhaci and his children married Mongols as well as Manchus, and each marriage cemented an alliance.

In 1616 he made his ambitions public. He called himself khan, a ruler's title on the steppe, of a revived Jin dynasty. In 1618 he listed his grievances against the Ming and went to war, and in 1621 his forces overran Liaodong. The portrait was painted after his death, to honor the founder of the dynasty.

Instructor note: the textbook says Nurhaci offered help "the next year" after 1590 and joins the 1616 proclamation to the declaration of war (Ebrey, p. 271). Japan invaded Korea in 1592, and Nurhaci announced his Seven Grievances and attacked Fushun in 1618. The slide gives the corrected dates.

Source beyond Ebrey: the 1592 offer and the 1618 declaration (Swope, 2009; Wakeman, 1985).

Q1 names · One people, several names

Names Box: Jurchen, Manchu, Jin, Qing

One People, One State, Several Names

Jurchen the older name of the people; their ancestors ruled north China as the Jin, 1127–1234

Manchu the name Hong Taiji ordered his people to use in 1635, replacing Jurchen

Jin the "revived Jin" Nurhaci proclaimed in 1616 (often called the Later Jin)

Qing "pure": the name Nurhaci's son Hong Taiji gave the state in 1636; the dynasty ruled China 1644–1911

Mukden the Jin capital in Manchuria, today's Shenyang

The same people appear as Jurchens and as Manchus.
The same state appears as Jin and as Qing.
After Nurhaci died in 1626, his son Hong Taiji ruled from Mukden and renamed the state Qing in 1636.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 270–272.

Before we go on, sort out the names, because this story uses several for the same people and the same state.

Jurchen is the older name of the people. Manchu is the name they adopted for themselves (Ebrey, p. 270); in 1635 Hong Taiji ordered his people to use it in place of Jurchen. From here on we will call them Manchus.

The state had two names. In 1616 Nurhaci proclaimed a revived Jin, reaching back to his Jurchen ancestors. Many books call it the Later Jin; that label comes from sources beyond Ebrey. After Nurhaci died in 1626, his son Hong Taiji ruled from Mukden, today's Shenyang, and in 1636 renamed the state Qing, meaning "pure" (p. 271). The Qing would rule China from 1644 to 1911.

Instructor note: the textbook does not date the new name. The 1635 decree is from Crossley (1997).

Q1 concept · Tribal society

Concept: In a Tribal Society, People Followed Their Own Chiefs

Concept

Tribal society

A society organized by kin groups, families and clans, whose members owe loyalty first to their own chief rather than to a state.

Like the Mongols, the Manchus had a tribal social structure.
A man's first loyalty went to his own group and its chief.
That is why the villages the Korean visitor saw were armed and quarrelling.
A leader who wanted a state had to make people loyal to him instead.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 271.

Here is the idea that unlocks Nurhaci's achievement. The textbook says the Manchus, like the Mongols, "had a tribal social structure" (Ebrey, p. 271).

In a tribal society, people are organized by kin: families, clans, and larger groups of related clans. Each group follows its own chief. Loyalty runs to the group, not to a distant state.

That explains the scene on slide d, korean-visitor. Small groups competed for resources, and every man went armed.

It also explains Nurhaci's problem. A chief could win battles, but his followers' first loyalty still belonged to their own clans. If he wanted a lasting state, he had to weaken those old ties and attach people to himself. The next slides show how he did it.

Q1 story · Nurhaci's new order · c. 1600–1640s

Nurhaci's Banners Held Soldiers, Their Families, and Their Slaves

Nurhaci created new units called banners, each identified by its color.
Each banner was made up of military companies, but it included the soldiers' families and slaves too.
Each company had a captain whose position was hereditary, and many commanders came from Nurhaci's own lineage.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 271.

Now the key invention. Nurhaci created a new social basis for his armies in units called banners, identified by their colors (Ebrey, p. 271).

Here is the textbook's central sentence, the one your Reading Guide points to: "Each banner was made up of a set of military companies but included the families and slaves of the soldiers as well." So a banner was not just a regiment. It was an army unit, a community, and a way of governing people, all at once.

Each company had a captain, and the captaincy was hereditary, passed from father to son. Many of the commanding officers came from Nurhaci's own lineage. Loyalty now ran up through the banner to Nurhaci's family.

The picture comes from the Manchus' own illustrated record of Nurhaci's life. Hong Taiji had two Chinese painters make the pictures in 1635, and the Qianlong court redrew them in 1779 to 1781. This scene shows Nurhaci's horsemen taking the walled city of Ula in 1613; Ula was one of the rival Jurchen tribes his banners swallowed up. The captions are in Manchu, Chinese, and Mongolian, the three languages of the new state. The date of the Ula campaign and the history of the pictures come from sources beyond Ebrey.

How do you stop a defeated clan from rising again? Nurhaci's answer was simple and effective. When new groups of Manchus were defeated, they were distributed among several banners "to lessen their potential for subversion" (Ebrey, p. 271).

Follow the diagram. A clan that had once fought together under its own chief now found its men in different banners, under different captains, living alongside strangers. It could not easily regroup.

The textbook makes a comparison you have seen before. Like Chinggis Khan, who had reorganized his armies to reduce the importance of tribal ties, Nurhaci built a new basis for loyalty. The link opens the Chapter 12 slide on Chinggis's war machine.

Why this matters: the banner turned many quarrelling groups into one people loyal to one ruler. That is the transformation Review Question 1 asks about.

The banners kept growing. New companies and new banners were formed, and by 1644 there were twenty-four banners: eight Manchu, eight Mongol, and eight Chinese (Ebrey, p. 271).

Think about what that means. The organization Nurhaci built did not only unite the Manchus. It also brought in Mongols and Chinese, many of them from Liaodong, under the same system. The textbook calls it a "social-political-military organization that brought together Manchus, Mongols, and Chinese."

Q2 story · The eastern end of the Great Wall · 1644

General Wu Sangui Opened the Gates, and in Weeks the Manchus Held Beijing

The Ming general Wu Sangui was guarding the eastern end of the Great Wall when he heard that Li Zicheng had taken Beijing.
Wu appealed to the Manchu regent Dorgon, and they joined forces against the rebels.
Wu opened the gates of the Great Wall to let the Manchus in.
Together they beat Li Zicheng's army at the pass, and within a couple of weeks they had occupied Beijing.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 272.

Now the turning point. Wu Sangui (1612–1678) was a distinguished Ming general from Liaodong, stationed near the eastern end of the Great Wall. That is where he heard the news: Li Zicheng had captured Beijing, and the emperor was dead (Ebrey, p. 272).

On the other side of the Wall waited the Manchus, now led by Dorgon, Hong Taiji's brother, who was regent for Hong Taiji's five-year-old son. A regent rules on behalf of a child emperor. Wu wrote to Dorgon asking for help against the rebels, and the two agreed to join forces.

The textbook's sentence is short: "Wu opened the gates of the Great Wall to let the Manchus in, and within a couple of weeks they had occupied Beijing." This is the sentence your Reading Guide points to for Review Question 2. Between the gate and Beijing came a battle: on 27 May 1644 Li Zicheng's army attacked Wu at the pass, and Manchu cavalry broke its flank. Li fled to Beijing and then abandoned the city.

Instructor note: the textbook has Dorgon propose the alliance and skips the battle (Ebrey, p. 272). In the sources Wu made the appeal; the slide follows them (Wakeman, 1985).

The photo shows the gate tower at that pass, rebuilt many times since. The name Shanhai Pass comes from sources beyond Ebrey.

Q2 story · North China and Nanjing · 1644–1647

The Manchus Refused to Stop, and Chinese Generals Joined Them

The Manchus made clear that they meant to take the throne themselves, and Wu and many other Chinese generals joined them.
Ming officials crowned a Ming prince at Nanjing, as the Song had done after losing the north · Ch. 8 L1 · fall-1127 ↗
Dorgon rejected the Ming offer to buy the Manchus off.
Wu and Manchu generals hunted down the rebels: Li Zicheng by 1645, Zhang Xianzhong by 1647.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 272; Ch. 8 L1 for the Song precedent.

Wu Sangui may have thought he was hiring help. He was wrong. Once in Beijing, the Manchus made clear that they meant to conquer the rest of the country and take the throne. Wu joined them anyway, and so did many other Chinese generals (Ebrey, p. 272).

In the south, Ming officials tried an old strategy. They chose a Ming prince as emperor and moved the capital to Nanjing, just as the Song had kept going after losing the north in the 1120s. They even offered to buy the Manchus off, as the Song had once bought off the Jurchens. The link opens the Chapter 8 slide on that earlier crisis.

Dorgon saw no reason to stop. He sent Wu and Manchu generals after the rebel armies. Li Zicheng was eliminated in 1645 and Zhang Xianzhong in 1647.

The portrait shows Dorgon, the regent who made these choices for a boy emperor.

Q2 story · Yangzhou · 1645

Shi Kefa Refused to Surrender Yangzhou, and the City Was Massacred

Many Ming generals defected and many local officials opened their city gates to the Qing armies.
Shi Kefa, a scholar-official serving as minister of war, held Yangzhou and refused to surrender.
After a five-day battle, the angry Manchu general let his army sack the city for about ten days, killing a huge number of people.
As southern cities fell, many Ming loyalists took their own lives, often joined by the women of their families.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 272–273.

As the Qing armies moved south, Chinese faced a choice, and they chose differently. Many generals defected and their soldiers joined the Qing armies. Many local officials simply opened their city gates and surrendered (Ebrey, p. 272).

Shi Kefa chose otherwise. He was a scholar-official who had risen to minister of war at Nanjing, and he took charge of the defense, stationing his army at Yangzhou. He refused to surrender. A five-day battle followed.

The Manchu general was so angered by the resistance that he let his army take revenge on the city (p. 273). The sack lasted about ten days. A survivor, Wang Xiuchu, wrote an eyewitness account; later writers put the dead at 800,000, but no one could count them. We will not dwell on the details.

Instructor note: the textbook's "hundreds of thousands" (p. 273) cannot be checked; the slide says "a huge number" (Struve, 1993; Meyer-Fong, 2003).

Others chose death over the new dynasty. As cities in the south fell, large numbers of Ming loyalists committed suicide, and their wives, mothers, and daughters often joined them.

Q2 story · The whole empire · summer 1645

In 1645 Every Chinese Man Was Ordered to Shave His Head Like a Manchu

In summer 1645 the Manchu command ordered every Chinese serving in its armies to shave the front of the head in the Manchu fashion.
The point was presumably to make it easy to tell which side a man was on.
Soon the order was extended to all Chinese men.
The hairstyle became a visible test of loyalty to the new dynasty.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 271, 273.

Remember the Manchu hairstyle from slide c: the front of the head shaved, the rest worn in a queue. In the summer of 1645, the Manchu command ordered all Chinese serving in its armies to shave their heads the same way, presumably so that it was easy to recognize which side they were on (Ebrey, p. 273).

Soon the order was extended to all Chinese men. A Chinese man who obeyed now looked like a subject of the Manchus. A man who refused marked himself as a rebel.

Why would hair matter so much? Chinese men traditionally let their hair grow long and wore it bound up, and many saw that as part of being Chinese and of honoring the bodies their parents gave them. That point about parents comes from sources beyond Ebrey; the textbook says only that the order "aroused deep resentment."

Instructor note: the textbook starts the story in 1645 (p. 273). Dorgon first ordered head-shaving in 1644, withdrew the order when it met resistance, and reimposed it in July 1645 (Kuhn, 1990; Wakeman, 1985). The slide's sequence stands.

The painting comes from Canton in 1826, nearly two centuries later. By then the shaved forehead and queue were simply how Chinese men looked.

Q2 story · The Yangzi delta · 1645

The Order Turned Surrender into Revolt, and Defiant Cities Paid in Blood

The head-shaving order aroused deep resentment among Chinese men.
It made it easier for Ming loyalists to organize resistance.
When newly conquered towns refused to shave, Manchu commanders ordered defiant cities such as Jiading, Changshu, and Jiangyin slaughtered.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 273. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

The order backfired, at least at first. It "aroused deep resentment," the textbook says, and made it easier for Ming loyalists to organize resistance (Ebrey, p. 273).

Think about the people involved. Many of them had already accepted the new dynasty. Their towns had surrendered. But being told to change their appearance, in public, as a sign of submission, was one demand too many.

The Manchu commanders answered with force. When newly conquered places refused to shave, the commanders felt justified in ordering the slaughter of defiant cities, among them Jiading, Changshu, and Jiangyin. Again, the textbook gives the facts briefly, and so will we.

Why this matters: a single order turned the conquest from a change of rulers into a test every man had to face personally.

Resistance did not end with Yangzhou or with the shaved heads. Ming loyalists kept fighting long after there was little hope. The Manchus did not defeat the two main loyalist camps until 1661 and 1662 (Ebrey, p. 273).

The last holdout was at sea. Zheng Chenggong, whom Europeans called Koxinga, drove the Dutch from Taiwan in 1662 and made the island his base, but he died that same year. His son and grandson held out until 1683, almost forty years after the Manchus entered Beijing. Look at the box on the slide: two names, one man.

Instructor note: the textbook says Zheng himself held out until 1683 (Ebrey, p. 273). He died in 1662; Zheng Jing and then Zheng Keshuang ruled until Shi Lang's fleet took Taiwan (Andrade, 2011).

Q2 concept · Loyalism

Concept: Loyalism Meant Honoring the Fallen Dynasty by Refusing the New One

Concept

Ming loyalism

Staying faithful to a fallen dynasty by refusing to serve the dynasty that replaced it, whether by fighting, by dying, or by withdrawing from public life.

Song loyalists had refused to serve the Mongols four centuries earlier · Ch. 12 L3 · song-loyalists ↗
Militant loyalists fought: Shi Kefa, the defiant cities, Koxinga.
Others died rather than live under the Qing.
Less militant loyalists refused office and wrote, and their writing questioned dynastic rule itself.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 273; Ch. 12 L3 for the Song loyalists.

Give a name to what you have been watching. Loyalism means staying faithful to a fallen dynasty by refusing to serve the new one. You met it in Chapter 12, when Song loyalists refused to serve the Mongols; the link opens that slide.

Ming loyalism came in more than one form. Some loyalists fought: Shi Kefa at Yangzhou, the cities that refused to shave, Koxinga on Taiwan. Some chose death.

The textbook then says that "Ming loyalism also took less militant forms" (Ebrey, p. 273). Several leading thinkers refused to serve the Qing, and that refusal gave them time to think and write. Their critiques of the Ming led them to question China's whole heritage of rule by dynasties.

Q2 story · Zhejiang · after 1649

Huang Zongxi Decided the Real Problem Was the Emperor Himself

Huang Zongxi (1610–1695) served the Ming resistance court and followed it in retreat.
After 1649 he lived in retirement at home in Zhejiang.

The first thinker. Huang Zongxi (1610–1695) served the Ming resistance in Zhejiang under the Prince of Lu and followed it in its retreat to the coastal islands. After 1649 he gave up and lived in retirement at his home in Zhejiang province (Ebrey, p. 273).

The textbook says the conquest was so traumatic that he reconsidered many of the basic ideas of Chinese political order. He decided that the Ming's problems were not minor ones, like poor supervision of eunuchs, but major ones, "such as the imperial institution itself."

That is a startling conclusion for a Confucian scholar. Think back to Chapter 14 and the Ming founder who ruled as a despot, with no chancellor to check him; the link opens that slide. Huang was asking whether the problem was not one bad emperor but the job of emperor.

The second thinker. Gu Yanwu (1613–1682) fought first: he took part in the defense of his native city. Then he watched his adoptive mother starve herself to death rather than live under Manchu rule (Ebrey, p. 273). She had been engaged to a young man of the Gu family who died before the wedding; she lived on as his faithful "widow," and Gu was adopted as his heir. Pierian Spring · Q14 and Q4 concept · Hereditary caste

Concept: A Hereditary Caste Was Born to Serve the State

Concept

Hereditary occupational caste

A group whose rank and work are fixed at birth: you belong because your parents did, and you are expected to do the job your group exists to do.

Bannermen became, in a sense, a hereditary occupational caste, ranked above others in society.
They were expected to devote themselves to service to the state.
They were expected to live apart from non-banner Chinese.
They were not allowed to intermarry with them.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 277.

Here is the idea behind the garrisons. The textbook says the bannermen became "in a sense a hereditary occupational caste, ranked above others in society" (Ebrey, p. 277).

Unpack that phrase. Hereditary: you were a bannerman because your father was. Occupational: your group existed to do one kind of work, serving the state as soldiers and officials. Caste: a group set apart, with its own rank.

The separation was deliberate. Bannermen were expected to live apart from non-banner Chinese, and they were not allowed to intermarry with them.

Notice the word "in a sense." The textbook is borrowing a term to describe the pattern; the Qing did not use it. Keep that caution when you write.

Pierian Spring · Q4 story · The courtroom · 1700s

In Court a Manchu Could Stand Where a Chinese Had to Kneel

Bannermen fell under imperial commissioners, not the local magistrate or prefect.
When a Chinese and a Manchu testified, the Chinese had to kneel before the magistrate, but the Manchu could stand.
For the same crime, a Manchu got a lighter punishment, such as sixty days in the cangue instead of exile for life.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 278. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

Bannermen had legal privileges too (Ebrey, p. 278).

First, they had their own courts. Bannermen came under the jurisdiction of imperial commissioners, not the local magistrate or prefect who judged everyone else.

Second, posture. If a Chinese and a Manchu were both brought into court to testify, the Chinese had to kneel before the magistrate. The Manchu could stand. Picture that scene: the difference in status was visible to everyone in the room.

Third, punishment. If both were found guilty of the same crime, the Manchu received a lighter sentence. The textbook's example: wearing the cangue, a large wooden collar, for sixty days, instead of being exiled for life.

Pierian Spring · Q4 story · Beijing and the hunting grounds · 1700s

They Spoke Beijing Chinese, and Their Archery Faded

Within a generation, bannermen spoke the Chinese dialect of Beijing, and Manchu became a language learned at school.
City life and long peace wore down their skills as horsemen and archers.
Qianlong, who knew how the Jin Jurchens had lost their ways in China, discouraged bannermen from Chinese culture.

Poverty was not the only worry. The bannermen were also losing what made them Manchu (Ebrey, pp. 278–279).

Within a generation of settling in China proper, they were using the Chinese dialect of Beijing as their common language. The emperors kept telling them to study spoken and written Manchu, but it became a second language learned at school. Some customs survived, such as using personal names alone.

The skills the state cared about most were horsemanship and archery, and city life and long peace wore them down. The painting shows the Qianlong emperor himself hunting hare on horseback, the example he wanted his bannermen to follow.

Qianlong knew history. He knew how the Jin Jurchens, back in Chapter 8, had seen their soldiers take up Chinese ways. So he discouraged bannermen from Chinese culture and told them that frugality, living simply, was a Manchu trait, even though his own court was as lavish as any in Chinese history.

Hong Taiji Built the Qing State, and Dorgon Carried It to Beijing

Nurhaci's son Hong Taiji succeeded him in 1626 and built up the Jin state from Mukden.
He grudgingly used Chinese officials but wanted a multiethnic elite equally skilled in war and in paperwork.

A last portrait to fill a gap in the story. Between Nurhaci's death and the gate at the Wall came Hong Taiji, Nurhaci's son, who ruled from 1626 to 1643 (Ebrey, p. 271).

From his capital at Mukden he consolidated the Jin state. He used Chinese bureaucrats, but grudgingly. His real goal was to replace them with a multiethnic elite "equally competent in warfare and documents," men who could fight and write.

Queue

The queue was the Manchu man's hairstyle: the front of the head shaved, and the rest of the hair grown long and worn in a single braid down the back (Ebrey, p. 271).

×

Banner

A banner was a Manchu unit identified by a color. It was made up of military companies but also included the soldiers' families and slaves, so it was an army, a community, and an administration at once (Ebrey, p. 271).

Members were called bannermen.

×

Stipend

A stipend is a regular allowance paid by the state. Banner stipends came from the rents on expropriated land and were paid partly in silver and partly in grain (Ebrey, p. 277).

×

Cangue

The cangue was a large wooden collar locked around a convicted person's neck as a public punishment (Ebrey, p. 278).

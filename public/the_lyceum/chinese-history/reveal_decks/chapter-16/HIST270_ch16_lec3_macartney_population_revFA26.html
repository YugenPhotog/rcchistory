The story in one breath. In 1793 a British lord arrived with five demands and six hundred cases of gifts, refused to kowtow, and went home with nothing, because the Qing court saw no merit in what he asked. We start with something you already know. In Chapter 14 you saw how the Ming handled the outside world. Foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts, called tribute, and received gifts in return; trade was folded into that ceremony. And the Ming said exactly which ports each country's merchants could use. The two links open those slides.

The Qing kept the same approach. It regulated relations with countries beyond its borders "through a diplomatic system modeled on the Ming one." Korea, Ryukyu, Japan, Vietnam, and many states of Southeast Asia sent envoys to Beijing (Ebrey, p. 280).

Europeans, the textbook says, "were not full players in this system, but they had a marginal presence." Hold on to that phrase. The British would try to change it. Our last lecture ended with their ship arriving in 1793; the last link takes you back to that slide.

Q6 story · London and Guangzhou · 1700s

Britain Was Buying Tea by the Shipload

European trade with China was concentrated at Guangzhou, in the far south.
Soon after 1600 the Dutch East India Company pushed aside the Spanish and Portuguese, and the British East India Company soon competed with the Dutch.
In the 1600s Europeans wanted porcelain and silk; in the 1700s they wanted tea.
By the end of the 1700s, tea made up 80 percent of Chinese exports to Europe.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280.

To understand why a British lord sailed to China, start with a cup of tea. Look at this English family around 1720: the silver kettle, the small porcelain cups, the teapot. Every leaf in that pot came from China, and so, very likely, did the cups. The painting is our addition, from outside the textbook.

European trade with China was concentrated at Guangzhou, which Europeans called Canton, in the far south. Soon after 1600 the Dutch East India Company had largely pushed the Spanish and Portuguese out of the trade with China, Japan, and the East Indies. Before long the British East India Company was competing with the Dutch (Ebrey, p. 280). These were private trading companies, licensed by their governments.

What did they buy? In the seventeenth century, mostly porcelain and silk. In the eighteenth century, tea. By the end of the century, tea made up 80 percent of Chinese exports to Europe. Europeans also admired China itself in these years, as a source of luxuries and as a land ruled by wise emperors.

Q6 story · Guangzhou · 1700s

At Guangzhou, Europeans Could Not Enter the City or Even Learn Chinese

Europeans could trade only at Guangzhou, though tea grew mostly in the Yangzi valley, far to the north.
Licensed Chinese merchants formed a guild and had to guarantee that the Europeans obeyed Qing rules.
Europeans had to pay cash, and could not enter the walled city, ride in sedan chairs, bring in women or weapons, or learn Chinese.
Historians call these rules the Canton system (outside the textbook).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280; the name "Canton system" and the date 1757 from outside the textbook.

Now look at where the Europeans actually lived. This row of buildings with flags stood on the riverbank outside the walls of Guangzhou. The British, Dutch, Swedes, and others each had one. They were called "factories," meaning the offices and warehouses of their "factors," or agents. The painting is from 1806, a little after our story; it is from outside the textbook.

The Qing, like the Ming, specified where merchants of each country could trade, and Europeans could trade only in Guangzhou. That was inconvenient. Tea grew mostly in the Yangzi valley, so it had to be carried hundreds of miles south, and the cost was added to the price (Ebrey, p. 280).

The Chinese merchants licensed to deal with the Europeans formed their own guild, and the government made them guarantee that the Europeans obeyed Qing rules. And the rules were strict. Europeans had to pay cash for what they bought. They were forbidden to enter the walled city of Guangzhou, to ride in sedan chairs, to bring women or weapons into their quarters, and to learn Chinese.

Historians call this arrangement the Canton system. The name, and the date when Guangzhou became the only port open to Europeans, 1757, come from outside the textbook. Click the term for a short definition. The system lasted until the Opium War, which you will meet in Chapter 18.

Q6 concept · Balance of trade

Concept: A Lopsided Balance of Trade Meant Silver Flowed One Way

Concept

Balance of trade

The difference between what a country sells to another and what it buys from it. When one side buys far more than it sells, it must make up the difference by paying in money, which in this period meant silver.

As British purchases of tea grew, the balance of trade became more and more lopsided.
British merchants could not find goods that Chinese merchants wanted to buy from them.
So the British paid in silver, much of it from the Americas (outside the textbook) · Ch. 14 L2 · silver-route ↗
Britain wanted that to change.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280; Ch. 14 L2 for silver; silver from the Americas from outside the textbook.

Here is the economic problem behind the whole story. A balance of trade compares what one country sells to another with what it buys from it.

The British bought more and more tea. But, as the textbook says, "British merchants could not find goods that Chinese merchants would buy from them" (Ebrey, p. 280). So the balance of trade "became more lopsided."

How do you pay for goods when the seller wants nothing you make? In cash, and cash meant silver. You saw in Chapter 14 how American silver poured into Ming China; the link opens that slide. The British were now paying for tea the same way. That detail is from outside the textbook.

Keep this in mind. A merchant nation watching its silver sail away will want to sell something to China. That is one reason Britain sent an ambassador.

Q6 story · London to China · 1792–1793

In 1793 Lord Macartney Arrived with Five Goals

The British government distrusted the rich East India Company and wanted direct diplomatic relations with China.
King George III sent Lord Macartney, a former ambassador to Russia and governor of Madras in India.
He was to win a trading place near the tea districts, a commercial treaty, a market for British goods, and an envoy in Beijing.
He was also to open Japan and Southeast Asia to British commerce.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280.

The merchants were not the only unhappy party. The British government was "suspicious of the British East India Company, which had made great fortunes from its trade with China." It wanted to open direct diplomatic relations with China, partly as a way to curb the company (Ebrey, p. 280).

So King George III sent Lord George Macartney, an experienced diplomat: a former ambassador to Russia and former governor of Madras in India. The portrait was painted just before he sailed by Thomas Hickey, who went along as the embassy's painter; that is from outside the textbook.

Count his goals as I read them. In the textbook's words, "Macartney was instructed to secure a place for British traders near the tea-producing areas, negotiate a commercial treaty, create a desire for British products, arrange for diplomatic representation in Beijing, and open Japan and Southeast Asia to British commerce as well." That is five. Your Reading Guide asks you to copy that sentence, on page 280.

Notice what they share. Every goal would have broken a Qing rule you saw on slide d.

Q6 story · The voyage · 1792–1793

He Brought Six Hundred Cases of Gifts, and His Only Chinese Speaker Was a Twelve-Year-Old Boy

Macartney traveled with an entourage of eighty-four people.
Six hundred cases held British goods to impress the court: clocks, telescopes, knives, globes, plate glass, Wedgwood pottery, paintings, woolens, and carpets.
The only member of the party who could speak Chinese was a twelve-year-old boy.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280; the boy's name and later life from outside the textbook.

Picture the embassy as it set out. Macartney traveled with an entourage of eighty-four and six hundred cases packed with British goods that he hoped would impress the court and attract trade: clocks, telescopes, knives, globes, plate glass, Wedgwood pottery, landscape paintings, woolen cloth, and carpets (Ebrey, p. 280). It was a sales catalogue as much as a gift.

Now the detail that tells you how little the two sides knew each other. The only member of the British party able to speak Chinese was a twelve-year-old boy, who had learned some Chinese by talking with Chinese on the long voyage.

The painting shows that boy. He was George Thomas Staunton, son of Macartney's deputy. Here he is home again, greeting his mother, with a Chinese boy carrying a chest of tea. He grew up to become one of Britain's first serious students of China and translated the Qing law code. All of that is from outside the textbook.

Q6 story · Beijing and the summer retreat · 1793

Macartney Refused to Kowtow, and Met Qianlong at His Summer Retreat

Macartney carried a letter from George III; it had been written in Chinese, but its language was not appropriate for addressing an emperor.
Still, the British party was allowed to go on to Beijing.
There Macartney objected to performing the kowtow.
Finally he was allowed to meet the Qianlong emperor more informally at his summer retreat.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 280; the place name Rehe, the date, and how Macartney knelt from outside the textbook.

When Macartney reached China in 1793 he asked to see the emperor to present a letter from King George III. The letter had been written in Chinese, but "its language was not appropriate for addressing an emperor" (Ebrey, p. 280). Still, the party was allowed to proceed to Beijing.

There the real obstacle appeared. When officials instructed him how to behave before the emperor, Macartney objected to performing the kowtow: kneeling on both knees and bowing the head to the ground. Click the word for a definition. To the Qing this was ordinary court ritual. To Macartney it meant admitting that his king was the emperor's inferior.

Finally a compromise. Macartney was permitted to meet the Qianlong emperor more informally at his summer retreat. That retreat was at Rehe, today Chengde, beyond the Great Wall, and the audience took place in a great tent on 14 September 1793, with Macartney kneeling on one knee as he would to his own king. Those details are from outside the textbook.

The picture is by William Alexander, the embassy's artist. Look for the round tent, the lines of officials, and the emperor carried in an open chair under parasols. Alexander was left behind in Beijing and painted it from a colleague's sketches, so it is a reconstruction; that fact is also from outside the textbook.

Q6 story · The court's answer · 1793

The Court Saw No Merit in His Requests and Kept Its System

No negotiations followed the meeting: the Qing court saw no merit in Macartney's requests.
The court was as interested in keeping its regulated trade as Britain was in doing away with it.
Qianlong's reply to the king, in a 1914 translation: "I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures" (outside the textbook).
The Chinese did not learn much about Europe or Britain from this encounter.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 280–281; the edict line from E. Backhouse and J. O. P. Bland, Annals and Memoirs of the Court of Peking (1914), outside the textbook.

After the audience, nothing. "No negotiations followed this meeting," the textbook says, "because the Qing court saw no merit in Macartney's requests. It was as interested in maintaining its existing system of regulated trade as Britain was in doing away with it" (Ebrey, p. 280).

Think about it from the emperor's side. He was eighty-two and had ruled for nearly sixty years. The Canton system worked: Europeans paid silver for tea, and they were kept at the edge. Every one of Macartney's goals would have weakened that control.

Qianlong sent his answer to George III as an edict. One famous line, in an English translation published in 1914: "I set no value on objects strange or ingenious, and have no use for your country's manufactures." The edict and its wording are from outside the textbook. Historians warn that this translation made the emperor sound more ignorant than he was; the court was refusing a political demand, not just mocking the gifts.

What came of it? The British went home and wrote books about China, which you can read about in the Pierian Spring. But the textbook ends with a sobering sentence: the Chinese "did not learn much about Europe or Britain from this encounter" (p. 281).

Q7 story · Across the empire · 1700–1800

In the 1700s China's Population Nearly Doubled, to About 300 Million

For more than a century three hard-working emperors gave China a respite from war and disorder.
The population seems to have nearly doubled, from 150–175 million to 300–325 million.
The Ming population had also grown fast; the Qing doubling came on top of it · Ch. 14 L3 · doubling ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 273; Ch. 14 L3 for Ming population growth; world share from outside the textbook.

Now we turn from the emperor's tent to the crowd. For more than a century, China was ruled by just three emperors, Kangxi, Yongzheng, and Qianlong, each "hard working, talented, and committed to making the Qing Dynasty a success." And the population grew. It seems to have nearly doubled during this period, from between 150 and 175 million to between 300 and 325 million. You saw in Chapter 14 that the Ming population had already grown; the link takes you back. The Qing doubling came on top of that.

To feel the scale: in 1800 roughly a third of the world's people may have lived in China. That comparison comes from outside the textbook. Q7 analysis · Why so many people?

Warmer Weather, American Crops, Fewer New Diseases, and Famine Relief

Historians point to four causes, and no single one explains the growth.
American crops let farmers use hilly or poor land they had rejected before · Ch. 14 L2 · new-crops ↗
When drought or flood struck, the Qing opened granaries and gave direct relief, so fewer people died in famines.
Fewer deaths in bad years meant more children grew up to have children.

🥔

New World crops spread: sweet potatoes, white potatoes, peanuts, maize.

✚

New diseases spread more slowly than in the 1500s, when global traffic first expanded.

⚖

Four factors from Ebrey, pp. 273, 284. Maize is from outside the textbook.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 273, 283–284; Ch. 14 L2 for New World crops.

Why did the population double? The textbook gives four factors in one sentence, the sentence your Reading Guide asks you to copy: "Population growth during the course of the eighteenth century has been attributed to many factors: global warming that extended the growing season; expanded use of New World crops; slowing of the spread of new diseases that had accompanied the sixteenth-century expansion of global traffic; and the efficiency of the Qing government in providing relief in times of famine" (Ebrey, p. 273).

Take them one at a time. A warmer climate meant a longer growing season. New World crops such as sweet potatoes, white potatoes, and peanuts grew on land "previously rejected as too hilly or infertile" (p. 283); you met them in Chapter 14. The terrible epidemics that came with the first wave of global travel in the 1500s had slowed.

The fourth factor is the government. When drought or floods ruined a harvest, starving families took to the roads, and epidemics swept the refugee camps. The Qing was "remarkably successful in curbing the death toll of major famines by careful administration of granaries and provision of direct relief," and "its success contributed to the rapid rise in population" (p. 284).

So good government had an unexpected result: more people than the land could easily feed. That is where the next slides go.

Q7 concept · Partible inheritance

Concept: Partible Inheritance Split the Family Farm Among All the Sons

Concept

Partible inheritance

The custom of dividing a family's land among all of its sons, rather than leaving it to one heir. With more surviving sons in each generation, each son's share grew smaller.

A farm that fed one family was divided among the sons, and then among their sons.
With population growing, the practice led to farms too small to support a family.
Small farmers easily fell into debt and sold or mortgaged their land.
Poorer families turned to side work, such as weaving mats and baskets, or sent men to town in winter.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 283–284; the equal-shares definition from outside the textbook.

Here is the concept that turns more people into a social problem. Partible inheritance means dividing the family land among all the sons, rather than handing it to one heir. The detail that the sons usually received equal shares comes from outside the textbook.

Do the arithmetic. A father with a farm just big enough for one family has three sons who live to adulthood. Each gets a third. If each of them has three sons, each grandson gets a ninth. In a century of rising population, the textbook says, "population growth, coupled with the practice of partible inheritance, led to the proliferation of farms too small to support a family" (Ebrey, p. 284).

Villages did nothing to protect these small farmers. Chinese villages were "open": they held no land in common, and small farmers "could easily fall into debt and sell or mortgage all or part of their land." Families without enough land put their women and children to sideline work such as weaving mats and baskets, and in winter the men went to nearby towns to look for temporary jobs (p. 283).

Q7 story · Villages and towns · 1700s

Concubinage and Infanticide Left Many Men with No One to Marry

Many men could not marry, because there were too few marriageable women.
The rich took extra women as concubines, and poor families who could not raise another daughter sometimes let newborn girls die.
The shortage fed a traffic in women: girls and women were sold as wives or concubines, wives were sold by desperate husbands, and some were kidnapped (outside the textbook).
A man with no land and no wife had little to keep him in his village.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 284; the traffic in women from outside the textbook (Matthew H. Sommer).

Population growth had a second, painful consequence, and the textbook states it plainly. "Many men could not marry because of a shortage of marriageable women" (Ebrey, p. 284).

Two things caused the shortage. At the top of society, "the rich" took "extra women as concubines," secondary wives who lived in the household. At the bottom, poor families "unwilling to rear another daughter" practiced sex-selective infanticide: they let some newborn girls die.

The shortage had a darker consequence that the textbook does not mention. From outside the textbook: the historian Matthew Sommer, working from more than a thousand Qing court cases, shows that a scarcity of wives among the poor created a widespread traffic in women. Poor families sold daughters into richer households as servants or concubines; a poor man could pay a bride price for a wife; and in a crisis a desperate husband might sell his own wife to another man. Traffickers also kidnapped or lured away women and girls to sell. Qing law punished most of these sales, but magistrates knew how common they were.

The picture shows the other side of the story: a wedding in a wealthy Suzhou household, with the bride's red sedan chair, musicians, and guests. For a poor man without land, a wedding like this was out of reach.

Put the last two slides together. A man with too little land, and no prospect of a wife or children, had little reason to stay in his village. On the next slide, we follow him out.

Q7 story · Roads, canals, and mines · 1700s

Landless Bachelors Hauled Boats, Carried Chairs, and Mined Copper

Men with little or no land and unable to marry often left their villages to look for work.
Some traveled with tools as carpenters or farmhands; others pulled grain boats up the Grand Canal.
In cities they worked as dockhands, sedan-chair carriers, and night-soil collectors; others walked to the copper mines of the southwest.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, p. 284; Ch. 16 L2 for the soul-stealing panic.

Now follow the man who left. "Men with little or no land and unable to marry commonly would leave their villages in search of work" (Ebrey, p. 284).

Some traveled with their tools to do carpentry or farm work. Others found jobs pulling the boats that carried grain up the Grand Canal. In the cities they worked as dockhands, sedan-chair carriers, or night-soil collectors, carrying human waste out to the fields as fertilizer. When copper mines opened in the southwest, unemployed men traveled long distances to work as miners.

Look at the engraving by the embassy's artist William Alexander. Teams of men turn big capstans to drag a loaded boat up a stone ramp from one canal to the next. The British crossed this one in November 1793. It is a different canal from the Grand Canal, but it is the same kind of muscle work.

These men made settled people nervous. "Both the government and settled villagers were wary of these rootless men. They were quick to suspect strangers when crimes occurred." Q7 story · The hills and frontiers · 1700s

Settlers Pushed into the Hills, Where the Miao and Other Peoples Lived

As the population grew, people pushed into peripheral areas, clearing hillsides and moving to the frontiers.
Sweet potatoes, white potatoes, peanuts, and tobacco let them farm land once thought too hilly or poor.
In the process they often pushed out peoples who had lived there for centuries, such as the Miao.
What happened to the Miao is the story of the Pierian Spring · t · ps-uplands ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 283–284, 286.

One more consequence, and it carries us to the edges of the empire. The richest areas were "core areas centered on major cities." Around them lay "more peripheral areas, where population density was much lower because the land was harder to farm" (Ebrey, p. 283).

"During the course of the eighteenth century, as the population grew, people pushed into these peripheral areas, clearing upland areas and moving to the frontiers." New World crops such as sweet potatoes, white potatoes, peanuts, and tobacco "made possible exploitation of land previously rejected as too hilly or infertile."

But that land was not empty. The settlers were "often pushing out aboriginal peoples who had lived in the area for centuries." In the south these peoples were called Miao, Yao, Zhuang, and many other names, and they were "often exploited and treated with contempt" (p. 284).

The picture comes from one of the illustrated albums that Qing officials made to describe the non-Han peoples of the southwest; here, Red Miao villagers go to their fields. The full story of the Miao is in the Pierian Spring, where it ends with Review Question 11.

Pierian Spring · Q11 story · The southern hills · 1700s

Settlers with Sweet Potatoes Climbed into the Southern Hills

As Han families multiplied, the crowded lowlands could not hold them all.
They pushed into peripheral areas, clearing upland forests and moving to the frontiers.
Sweet potatoes, white potatoes, peanuts, and tobacco grew on slopes too steep or poor for rice.
The hills they cleared already had owners · p · uplands ↩

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 283, 286. Image: AI-generated interpretation.

This story begins where slide p left off. The Han population doubled in a century, and the land in the river valleys was already full. So people "pushed into these peripheral areas, clearing upland areas and moving to the frontiers" (Ebrey, p. 283).

What made the hills worth the effort was a set of crops from the Americas: sweet potatoes, white potatoes, peanuts, and tobacco, which "made possible exploitation of land previously rejected as too hilly or infertile." A family could burn off a slope, cut a terrace, and plant sweet potatoes where rice would never grow.

But, as the textbook adds, settlers in these areas were "often pushing out aboriginal peoples who had lived in the area for centuries." The next slide introduces those peoples.

The government did see the danger. Officials "repeatedly tried to find ways to separate the Miao and the Han settlers, but with little success" (Ebrey, p. 286).

What did separation look like? From outside the textbook: at different times, rules forbade Han settlers to buy Miao land or to marry Miao, and soldiers garrisoned walls, watchtowers, and forts along the Hunan–Guizhou border. The aim was to draw a line on the map and keep each people on its side.

It did not work. Settlers were hungry for land, Miao families sold or mortgaged land when they fell into debt, and local officials and clerks often sided with the Han. Meanwhile, the textbook reminds us, the indigenous peoples of the south were "often exploited and treated with contempt" (p. 284).

The pressure finally broke in 1795. "In 1795 there was a great revolt of Miao along the Hunan-Guizhou border, where a Han ethnic group called the Hakka had recently been moving in" (Ebrey, p. 286).

From outside the textbook: the rebel leaders called on their followers to drive out the "guest people," the newcomers, and recover Miao land. The emperor sent his best generals, and two of them died during the campaign. The main fighting lasted into 1797.

The painting is one of a set the Qing court made to celebrate the campaign; the inscription at the top is dated to the spring of 1795. Look at the columns of soldiers with banners and muskets winding through impossible mountains. The court wanted a victory picture, but the terrain shows why the war was so hard.

And it was hard. The textbook says the Qing "found the Miao uprisings to be as difficult to suppress as the concurrent White Lotus ones," the religious revolt you will meet in our next lecture. In the last years of Qianlong's reign, the empire was fighting at its edges and in its heart at once.

Pierian Spring · More to explore · London · 1790s

Macartney's Men Went Home and Wrote One-Sided Books About China

Several members of the mission wrote books about China on their return, often illustrated, and less rosy than the Jesuits' reports.
The official account, by George Staunton, said Chinese women were bred to "implicit obedience" to fathers and husbands.
Almost none of the authors spoke Chinese or could read Chinese sources; they reported what they saw from a closely escorted journey (outside the textbook).
So the books were outsiders' views, often ethnocentric, judging China by British standards (outside the textbook).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 280–281; the authors' limits and the younger Staunton's later work from outside the textbook.

The embassy failed in Beijing, but it succeeded in London, as publishing. "Several members of the Macartney mission wrote books about China on their return. These books, often illustrated, contained descriptions of many elements of Chinese culture and social customs, less rosy than the reports of the Jesuits a century or two earlier" (Ebrey, p. 280).

The official account was written by George Staunton, the boy's father. He described Chinese women as subjugated: "Women, especially in the lower walks of life, are bred with little other principle than that of implicit obedience to their fathers or their husbands." Peasant wives did all the weaving and worked very hard, he wrote, yet their husbands did not always allow them to sit at the table (pp. 280–281).

Now read these books critically. From outside the textbook: apart from the boy, none of the British could speak Chinese, and none could read Chinese books. They depended on hired interpreters, and Qing officials escorted them along a fixed route and kept them from wandering. So they reported only what they saw from the road, and they judged it by British standards. That is what historians call ethnocentric: seeing another culture through the lens of your own as the norm. The Jesuits, by contrast, had lived in China for decades and read Chinese.

The textbook suggests these books helped Europeans see "more of the complexity of China." A fairer way to put it: they replaced one stereotype, the Jesuits' wise and orderly empire, with another, a backward and stagnant one, which shaped British attitudes in the decades before the Opium War.

To be fair to one of them: the boy, George Thomas Staunton, really did learn Chinese. He later worked for the East India Company at Guangzhou, and in 1810 he published the first English translation of the Qing legal code. That is from outside the textbook.

William Alexander's picture is a good example of the limits. It shows a lady of a wealthier household with her son and a servant, and his text says that women of rank were scarcely ever seen outside their homes. He could draw what he glimpsed; he could not ask her about her life.

Did Ordinary Chinese Live as Well as Europeans?

Some scholars argue that China's standard of living in the mid-1700s was comparable to Europe's.
Jiangnan, China's richest region, compared well with England and the Netherlands, Europe's richest.
Life expectancy, food consumption, and even transportation were at similar levels.
Its richest men were the salt merchants · L2 kk · ps-jiang-chun ↗

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 16, pp. 273–274; the historian's name from outside the textbook.

A final puzzle. If China was so crowded, were its people poor? Not necessarily. "Some scholars have recently argued that China's overall standard of living in the mid-eighteenth century was comparable to Europe's and that the standards of China's most developed regions, such as the Jiangnan region, compared favorably to the most developed regions of Europe at the time, such as England and the Netherlands." Life expectancy, food consumption, and even facilities for transportation were at similar levels (Ebrey, pp. 273–274).

The best-known of those scholars is Kenneth Pomeranz, whose book The Great Divergence (2000) asked why Europe and China, so alike in 1750, went such different ways after 1800. His name and book are from outside the textbook.

You met one of the richest men of Jiangnan in our last lecture: the salt merchant Jiang Chun, who hosted the emperor in his garden. The link takes you back.

Hold this together with today's lecture. Both can be true: a prosperous core, and millions of landless men and pushed-out hill peoples at its edges.

Night soil

Human waste collected from city households and latrines, usually before dawn, and carried out to be sold to farmers as fertilizer.

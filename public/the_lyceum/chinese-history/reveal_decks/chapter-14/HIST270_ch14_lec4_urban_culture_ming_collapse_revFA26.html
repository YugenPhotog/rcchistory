HIST 270 · History of China · Chapter 14 · Lecture 4

The Garden and the Flood

Late-Ming Cities Flourished While the Dynasty Ran Out of Money, Grain, and Silver

1550s–1644 · Study Buddy edition

Welcome to the last lecture on the Ming. This deck is your Study Buddy for the end of the dynasty. Work through it at your own pace. Lecture 1 built the throne, Lecture 2 followed the frontiers, and Lecture 3 went into the exam halls and the villages. Now we go to the cities, and then we watch the dynasty fall.

Here is the story in one breath. Late-Ming commercial wealth paid for one of the richest city cultures in Chinese history: gardens, printed books, novels and musical plays. But after 1600 the government was nearly bankrupt, officials fought one another, a colder climate shrank harvests, the supply of silver suddenly dropped, and peasant rebels rose. In 1644 rebels, not foreign invaders, took Beijing (Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 241–245).

Q10 story · The cities of Jiangnan · 1500s–1600s

Rich Cities Created a New Culture of Entertainment

Many literati with ample means chose to live in cities, where they could pursue an elegant life.
As cities grew and commerce thrived, people with money to spend paid for entertainment of many kinds.
This city culture belonged to merchants and townspeople, not only to scholars.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 227, 241–242. Scroll: Liaoning Provincial Museum via Wikimedia Commons.

Lecture 3 ended in the market towns. Follow the money into the cities.

Many literati, especially those with ample means, lived in cities, where they could pursue what the textbook calls the elegant life (Ebrey, p. 241). But literati were not the only cultural force in the cities. As cities grew and the commercial economy thrived, a distinctive urban culture emerged, a culture of those with money to spend, centered on entertainment of many sorts (p. 242).

The chapter's opening puts it in one phrase: the merchant-centered culture of the cities found expression in vernacular fiction and drama, published in increasing quantity and accessible even to people with only rudimentary educations (p. 227). That sentence is the map for the next twelve slides.

Q10 story · Suzhou · 1500s

Wealthy Jiangnan Families Built Gardens as Works of Art

Well-off families built gardens inside the walls that surrounded their homes.
Like landscape painters, garden designers used bamboo, twisted pines, and craggy rocks to capture nature.
About twenty Ming gardens still survive in Suzhou.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 241 (Material Culture: Gardens of Suzhou). Painting: Metropolitan Museum of Art; the painter and date are outside the textbook.

The textbook's Material Culture feature takes us into the gardens of Suzhou (Ebrey, p. 241).

Well-to-do families in the Jiangnan region often built gardens within the walls that enclosed their homes, and the gardens of Suzhou became famous for their sophisticated beauty. They were places to entertain friends and pass leisure hours, and they were treated as works of art in progress.

Like landscape painters, garden designers tried to capture the essential features of nature. They used objects heavy with symbolic meaning, such as bamboo, gnarled pines and craggy rocks. About twenty Ming gardens still survive in Suzhou.

The painting is real, and the link between garden and painting was literal. From outside the textbook: the Suzhou painter Wen Zhengming painted this album of views of the Humble Administrator's Garden in 1551. The textbook pictures the same garden on page 241.

Why this matters: gardens show where late-Ming wealth went: into comfort, beauty and entertaining.

Q10 story · City bookshops · 1500s–1600s

Publishers Printed Books for Readers with Only Basic Schooling

Publishers printed large numbers of books that the urban middle classes could read.
Reference books and popular religious tracts were common, including ledgers for tallying good and bad deeds.
Publishers hired artists to illustrate books so they would sell better.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 227, 242–243. Encyclopedia: details are outside the textbook.

Now walk into a bookshop. Books accessible to the urban middle classes were published in large numbers (Ebrey, p. 242).

They included reference books of all sorts and popular religious tracts. One kind was the morality ledger, a book for calculating the moral value of your good deeds and subtracting the demerits of your bad ones. Readers could keep score of their own goodness.

To make their books attractive in the marketplace, business-minded publishers hired artists to illustrate them (p. 243). The chapter's opening says these books reached even people with rudimentary educations (p. 227).

The page is real. From outside the textbook: it comes from the Sancai tuhui, an illustrated encyclopedia printed in 1609, one of the reference books of all sorts that the textbook mentions. Its world map shows how much a Ming reader could learn from a printed page.

Why this matters: cheap printed books are the first piece of evidence that taste in the cities was changing.

Q10 story · A tea stall · 1500s

Stories Appeared in the Language People Actually Spoke

Vernacular means the everyday spoken language, not the classical written language of officials.
By the mid-Ming, writers used the vernacular for short stories, novels, and plays.
Vernacular stories were full of shop clerks, merchants, monks, students, and matchmakers, people like their readers.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 243–244, 246.

By the sixteenth century more and more books were published in the vernacular, the language people actually spoke (Ebrey, p. 243). The summary contrasts it with the literary language used for government documents, poetry and essays (p. 246).

Writing in the vernacular was not brand new. It had begun on a small scale in Tang and Song times, when it was used to record the oral teachings of Buddhist and Confucian teachers. By the mid-Ming it was widely used for short stories, novels and plays.

Ming vernacular short stories showed a world much like that of their readers, full of shop clerks and merchants, monks and prostitutes, students and matchmakers (p. 244). Readers could see themselves on the page.

Q10 story · The novel appears · 1500s

The First Full-Length Novels Grew Out of Storytellers' Tales

The plots of the early novels came from story cycles that oral storytellers had developed over centuries.
Water Margin tells of a band of bandits at the end of the Northern Song.
Romance of the Three Kingdoms retells the struggles of generals and statesmen at the end of the Han.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244. Illustration: the edition and scene title are outside the textbook.

It was during the Ming that the full-length novel appeared in China (Ebrey, p. 244).

The plots of the early novels owed a great deal to story cycles that oral storytellers had developed over several centuries. The novels gathered tales that audiences already loved.

Water Margin is an episodic story about a band of bandits, set at the end of the Northern Song. The Romance of the Three Kingdoms is historical fiction based on the exploits of the generals and statesmen who fought for power at the end of the Han dynasty. You met both periods earlier in the course.

Q10 story · Rival editions · 1590s–1620s

Two More Novels Offered Fantasy and Daily Life

Journey to the West is a fantastic tale of the Tang monk Xuanzang's trip to India, with a magical monkey and a pig as companions.
Plum in the Golden Vase is a novel of manners about a lustful merchant, his wife, and five concubines.
Little is known about these authors, and rival publishers sold competing illustrated editions.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244. Illustration: the edition and scene are outside the textbook.

Two more novels round out the famous four (Ebrey, p. 244).

The Journey to the West is a fantastic account of the Tang monk Xuanzang's travels to India. In the novel he is accompanied by a monkey with supernatural powers and by a pig.

Plum in the Golden Vase is a novel of manners about a lustful merchant with a wife and five concubines. It is full of the details of daily life, and of the quarrels and scheming of the women in his household.

In none of these cases is much known about the author. Competing publishers brought out their own editions, sometimes adding new illustrations or commentaries. That is commerce shaping literature.

Q10 story · Banquets and troupes · 1583–1610

Troupes Performed Musical Plays at Banquets That Could Last Ten Hours

The Jesuit missionary Matteo Ricci, in China from 1583 to 1610, saw acting troupes in cities and on the road.
Troupe leaders bought young children and trained them to sing and perform.
Ricci wrote that guests enjoyed the plays so much that "the banquet at times may last for ten hours."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244, quoting Louis J. Gallagher, tr., China in the Sixteenth Century: The Journals of Matthew Ricci, 1583–1610 (1953), p. 23, quoted in Ebrey, p. 244. Portrait: Wikimedia Commons; the painter and date are outside the textbook.

Musical drama was also a major element of Ming urban culture, and our best witness is a foreigner (Ebrey, p. 244).

The Jesuit missionary Matteo Ricci, who lived in China from 1583 to 1610, described resident troupes in large cities and traveling troupes that, in his words, "journey everywhere throughout the length and breadth of the country" putting on operas. The leaders of the troupes bought young children and trained them to sing and perform.

Ricci thought too many people were addicted to these performances. He wrote that the actors were hired for all grand banquets; the host was handed a volume of plays and chose one or several; and the guests followed the plays between eating and drinking "with so much satisfaction that the banquet at times may last for ten hours." The quotation is the textbook's, from the Gallagher translation of Ricci's journals.

The portrait is real. From outside the textbook: it was painted by a Chinese member of the Jesuit order, You Wenhui, around the time of Ricci's death in 1610. Notice that Ricci wears the cap and robe of a Chinese scholar.

Q10 story · A garden pavilion · 1590s

People Read Play Scripts as Eagerly as They Watched Plays

Scripts were largely poetry, so people bought them to read as well as to perform.
Playwrights were less likely than novelists to hide their names.
The greatest Ming playwright, Tang Xianzu, was loved for his love stories and social satires.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244.

People did not only watch and listen to plays. They also read the scripts eagerly (Ebrey, p. 244).

Perhaps because so much of a dramatic script was made of poetry, playwrights were less likely than novelists to hide who they were. Poetry was respectable, so a playwright could sign his work.

The greatest of the Ming playwrights was Tang Xianzu, whose love stories and social satires were very popular. The next two slides take one of each.

Q10 story · The Peony Pavilion · 1598

Tang Xianzu's Peony Pavilion (1598) Told of a Love That Conquered Death

A young woman from an official family falls in love with a man she meets in a dream, then pines away and dies.
The young man finds the portrait she buried in the garden, and his love brings her back to life.
In one comic scene the old tutor insists that a woman "at every step … should be screened from view."

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 242–243 (Documents: Scene from The Peony Pavilion), quoting Tang Xianzu, The Peony Pavilion, tr. Cyril Birch (1980), Scene 9, quoted in Ebrey, p. 243. Edition: outside the textbook.

The Peony Pavilion, written in 1598, is probably Tang Xianzu's best-loved play, and the textbook prints a scene from it (Ebrey, pp. 242–243).

The main characters are a young woman from an official family, who falls in love with a young man she meets in a dream, and then pines away for him. Before she dies she buries a portrait of herself in the garden. Her family moves away; the young man moves into the garden, finds the portrait and falls in love with her picture. His love is so strong that it revives her.

The play has fifty-five scenes and was rarely performed in full, but people knew it and asked for particular scenes. In Scene 9, "Sweeping the Garden," the maid Fragrance plans a stroll in the garden behind the house for her young mistress, and the elderly Tutor Chen objects: when a woman walks abroad, "at every step she should be screened from view." The tutor adds that in sixty years he has never felt "spring-struck" nor strolled in any garden. Read the whole scene in the textbook.

The illustration is real. From outside the textbook: it comes from a printed edition of the Wanli era, the years when the play was new, and shows the dream in a cloud above the sleeping young woman.

Q10 story · A roadside inn · Tang Xianzu's satire

In Dream of Handan, a Whole Life Passed While Dinner Cooked

In another Tang Xianzu play, a young man falls asleep while his dinner cooks.
He dreams he comes first in the jinshi exams, rises high, is condemned to death, then is cleared and promoted.
He wakes to find dinner nearly ready and sees that life passes as quickly as a dream.

Tang Xianzu also wrote social satire. The textbook's example is Dream of Handan, which it spells "Han Tan" (Ebrey, p. 244).

A young man falls asleep while his meal is cooking. In his dream he sees his whole life. He comes in first in the jinshi examinations, rises to high office, is unfairly slandered and condemned to death, and is then cleared and promoted. At the point of death he wakes up and sees that his dinner is nearly done. He realizes that life passes as quickly as a dream.

Q10 story · A canal at night · 1600s

Fiction Changed How Educated People Judged Real Life

Readers began to judge themselves and others by the pure feelings of literary characters.
Intense, almost obsessive attachments came to be admired.
Courtesan culture flourished, and writers compared frustrated officials to talented women waiting to be appreciated.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244.

Fiction and plays were so eagerly consumed in Ming times that the values in them began to shape the culture of the literati themselves (Ebrey, p. 244).

Educated men and women often seem to have judged themselves and others by the standards of purity of feeling they had come to expect in literary characters. Headstrong attachments, close to obsessions, came to be admired.

Courtesan culture flourished in this setting. Writers told of romances between well-known writers and famous courtesans. Because they associated courtesans with high hopes and disappointment, writers saw a parallel between the frustrated official and the talented but powerless woman waiting for her lover to appreciate her full worth.

Why this matters: the influence ran both ways. City entertainment, made for the middle classes, reshaped the tastes of the elite.

Q11 story · The treasury · after 1600

After 1600 the Ming Government Was Nearly Bankrupt

Defending Korea against Japan had cost the government heavily.
The government had to support an imperial clan that kept growing.
Relief for natural disasters drained what money remained.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244; see also Chs. 13 and 15.

Now the second story: how the Ming fell. It begins with money (Ebrey, p. 244).

Even before 1600 there were signs of trouble. By that year the government was nearly bankrupt. The textbook gives three reasons.

One: the costly defense of Korea against Japan, which the textbook treats in Chapters 13 and 15. From outside the textbook: the Japanese invasions of Korea came in 1592 and 1597, and Ming armies fought there until 1598.

Two: the government had to support an ever-increasing imperial clan. Every generation of the emperor's relatives was larger than the last.

Three: the cost of relief for natural disasters.

Q11 story · The court · early 1600s

Officials Saw Every Problem in Moral Terms, and Factions Grew Fierce

Officials saw the dynasty's problems in moral terms, so the cure was to remove immoral men from power.
That view led to fierce factionalism, with accusations and counteraccusations.
Emperors grew weary of their officials and their infighting.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 244.

Money troubles were made worse by a court that could not work together (Ebrey, p. 244).

The bureaucracy did not pull together to meet these challenges. Officials diagnosed the dynasty's problems in moral terms and saw removing the immoral from power as the solution. That led to fierce factionalism. Think about why: you do not compromise with men you have called immoral.

Accusations and counteraccusations flew so often that emperors wearied of their officials and their infighting. Recall the Wanli emperor from Lecture 1, who stopped holding court. With no chancellor and an absent emperor, nothing could be settled.

Q11 story · Wuxi, Jiangsu · early 1600s

Q11 story · Nagasaki and Manila · 1639

In 1639 the Flow of Silver Suddenly Dropped

In 1639 Japan refused to let traders from Macao into Nagasaki. Lecture 2 · t ↩
Months later, trade with the Spanish in the Philippines stopped after a slaughter of Chinese residents there.
Less silver meant deflation, so real rents rose, and hoarding of silver and grain created shortages.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 234, 245. Ingot date and museum: outside the textbook.

Then the money supply failed (Ebrey, p. 245).

Remember from Lecture 2, slide t silver-route, that the Ming economy had come to depend on silver from Japan and from the Americas through Manila. In 1639 Japan refused to allow traders from Macao into Nagasaki, cutting off much of the Japanese silver. A few months later, trade with the Spanish in the Philippines came to a halt after a slaughter of Chinese residents there.

The sudden drop in the supply of silver led to deflation, and deflation meant that real rents rose: the same rent was now harder to pay. The textbook says this is part of why people rioted over rents. The drop in silver imports also led to hoarding of both silver and grain, creating artificial shortages.

Q11 story · Beijing · 1644

In 1644 Li Zicheng Took Beijing, and the Last Ming Emperor Took His Own Life

In 1644 Li Zicheng's rebels moved through Hebei and into Beijing.
The last Ming emperor, in despair, took his own life.
Rebels, not foreign invaders, brought down the Ming.

The end came quickly (Ebrey, p. 245).

In 1644 Li Zicheng's forces advanced through Hebei and into Beijing. In despair, the last Ming emperor took his own life. The dynasty that Taizu had founded in 1368 ended after 276 years.

Notice who did it. The Ming fell to its own rebels, men like a former shepherd and postal worker, not to a foreign army.

The photo is real. From outside the textbook: tradition holds that the last emperor, the Chongzhen emperor, died on Jingshan, the hill just behind the Forbidden City. The pavilion on top was built later, under the Qing, so it is a place of memory, not a building from 1644.

Fiscal and political. A nearly bankrupt state, bitter factions, and failed reforms. o ↩ p ↩ q ↩

Pause and put the collapse together. Each link goes back to its slide.

Fiscal and political: the treasury was nearly empty by 1600, factions fought in moral terms, and the Donglin reformers could not fix it. Slides o, p and q.

Environmental: the little ice age shrank harvests and turned hunger into rebellion in Shaanxi. Slides r and s.

Economic and disaster: the 1639 silver shock caused deflation, higher real rents and hoarding, and the 1642 flood brought smallpox. Slides u and w.

Military: deserters and laid-off soldiers became rebel armies, and Li Zicheng took Beijing. Slides t and x.

The key word for Review Question 11 is combination. Each crisis made the others worse: disaster relief drained an already empty treasury, hungry farmers joined the rebel gangs, and fighting the rebels meant heavier taxes on people who could not pay.

A side trip into garden design (Ebrey, p. 241).

The textbook explains that Suzhou garden designers arranged their bamboo, pines and rocks to be viewed in particular ways. Doorways and lattice windows offered intimate views, little scenes glimpsed as you walked.

A side trip to the west (Ebrey, p. 245).

While Li Zicheng was moving on Beijing, Zhang Xianzhong headed into Sichuan. His attacks on Chongqing and Chengdu led to widespread slaughter. Like Li, he announced a new dynasty, appointed officials and minted coins, but he could not pacify the region.

The coin is real. From outside the textbook: coin dealers and collectors attribute this coin, inscribed Da Shun tong bao, to Zhang's regime in Sichuan, dated 1644 to 1647. Minting coins was one of the first things any would-be dynasty did, to claim it was a government and not a gang.

Pierian Spring · The Ming world in 1600

PS: Between 1368 and 1600 China Changed in Many Ways

By 1600 the population had reached perhaps 175 million.
More books aimed to entertain, and Jiangnan stayed several steps ahead of other regions.
Thinkers criticized inherited ideas more sharply, and the Great Wall remained a trace of old fears of the Mongols.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 246 (Summary).

A last look back over the whole dynasty, from the textbook's summary (Ebrey, p. 246).

Between 1368 and 1600 China changed in many ways. The population grew to perhaps 175 million. Many more books were in circulation, and more of them were aimed at readers who wanted to be entertained rather than educated. Regional differences may have grown, as the Jiangnan region stayed several steps ahead of other regions.

Intellectually, China was much livelier in 1600, with writers and thinkers offering sustained critiques of inherited ideas. Think of Wang Yangming and his followers in Lecture 3. The fear of the Mongols had largely faded, but it left its trace in the Great Wall, the subject of Lecture 2.

Two Frontiers

The Ming Closed the Sea and the Steppe, and Silver Came In Anyway

1405–1600 · Study Buddy edition

Welcome back! This deck is your Study Buddy for the Ming dynasty's frontiers. Work through it at your own pace, as often as you like. Lecture 1 built the Ming throne; this lecture looks outward, to the sea and to the steppe.

Here is the story in one breath. The early Ming emperors expected the world to come to them, as envoys bearing gifts. Chengzu sent huge fleets under the eunuch Zheng He across the Indian Ocean, then the court stopped the voyages. On the northern steppe, the early emperors marched armies into Mongolia, then gave up and built walls. Along the coast, the court tried to keep trade inside its rules, but merchants, pirates and American silver forced their way in anyway (Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233–234).

Q4 story · The Ming court · 1400s

Ming Emperors Expected Foreign Rulers to Send Envoys and Gifts

Under the tribute system, foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts to honor the Chinese emperor.
The emperor answered with lavish gifts of his own, so tribute was both diplomacy and trade.
The Ming wanted all foreign trade to pass through this system of envoys.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233–234; Asia for Educators, "The Ming Voyages" (outside the textbook).

Start with how the Ming court imagined the world. In the tribute system, rulers of other countries sent envoys to the Chinese court bearing gifts, called tribute, to honor the emperor. The court received them with ceremony, and the emperor sent them home with generous gifts of his own (Ebrey, pp. 230, 233).

Chengzu had expanded and regularized this system (p. 230), and the court used it as a tool: it wanted the Mongols to trade only through the envoy system, and it wanted coastal trade "subordinated to diplomacy" (pp. 233–234).

Asia for Educators, a Columbia University teaching site and so a source from outside the textbook, explains the bargain: neighboring countries recognized the emperor as their superior, and in exchange received benefits such as trade. Both sides gained something, which is why tribute was diplomacy and trade at the same time.

Why this matters: the tribute system is the frame for this whole lecture. Each frontier story asks what happened when trade would not stay inside it.

Q4 story · Nanjing · 1405

Chengzu Chose a Muslim Eunuch, Zheng He, to Command a Great Fleet

Zheng He was a Muslim palace eunuch who had served Chengzu since his days as a prince.
Beginning in 1405, Chengzu sent him to sea with a huge fleet of very large ships and thousands of men.
The fleet's mission was to visit foreign rulers, announce China's peaceful intentions, and hand out lavish gifts.

Sources: Bullets 1–2: Britannica, "Zheng He"; Asia for Educators, "The Ming Voyages" (outside the textbook, because textbook pp. 231–232 are missing). Bullet 3: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The textbook's pages on Zheng He's early life (pp. 231–232) are missing from our copy, so the next two paragraphs come from outside the textbook: Britannica and Asia for Educators.

Zheng He was born around 1371 into a Hui, that is Chinese Muslim, family in Yunnan, under the name Ma He. His father had made the pilgrimage to Mecca. When Ming armies conquered Yunnan in 1381, the boy was captured, castrated and sent into the army as an orderly. By 1390 his unit served under the Prince of Yan, the future Chengzu. After Chengzu seized the throne, he gave his trusted eunuch the new surname Zheng (Britannica).

In 1405 Chengzu put Zheng He in command of the first great fleet. Britannica counts 62 large ships and about 27,800 men; Asia for Educators, counting support vessels too, gives 317 ships. Sources disagree, so treat any single figure with care.

Back to the textbook: the fleet's purpose was to visit foreign rulers, transmit messages of China's peaceful intentions, and bestow lavish gifts (p. 233). The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Q4 story · The Indian Ocean · 1405–1433

Seven Voyages Reached Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa

Between 1405 and 1433 the fleet made seven voyages.
Its ships stopped in Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf and on the coast of East Africa.
From the third voyage on, the fleet offered rulers or their envoys a ride to China.

Sources: Bullets 1–2: Britannica, "Zheng He"; Asia for Educators, "The Ming Voyages"; Mills, app. 2 (outside the textbook). Bullet 3: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Between 1405 and 1433 Zheng He's fleets made seven voyages. This and the next paragraph come from outside the textbook (Britannica; Asia for Educators). The early voyages went through Southeast Asia, by way of Champa, Java and Malacca, to Ceylon and to Calicut on India's southwest coast.

Later voyages went farther. Mills and Asia for Educators date the first visit to Hormuz on the Persian Gulf to the fourth voyage, which left in 1413; Britannica places it on the third. Detachments of later fleets visited Aden in Arabia and ports on the East African coast in what are now Somalia and Kenya. The map shows the seventh voyage.

One detail is in the textbook: beginning with the third voyage, rulers who were invited to come to China, or to send envoys, were offered passage on the fleet (p. 233).

The small chart on the right is the Mao Kun map, printed in the military encyclopedia Wubei zhi in 1621 and thought to be based on the fleet's own sailing directions (Mills, appendix 2). It shows coasts as a long strip, with sailing routes marked between them.

Q4 story · Nanjing and Beijing · 1414

Zheng He Brought Home Envoys, Giraffes and Spices to Delight the Emperor

The fleet returned with envoys from foreign courts.
It also brought giraffes and lions from Africa, fine cotton cloth from India, and gems and spices from Southeast Asia.
Such exotic gifts showed the court that distant lands honored the Ming emperor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233; giraffe dates: Mills, p. 155 n. 10 (outside the textbook). Painting: Philadelphia Museum of Art record via Wikimedia Commons.

The fleets came home full. Besides foreign envoys, Zheng He brought back exotic animals and goods likely to delight the emperor: giraffes and lions from Africa, fine cotton cloth from India, and gems and spices from Southeast Asia (Ebrey, p. 233).

The giraffe became the most famous of these gifts. From outside the textbook: Mills, the translator of Ma Huan, notes that the emperor received his first giraffe from Bengal in 1414, then others from Malindi in East Africa in 1415, from Aden in 1419, and from Mecca in 1433 (Mills, p. 155 n. 10). The one in this painting is the Bengal giraffe of 1414, according to the museum record.

Think about what the emperor gained. A giraffe was not much use, but it was a living sign that faraway lands honored the Ming. Gifts like these turned diplomacy into a show of prestige.

The painting is real, though the museum thinks this version is a later copy of Shen Du's original.

Why this matters: remember the giraffe when officials complain about the cost. Prestige was hard to measure in silver.

Q4 story · Calicut · 1413–1433

A Muslim Interpreter, Ma Huan, Wrote Down What the Fleet Saw

Ma Huan, another Muslim, sailed on three of the voyages.
His book described the places they visited: geography, politics, climate, economy, and local customs.
His account is one of the best eyewitness records of the voyages.
[This is] the great country of the Western Ocean.Ma Huan on Calicut, Yingya shenglan, tr. J. V. G. Mills (1970), p. 137 (outside the textbook)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233; Ma Huan, Ying-yai sheng-lan, tr. J. V. G. Mills (1970), pp. 34–35, 137–138 (outside the textbook).

Another Muslim who sailed with Zheng He, Ma Huan, went on three of the voyages and wrote a book-length account of the places they visited. It gave details about their geography, politics, climate, economy, and local customs (Ebrey, p. 233).

From outside the textbook: Ma Huan could read Arabic script and knew Arabic or Persian, so he joined the fleet as an official translator in 1413, on the first voyage to reach Hormuz (Mills, pp. 34–35). His book is called Yingya shenglan, "The Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores."

Here is one line from it, in the English translation by J. V. G. Mills. It is how Ma Huan opens his chapter on Calicut, the great port on India's southwest coast: "[This is] the great country of the Western Ocean" (Mills, p. 137). He goes on to record that the fleet set up a stone there with an inscription declaring that, though the two lands were more than a hundred thousand li apart, their people were "very similar, happy and prosperous" (p. 138).

Q4 story · Beijing · 1424

Officials Said the Voyages Cost Too Much and Brought Little Back

The voyages did not bring the court much of a material return.
Officials complained that the expeditions were wasteful.
After Chengzu died in 1424, the next emperors cared more about the rising Mongol threat.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The textbook asks the question directly: why were these voyages abandoned? It offers two answers (Ebrey, p. 233).

The first is money. The voyages did not bring much of a return. Officials complained about the cost of the expeditions, which they saw as wasteful. Building and crewing fleets of very large ships, and handing out lavish gifts at every port, was expensive, and giraffes did not pay for it.

The second is the emperor. The voyages may have appealed especially to the Yongle emperor, Chengzu, who died in 1424 after the sixth expedition. The next two emperors had other priorities, including dealing with the rising power of the Mongols, the subject of the second part of this lecture.

Notice that the textbook says "one likely reason" and "another possibility." Historians are not certain, and a good answer keeps that uncertainty. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Q4 story · Nanjing · 1433–1474

Zheng He Died on the Last Voyage, and the Great Ships Were Broken Up

Zheng He made one final voyage in 1430–1433 and died during it.
Afterward the ships sat in harbor.
In 1474 all the remaining ships with three or more masts were broken up and used for lumber.

Independent study: the Ming fleet and Europe's explorers (slide aa)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233. Place of death: Britannica, "Zheng He" (outside the textbook).

Zheng He did get one more voyage after his patron's death. He sailed on the seventh and final expedition in 1430–1433, and he died during it (Ebrey, p. 233). Britannica, from outside the textbook, places his death at Calicut in 1433.

Then the fleet simply stopped. The boats sat in harbor. Four decades later, in 1474, all the remaining ships with three or more masts were broken up and used for lumber.

Pause on that image. The largest fleets of their age were not lost in battle or storm; they were taken apart for wood. The textbook adds that, not long after, the much more modest expeditions of Vasco da Gama and Christopher Columbus changed the course of world history (p. 233). The Pierian Spring returns to that comparison on slide aa, ps-europe.

Why this matters: ending the voyages was a choice. The court decided that the sea mattered less than the steppe.

Q5 story · Mongolia · 1370s–1424

Taizu and Chengzu Marched Huge Armies into Mongolia

Both emperors feared the Mongols could build another war machine like Chinggis Khan's.
They were determined not to pay off their northern neighbors, as the Song had done.
They sent armies of up to 250,000 men into Mongolia, and Chengzu died on his fifth campaign in 1424.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Now turn north. The early Ming emperors held Mongol fighting men in awe. They had seen what the Mongols had done under Chinggis Khan, and they feared someone might put together another great military machine of the same kind (Ebrey, p. 233).

They also remembered the Song dynasty, which had paid its powerful northern neighbors to keep the peace. Both Taizu and Chengzu were determined not to suffer that fate. Their answer was to attack first.

Under these two emperors the Ming sent large, well-provisioned armies into Mongol territory, with as many as 250,000 troops. Both emperors led armies into Mongolia in person. Chengzu, in fact, died on his fifth campaign, in 1424, at the age of sixty-four. You met him in the saddle on slide u of Lecture 1, yongle-wars.

Q5 story · The steppe · 1400s

The Campaigns Cost Enormous Sums and Won Little

Mongol horsemen could simply ride away from slow Ming armies.
After losing China, the Mongols split into rival groups and never formed a united federation.
Later Ming rulers stopped invading Mongolia and concentrated on defending the border.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The great campaigns were extremely expensive, and they did not accomplish much, given the Mongols' mobility (Ebrey, p. 233). An army of foot soldiers and supply carts could march for weeks into the grasslands, and the Mongols could simply move their herds and tents out of the way.

The danger the emperors feared also turned out to be smaller than they thought. After the last Yuan emperor retreated to Mongolia, his loss of China discredited him, and he could not keep the Mongols united. Civil wars followed. Through much of the Ming, the three million or so Mongols were loosely divided into six groups and never formed the kind of federation that could seriously threaten China.

So the policy changed. Later in the dynasty the Ming was less inclined to send armies into Mongolia and concentrated on defending its borders against attack. That shift, from offense to defense, is the core of Review Question 5.

Q5 story · Northwest of Beijing · 1449

In 1449 a Mongol Khan Captured the Ming Emperor in the Field

Esen, khan of the Western Mongols, led a raid that the young emperor Yingzong marched out to meet.
The Ming army was destroyed and the emperor was taken prisoner.
It was one of two times in the Ming that Mongols seriously threatened the dynasty (recap: Lecture 1, slide cc, yingzong-1449 ↩).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233. Battle name and place: Elverskog, The Silk Road 15 (2017) (outside the textbook).

Defense did not mean safety. Groups of Mongols could and did raid, and twice in the Ming they seriously threatened the dynasty. The first time was in 1449, when Esen, khan of the Western Mongols, captured the emperor (Ebrey, p. 233).

You met that emperor in Lecture 1, slide cc, yingzong-1449; the link on the slide takes you back there. At twenty-one, encouraged by his favorite eunuch, Yingzong led an army against the Mongols himself. The army was destroyed and he was captured. The Mongols returned him the next year (p. 230).

From outside the textbook: historians call this the Tumu Incident, after Tumu, the place about fifty miles northwest of Beijing where the Ming army was caught (Elverskog, 2017). The textbook gives the army as fifty thousand men; other accounts give much larger figures, so the true size is uncertain.

Q5 story · The northern border · 1400s–1570

The Mongols Asked for Trade, and the Court Kept Saying No

Mongol envoys said raids would ease if they could trade regularly at border markets.
The court insisted that Mongol groups trade only through tribute envoys.
In 1550 Altan Khan's forces surrounded Beijing, and the court finally agreed to trade with him in 1570.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Here the steppe story meets the tribute system from slide b, tribute-order. The Ming was very reluctant to grant Mongol leaders any privileges, such as trading posts along the border. It wanted the different groups of Mongols to trade only through the envoy system (Ebrey, p. 233).

Mongol envoys repeatedly made a simple argument: friction could be reduced if regular trade were introduced. The logic is easy to follow, though the textbook does not spell it out: people who could not trade for the goods they wanted might take them by raiding. The court refused, again and again.

The second great threat came in 1550, when the forces of Altan Khan, khan of the Mongols in Inner Mongolia, surrounded Beijing itself. Only in 1570 did the court reach an agreement with Altan Khan.

Q5 story · The western border · mid-1400s

Building Walls Settled the Court's Argument over the Mongols

Some officials wanted to allow more trade with the Mongols, while others refused any concession.
By the mid-1400s, border commanders began linking forts with walls and trapped some Mongol raiders.
Extending the wall became the answer that ended the deadlock.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

At court, officials split into two camps. One side argued that the Mongols could be managed by allowing more trade. The other insisted that no concessions be made to them at all (Ebrey, p. 233).

Meanwhile, out on the frontier, something practical was happening. By the mid-fifteenth century, administrators of the western sections of the border began connecting their main garrison points with walls. They had some success in trapping groups of Mongol cavalry between them.

Walls offered a way out of the argument. Extending the wall was later seen as a solution to the deadlock: the court did not have to choose between trading with the Mongols and fighting them in the open. It could build.

Why this matters: the Great Wall you know was not one ancient project. It grew out of this Ming policy debate and a shift from offense to defense.

Q5 story · North of Beijing · 1500s

The Ming Wall Is the Great Wall Visitors See Today

The Ming wall runs about 1,500 miles, from northeast of Beijing into Gansu province.
Along its eastern 500 miles, it averages about 35 feet high, with lookout towers every half mile.
Its brick facing greatly impressed the first Westerners who saw it.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The Great Wall at Mutianyu, north of Beijing. Photo: Lloyd Tudor. CC BY-SA 4.0, Wikimedia Commons.

Much of the Ming Great Wall survives today, and it is the wall tourists walk on. It runs about 1,500 miles, from northeast of Beijing into Gansu province in the west (Ebrey, p. 233).

In the eastern 500 miles, closest to the capital, the walls average about 35 feet high and 20 feet across, with towers every half mile for lookouts. For much of its length the wall is faced with brick. That brick facing gave it an imposing appearance that greatly impressed the first Westerners who saw it.

The photo is real. It shows the section at Mutianyu, north of Beijing, climbing steep ridges with its watchtowers. Ask yourself how many workers, bricks and years a single mile of this took.

Earlier dynasties had built walls too, as you saw in earlier chapters, but the textbook ties this wall to the Ming policy you have just followed: the campaigns failed, the Mongols kept raiding, the court refused to trade, and building became the answer.

Why this matters: the Great Wall is the physical result of a policy change from offense to defense.

Q6 story · Ningbo and Guangzhou · 1400s–1500s

The Court Allowed Foreign Trade Only Through Assigned Ports and Missions

The court wanted trade to stay under the control of diplomacy.
Envoys from each country had to use one assigned port, such as Ningbo for Japan and Guangzhou for Indonesia.
Japan could send an embassy only once every ten years, with no more than two ships and 300 men.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234.

Now the third frontier: the coast. The textbook makes a point worth noticing. The court's obsession with defending against the Mongols did not mean its other borders posed no problems (Ebrey, p. 234).

On the coast, as on the steppe, the court wanted trade subordinated to diplomacy, that is, kept inside the tribute system you saw on slide b, tribute-order. So it assigned ports. Envoys from the Philippines were supposed to enter only through Fuzhou, those from Japan only through Ningbo, those from Indonesia only through Guangzhou, and so on.

It also limited how big and how frequent the missions could be. Japanese embassies, for instance, were not to come more than once in ten years, or to bring more than two ships with three hundred men.

Picture what that meant for merchants on both sides of the sea who wanted to trade every season, not once a decade. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: these rules set up the clash in the next two slides between official policy and what traders actually did.

Q6 story · The China seas · 1500s

Merchants from Many Lands Traded Anyway, Openly and in Secret

In the 1500s Japanese, Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch and Chinese traders formed an East Asian sea-trading community.
Profits from sea trade were so high that legal and illegal trade went on all along the coast.
The formal tribute rules could not contain this trade.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234. Screen details: Wikimedia Commons record.

In the sixteenth century, the court's formal system ran into something it could not control. An international East Asian maritime trading community had formed, made up of Japanese, Portuguese, Spanish, Dutch and Chinese merchants and adventurers (Ebrey, p. 234).

Because the profits to be had from maritime trade were high, both open and clandestine, that is secret and illegal, trade took place all along the coast. The textbook's verdict is careful: the formal system "proved unable to contain" this community. It did not stop trade; it only pushed much of it outside the law.

The screen is real, though it comes from Japan rather than China. Japanese painters called the Portuguese nanban, "southern barbarians," because their ships arrived from the south, and they painted their huge ships and crowds of traders on gold folding screens. That background is from outside the textbook.

Why this matters: restrictive rules did not create this trading world, but they could not hold it back. Be precise about that when you answer Review Question 6.

Q6 story · From Shandong to Guangzhou · 1500s

Pirates Raided the Coast, and the Ming Moved People Inland

Ships loaded with valuable goods attracted pirates.
Pirates grew strong enough to raid the coast from Shandong to Guangzhou.
Instead of building a navy, the government forced coastal people to move inland, hoping to starve the pirates out.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234; on wokou: Britannica, "Wakō" (outside the textbook).

Wherever there is valuable cargo, there are people who want to steal it. Boats laden with goods attracted pirates, and the pirates grew so strong that they raided the coast from Shandong in the north to Guangzhou in the south (Ebrey, p. 234).

From outside the textbook: Chinese sources called these raiders wokou, "Japanese pirates." Britannica notes that they were mainly Japanese at first but of mixed origin later, and that by the early 1500s most of them were probably Chinese.

The Ming response is the surprising part. Instead of trying to suppress the pirates by expanding its navy, the government forced people to move away from the coast, hoping to starve the pirates out. Remember that the court had broken up its great ships in 1474, on slide h, fleet-ends.

The scroll is real. It is a Ming painting of soldiers fighting the raiders, now in the National Museum of China.

Why this matters: the textbook does not say the trade rules caused the piracy. It says trade drew pirates, and the court chose evacuation over a navy.

Q6 story · Manila and the Pacific · late 1500s

Silver from American Mines Crossed the Pacific to Pay for Chinese Silk and Porcelain

Ships leaving China carried silk and porcelain.
Spanish ships brought silver mined in Peru and Mexico across the Pacific to Manila in the Philippines.
Chinese merchants exchanged their goods for that silver and carried it home.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234. Coin: Portable Antiquities Scheme record via Wikimedia Commons.

Follow the cargo. Boats leaving China carried silk and porcelain. Boats entering China brought silver from mines in Peru and Mexico, transported by way of Manila, to pay for the Chinese goods (Ebrey, p. 234).

From outside the textbook: Spain ruled the Philippines from Manila, and its ships crossed the Pacific between Manila and Acapulco on the west coast of Mexico. Chinese merchants sailed the short distance from the China coast to Manila, sold their silk and porcelain, and took the silver home. Q6 story · Macao · 1557

The Court Eased Its Restrictions, and Portugal Settled Macao in 1557

Efforts against the pirates had little success until the court eased its trade restrictions in the late 1500s.
Under the new policies, Portugal was allowed to set up a trading base at Macao in 1557.
Portugal held Macao until 1999.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234. Engraving: Wikimedia Commons record.

Moving people inland did not defeat the pirates. Anti-pirate efforts did not have much success until maritime trade restrictions were eased in the late sixteenth century (Ebrey, p. 234).

Think about why that might work. Once more trade was legal, merchants had less reason to deal with smugglers and pirates, and coastal people could make a living from the sea openly. The textbook gives the sequence, not that explanation, so treat it as a reasonable inference.

Under the new policies, Portugal was permitted to set up a trading base at Macao, on the coast near Guangzhou, in 1557. Portugal held it until 1999, so the arrangement lasted more than four centuries.

The engraving is real, made in the workshop of the engraver Theodor de Bry in Frankfurt in the late 1500s. Like many European images of Asia at the time, it was based on travelers' reports rather than the artist's own visit, so read the details with care.

Why this matters: this is how China joined the new global trading network: not by a grand decision, but by easing rules that were not working.

Ships Also Brought American Crops That Fed a Growing Population

Sweet potatoes, maize, peanuts, tomatoes, chili peppers and tobacco quickly spread in China.
Sweet potatoes and maize could grow on sandy or steep land that had never been farmed.
Spanish and Portuguese ships also brought Christian missionaries with very different ideas about the world.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234.

Silver was not the only cargo. Besides stimulating the Ming economy, the expansion of maritime trade brought crops from the Americas, the "New World," to China. Sweet potatoes, maize, peanuts, tomatoes, chili peppers, tobacco and other crops were quickly introduced (Ebrey, p. 234).

Two of them mattered most. Sweet potatoes and maize could be grown on land that had never been cultivated because it was too sandy or too steep. That made it possible to farm hillsides and poor soils, and so to feed more people. The textbook says these crops "facilitated population growth."

The same Spanish and Portuguese ships also began to bring Christian missionaries, with radically different ideas about the nature of the world. You will meet one of them, Matteo Ricci, later in the chapter.

The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: global trade changed what Chinese farmers grew and how many people the land could feed.

Pierian Spring · Tibet and Mongolia · 1577

PS: In 1577 Altan Khan Made Tibetan Buddhism the Mongols' Religion

Tsong-kha-pa founded the Gelug-pa (Yellow Hat) order, whose heads became the Dalai Lamas.
In 1577 the third Dalai Lama visited Altan Khan, who declared Tibetan Buddhism the religion of all Mongols.
The Dalai Lama called the khan "King of Religion," and the next Dalai Lama was found to be Altan's great-grandson.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 233–234.

The textbook names two developments that shaped later Ming–Mongol relations: the Great Wall, and the Mongols' close ties with Tibetan Buddhism (Ebrey, p. 233). This slide is the second.

Ming China had considerable trade with Tibet through Sichuan and Yunnan but few close diplomatic ties. Tibet was then largely ruled by its major monasteries, and when they needed military help they called on rival Mongol leaders. Tsong-kha-pa (1357–1419) founded the Yellow Hat, or Gelug-pa, order, whose heads later became known as the Dalai Lamas (p. 234).

In 1577 the third Dalai Lama accepted Altan Khan's invitation to visit Mongolia. The khan declared Tibetan Buddhism the official religion of all the Mongols, and swore that they would give up blood sacrifice. The Dalai Lama gave him the title "King of Religion." When the third Dalai Lama's reincarnation was found to be Altan Khan's great-grandson, the ties grew even stronger.

A closer look at the wall from slide o, great-wall. Much of the Ming Great Wall survives today (Ebrey, p. 233).

Tribute system

In the tribute system, foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts ("tribute") to the Chinese emperor and acknowledged his superior status. The emperor received them with ceremony and sent them home with gifts of his own.

Chengzu expanded and regularized the court's system for receiving envoys (Ebrey, p. 230).

×

Eunuch

A eunuch was a castrated man who served in the imperial palace. Because eunuchs could have no sons of their own, emperors often trusted them as personal servants and agents (see Lecture 1, slide aa, eunuchs).

×

Federation

A federation here means many separate Mongol groups united under one leader, as they had been under Chinggis Khan. In Ming times the Mongols were loosely divided into about six groups (Ebrey, p. 233).

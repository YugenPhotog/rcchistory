Q4 story · The Ming court · 1400s

Ming Emperors Expected Foreign Rulers to Send Envoys and Gifts

Concept

Tribute system

A ranked order of diplomacy. Foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts to honor the Chinese emperor, and the emperor answered with gifts of his own and the right to trade.

Under the tribute system, foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts to honor the Chinese emperor.
The emperor answered with lavish gifts of his own, so tribute was both diplomacy and trade.
The Ming wanted all foreign trade to pass through this system of envoys.
Envoys performed the ketou (kowtow), kneeling and touching their heads to the floor to show their ruler's lower rank.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233–234.

Start with how the Ming court imagined the world. The gold card names the system. In the tribute system, rulers of other countries sent envoys to the Chinese court bearing gifts, called tribute, to honor the emperor. The court received them with ceremony, and the emperor sent them home with generous gifts of his own (Ebrey, pp. 230, 233).

The Yongle emperor had expanded and regularized this system (p. 230), and the court used it as a tool: it wanted the Mongols to trade only through the envoy system, and it wanted coastal trade "subordinated to diplomacy" (pp. 233–234).

The bargain was simple. Neighboring countries recognized the emperor as their superior, and in exchange they received benefits, above all the chance to trade.

The ceremony mattered as much as the gifts. Envoys performed the ketou, "knocking the head," spelled kowtow in English: they knelt and touched their foreheads to the floor. The court spoke of the emperor as a parent and of foreign rulers as junior members of the family, a ranking historians call suzerain and vassal. The system asked for recognition, not land: it was defensive, not a plan of conquest.

Why this matters: the tribute system is the frame for this whole lecture. Each frontier story asks what happened when trade would not stay inside it.

Q4 concept · The Ming view of the world

Concept: Ming Elites Saw China as Civilization Itself, Not One Nation Among Many

Concept

Culturalism

Historians' name for loyalty to a shared civilization, its classics, rituals and writing, rather than to a nation-state. Empire and culture seemed to be one thing, and outsiders were judged by how far they had taken up Chinese ways.

The emperor claimed to rule "all under Heaven" (tianxia), with China as the source of civilization.
Good change meant returning to the sages' ways, not progress toward something new.
Foreign lands could send honored guests and gifts, but they seemed to have little to teach.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233, for the tribute order; the concept follows Levenson and Fairbank.

Look at the map first. Ming court mapmakers painted the Great Ming across the middle of the silk and squeezed foreign lands toward the edges. That picture of the world is the idea behind the tribute system.

Historians have a name for this outlook: culturalism. It is a modern historians' word, not one the Ming used. Culturalism resembles nationalism, because it is a strong sense of belonging. But the loyalty was to a civilization, its classics, rituals and writing, not to a nation-state with fixed borders. The emperor ruled tianxia, "all under Heaven," and the tribute order put that claim into ceremony (Ebrey, pp. 230, 233).

Two habits followed. Change was supposed to happen within tradition, by restoring the sages' models, so there was no ideology of progress. And foreign goods could be admired as tribute without seeming worth copying.

Keep one more point in mind. Even inside this outlook the Yongle court was curious about the wider world, as the giraffe later in this segment will show. A belief that China was the center did not stop people from wanting to see what lay beyond it.

The map is a real Ming map of the late 1300s.

Why this matters: culturalism is one of the deeper reasons the court later gave up the sea.

Q4 story · Nanjing · 1405

The Yongle Emperor Chose a Muslim Eunuch, Zheng He, to Command a Great Fleet

Zheng He was a Muslim palace eunuch who had served the Yongle emperor since his days as a prince.
In 1405 the Yongle emperor sent him to sea with about 27,800 men and more than 250 ships, some 62 of them giant "treasure ships."
The fleet's mission was to visit foreign rulers, announce China's peaceful intentions, and hand out lavish gifts.

Independent study: how big were the treasure ships? (slide gg)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233; Britannica, "Zheng He"; Edward L. Dreyer, Zheng He (2007).

Zheng He was born around 1371 into a Hui, that is Chinese Muslim, family in Yunnan, under the name Ma He. His father had made the pilgrimage to Mecca. When Ming armies conquered Yunnan in 1381, the boy was captured, castrated and sent into the army as an orderly. By 1390 his unit served under the Prince of Yan, the future Yongle emperor. After the prince seized the throne, he gave his trusted eunuch the new surname Zheng.

In 1405 the Yongle emperor put Zheng He in command of the first great fleet. By the official count it had 62 great "treasure ships" and 27,800 men, along with many smaller vessels, perhaps about 250 ships in all. It was one of the largest fleets of its age, and numbers for it vary from one record to another.

Back to the textbook: the fleet's purpose was to visit foreign rulers, transmit messages of China's peaceful intentions, and bestow lavish gifts (p. 233). The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: the Yongle emperor trusted a eunuch from his own household, not a scholar-official, with the grandest venture of his reign.

Q4 story · The Indian Ocean · 1405–1433

Seven Voyages Reached Southeast Asia, India, Arabia and East Africa

Between 1405 and 1433 the fleet made seven voyages.
Its ships stopped in Southeast Asia, India, the Persian Gulf and on the coast of East Africa.
From the third voyage on, the fleet offered rulers or their envoys a ride to China.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233; Britannica, "Zheng He"; Asia for Educators, "The Ming Voyages."

Between 1405 and 1433 Zheng He's fleets made seven voyages. The early voyages went through Southeast Asia, by way of Champa, Java and Malacca, to Ceylon and to Calicut on India's southwest coast.

Later voyages went farther. The fourth voyage, which left in 1413, reached Hormuz on the Persian Gulf. Detachments of later fleets visited Aden in Arabia and ports on the East African coast in what are now Somalia and Kenya. The map shows the seventh voyage.

One detail is in the textbook: beginning with the third voyage, rulers who were invited to come to China, or to send envoys, were offered passage on the fleet (p. 233).

The small chart on the right is the Mao Kun map, printed in the military encyclopedia Wubei zhi in 1621 and thought to be based on the fleet's own sailing directions. It shows coasts as a long strip, with sailing routes marked between them.

Why this matters: the map turns "seven voyages" into distances you can see. Ming ships reached East Africa decades before Portuguese ships reached India.

Q4 story · Nanjing and Beijing · 1414

Zheng He Brought Home Envoys, Giraffes and Spices to Delight the Emperor

The fleet returned with envoys from foreign courts.
It also brought giraffes and lions from Africa, fine cotton cloth from India, and gems and spices from Southeast Asia.
Such exotic gifts showed the court that distant lands honored the Ming emperor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233. Painting: Philadelphia Museum of Art record via Wikimedia Commons.

The fleets came home full. Besides foreign envoys, Zheng He brought back exotic animals and goods likely to delight the emperor: giraffes and lions from Africa, fine cotton cloth from India, and gems and spices from Southeast Asia (Ebrey, p. 233).

The giraffe became the most famous of these gifts. The emperor received his first giraffe from Bengal in 1414, then others from Malindi in East Africa in 1415, from Aden in 1419 and from Mecca in 1433. The one in this painting is the Bengal giraffe of 1414, according to the museum record.

Think about what the emperor gained. A giraffe was not much use, but it was a living sign that faraway lands honored the Ming. Gifts like these turned diplomacy into a show of prestige.

The painting is real, though the museum thinks this version is a later copy of Shen Du's original.

Why this matters: remember the giraffe when officials complain about the cost. Prestige was hard to measure in silver.

Q4 story · Calicut · 1413–1433

A Muslim Interpreter, Ma Huan, Wrote Down What the Fleet Saw

Ma Huan, another Muslim, sailed on three of the voyages.
His book described the places they visited: geography, politics, climate, economy, and local customs.
His account is one of the best eyewitness records of the voyages.
[This is] the great country of the Western Ocean.Ma Huan on Calicut, Yingya shenglan, tr. J. V. G. Mills (1970), p. 137

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Another Muslim who sailed with Zheng He, Ma Huan, went on three of the voyages and wrote a book-length account of the places they visited. It gave details about their geography, politics, climate, economy, and local customs (Ebrey, p. 233).

Ma Huan could read Arabic script and knew Arabic or Persian, so he joined the fleet as an official translator in 1413, on the first voyage to reach Hormuz. His book is called Yingya shenglan, "The Overall Survey of the Ocean's Shores."

Here is one line from it, in J. V. G. Mills's English translation. It is how Ma Huan opens his chapter on Calicut, the great port on India's southwest coast: "[This is] the great country of the Western Ocean." He goes on to record that the fleet set up a stone there with an inscription declaring that, though the two lands were more than a hundred thousand li apart, their people were "very similar, happy and prosperous."

Q4 story · Beijing · 1424

Officials Said the Voyages Cost Too Much and Brought Little Back

The voyages did not bring the court much of a material return.
Officials complained that the expeditions were wasteful.
After the Yongle emperor died in 1424, the next emperors cared more about the rising Mongol threat.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The textbook asks the question directly: why were these voyages abandoned? It offers two answers (Ebrey, p. 233).

The first is money. The voyages did not bring much of a return. Officials complained about the cost of the expeditions, which they saw as wasteful. Building and crewing fleets of very large ships, and handing out lavish gifts at every port, was expensive, and giraffes did not pay for it.

The second is the emperor. The voyages may have appealed especially to the Yongle emperor, who died in 1424 after the sixth expedition. The next two emperors had other priorities, including dealing with the rising power of the Mongols, the subject of the second part of this lecture.

Notice that the textbook says "one likely reason" and "another possibility." Historians are not certain, and a good answer keeps that uncertainty. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Q4 analysis · Beijing · 1424–1474

Behind the Complaints Lay Four Deeper Reasons the Court Gave Up the Sea

1 · Economic

The Ming state lived on land taxes, not trade taxes, so the fleets cost money and earned the treasury little.

2 · Institutional

Officials came from landowning families and rose through the exams; the state kept merchants in a lower place.

3 · Ideological

Culturalism and Confucian distaste for profit made the voyages look needless, and eunuchs, whom officials distrusted, ran them.

4 · Strategic

The Mongol frontier and the new capital at Beijing pulled money and attention north.

Recap: culturalism (slide dd) · Independent study: did success hold China's economy back? (slide hh)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233; Dreyer, Zheng He (2007), ch. 8.

The officials' complaints about cost, and the change of emperors in 1424, tell you what happened (Ebrey, p. 233). Historians also ask a deeper question: why did no one in the government fight to keep the fleets? Four kinds of reasons answer it.

Economic. The Ming state lived mainly on the land tax paid by farmers. Taxes on trade brought in little, so the voyages looked like pure expense. The new Hongxi emperor halted them in 1424, on the advice of officials worried about spending.

Institutional. Scholar-officials came from landowning families and rose through the examinations. In the official ranking of occupations, merchants came below farmers, and the state kept them subordinate.

Ideological. Culturalism said China needed nothing from abroad, and Confucian teaching looked down on chasing profit. The fleets were also commanded by eunuchs, whom officials resented.

Strategic. The Mongols were the real danger, and Beijing sat close to the steppe, as the second part of this lecture shows.

Ming society was not anti-trade: private merchants kept sailing all through the dynasty. The distaste belonged to the state.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q4 (p. 233) asks for economic and political factors, and these four sort into those groups.

Check yourself: Which reason is economic, and why might the other three count as political?

Q4 story · Nanjing · 1433–1474

Zheng He Died on the Last Voyage, and the Great Ships Were Broken Up

Zheng He made one final voyage in 1430–1433 and died during it.
Afterward the ships sat in harbor.
In 1474 all the remaining ships with three or more masts were broken up and used for lumber.

Independent study: the Ming fleet and Europe's explorers (slide aa)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Zheng He did get one more voyage after his patron's death. He sailed on the seventh and final expedition in 1430–1433, and he died during it (Ebrey, p. 233), probably at Calicut in 1433.

Then the fleet simply stopped. The boats sat in harbor. Four decades later, in 1474, all the remaining ships with three or more masts were broken up and used for lumber.

Pause on that image. The largest fleets of their age were not lost in battle or storm; they were taken apart for wood. The textbook adds that, not long after, the much more modest expeditions of Vasco da Gama and Christopher Columbus changed the course of world history (p. 233). Q4 story · Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean · 1405–1433

The Fleets Showed Ming Power Across Asia but Founded No Overseas Empire

The fleets carried soldiers and used them: they crushed a pirate chief at Palembang in 1407 and captured a king of Ceylon in 1411.
Zheng He's own inscription of 1431 counts more than thirty countries reached.
The Ming took no colonies overseas; it wanted rulers to honor the emperor and send tribute · on land it held Vietnam: L1 · yongle-wars ↗
Chinese communities in Southeast Asia still honor Zheng He in temples.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 233, 244; Zheng He's Liujiagang inscription of 1431, tr. Dreyer; Ma Huan.

So what did the voyages achieve? Begin with what the textbook's chapter summary says: Zheng He sailed to enroll more countries in the tribute system (Ebrey, p. 244). In 1431 Zheng He had his own record carved on a stone at Liujiagang, the fleet's home port, and he counted more than thirty countries reached.

The fleets were diplomatic, but they were armed. In 1407 they captured the pirate chief Chen Zuyi at Palembang on Sumatra, and Ma Huan reports that he was executed at court. In 1411 they seized a king of Ceylon who had attacked them and brought him to Nanjing; the emperor later sent him home. The fleets also backed the new port-state of Malacca.

Was this imperialism? At sea the Ming founded no colonies, unlike the Portuguese after 1500; the fleets wanted rulers to honor the emperor and send tribute. But the fleets were armed and used force when they chose to. And on land, remember, the Yongle emperor occupied Vietnam for twenty years.

The photograph is real: a temple in Java where Zheng He is honored today.

Why this matters: Reading Guide Q4 asks about goals; display, tribute and armed diplomacy are all part of a full answer.

Q5 story · Mongolia · 1370s–1424

The Hongwu and Yongle Emperors Sent Huge Armies into Mongolia

Both emperors feared the Mongols could build another war machine like Chinggis Khan's.
They were determined not to pay off their northern neighbors, as the Song had done.
They sent armies of up to 250,000 men into Mongolia, and the Yongle emperor died on his fifth campaign in 1424.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Now turn north. The early Ming emperors held Mongol fighting men in awe. They had seen what the Mongols had done under Chinggis Khan, and they feared someone might put together another great military machine of the same kind (Ebrey, p. 233).

They also remembered the Song dynasty, which had paid its powerful northern neighbors to keep the peace. Both emperors were determined not to suffer that fate. Their answer was to attack first.

Under these two emperors the Ming sent large, well-provisioned armies into Mongol territory, with as many as 250,000 troops. The Hongwu emperor sent his generals: Xu Da led the great drive of 1372, and Lan Yu routed the Mongols at Lake Buyur in 1388. The Yongle emperor led his five campaigns himself, and he died on the fifth, in 1424, at the age of sixty-four. You met him in the saddle in Lecture 1, on the yongle-wars slide.

Q5 story · The steppe · 1400s

The Campaigns Cost Enormous Sums and Won Little

Mongol horsemen could simply ride away from slow Ming armies.
After losing China, the Mongols split into rival groups and rarely stayed united in a federation for long.
Later Ming rulers stopped invading Mongolia and concentrated on defending the border.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

The great campaigns were extremely expensive, and they did not accomplish much, given the Mongols' mobility (Ebrey, p. 233). An army of foot soldiers and supply carts could march for weeks into the grasslands, and the Mongols could simply move their herds and tents out of the way.

The danger the emperors feared also turned out to be smaller than they thought. After the last Yuan emperor retreated to Mongolia, his loss of China discredited him, and he could not keep the Mongols united. Civil wars followed. Through much of the Ming, the three million or so Mongols were loosely divided into six groups and rarely formed the kind of federation that could seriously threaten China. When one leader did reunite them, Esen in the 1440s, the result was the disaster of 1449 on the next slide.

So the policy changed. Later in the dynasty the Ming was less inclined to send armies into Mongolia and concentrated on defending its borders against attack. That shift, from offense to defense, is the core of Review Question 5.

Q5 story · Northwest of Beijing · 1449

In 1449 a Mongol Leader Captured the Ming Emperor in the Field

Esen, leader of the Western Mongols (Oirats), led a raid that the young emperor Yingzong marched out to meet.
The Ming army was destroyed and the emperor was taken prisoner.
It was one of two times in the Ming that Mongols seriously threatened the dynasty (recap: Lecture 1, slide cc, yingzong-1449 ↩).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233.

Defense did not mean safety. Groups of Mongols could and did raid, and twice in the Ming they seriously threatened the dynasty. The first time was in 1449, when Esen, leader of the Western Mongols, the Oirats, captured the emperor (Ebrey, p. 233).

You met that emperor in Lecture 1, on the yingzong-1449 slide; the link on this slide takes you back there. At twenty-one, encouraged by his favorite eunuch, Yingzong led an army against the Mongols himself. The army was destroyed, he was captured, and the Mongols returned him the next year (p. 230).

Historians call this the Tumu Incident, after Tumu, the place about fifty miles northwest of Beijing where the Ming army was caught. Accounts of the army's size vary widely, from fifty thousand men to far more.

Q5 story · The northern border · 1400s–1571

The Mongols Asked for Trade, and the Court Kept Saying No

Mongol envoys said raids would ease if they could trade regularly at border markets.
The court insisted that Mongol groups trade only through tribute envoys.
In 1550 Altan Khan's forces surrounded Beijing, and the court finally agreed to trade with him in 1571.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

Here the steppe story meets the tribute system from the tribute-order slide. The Ming was very reluctant to grant Mongol leaders any privileges, such as trading posts along the border. It wanted the different groups of Mongols to trade only through the envoy system (Ebrey, p. 233).

Mongol envoys repeatedly made a simple argument: friction could be reduced if regular trade were introduced. The logic is easy to see: people who could not trade for goods might raid for them. The court refused, again and again.

The second great threat came in 1550, when the forces of Altan Khan, khan of the Mongols in Inner Mongolia, surrounded Beijing itself. Only in 1571 did the court reach an agreement with Altan Khan. It named him a Ming prince and opened border markets where Mongols could trade horses for Chinese goods.

Q5 story · The western border · 1470s

Building Walls Became the Court's Compromise over the Mongols

Some officials wanted to allow more trade with the Mongols, while others refused any concession.
From the 1470s, border commanders linked forts with long walls and trapped some Mongol raiders.
Extending the wall became the court's compromise, though the quarrel over trade went on until 1571.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

At court, officials split into two camps. One side argued that the Mongols could be managed by allowing more trade. The other insisted that no concessions be made to them at all (Ebrey, p. 233).

Meanwhile, on the frontier, by the mid-fifteenth century, administrators of the western sections of the border began connecting their main garrison points with walls. They had some success in trapping groups of Mongol cavalry between them.

The decisive step came in 1474, when the court approved the plan of the official Yu Zijun. In a few months, 40,000 troops built about 600 miles of wall along the Ordos frontier.

Walls offered a way around the argument. Extending the wall was later seen as a solution to the deadlock: the court did not have to choose between trading and fighting. It could build.

Q5 story · North of Beijing · 1500s

The Ming Wall Is the Great Wall Visitors See Today

Much of the Ming Great Wall survives today, and it is the wall tourists walk on. It runs from northeast of Beijing into Gansu province in the west (Ebrey, p. 233). Q6 story · From Shandong to Guangzhou · 1500s

Pirates Raided the Coast, and the Ming Moved People Inland

Ships loaded with valuable goods attracted pirates.

Boats laden with goods attracted pirates, and the pirates grew so strong that they raided the coast from Shandong in the north to Guangzhou in the south (Ebrey, p. 234).

Chinese sources called these raiders wokou, "Japanese pirates." They were mainly Japanese at first but of mixed origin later, and by the early 1500s most of them were probably Chinese.

The Ming response is the surprising part. Instead of expanding its navy, the government forced people to move away from the coast, hoping to starve the pirates out. Remember that the court had broken up its great ships in 1474, on the fleet-ends slide.

Evacuation was not the whole answer. In the 1550s Hu Zongxian took the surrender of the smuggler chief Wang Zhi, and Qi Jiguang drilled a new army of country recruits that by 1563 had destroyed the last major pirate base in Fujian.

Follow the silver one step further, from the docks into the county tax office. The textbook shows silver flooding into China to pay for silk and porcelain (Ebrey, p. 234). The state soon wanted its share in the same metal.

Under Taizu's system, families paid the land tax mostly in grain and cloth and owed unpaid labor service, assigned household by household. Collecting so many different dues was slow, and the rich found ways to push them onto the poor.

From the 1530s, reforming officials in the Yangzi delta and the southeast began to bundle the land tax and the labor dues into a single payment, assessed mostly on land and paid in silver. The gold card gives the short definition. It was called the single whip, a pun on a phrase meaning "one combined item." The reform spread county by county, in fits and starts.

In 1581 the grand secretary Zhang Juzheng, after a farmland survey begun in 1578, ordered the method extended to the whole empire. Even then, remote regions adopted it years later.

Pierian Spring · Nanjing · 1400s and today

The official Ming History says the largest ships were about 137 meters (450 feet) long.

This slide takes a closer look at the "treasure ships" you met at the start of the voyages story. The textbook says that in 1474 the Ming broke up its remaining ships with three or more masts (Ebrey, p. 233). How big were the biggest?

The official Ming History, finished in 1739, says the largest measured 44 zhang by 18, roughly 137 by 56 meters, or 450 by 183 feet. That would make them far larger than any wooden ship known from archaeology.

In 1957 a rudder post more than 11 meters long was found at the Treasure Boat Shipyard in Nanjing, and two more turned up later. Some scholars used it to argue for ships of 140 meters or more. Others point out that the calculation used a formula for modern steel ships, and they argue for much smaller vessels; some suspect that the official dimensions came from a popular novel about Zheng He published in 1597. Still others accept very large flagships.

Pierian Spring · A historians' debate

PS: One Historian Argued China's Success Kept It from Industrializing, and Others Disagreed

Mark Elvin (1973) argued that Ming and Qing farming was so efficient, and labor so cheap, that labor-saving machines did not pay.
He called this a "high-level equilibrium trap."
Kenneth Pomeranz (2000) found the Yangzi delta about as prosperous as England until about 1750, and credited Europe's later lead to coal and colonies.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 234, 240; Elvin, The Pattern of the Chinese Past (1973); Pomeranz, The Great Divergence (2000).

This slide follows a question that the end of the fleet raises. The textbook shows a booming Ming economy: the population more than doubled and market towns spread (Ebrey, pp. 234, 240). If Ming China was so rich and skilled, why did it not go on to an industrial revolution?

The economic historian Mark Elvin gave a famous answer in 1973. By late imperial times, he argued, Chinese farming was highly efficient, with irrigation, fertilizers and devices like the treadle chain pump pictured here. The population was huge and labor was cheap, so investing in labor-saving machines did not pay. He called this a high-level equilibrium trap: the economy worked so well at its level that nothing pushed it to the next one.

Many historians now disagree. Kenneth Pomeranz, in The Great Divergence (2000), compared the Yangzi delta with England and found them roughly equal until about 1750. Europe pulled ahead, he argued, largely because of cheap coal and the resources of its American colonies. Notice that the question "Why not China?" assumes Europe's path was the normal one; you can ask whether that is fair.

The picture is an AI-generated artist's impression, not a historical source, and its pump is simplified: a real chain pump pulled a loop of wooden paddles up through a closed wooden trough.

Why this matters: "China fell behind" is an argument, not a fact, and historians still debate it.

Pierian Spring · Jinshanling · 1500s

PS: The Wall Was Built in Brick, Tower by Tower

Much of the Ming wall survives today.
Brick facing made it look imposing.
Towers every half mile served as lookouts.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 233.

A closer look at the wall from the great-wall slide. Much of the Ming Great Wall survives today (Ebrey, p. 233).

For much of its length the wall is faced with brick, which gives it its imposing appearance. In the eastern sections, towers stand about every half mile. Soldiers in them kept watch for raiders.

The photo is real. It shows the inside of one watchtower at Jinshanling, northeast of Beijing: thick brick walls, arched openings for looking out, and stairs up to the roof. Imagine standing guard here through a winter night, watching the hills to the north.

Notice how the wall and its towers fit the policy you followed in the lecture. Instead of marching armies into Mongolia, the Ming now kept soldiers in fixed posts and waited for raiders to come to them.

Why this matters: the wall's towers and brick show the defensive policy in physical form: soldiers waiting at fixed posts instead of marching onto the steppe.

Tribute system

In the tribute system, foreign rulers sent envoys with gifts ("tribute") to the Chinese emperor and acknowledged his superior status. The emperor received them with ceremony and sent them home with gifts of his own.

The Yongle emperor expanded and regularized the court's system for receiving envoys (Ebrey, p. 230).

Eunuch

A eunuch was a castrated man who served in the imperial palace. Because eunuchs could have no sons of their own, emperors often trusted them as personal servants and agents (see Lecture 1, slide aa, eunuchs).

×

Federation

A federation here means many separate Mongol groups united under one leader, as they had been under Chinggis Khan. In Ming times the Mongols were loosely divided into about six groups (Ebrey, p. 233).

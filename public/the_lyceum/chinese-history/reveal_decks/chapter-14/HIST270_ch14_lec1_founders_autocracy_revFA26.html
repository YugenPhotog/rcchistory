From Beggar Monk to Autocrat

A Beggar Monk Founded the Ming and Built a Throne for Giants

1368–1644 · Study Buddy edition

Welcome! This deck is your Study Buddy for the founding of the Ming dynasty. You can work through it on your own, at your own speed, as many times as you like.

Here is the big story in one breath. Zhu Yuanzhang (1328–1398) began life at the very bottom of society. Famine killed most of his family, a poor monastery sent him out to beg, and a religious rebellion gave him an army. In 1368 he founded the Ming, the "bright" dynasty. He and his son Chengzu, the Yongle emperor, built a government in which the emperor made every important decision. That worked while the man on the throne was strong. Later emperors were not, and the deck ends by watching the system sag under them (Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 227–230).

The portrait is real: an official court painting of the founder, now in Taipei. Real images in this deck carry full captions. Scene illustrations marked as AI-generated are modern interpretations, not historical sources.

Q1 story · Anhui · 1340s

Drought and Plague Left Sixteen-Year-Old Zhu Yuanzhang with Three Bodies to Bury

Drought and then plague struck Zhu's home region in Anhui province in the 1340s.
When Zhu was sixteen, his father, his oldest brother and that brother's wife all died.
A neighbor let the two surviving boys bury them in his field, but they had no coffins and no food.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

Picture a dry field in Anhui province in the 1340s. First drought withered the crops, and then plague swept through the villages. Zhu Yuanzhang was sixteen. Within a short time his father, his oldest brother and that brother's wife were all dead, and two penniless boys were left with three bodies to bury (Ebrey, p. 228).

His family had always lived on the edge. His parents moved again and again, looking for work or slipping away from rent collectors. Now there was no money for coffins and nothing to eat. A neighbor finally let the brothers bury their dead in his own field.

Born in 1328, Zhu turned sixteen around 1344, which is where this slide's name comes from. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: the future emperor knew hunger, debt and helplessness from the inside. Keep that boy in mind when you meet the emperor who tried to protect the poor and trusted almost no one.

Q1 story · Central China · 1340s

A Poor Monastery Took Zhu In, Then Sent Him Out to Beg

With no relatives to turn to, Zhu asked a Buddhist monastery to take him in as a novice.
The monastery was short of money, and within two months it sent him out to beg for food.
He wandered central China for three or four years and learned to read only after he returned.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

With his family gone and no relatives willing to help, Zhu Yuanzhang knocked on the gate of a Buddhist monastery and asked to be taken in as a novice, a beginner who had not yet taken full vows (Ebrey, p. 228).

The monks were nearly as poor as he was. Their tenant farmers, hit by the same hard times, could not pay rent, so the monastery had little food to share. In less than two months they handed the boy a bowl and sent him out to beg.

For the next three or four years Zhu walked the roads of central China. He saw villages, markets and hunger up close, and he lived on what strangers gave him. Only when he came back to the monastery did he learn to read. Remember that detail; his reading will matter later.

The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: a common shorthand calls Zhu a "wandering monk." It is more accurate to say he was a novice sent out to beg, a man who learned his letters late.

Check yourself: Why did the monastery send Zhu away so soon after taking him in?

Q1 story · The Yuan in crisis · 1351

In 1351 the Red Turbans Rose, Promising a Buddha Who Would End Suffering

In 1351 a religious rebel movement called the Red Turbans rose against the Mongol Yuan dynasty.
Its members belonged to the White Lotus Society, which taught that the future Buddha, Maitreya, would soon bring paradise to earth.
The rebels won real victories, even defeating Mongol cavalry.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

In 1351, a few years after Zhu came back to his monastery, rebellion broke out against the Mongol Yuan dynasty. The rebels wore red headcloths, so they were called the Red Turbans (Ebrey, p. 228).

They were a millenarian sect, meaning they believed the present suffering world was about to end and a perfect age was about to begin. Their White Lotus teachings mixed two traditions. One came from Manichaeism, a religion that saw the universe as a battle between the forces of light and darkness. The other was the cult of Maitreya, the Buddha of the future, who would come down to earth and end human suffering.

Beliefs like these gave poor farmers courage. The Red Turbans won real battles, even against Mongol cavalry, the most feared soldiers of the age. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: the Ming began inside a religious uprising. That origin will later embarrass the founder's educated advisers.

Check yourself: What did the Red Turbans believe Maitreya would do?

Q1 story · Anhui · 1352

When Yuan Troops Burned His Temple, Zhu Joined the Rebels and Rose Fast

While fighting the rebels, Yuan soldiers burned down Zhu's monastery.
At twenty-four, Zhu joined the Red Turbans, whose leaders were also men of humble birth.
A commander let Zhu marry his daughter, and within a few years 20,000–30,000 men fought under him.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

Government troops hunting the rebels came through Zhu's district and burned his temple to the ground. He had lost his family, and now he had lost his refuge too. At twenty-four (about 1352 by his birth year), he joined the Red Turbans (Ebrey, p. 228).

The rebel army suited him. Its leaders were men of modest origins like himself, so a former beggar could rise on talent alone. Zhu rose fast. One of the commanders let him marry his daughter, a sign of real trust. Within a couple of years Zhu had between twenty and thirty thousand men fighting under his command.

Notice the speed of the change. In a few years the boy who buried his family without coffins had become a general with an army the size of a city. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: Zhu learned to lead in a world of rebels, loyalty and force, not in a world of books and exams.

Q1 story · Nanjing · 1356

Educated Advisers Steered Zhu Away from the Sect, and He Made Nanjing His Base

Scholars joined Zhu because they thought he could win and hoped to shape his government.
They urged him to distance himself from the Red Turbans, whose religious beliefs educated men disliked.
In 1356 Zhu took Nanjing and kept his soldiers disciplined to win over local people.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228. Wall dates: Nanjing City Wall site information (outside the textbook).

In the 1350s China was full of strongmen. (Ebrey, p. 228).

Zhu stood out, and educated men noticed. These literati, men trained in the classics who expected to serve in government, joined him because they thought he might be the final winner and they hoped to shape the government he would build. They gave him a piece of advice: slowly put distance between yourself and the Red Turbans. Educated men did not share the sect's belief that the world was about to end.

In 1356 Zhu captured Nanjing on the Yangzi River and made it his base. He kept his soldiers disciplined so that they would not loot, which helped win over the local population.

The photo shows the stone city wall Zhu later raised around Nanjing. It is real, but it was built after 1356, so it shows the city he made, not the city he captured.

Why this matters: Zhu needed educated men to win and to rule, yet they were never quite his own kind. Hold on to that tension.

Q1 story · Dadu and Nanjing · 1368

In 1368 Zhu Took the Yuan Capital and Founded the "Bright" Dynasty

Zhu's generals defeated rival warlords one after another.
In 1368 his army took the Yuan capital days after the Mongol emperor fled north.
Zhu, now forty, named his dynasty Ming, meaning "bright," and his reign Hongwu, "abundantly martial."

Independent study: why one man has three names (slide ll)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

Many of Zhu's followers grew into brilliant generals, and one by one they defeated his rivals. In 1368 his armies marched into the Yuan capital. The Yuan emperor and his closest followers had fled north just days before (Ebrey, p. 228).

Zhu, now forty, declared himself emperor. He named the dynasty Ming, "bright," a word that echoed the light-against-darkness ideas of the Red Turbans. He called his reign period Hongwu, "abundantly martial," and he never changed it, so he is often called the Hongwu emperor. After his death he received the temple name Taizu. That is why one man has three names in this deck.

Now look at the portrait. Compare it with the calm, handsome court portrait on slide a, title. Both kinds of image of Taizu survive in the palace collection. A portrait is not a photograph; it is a choice about how a ruler should be remembered.

Why this matters: the beggar novice had become the founder of a dynasty, and he would now try to remake China in his own image.

Q1 story · The new state · 1370s–1380s

Taizu Counted Every Field and Household So Taxes Would Fall More Fairly

Taizu remembered how tax collectors had hounded his own parents.
He ordered a full registration of farmland and population across the empire.
Officials used the registers to share out taxes and labor service more fairly among households.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 228, 240.

As emperor, Taizu, the name we will use from now on, did not forget where he came from. His family had moved from place to place to dodge rent collectors, and the textbook says they lived in fear of greedy tax collectors (Ebrey, pp. 228, 240).

He wanted a world where people obeyed their superiors and bad deeds were punished quickly, but he also wanted to lighten the load on the poor. So he ordered a full-scale registration of all cultivated land and all people. With the registers in hand, officials could assess labor service, the unpaid work households owed the state, and taxes more fairly, so that the rich could not slide their burdens onto the poor (p. 228).

The photo shows a real Ming land register of the kind this survey produced. Each little drawing marks a plot of land. People called them "fish-scale" registers because the plot maps fit together like scales.

Why this matters: the beggar's memories shaped the emperor's policies. Fairness to the poor and control by the state went together.

Q1 story · Nanjing and the villages · 1370s–1390s

Taizu Rewrote the Law and Told Villagers How to Behave

Taizu had a new law code drafted and personally oversaw five revisions of it.
Experts compared each law with the old Tang code in front of him, but he made every final decision.
His instructions, read aloud in villages, told people to obey their parents, live in peace with neighbors, and avoid evil.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

Taizu did not leave the law to experts. He ordered a new law code and took it through five revisions. Legal specialists sat with him and compared every statute with the code of the Tang dynasty, the great model from centuries earlier, but the emperor himself made the final decisions (Ebrey, p. 228).

He also spoke directly to ordinary people. Taizu issued instructions that were read aloud to villagers, most of whom could not read. They told people to be filial, that is, to respect and obey their parents, to live in harmony with their neighbors, to work contentedly at their occupations, and to refrain from evil.

The textbook suggests where his model of rule came from. In the world where he grew up, Daoist temples held gods called "emperors," all-powerful divine rulers. Taizu seems to have wanted to lift the emperor up to their level. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: one man tried to reach from the palace into every village, with laws and with lessons.

Q1 story · Garrisons and workshops · 1368–1398

Taizu Made Soldiering and Craft Work Duties That Families Inherited

Taizu kept a Mongol practice: some families owed the state a soldier or craft work generation after generation.
A military household had to replace its soldier whenever he died, was injured, or deserted.
The Ming army reached about one million men, mostly stationed along the northern border and near the capital.

Independent study: Mongols in Taizu's army (slide kk)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 228–229.

Taizu threw out much of Mongol rule, but he kept some Yuan habits. One was the use of hereditary obligations, duties a family passed down from parent to child. Artisan households had to supply the palace or the government with craft work as their tax (Ebrey, pp. 228–229).

The army worked the same way. The Mongols had made military service hereditary, and the Ming took the practice over. Once a family was classed as a military household, it had to supply one soldier, then another, generation after generation. If the soldier was injured, died or deserted, the family sent a replacement.

Under Taizu the army grew to about one million soldiers. They came from the armies that had fought for China, plus some conscripts and some convicts. Garrisons clustered along the northern border and near the capital. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: Taizu wanted a world where everyone had a fixed place and a fixed duty. The next slide shows how well that worked.

Q1 story · The northern frontier · late 1300s

The Army Was Supposed to Feed Itself, but It Rarely Did

Each garrison received land that its soldiers farmed in turns to grow their own food.
In practice the garrisons were rarely self-sufficient.
Men forced to be soldiers often fought poorly, and desertion was hard to stop.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 229.

On paper, the plan was clever. Each garrison received a tract of land, and the soldiers took turns farming it to grow their own food. China had tried this idea again and again since the Han dynasty. In theory it should have given the Ming a large army that cost the government very little (Ebrey, p. 229).

Reality was less satisfying. Just as in earlier dynasties, the garrisons were rarely self-sufficient, meaning they could not grow enough to feed themselves. Men who were soldiers only because their family was registered as a military household did not necessarily make good fighting men. And men who did not want to be there ran away, a problem that was very hard to stop.

Look for the man slipping over the ridge in the scene image. Desertion was the quiet way a family duty failed. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: Taizu's fixed system looked orderly from the palace, but it depended on people who could not be ordered into enthusiasm.

Q1 story · Nanjing · 1376

Taizu Executed Thousands of Officials over a Paperwork Shortcut

Taizu distrusted educated men even though he needed them to run his government.
In 1376 officials were found to have taken a shortcut in handling grain-tax paperwork.
Taizu had thousands of them killed.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 229.

Taizu needed educated officials to run the registers, the law courts and the tax system. Yet the textbook says he had "deeply ambivalent feelings" about men of education. He relied on them and distrusted them at the same time (Ebrey, p. 229).

In 1376 that suspicion turned deadly. Investigators found that officials had taken a shortcut in handling the paperwork for the grain tax. Taizu did not treat it as a clerical slip. He had thousands of them killed.

Think about the scale. The offense was about forms and procedures, and the punishment was death for thousands. The textbook does not record Taizu's reasoning, so be careful about guessing his motives; what the record shows is how far he would go. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation that implies the violence rather than showing it; it is not a historical source.

Why this matters: officials learned that even routine paperwork could cost them their lives. The next purge would reach the most powerful official of all.

Q1 story · Nanjing · 1380

In 1380 Taizu Accused His Chancellor of Treason and Abolished the Office

In 1380 Taizu decided that his chancellor, Hu Weiyong, was plotting to assassinate him.
Investigations went on for nearly ten years, and as many as 15,000 people were executed.
Taizu abolished the post of chancellor and dealt directly with every ministry himself.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 229.

In 1380 Taizu concluded that his chancellor, Hu Weiyong, was plotting to assassinate him. The chancellor was the head of the civil government, the official who stood between the emperor and the ministries (Ebrey, p. 229).

What followed was not a single trial. Anyone even remotely connected to Hu was executed. The investigations dragged on for nearly a decade, and as many as fifteen thousand people lost their lives.

Then Taizu did something with very long consequences. He abolished the office of chancellor. From 1380 on, the Hongwu emperor acted as his own chancellor and dealt directly with the heads of the departments and ministries. Every important decision now had to cross the emperor's desk.

Why this matters: a hardworking, suspicious founder could carry that load. Would every future emperor?

Q1 story · The palace in Nanjing · 1368–1398

Officials Knelt Before Taizu and Could Be Beaten in Open Court

Taizu required his officials to kneel when they spoke to him.
He did not hesitate to have officials beaten in front of the whole court.
His behavior was so erratic that historians think he probably suffered from a mental illness.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 228–229.

Step inside Taizu's audience hall. Every official who addressed the emperor did so on his knees. That was Taizu's rule, part of his effort to raise the emperor far above everyone else, like the god-emperors of the Daoist temples he had known as a boy (Ebrey, p. 228).

Kneeling was not the worst of it. Taizu did not hesitate to have officials beaten in open court, in front of their colleagues. The textbook says he sometimes brutally humiliated men of education in this way (p. 229). For scholars raised to believe that the ruler should treat ministers with respect, this was a deep shock.

The textbook goes further than most: his behavior was so erratic that he most likely suffered from some form of mental illness. Notice how carefully that is worded. Historians can judge behavior from the record, but they cannot examine a patient who died in 1398. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: the emperor's power over officials had become personal, physical and unpredictable.

Q1 story · Nanjing · 1380s

Taizu Hunted for Hidden Insults and Made Refusing Office a Crime

As he learned to read better, Taizu suspected scholars of mocking him with words that sounded like "bandit" or "monk."
When educated men began avoiding government service, he made it illegal to refuse or resign an office.
Only Empress Ma could calm his rages, and after she died in 1382 no one could.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 229.

Remember that Zhu learned to read late, after his years of begging. As his reading improved, he discovered something frightening. Scholars could criticize him in hidden ways, with phrases that had double meanings or sounded like the words for "bandit" or "monk," his own past. Even poems passed around in private could be treated as evidence of disloyalty (Ebrey, p. 229).

Educated men reacted the only safe way they could: they avoided official life. Taizu answered by making it illegal to turn down an appointment or to resign from office. Serving a frightening emperor was now compulsory.

One person could still reach him. Taizu fell into rages that only his wife, Empress Ma, could stop. After her death in 1382, no one could calm him. The portrait is a real Ming court painting of her, an official likeness rather than a sketch from life.

Why this matters: by the 1380s, serving the emperor meant serving someone who might read treason into a pun.

Q2 story · Nanjing · 1398

Taizu Outlived His Heir, and the Throne Passed to a Young Grandson

Taizu's eldest son died before his father, so Taizu named that son's eldest son as heir.
In 1398 the grandson, Huidi (the Jianwen emperor), came to the throne at twenty-one.
Taizu's other sons had been sent out to fiefs, but some still commanded troops.

Independent study: Taizu's 26 sons and the southwest (slide jj)

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 229.

Taizu lived a long life, to seventy-one sui, the traditional Chinese way of counting age in which a baby is one at birth. He outlived his eldest son, the heir apparent. So he named that son's eldest son, his grandson, as the next emperor (Ebrey, p. 229).

When Taizu died in 1398, the grandson came to the throne at twenty-one. He is known as Huidi. His reign name was Jianwen, so you may also see him called the Jianwen emperor.

The danger sat in the provinces. Taizu had twenty-six sons. He had sent them out of the capital to fiefs and barred them from exams and office, but he allowed them military assignments (p. 229). Some of these uncles of the new emperor were grown men with troops. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: Taizu planned an orderly succession by birth order. He did not plan for an uncle who wanted the throne.

Q2 story · From Beijing to Nanjing · 1399–1402

Huidi's Uncle, the Prince of Yan, Took the Throne After Three Years of War

Almost at once, the Prince of Yan, Taizu's eldest surviving son by the empress, rebelled against his nephew.
He led his troops in person and won after three years of civil war.
He ruled as Chengzu, the Yongle emperor.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 229–230, 233.

Almost immediately after Huidi took the throne, his uncle struck. The Prince of Yan was the eldest of Taizu's surviving sons by the empress, and Beijing was his base as a prince (Ebrey, pp. 229–230). He launched a military campaign to take the throne for himself.

He was a military man like his father, married to the daughter of a leading general, and he directed the civil war himself, often leading troops into battle. After three years of fighting, he won. The new emperor is known by his temple name, Chengzu, and by his reign name, Yongle.

A note on dates: the textbook gives his reign as 1403–1425 but also says he died in 1424 (pp. 229, 233). Most reference works date the reign 1402–1424, so treat 1424 as his death year.

The portrait is a real court painting, made to show a calm and rightful ruler. That message mattered for a man who had taken the throne by force.

Why this matters: the second Ming emperor was a usurper, a ruler who seized the throne from its rightful holder.

Q2 story · Nanjing · 1402

Fang Xiaoru Refused to Write the Usurper's Proclamation, and Chengzu Destroyed Him

Many officials who had served Huidi refused to accept the new emperor.
The leading Confucian scholar Fang Xiaoru refused to write the proclamation of Chengzu's accession.
Chengzu had him executed, along with relatives and associates "to the tenth degree," tens of thousands of people.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230.

Winning the war did not win the officials. Quite a few men who had served Huidi resisted the usurpation. Like his father, Chengzu was willing to use terror to keep officials in line (Ebrey, p. 230).

The most famous resister was Fang Xiaoru, the leading Confucian scholar of the day. Chengzu needed a proclamation announcing his accession, his coming to the throne, and Fang refused to draft it.

The punishment was meant to be remembered. Fang was executed by dismemberment. Then Chengzu had his relatives and associates executed "to the tenth degree," including the men who had passed the civil service examinations when Fang conducted them. Tens of thousands were killed. The scene image for this slide shows the refusal, not the killing; it is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: one scholar's refusal shows what Confucian loyalty could mean, and what an emperor could do to crush it.

Q2 story · Nanjing · early 1400s

The Same Emperor Put Two Thousand Scholars to Work on an Encyclopedia

Chengzu had 2,000 scholars compile a 22,938-chapter collection of knowledge drawn from 7,000 books.
He ordered a collection of Cheng-Zhu Confucian texts to help students prepare for the exams.
He also expanded and regularized the court's system for receiving foreign envoys.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230. Copy date: Chester Beatty Library record (outside the textbook).

The textbook turns with one word: "Yet." The same emperor who destroyed Fang Xiaoru also had impressive accomplishments (Ebrey, p. 230).

He put two thousand scholars to work on a compendium, a giant collection of knowledge, drawn from seven thousand books. It ran to fifty million words in 22,938 chapters. We call it the Yongle Encyclopedia.

He also had scholars compile a selection of texts from the Cheng-Zhu school, the version of Confucian teaching built by the Song thinkers Cheng Yi and Zhu Xi, to help students preparing for the civil service examinations. And he expanded and regularized the court's diplomatic system for receiving envoys from foreign rulers.

The page on the slide is real, but it comes from a copy made about 150 years later. Most of the encyclopedia has been lost, and the surviving volumes are scattered across libraries around the world.

Why this matters: Chengzu combined terror and patronage. He punished scholars who defied him and employed thousands who served him.

Q2 story · The steppe and Vietnam · 1400s–1420s

Chengzu Fought in Person, from the Mongol Steppe to Vietnam

Chengzu, married to a leading general's daughter, often led troops into battle himself.
He won victories over the Mongols on the northern steppe.
In 1406 he invaded Vietnam, which the Ming held for only about two decades.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 229–230, 233.

Chengzu was a soldier first. He was married to the daughter of a leading general, who encouraged his military interests, and he often led troops into battle himself, winning victories over the Mongols (Ebrey, pp. 229–230). He kept campaigning on the steppe, the grasslands north of China, for the rest of his life. He died on his fifth campaign into Mongolia in 1424, at sixty-four (p. 233).

He also looked south. In 1406 he authorized a major expedition into Vietnam, which had been independent for more than four centuries. The campaign succeeded, but the Ming held the region for only about two decades.

Why this matters: like his father, Chengzu ruled as a commander. The government was built around an active emperor.

Q2 story · Beijing · 1407–1420

Chengzu Built a New Capital at Beijing, His Old Base as a Prince

Chengzu moved the capital from Nanjing to Beijing, his base as a prince and the old Yuan capital.
Hundreds of thousands of workers built the city between 1407 and 1420.
Its outer walls stood forty feet high, ran nearly fifteen miles around, and had nine gates.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230. Scroll details: British Museum collection record.

Early in his reign, Chengzu made a decision that still shapes China: he moved the capital from Nanjing in the south to Beijing in the north. Beijing had been his own base as the Prince of Yan, and it had been the capital in Yuan times (Ebrey, p. 230).

Building it took hundreds of thousands of workers and lasted from 1407 to 1420. Like Chang'an under the Sui and Tang, Beijing was a planned city. Its main outer walls were forty feet high and nearly fifteen miles around, pierced by nine gates.

The painting is real. A Ming artist placed an official in the foreground and the walls, gates and golden roofs of the palace behind him, so the city itself becomes a symbol of imperial order. It was painted in the 1500s, long after the city was built.

Little of the original city walls and gates survives today, but the palace does, as the next slide shows.

Why this matters: the capital now sat close to the northern frontier and the emperor's own power base, far from the rich south.

Q2 story · The Forbidden City · 1420

At Beijing's Center, the Forbidden City Was Built to Awe Everyone Who Entered

Beijing was planned as boxes within boxes: the outer city, the Imperial City, and the palace at the center.
The palace, called the Forbidden City, had close to 10,000 rooms.
Great audience halls stood on a north–south line, with vast courtyards where officials stood or knelt.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230.

The Forbidden City seen from Jingshan, looking south along the central axis. Photo: Gary Todd, 1991. CC0, Wikimedia Commons.

Walk into the middle of Chengzu's new capital. Beijing was built on a north–south axis and laid out as boxes within boxes. The outer walls enclosed the whole city. Inside them was the Imperial City, with government offices. Inside that was the Forbidden City, the palace itself, with close to ten thousand rooms (Ebrey, p. 230).

The main audience halls stood one behind another along the central axis. Between them lay vast courtyards where attending officials stood or knelt.

The textbook says the design, "as intended, awes all who enter." The photo is real and was taken from Jingshan, the hill just north of the palace. The palace complex still reflects its fifteenth-century layout and architecture, even though buildings have been repaired and rebuilt over the centuries.

Why this matters: architecture taught the same lesson as kneeling in Taizu's court. Everything led to the emperor, and everyone else was small.

Q2 story · The Grand Canal · 1400s

Beijing Lived on Southern Grain Hauled Up the Grand Canal

The land around Beijing grew far less grain than the land around Nanjing.
Chengzu had the Grand Canal broadened, deepened, and fitted with more locks and dams.
15,000 boats and 160,000 transport soldiers pulling barges became the capital's lifeline.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230.

A capital is a city full of people who do not farm: officials, soldiers, palace staff, craftsmen. They all have to eat. The trouble was that the land around Beijing was not nearly as productive as the land around Nanjing (Ebrey, p. 230).

Chengzu's answer was the Grand Canal, the long chain of waterways linking the rice-rich south to the north. To supply Beijing with grain, the canal was extensively renovated. Workers broadened it, deepened it, and fitted it with more locks, gated chambers that raise or lower boats from one water level to another, and more dams.

Then the state staffed it. Fifteen thousand boats and 160,000 soldiers of the transport army, men who hauled loaded barges from towpaths along the banks, became the lifeline of the capital. Trace the canal on the map from Hangzhou in the south to Beijing in the north.

Why this matters: moving the capital north tied the dynasty's survival to one long, expensive waterway.

Go back to slide m, hu-weiyong-1380, for a moment. In 1380 Taizu abolished the office of chancellor and ran the ministries himself (Ebrey, p. 229). His successors still needed help, but there was no longer a chief minister to give it.

So emperors turned to the inner court, the people who worked inside the palace near the emperor, rather than the outer court of regular officials in the ministries. At first, relatively junior men from the Hanlin Academy served as secretaries. Over time the practice became regular, a kind of cabinet of grand secretaries (p. 230).

The grand secretaries were given extra titles as vice ministers to raise their standing, but they lacked real administrative experience. That weakened them when they dealt with the experienced officials of the outer court.

Q3 story · The inner palace · 1400s–1500s

Grand Secretaries Had to Work with Palace Eunuchs, and Eunuch Power Grew

Eunuchs, castrated men who served inside the palace, helped manage the flow of paperwork to the emperor.
The grand secretaries had to cooperate with them, and some of the eunuchs' bad reputation rubbed off on them.
Eunuchs became as serious a problem under the Ming as under the late Han and late Tang.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 245. Painting details: Palace Museum, Beijing.

Eunuchs were castrated men who served inside the palace. Because they lived and worked so close to the emperor, they could become his most trusted servants, and his gatekeepers.

The grand secretaries could not do their job alone. They had to work with the eunuchs, who helped manage the flow of paperwork to and from the emperor. Officials looked down on eunuchs, and some of that stigma spilled over onto the grand secretaries who worked with them (Ebrey, p. 230).

The textbook draws a comparison with earlier history: eunuchs became as serious a problem in Ming times as they had been in the late Han and late Tang. Its chapter summary adds that palace eunuchs, given the job of investigating charges of corruption and sedition, came to rival the civil service in numbers and power (p. 245).

The painting is a real Ming court scroll. It shows the Xuande emperor, who reigned in the 1420s and 1430s, at leisure among palace attendants. Read it as a picture of palace life, not as a labeled portrait of eunuchs.

Why this matters: the men closest to the emperor gained power that the regular officials could not easily check.

Q3 story · The palace · 1400s–1600s

A Rigid Succession Rule Put One Weak Emperor After Another on the Throne

Taizu ruled that the throne must pass to the empress's eldest son, or to that son's eldest son.
The rule gave the court no way to choose an able heir.
In Ming times one mediocre or erratic emperor followed another.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230.

Taizu wanted no arguments about who would rule next. He laid down a fixed rule: the throne would pass to the eldest son of the empress, or, if that son died before his father, to the son's own eldest son. That is exactly how his grandson Huidi became emperor on slide q (Ebrey, pp. 229–230).

Earlier dynasties had generally followed the same principle, but not inflexibly. They could bend it when the eldest son was clearly unfit. The Ming made it rigid.

A fixed rule prevents some quarrels, but it cannot guarantee ability. Whoever was born first got the throne, whether he was wise, foolish, a child or ill. The textbook says that in Ming times, the flaws in this system became apparent as "one mediocre, obtuse, or erratic emperor followed another." The next three slides show what that looked like. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: Taizu built a government that needed a strong, hardworking emperor, then chose a succession rule that could not promise one.

Q3 story · The northern frontier · 1449

A Young Emperor Who Liked Playing Soldier Was Captured by the Mongols in 1449

Yingzong became emperor at eight and grew up loving to play soldier.
At twenty-one, urged on by his favorite eunuch, he led an army against the Mongols himself.
His 50,000-man army was destroyed and he was captured; the Mongols sent him back the next year.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 230, 233.

Here is the first case. Yingzong came to the throne at the age of eight. As a boy he liked to play soldier (Ebrey, p. 230).

At twenty-one, encouraged by his favorite eunuch, he decided to lead an army against the Mongols in person, the way his great-grandfather Chengzu had done. Chengzu was an experienced general. Yingzong was not. His fifty-thousand-man army was destroyed, and the emperor himself was captured. The textbook names the man who captured him: Esen, khan of the Western Mongols (p. 233).

The ending is almost comic. The Mongols found the captive emperor so useless that they returned him the next year, by which time his brother had already been put on the throne. The textbook does not tell the rest, but Yingzong later regained the throne.

Notice the eunuch in this story. A palace favorite had the emperor's ear on a question of war. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: an emperor who was raised in the palace, and trusted those around him there, could lead the empire into disaster.

Q3 story · The palace · 1465–1567

The Next Emperors Let Favorites Rule, Drank, or Raged

Xianzong let a palace lady nearly twenty years older control him, and she had his children by other women killed.
Wuzong defied court customs and spent much of his time drunk.
Shizong raged at his palace women so cruelly that a group of them tried to murder him in 1542.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230.

The textbook gives three more emperors in quick succession (Ebrey, p. 230).

Xianzong (r. 1465–1488) came to the throne at sixteen and let himself be manipulated by a palace lady almost twenty years older than he was. She had his children by other women systematically killed.

Wuzong (r. 1505–1521) willfully defied established practices, the customs that governed how an emperor should behave, and spent much of his time drunk.

Shizong (r. 1522–1567) refused to treat his predecessor as his adoptive father, as court custom expected. He was subject to fits of rage and was so cruel to his palace ladies that a group of them tried to murder him in 1542.

Each of these men inherited a throne that Taizu had designed for a ruler who would decide everything himself. None of them was that ruler. The scene image for this slide is an AI-generated interpretation, not a historical source.

Why this matters: when emperors would not govern, someone else in the palace would.

Q3 story · Beijing · 1565

In 1565 Hai Rui Told the Emperor He Had Failed as a Man, a Father, and a Ruler

Hai Rui was an official famous for his honesty and courage.
In a memorial (a formal written report to the emperor), he told Shizong he had been a disaster for the country.
Few officials dared to criticize an emperor so directly.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230. Portrait attribution: Wikimedia Commons record.

One official said out loud what many thought. Hai Rui was famous for his honesty and his courage (Ebrey, p. 230).

In 1565 he submitted a memorial, a formal written report to the emperor, addressed to Shizong. In it, as the textbook summarizes it, he said that the emperor had failed as a man, a father, and a ruler, and had been a disaster for the country. These are the textbook's words describing the memorial, not a quotation from it, so we give no quotation marks on the slide.

Think back to Taizu's court, where officials knelt and could be beaten for much less. Hai Rui knew the risks of criticizing an emperor directly, and few officials dared to do it.

The portrait was painted in the Qing dynasty, long after Hai Rui died, so it is an image of his later reputation rather than a likeness from life.

Why this matters: Confucian officials still believed they had a duty to correct the ruler, even when the system gave them little power to do it.

Q3 story · The Forbidden City · 1573–1620

The Wanli Emperor Simply Stopped Holding Court

The Wanli emperor (r. 1573–1620) was intelligent but refused to meet his officials for years at a time.
Memorials piled up unopened, and empty posts went unfilled.
With no chancellor, no one else had the authority to run the government.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 230. The link between the missing chancellor and the paralysis is this lecture's inference from the textbook's account.

The last case is the strangest. Shenzong, the Wanli emperor, reigned from 1573 to 1620, nearly half a century. The textbook calls him intelligent (Ebrey, p. 230).

Yet he refused to hold court for years at a time. Holding court meant meeting officials, hearing reports and making decisions. Under Wanli, memorials piled up unopened, and vacancies in the government went unfilled.

Now put this together with slide m, hu-weiyong-1380, and slide z, grand-secretaries. In earlier dynasties, a chancellor might have kept business moving when the ruler withdrew. The textbook does not draw that link in these words; it is our inference from its account. But in a system where every decision had to come from the emperor, an emperor who stopped deciding left the government stuck.

The portrait is a real court painting. It shows the dignified ruler the court wanted to see, not the absent one officials actually dealt with.

Why this matters: Taizu built a throne only a strong emperor could carry. Wanli shows what happened when the emperor put it down.

PS: Taizu Sent His 26 Sons to Fiefs and Pushed the Empire into the Southwest

Taizu sent his sons out of the capital and barred them and their descendants from exams and office.

In the 1380s the Ming took Yunnan and created Guizhou province.

Taizu had twenty-six sons, several already teenagers when he became emperor. He took measures so that they and their descendants would not interfere in the government. Pierian Spring · Names · Ming dynasty

PS: Reign Names and Temple Names Explain Why One Man Has Three Names

Zhu Yuanzhang was his personal name.
Hongwu was his reign name, which he never changed, starting a Ming custom.
Taizu was the temple name used in sacrifices after his death.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 228.

One man, three names. Zhu Yuanzhang is his personal name, the one he had as a boy and a rebel.

Hongwu, "abundantly martial," was the name of his reign period. Earlier emperors often changed reign names several times, but he kept one for his whole thirty-year reign, and later Ming emperors followed his example. So historians often name Ming rulers by their reign names: the Hongwu emperor, the Yongle emperor, the Wanli emperor (Ebrey, p. 228).

Taizu, "grand progenitor," was his temple name, given after death and used in the sacrifices offered to him. That is why he is also called Ming Taizu. The meaning "grand progenitor" is a standard translation, not from the textbook.

Chengzu and Yongle work the same way: temple name and reign name for one man.

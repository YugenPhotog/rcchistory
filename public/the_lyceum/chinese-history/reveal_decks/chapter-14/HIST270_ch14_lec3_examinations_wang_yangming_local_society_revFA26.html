Q7 story · Across the empire · 1370s–1500s

The Ming Made the Examinations the Most Prestigious Road to Office Again

Under the Yuan dynasty, examinations had played only a tiny part in choosing officials.
Under the Ming, passing the exams again became the most respected way into government.
Educated men eagerly sought office even though emperors often treated officials harshly.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234. Portrait: Freer Gallery of Art record via Wikimedia Commons.

Start with a puzzle. Lecture 1 showed Ming emperors beating officials in open court and purging thousands. You might expect educated men to stay away. They did not. The textbook says they were as eager to enter the bureaucracy as in earlier ages (Ebrey, p. 234).

Recall from Chapter 12 that under the Mongol Yuan dynasty, the civil service examinations played only a very small part in choosing officials. Under the Ming, the examinations more than regained their old importance and became the most prestigious way to enter government service.

The portrait is real, a Ming painting from the late 1500s. Notice the square panel on his chest. The textbook's own portrait of the official Jiang Shunfu (p. 239) explains that such a panel was a "rank badge" showing an official's grade. Office meant honor that everyone could see.

The Yongle emperor made the system routine. Under him the exams ran on a regular three-year cycle, and in 1404 a single metropolitan exam passed 472 men as jinshi, one of the largest classes of the dynasty.

Why this matters: because office was the highest honor, the exams shaped the ambitions of every educated family.

Q7 recap · The exam video · Ming scale

You Saw the Exam System in the Video, and the Ming Ran It on a Huge Scale

You saw this in the civil service exam video earlier in the semester: the three degrees, the cells, the searches and the recopied papers (watch again ↗).
In each Ming provincial capital, five to ten thousand candidates sat the exam, and a man caught in a cheat-sheet gown was thrown out.
By 1500 China had about 30,000 shengyuan who had to keep retesting, so for most of them exams became a way of life.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 236–237; "China's Examination Hell: Video Lecture" (S. Austin, 2013), YouTube.

You already know how the exam system worked. You saw it in the civil service exam video earlier in the semester: the three degrees, the provincial exam every three years, the halls of tiny cells, the searches for crib sheets, and the clerks who recopied every paper so no examiner could recognize a candidate. The link on the slide replays it.

The video describes the system at its height under the Qing, so a few details differ: our textbook says Ming cells had two boards, and, as slide c shows, the Ming dropped the poetry question.

What matters here is the Ming scale. Five to ten thousand candidates crowded each provincial capital, and a man caught in a cheat-sheet gown was thrown out, banned from the next session, and might lose his degree (Ebrey, pp. 236–237). By 1500 there were about thirty thousand shengyuan, about one in every three thousand people, and they had to pass tests again and again to keep their status (p. 236).

Q7 · Q8 story · The classroom · 1300s–1500s

Every Candidate Studied Zhu Xi's Explanations of the Four Books

The Four Books, as explained by the Song thinker Zhu Xi, were the official course of study.
Zhu Xi's teachings were treated as orthodox, meaning the officially correct version of Confucianism.
Because Zhu Xi thought poetry did nothing for moral growth, the poetry test was dropped.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234. Portrait: Philadelphia Museum of Art record via Wikimedia Commons.

What did candidates study? The Four Books of Confucianism, read through the explanations of Zhu Xi, the great Song-dynasty thinker you met in Chapter 12. The textbook calls the orthodoxy of Zhu Xi's teachings an integral part of the Ming system: every candidate had to study the Four Books and Zhu Xi's interpretations of them (Ebrey, p. 234).

Orthodox means the officially approved, correct version of a teaching. An answer that disagreed with Zhu Xi was, in exam terms, a wrong answer.

Zhu Xi's views even changed the format of the exam. He considered writing poetry of no value for moral cultivation, so the Ming dropped the poetry composition section that earlier dynasties had tested.

If you remember a poetry question from the exam video, that belonged to other periods; the Ming dropped it.

The portrait is real, from an album of famous men now in the Philadelphia Museum of Art; like most such portraits, it was painted long after Zhu Xi's lifetime.

Why this matters: remember Zhu Xi. Wang Yangming will spend years struggling with his ideas, starting on slide o.

Q7 · Q8 story · The study · 1400s–1500s

All Candidates Had to Write the Same Rigid "Eight-Legged" Essay

Every candidate learned to write the approved "eight-legged" style of essay.
The style built arguments by analogy and by pairing balanced statements.
Shared texts and a shared style created one exam culture across the whole empire.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 234.

Knowing the right books was not enough. Every candidate also had to write in one approved form, the "eight-legged" essay. The textbook says this style emphasized reasoning by analogy and pairing statements (Ebrey, p. 234). Picture an argument built from matched halves, each claim balanced against another.

The form became compulsory from the late 1470s or 1480s, a century after the exams were revived (historian Charles Hucker).

Because everyone studied the same books and wrote in the same form, the textbook says the Ming examination system created a nationwide culture for all who took part, more so than in Tang or Song times. A candidate from Guangdong and one from Shandong had memorized the same texts and practiced the same moves.

That shared culture had a cost. The textbook later asks whether such intellectual conformity would stifle thought, and answers that the government's power to channel the elite should not be overestimated (p. 237). Hold on to that question.

Q7 story · Province and capital · 1400s–1600s

Fewer Men Reached the Top Under the Ming Than Under the Song

The Ming added a new provincial screening test, so candidates had to pass more exams than before.
The Ming produced only about 89 jinshi a year.
The Southern Song had produced 135 a year, and the Jin another 149.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 235–236, including the table "Ming Examination Degrees," p. 236.

You already know the three degrees from the exam video: shengyuan, juren from the provincial exam held every three years, and jinshi from the capital and palace exams. Here is what the Ming changed.

First, the Ming added a new screening test at the province level, so a successful candidate had to pass more tests than before (Ebrey, p. 235).

Second, the top was narrower. When China was divided, the Southern Song produced about 135 jinshi a year and the Jin another 149. The Ming, ruling all of China, averaged only about 89 a year.

The diagram turns the textbook's table into a ladder (p. 236). Read the Ming numbers: most juren were twenty to thirty, most jinshi thirty to forty, and only about 2–4 percent passed the provincial exam and 7–9 percent the capital exam.

Why this matters: more tests and fewer winners meant fiercer competition than in Song times.

Q7 story · A family courtyard

Families Started Boys on the Classics at Age Four or Five

The exams were written in classical Chinese, a language very different from everyday speech.
Literati families who began teaching their sons young had a big advantage.
Lineages set up schools and paid for talented boys, because the whole lineage shared in their success.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 236.

Preparing for the exams meant, in effect, learning a different language: the classical written language, quite different from everyday speech. So education was best started young, and the young are better at memorizing the long texts the exams required (Ebrey, p. 236).

Handwriting counted too, at the lower levels, because papers were not recopied by clerks until the provincial exams. Literati families who began teaching their sons at age four or five had a significant advantage.

Well-off families hired tutors, but schools became more and more available in Ming times. Lineages, groups of relatives descended from one ancestor, were especially active in setting up and sometimes endowing schools. Because the whole lineage would share the prestige of a success, its leaders were willing to invest in any talented boy. Lineages return on slide bb.

Q7 story · A family of doctors · 1461–1554

Exam Success Raised a Family of Doctors, and Its Daughter Became a Physician to Women

As schools spread, families of physicians and merchants had more chances to succeed in the exams.
Tan Yunxian's grandfather married into a doctor's family, and two of his sons passed the jinshi.
Her grandparents taught her medicine; she treated only women patients and lived to ninety-three.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 236, 238 (Biography: Tan Yunxian, Woman Doctor).

The textbook connects the spread of schools to one remarkable family. As schools became more available, families that had followed other careers for generations, such as physicians or merchants, had more chances than ever to succeed through the exams (Ebrey, p. 236).

Tan Yunxian (1461–1554) shows how. Her grandmother was a physician's daughter, and her grandfather married into his wife's family to learn medicine himself. At least two of their sons, including Tan's father, passed the jinshi exam and became officials, which raised the family's standing considerably (p. 238).

The grandparents found Yunxian very bright and passed their medical knowledge on to her. She married and raised four children, but she also practiced medicine, confining her practice to women. She died at the age of ninety-three.

Q7 story · Nanjing · 1397

When Every Winner in 1397 Was a Southerner, Taizu Executed the Examiners

The wealthy Jiangnan region produced the best-prepared candidates.
In 1397 every man who passed the palace exam was a southerner.
Taizu executed two examiners, had everyone retested, and passed only northerners.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 235–236.

Now the second half of the review question: regional balance. Remember from Lecture 1 how Taizu treated officials. Here is one more example.

The Jiangnan region, the lower Yangzi valley, was wealthy and cultured. Its families could afford tutors and books, so its candidates were the best prepared. The textbook says the Ming made a more concerted effort than the Song to keep Jiangnan from dominating the results (Ebrey, p. 235).

In 1397 everyone who passed the palace examination was a southerner. Taizu responded by executing two of the examiners. He then had everyone retested, and this time passed only northerners (pp. 235–236). The chief examiner, the elderly Liu Sanwu, was spared and sent into exile; his colleagues were put to death (historian John Langlois).

Q7 story · The provinces · after 1397

Regional Quotas Kept One Region from Winning Every Place

After 1397, examiners took a candidate's home region into account.
Each province had a quota for how many candidates it could send on to the capital.
After 1427, northern candidates were guaranteed 35 percent of the places.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 235–236. Map: Wikimedia Commons.

After 1397 the court built balance into the rules. Thereafter, the examiners considered regional origin and tried for a balance (Ebrey, p. 236).

The main tool was the quota. Each province had a set number of candidates it could send on to the capital examinations (p. 235). A brilliant man from crowded Jiangnan competed against his neighbors for a limited number of places, while a man from a poorer province had a fairer chance.

After 1427, northern candidates were guaranteed 35 percent of the places at the top.

The map is real, though its labels are in French. Find Jiangnan, the area around Zhili Sud, or "Southern Metropolitan Region," and Zhejiang, then compare it with the much larger north.

Why this matters: quotas answer the second half of Review Question 7 directly.

Q8 story · Jiangnan and Beijing · 1472–1499

Wang Yangming's Father Placed First, and Wang Failed Twice

Wang Yangming was born in 1472 into a literati family in the Jiangnan region.
When he was ten, his father placed first in the exams, and the family moved to Beijing.
Wang failed the jinshi exam twice before passing in 1499 (recap: slide c, zhu-xi-orthodoxy ↩).

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 237–238. Portrait: Shanghai Museum record via Wikimedia Commons.

Now follow one life through this world. The textbook says the exams did not stifle thought as much as one might expect, and that in the sixteenth century Confucian thought moved in new directions, led by a remarkable official and teacher, Wang Yangming (Ebrey, p. 237).

Wang was born in 1472 in the Jiangnan region, into a literati family that had not produced an official in a century. When he was ten, his father passed the examinations in first place, which guaranteed him a prominent career in the capital. The boy went with him to Beijing, where he met court officials, poets and thinkers, and took up military strategy, horsemanship and archery.

Like so many others, he failed the jinshi exam the first two times, and passed in 1499 (p. 238). Everything he studied for it was Zhu Xi's reading of the classics; the recap link takes you back to slide c.

Q8 story · Beijing to Guizhou · 1506–1508

Wang Defended Two Officials, Was Beaten, and Was Exiled to Guizhou

In the early 1500s the eunuch Liu Jin dominated the court.
Wang defended two officials who had criticized Liu, and he was imprisoned and severely beaten.
He was then sent to a lowly post in Guizhou, in the far southwest, where he tried to understand the Miao people.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 238–239.

Wang's first term in office was cut short by illness, and back home in Zhejiang he grew more interested in philosophy. When he returned to the capital in 1504, the court was in the grip of the eunuch Liu Jin, whom the textbook calls a "eunuch dictator." Recall from Lecture 1 how much power favorite eunuchs could gain (Ebrey, p. 238).

Wang defended two officials who had sent memorials condemning Liu. For that he was arrested, sent to the eunuch-controlled secret service prison, and severely beaten.

On his release he was given a banishment post in an aboriginal region of Guizhou province, in the far southwest. He took the post seriously anyway, doing his best to understand the problems of the Miao people there (pp. 238–239).

The memorial that ruined him came early in 1506, and he reached his post at Longchang in Guizhou in the spring of 1508. Those are the years shown at the top of the slide (historian Wing-tsit Chan).

Q8 story · Guizhou · early 1500s

In Guizhou Wang Decided That Moral Truth Was Already in Every Mind

Zhu Xi had taught that people learn moral principles by carefully studying things and books.
Wang came to believe that these principles already existed in every person's mind, hidden by selfish desires.
He wrote that if even Confucius's words seemed wrong when tested in the mind, he would not accept them.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 239; quotation from Wing-tsit Chan, tr., Instructions for Practical Living (1963), p. 81, quoted in Ebrey, p. 239.

It was in Guizhou that Wang had his spiritual and intellectual breakthrough (Ebrey, p. 239).

He had long struggled with Zhu Xi's idea of "the extension of knowledge": gaining understanding through careful, rational investigation of things and events, usually by studying the classics and other books.

Wang came to realize that universal principles already existed in every person's mind. People could find them by clearing away obstructions such as selfish desires and letting their inborn knowledge surface. The teachings of others, even Confucius and Mencius, were only aids, not the source of truth.

The textbook quotes him, in Wing-tsit Chan's translation: "If words are examined in the mind and found to be wrong, although they have come from the mouth of Confucius, I dare not accept them as correct."

Q8 story · Village and lecture hall · 1500s

Wang Taught That Ordinary People Could Become Sages

Because every mind had the same capacity, Wang said common people could become sages too.
Books and teachers, even Confucius and Mencius, were only aids, not the source of truth.
Critics charged that his ideas were tainted by Chan (Zen) Buddhism.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 239.

Follow Wang's idea one step further. If the truth is already inside every mind, then the scholar who has spent his life over the classics has no monopoly on it. Because everyone has a mind of similar capacity, the textbook says, common people have just as much potential to become sages as lifelong scholars (Ebrey, p. 239).

Wang wanted his followers to concentrate on basic moral truths that everyone could understand. He once said that what was truly heterodox, meaning wrong teaching, was not Buddhism but ideas that average people could not understand.

That made him enemies. Critics saw his teachings as dangerously contaminated with Chan Buddhist ideas, the meditative school known in Japan as Zen.

Q8 story · A magistrate's office · 1500s

Wang Taught That Knowing What Is Right Means Doing It

Wang refused to separate knowing from acting.
He said no one truly understands filial piety without practicing it, just as no one knows pain without feeling it.
He told a busy official that real learning could be found in record-keeping and legal cases.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 239; quotation from Patricia Buckley Ebrey, ed., Chinese Civilization: A Sourcebook (1993), p. 258, quoted in Ebrey, p. 239.

Wang's second big idea joins thinking to doing. He argued against separating knowledge and action. Moral action, he said, flows naturally from true understanding (Ebrey, p. 239).

His example: one does not truly understand filial piety, devotion to one's parents, if one does not practice it, any more than one understands taste, smell or pain without experiencing them. True knowledge compels action.

So Wang believed people could pursue sagehood in the middle of everyday life. When an official complained that his duties left no time for study, Wang told him not to abandon his work: "Real learning can be found in every aspect of record-keeping and legal cases. What is empty is study that is detached from things."

Q8 story · Southern Jiangxi to Guangxi · 1510–1529

Wang Lived His Ideas as a Governor and General

Conservative officials sent him away from the capital to govern a troubled military district in southern Jiangxi.
There he led armies, captured a rebel prince, and set up community compacts for local people.
He died in 1529 on his way home from campaigns in Guangxi.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 239–241.

Wang lived up to his own ideal of joining knowledge and action. Even with dozens of disciples, he did not give up his career. After Liu Jin was executed in 1510, Wang took high posts in Nanjing and Beijing while lecturing to a growing circle of students (Ebrey, pp. 238–239).

Conservative officials, disturbed by his message, got him out of the capital by having him named governor of a special military district in southern Jiangxi, an area with many non-Chinese peoples. There he had to lead the regional armies and set up a governing structure for the local people. When a prince rebelled nearby, it was Wang who led troops to capture him (p. 239).

Later in the lecture you will see that the organizations he set up to pacify rebel areas were called community compacts (pp. 240–241). Called out of retirement in 1527 to put down uprisings in Guangxi, he died on his way home in 1529.

Q8 story · The salt coast · 1500s

A Salt Worker's Son Took Wang's Ideas to the Crowds

Some of Wang's followers questioned old rankings, such as placing scholars above farmers.
Wang Gen, from a family of salt workers, gave lectures to crowds of ordinary people.
He spoke about problems in their lives and urged them to seek education to improve their lot.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 239–240.

In the century after Wang Yangming's death, his followers carried his ideas in many directions. Some took a new interest in Daoism and Buddhism. Some questioned the traditional social hierarchy, such as ranking the scholar above the farmer (Ebrey, p. 239).

One of his most enthusiastic followers, Wang Gen, came from a commoner family of salt workers. He gave lectures to crowds of ordinary people. He focused on issues important in their lives and encouraged them to pursue education to improve their lot (p. 240).

Notice how this fits Wang Yangming's teaching. If every mind can know the good, then a salt worker's son can teach it, and farmers and laborers are worth teaching.

Q8 story · Late Ming · late 1500s

Two Later Followers Attacked Old Conventions and Died in Prison

He Xinyin called the family selfish and said loyalty to friends ranked above loyalty to family.
Li Zhi defended feelings and passion and argued that women were men's intellectual equals and deserved fuller educations.
Both men were arrested for spreading dangerous ideas and died in prison.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 240.

In the next generation, some of Wang's followers went much further. He Xinyin challenged the age-old elevation of the family. To him the family was a restrictive, selfish and exclusive institution, and loyalty to family was inferior to loyalty to friends (Ebrey, p. 240).

His contemporary Li Zhi championed the validity of feelings and passion and ridiculed conforming to conventional behavior. He argued that women were the intellectual equals of men and should be given fuller educations. He even reinterpreted history to present some great villains as heroes.

The textbook stresses that only a small minority of late Ming literati were ready to hear these messages. Both He Xinyin and Li Zhi were arrested on charges of spreading dangerous ideas, and both died in prison.

Q9 story · Market towns everywhere · 1368–1644

China's Population More Than Doubled Under the Ming

China grew from about 60–80 million people to about 150–200 million.

Q9 story · Yangzi delta, Fujian, Jiangxi · late 1500s

Regions Began to Specialize in Crops and Crafts for Sale

Q9 story · Every village · from 1381

Taizu Made Better-Off Villagers Collect Taxes Without Pay

Taizu wanted to protect villagers from the greedy tax collectors his family had feared (recap: Lecture 1, slide h, hongwu-registers ↩).
Under the lijia system, better-off families in each village did police, legal, and tax-collecting work without pay.
Villagers themselves assessed, collected, and delivered the taxes, which were paid mostly in grain.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 240.

How was this growing countryside governed? Go back to the founder. Taizu had grown up in a family that lived in fear of greedy tax collectors, and he redesigned village tax collection so that future families would not suffer as his had (Ebrey, p. 240). Lecture 1 showed his registers of people and land; the recap link takes you there.

In each village, the better-off families were identified and assigned to perform low-level judicial, police and tax-collecting services without pay. This was the lijia system, set up across the empire in 1381.

In other words, villagers themselves, not the magistrate's underlings, were responsible for assessing, collecting and transporting taxes, which were paid mostly in grain.

Q9 story · The countryside · 1400s–1500s

The Unpaid System Created the Abuses Taizu Had Wanted to Prevent

Local officials had so little legal income that they had to collect illegal fees to keep services running.
Many households were ruined by unpaid duties, such as delivering taxes or running hostels for traveling officials.
The government finally converted most of these duties into a money tax.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 240.

Taizu's plan backfired. The textbook says his effort to run government on unpaid service created many headaches for later administrators (Ebrey, p. 240).

Local officials found that their legal sources of revenue were so limited that they had no choice but to collect extralegal fees, outside the law, just to keep basic services going. That led to exactly the kind of abuse Taizu had wanted to prevent.

Ordinary households, for their part, were often devastated by unpaid responsibilities, such as delivering the taxes or keeping up local hostels for government travelers. Picture a family hauling the village's tax grain over long roads at its own expense.

Eventually reforms had to convert most of these obligations into a money tax.

Q9 story · South China · 1400s–1600s

Relatives Formed Lineages Around a Shared Ancestor

A lineage was a group of relatives descended from one ancestor who agreed to cooperate.
Lineages often owned land together to pay for ancestral rites, and sometimes for an ancestral hall or school.
Lineages were more common in the south than in the north, perhaps because of migration patterns.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 240. Hall: Wikimedia Commons.

Rules from the capital were supposed to be the same everywhere, but local society varied from place to place, depending on the crops grown, whether there were large non-Han populations, when migrants had arrived, and so on (Ebrey, p. 240).

In the Ming, voluntary associations that included both educated men and ordinary villagers became common. Some were religious associations that supported a temple. Others were lineages, formed to promote cooperation among relatives descended from a common ancestor.

A lineage often held land in common, and the income paid for joint ancestral rites. When there was enough income, a lineage might build an ancestral temple or a school. Lineages were generally more common in the south than in the north, perhaps reflecting migration patterns.

The photo is real. This is the hall of the Luo lineage in Chengkan, a Huizhou village. Chinese Wikipedia says it was begun in the Jiajing reign (1522–1566) and finished under Wanli, so it was built by a Ming lineage.

Q9 story · Fujian, Huizhou, Jiangxi, Guangdong

Lineages Grew for Different Reasons in Different Places

In Fujian, large lineages dated back to Song times, and in Huizhou they grew strong on merchant wealth.
In Jiangxi, educated men formed lineages to raise their status, while in Guangdong most took shape from the 1500s on.
Some lineages were started by educated men, and others by ordinary people who saw benefits in banding together.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 240.

Here is the most important point about lineages: there was no single cause. The textbook walks through four regions (Ebrey, p. 240).

In Fujian, large lineages were already visible in Song times and stayed prominent through the Ming. In Huizhou, in Anhui province, lineages flourished in the mid-Ming, their strength owing much to the wealth of local merchant families, who paid for ancestral halls, lineage schools and their sons' study for the exams.

In one county of Jiangxi, most lineages were deliberately formed in the early Ming by educated men, to raise their own status at a time of intense competition for prestige. The county is Taihe. In Guangdong, by contrast, the great lineages of the Pearl River delta took shape from the sixteenth century on, as families registered land, reclaimed fields from the river sands and adopted state-approved rites.

Two details differ from our copy of the textbook (p. 240). It reads "Taichang" for the Jiangxi county, which is Taihe, and it dates most Guangdong lineages to "the seventeenth century or later," while the historian David Faure dates the delta lineages from the sixteenth century.

In some areas the educated class started lineages; in others, ordinary people saw advantages in banding together. The diagram is a schematic made for this deck, not a map to scale.

Q9 story · Village shrines · 1500s

Community Compacts Bound Villagers to Help and Correct One Another

Compact members agreed to correct each other's faults, help those in need, and expel anyone who would not cooperate.
Zhu Xi had praised compacts, and Wang Yangming used them to restore order in rebel areas.
Some lineages adopted similar written rules to discipline members and settle disputes.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, pp. 240–241.

Lineages were one way villagers organized; community compacts were another. Members of a compact agreed to correct each other's faults, offer help to those in need, and expel anyone who failed to cooperate (Ebrey, p. 240).

Notice who supported the idea. Zhu Xi had praised compacts, believing they could promote moral renewal. Wang Yangming used the name community compact for the organizations he set up in his program to pacify rebel areas, which you saw on slide t wang-official (pp. 240–241). Here, for once, the orthodox thinker and his challenger agreed.

The two forms blended. By the mid-Ming, lineages in some regions had set up systems to discipline and control members, with long lists of rules and ways to handle disputes, much like compacts.

Q9 story · Town and country · late 1500s

Scholars Feared That Society's Moral Fabric Was Unraveling

Many complained that rich and poor no longer helped each other but saw each other as enemies.
Wang's followers urged villagers to form compacts and strive for goodness together.
Some educated men turned to charity, such as repairing roads and bridges or helping pay for funerals.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 241.

Why did Confucian scholars push for these voluntary groups? The textbook names a common fear: that the moral fabric of society was unraveling. Many complained that the rich and the poor no longer helped each other but looked on each other as enemies (Ebrey, p. 241).

Think about the slides you have just seen: a doubling population, cash crops and market towns, and a tax system that ruined some households. It is not hard to see why people felt the old village bonds were fraying.

Wang Yangming's followers made even broader use of the compact, urging villagers to form compacts in which everyone encouraged each other to strive for goodness. Some educated men turned to charitable works to ease social tensions, such as repairing roads and bridges or helping families pay for funerals.

Who led these efforts? Usually the gentry: degree-holders and retired officials living at home, who ran lineages, schools and charities and spoke for their counties to the magistrate. Rich merchants pressed into this group, blurring the old ranking of scholar, farmer, artisan and merchant; Lecture 4's Pierian Spring follows them.

Pierian Spring · A woman doctor's casebook · c. 1511

PS: Tan Yunxian Recorded Thirty-One Patients, and Often Diagnosed Overwork

At fifty she wrote Sayings of a Female Doctor, describing how her grandmother taught her.
She used herbal medicines and moxibustion, which required touching the patient.
She blamed working women's illnesses on overwork, and upper-class women's on hidden emotions.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 238 (Biography: Tan Yunxian, Woman Doctor), based on Charlotte Furth, A Flourishing Yin (1999).

At about fifty, Tan Yunxian wrote an autobiographical account, Sayings of a Female Doctor. She described memorizing the classic medical texts under her grandmother's guidance, treating her own children first, and inheriting her grandmother's notebook of prescriptions (Ebrey, p. 238).

Her book records thirty-one patients, all women, most with chronic complaints. Like other literati physicians she prescribed herbal medicines, and she also practiced moxibustion, burning dried artemisia at points on the body. Because it required touching the patient, male physicians could not perform it on women.

She often traced working women's problems to overwork: a woman who made bricks and tiles at a kiln with her husband, a boatman's wife who worked in wind and rain. When patients came from upper-class families, she saw hidden negative emotions as the root of the trouble.

Pierian Spring · Jiangxi and Guangxi · 1510s–1529

PS: Wang Yangming the General, from Jiangxi to Guangxi

Wang governed a district with many non-Chinese peoples.
He captured a rebel prince.
In 1527 he was recalled to put down uprisings in Guangxi.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 239.

The textbook gives Wang's military career only a few lines, but they are striking for a philosopher (Ebrey, p. 239).

His district in southern Jiangxi was a special military district with many non-Chinese ethnic groups. He had to lead the regional armies into battle and set up a governing structure for the local people, and he proved a successful commander. When a prince rebelled nearby, Wang led the troops that captured him.

On his way back to the capital his father died, and custom required him to retire from office to mourn. In 1527 he was called out of retirement because of uprisings among the non-Chinese in Guangxi. He spent a year there directing campaigns, winning victories and negotiating surrenders, but his health was failing, and he died on his way home in 1529.

Pierian Spring · A late-Ming town · c. 1600

PS: The Society for Sharing Goodness Paid Monthly Dues for Good Works

Around 1600 one man founded a society whose members paid monthly dues.
The money repaired roads and bridges.
It also helped families who could not afford funerals.

Sources: Ebrey, Ch. 14, p. 241.

The textbook gives one concrete example of late-Ming charity (Ebrey, p. 241).

At the end of the sixteenth century, one man set up the Society for Sharing Goodness. Its members paid monthly dues, and the money supported projects such as repairing roads and bridges, or helping families who could not cover funeral expenses.

Think about why funerals mattered so much. Proper rites for parents were at the heart of filial piety, so a family unable to bury its dead properly faced shame as well as grief.

Notice too how this society worked like a lineage or a compact: a voluntary group with rules, shared money and a moral purpose. The textbook presents such charity as one way educated men tried to lessen the social tensions they feared.

Orthodox

Orthodox means officially approved as the correct teaching. In the Ming, Zhu Xi's interpretations of the Four Books were the standard for the civil service examinations (Ebrey, pp. 234, 237).

Quota

A quota is a fixed number. Each Ming province could send only a set number of candidates on to the capital examinations, and after 1427 northerners were guaranteed 35 percent of the places (Ebrey, pp. 235–236).

Chan Buddhism

Chan (Zen in Japanese) is a school of Buddhism that stresses meditation and sudden insight into one's own mind rather than study of texts. Wang's critics thought his focus on the mind sounded too much like Chan (Ebrey, p. 239).

Community compact

A community compact was a voluntary village agreement. Members promised to correct each other's faults, help those in need, and expel anyone who would not cooperate. Wang used compacts in his program to pacify rebel areas (Ebrey, pp. 240–241). Slide dd returns to them.

Cash crops

Cash crops are grown to be sold for money rather than eaten by the farm family, such as cotton, tobacco and sugar cane (Ebrey, p. 240).

Lijia

The lijia (lee-jya) system was Taizu's plan for village administration. Better-off families did unpaid police, legal and tax-collecting work, so villagers, not officials' underlings, collected the taxes (Ebrey, p. 240).

Lineage

A lineage was a group of relatives descended from one ancestor and organized to cooperate: holding land in common, performing ancestral rites, and sometimes running a school (Ebrey, p. 240).

Moxibustion

Moxibustion means burning moxa (dried artemisia) at chosen points on the body. The theory was similar to acupuncture: it stimulated the circulation of qi (Ebrey, p. 238).

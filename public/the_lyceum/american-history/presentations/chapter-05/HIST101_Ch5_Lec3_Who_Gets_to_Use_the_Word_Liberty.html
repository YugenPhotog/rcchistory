Charleston, 1765: Two Parades

The first march

  • The Sons of Liberty fill the streets
  • Chanting: "Liberty and No Stamps"

The second march

  • Enslaved people stage a procession of their own
  • Shouting: "Liberty! Liberty!"
Merchant Henry Laurens tried to convince himself they probably did not understand the meaning of the word.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two parades in one city in one year, both shouting the same word. The Sons of Liberty marched through Charleston chanting against the Stamp Act. Shortly afterward, enslaved people in the same city staged a procession of their own, calling out "Liberty!" And a wealthy merchant spent considerable energy convincing himself they could not possibly have meant what he meant. That gap — between the word as elites used it and the word as everyone else heard it — is the entire lecture.

Breaking It Down

The first march: Charleston's Sons of Liberty demonstrating against the Stamp Act, using the standard vocabulary of colonial resistance — liberty, rights, no taxation without consent.

The second march: Enslaved people in Charleston, having watched the first, staged their own procession using the same word.

Henry Laurens's reaction: Laurens was a Charleston merchant deeply involved in the slave trade, and a patriot. He insisted the marchers had simply misunderstood the slogan — that they were parroting a sound without grasping its meaning.

Why his reaction is the evidence: Notice that Laurens had to work at this interpretation. The alternative explanation was obvious and immediately available. His insistence tells you he perceived the threat clearly and needed a way to dismiss it.

Why This Matters

This is not a colorful anecdote decorating a lecture about something else. It is the lecture's central problem in miniature: once a political word is loose in public, the people who popularized it cannot control who picks it up or where they point it. Hold the Charleston scene in mind — the last third of class returns to it and asks you to judge Laurens's claim.

Breaking It Down

Consider what each answer actually commits you to.

If you say yes, they misunderstood: you are claiming the word had a fixed, technical meaning — a constitutional relationship among British subjects concerning taxation and representation — and that applying it to chattel slavery was a category error. There is a real argument here: the patriots' liberty was a specific legal claim, not a general moral principle.

If you say no, they understood perfectly: you are claiming the word carried a plain meaning that any listener could hear, and that the marchers applied it to the most obvious case of unfreedom in the city. This too has real support — including from the discomfort visible in Laurens's own reaction.

What to watch for: as the lecture proceeds, notice how many different groups make the same move the marchers made. If the marchers "misunderstood," so did an awful lot of people.

Why This Matters

Hold your vote. This is not a warm-up question that gets abandoned — the lecture's resolution answers it directly, and your answer will be more interesting to you if you have staked something on it first.

The Essential Question

Who possessed the authority to define liberty — and to decide whose claims counted?

Today's key idea: liberty worked as a political grammar, not a fixed program. It supplied terms for grievance — rights of Englishmen, consent, property, resistance to arbitrary power — without prescribing one social settlement.

The language was contagious precisely because it was general enough to travel.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the essential question and the key concept behind it. Notice how the question is framed: not "what did liberty mean," but who possessed the authority to define it and to decide whose claims counted. That shifts the inquiry from vocabulary to power.

Breaking It Down

Liberty as grammar, not program: A grammar gives you rules for constructing sentences — it does not tell you what to say. Revolutionary liberty worked the same way. It supplied terms and relationships (consent, arbitrary power, rights, slavery as the opposite of freedom) through which very different groups could articulate very different grievances. It did not prescribe a single social settlement.

The rights of Englishmen: inherited, historical rights of English subjects — consent to taxation, trial by jury, security of property, protection from arbitrary power. This language could be quite conservative, because it defended established provincial practice against innovation.

The radical turn: it could also become radical the moment a speaker moved from historical rights held by Englishmen to natural rights possessed by all human beings. That move was neither automatic nor complete — but once available, it could be made by anyone.

Why This Matters

The bottom line on the slide is the crucial one: the language was contagious because it was general enough to travel. Elites did not choose a portable vocabulary by mistake. They needed language broad enough to unite thirteen colonies with different economies, religions, and interests. That same breadth is exactly what let tenants, artisans, women, enslaved people, and Native nations pick it up and aim it in directions no patriot leader intended.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Bernard Bailyn – The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution (1967)

Bailyn demonstrated that colonial political language was coherent and deeply felt rather than mere rhetorical cover for economic interest. Colonists had inherited an opposition tradition that taught them to read standing armies, appointed officeholders, taxes, and patronage as connected threats to liberty. His work remains the necessary starting point — and this lecture's project is to ask what happened when that powerful language reached people Bailyn's writers never had in mind.

Liberty from Parliament — or from the Crowd?

Patriot liberty

  • Resistance to arbitrary power

Loyalist & neutral liberty

  • Security of conscience, speech, property, safety

📖 Understanding This Slide

First claim, and it is the one that most directly upsets a familiar picture. We tend to imagine patriots defending liberty against loyalists defending authority. But loyalists and neutrals used the word liberty too — and they used it against patriot committees.

Breaking It Down

Patriot liberty: consent to taxation, jury trial, security of property, resistance to arbitrary power. The vocabulary you met in Lecture 2.

Loyalist and neutral liberty: security of conscience, speech, property, and personal safety — against committees that published names, demanded public recantations, regulated trade, and pressured inhabitants to sign associations. From the patriot side, that discipline defended a community under attack. From the receiving end, it could look exactly like the arbitrary power patriots condemned.

Why allegiance was fluid: religion, ethnicity, family ties, trade relationships, local rivalries, military occupation, and plain calculations of safety all shaped political choice. Many people opposed particular parliamentary measures while rejecting independence. Others feared imperial reform but feared their neighbors' committees more.

The numbers warning: you may have encountered confident percentages — a third patriot, a third loyalist, a third neutral. Treat such figures with caution. They imply that allegiance was stable, measurable, and mutually exclusive, and it was none of those things.

Why This Matters

Recognizing loyalists and neutrals converts the Revolution from a unanimous national awakening into a civil war whose categories hardened over time. That is not a minor adjustment. It changes what independence cost, who paid, and how we should read the coercion the last lecture examined.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Travis Glasson – Nobody Men (2025)

Glasson follows a family that tried to avoid the patriot-loyalist choice altogether, and argues that neutrality was an active, difficult, and consequential position rather than mere indifference. His book is a timely warning against repeating tidy allegiance percentages as if people had filled out a form and stuck with their answer.

Same Taxes. No Revolution. Why?

Jamaica & Barbados
Enslaved majorities left white colonists dependent on British troops
Quebec
Catholic population received protections under the Quebec Act
Nova Scotia
Naval presence and recent settlement limited insurgent organization

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is a comparison that should stop you short. Britain had many colonies. Only thirteen rebelled. If taxation and imperial regulation caused the Revolution, why did colonies experiencing the same policies stay put? The answer reveals that structure — not grievance — determined what liberty could mean in each place.

Breaking It Down

Jamaica and Barbados: These were enormously wealthy sugar colonies with white colonists who had every economic reason to resent imperial taxation. They also had enslaved majorities — in some places overwhelming ones — and white planters depended on British troops for security against the people they enslaved. Rebellion would have meant removing the force that protected their position.

Quebec: Recently conquered, with a large Catholic French-speaking population that received significant protections under the Quebec Act of 1774. British rule had just delivered something valuable; the calculation looked different there.

Nova Scotia: A substantial naval presence and recent, scattered settlement patterns limited the capacity to organize insurgency, whatever individuals may have thought.

Why This Matters

The conclusion is worth writing down: tax grievances did not automatically produce revolution. What mattered was the local structure — demography, military dependence, economic position, and political organization. This is a powerful analytical habit. When something happens in one place and not another, look for the structural difference rather than assuming the people were simply more freedom-loving.

It also delivers an uncomfortable insight for this lecture specifically: in the Caribbean, the presence of enslaved majorities made white colonists less likely to demand liberty, not more. The relationship between slavery and revolutionary politics was tangled in ways that resist any simple story.

Liberty Points Sideways

  • Artisans vs. gentlemen: public virtue and sacrifice — not inherited gentility — entitle a man to speak for the community
  • Tenants vs. landlords: Hudson Valley tenants turn "arbitrary power" against manorial lords; settlers defy New York proprietors in the Green Mountains
  • Regulators vs. courthouse elites: backcountry protest against fees, corruption, underrepresentation
Alamance, 1771: colonial militia crushed the North Carolina Regulators — and some leaders who suppressed them later fought Britain. Patriotism was not the same thing as democracy.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Second claim. So far liberty has pointed across the Atlantic at Parliament. Now watch it swing sideways — aimed by colonists at other colonists. Landlords, courthouse officials, and wealthy gentlemen discovered that the language they were using against London worked just as well against them.

Breaking It Down

Artisans against gentlemen: Urban resistance depended on artisans, sailors, laborers, apprentices, and small shopkeepers. Having supplied the crowds, they could argue that public virtue and sacrifice — not inherited gentility — entitled a man to speak for the community. That is a direct challenge to deference, the assumption that ordinary people should defer to their social betters in politics.

Tenants against landlords: In the Hudson Valley, tenants on great manorial estates turned the vocabulary of arbitrary power against their landlords. In the territory that became Vermont, settlers defied New York proprietors claiming their land.

Regulators against courthouse elites: Backcountry protests in the Carolinas against fees, taxes, corrupt court practices, and underrepresentation. Crucially, many of these conflicts predated the imperial crisis. The crisis did not create them; it gave them a broader vocabulary and sometimes weakened the authorities that had contained them.

Alamance, 1771: Colonial militia defeated the North Carolina Regulators in battle. Several men who helped suppress them later opposed Britain in the name of liberty.

Why This Matters

That last fact is the one to sit with. Patriotism was not the same thing as democracy. Colonial elites could condemn metropolitan power over them while using armed force against local challengers beneath them. If you have absorbed the idea that the Revolution was a straightforward expansion of freedom, Alamance is the counterexample that requires you to be more precise about whose freedom, and against whom.

Participation Without Citizenship

  • Nonimportation could not function without women's labor and cooperation
  • Tea, textiles, mourning dress, household production — all politically charged
  • Public spinning gatherings made women visible as political actors
  • Yet patriot rhetoric praised them for self-sacrifice within traditional roles — and the new states did not grant equal rights

An opening in political practice — not a completed emancipation.

London's satire of the Edenton signers, 1775 — mockery is itself evidence of how threatening women's public politics appeared

📖 Understanding This Slide

Third claim. Lecture 2 showed you that nonimportation moved politics into the household. This slide asks the obvious follow-up: if the boycott could not function without women's labor and decisions, what does that make them? The answer is genuinely complicated — political actors without political rights.

Breaking It Down

The dependency: Nonimportation required decisions about tea, textiles, clothing, mourning dress, and household production — and it required the labor of making substitutes. Women made those decisions and did that work. Without their cooperation the strategy simply fails.

Public visibility: Spinning gatherings turned domestic labor into public political demonstration. Women appeared in the newspapers as participants in the cause.

The rhetorical containment: patriot writers praised women in terms that emphasized self-sacrifice within traditional roles — the virtuous wife supporting the cause, not the citizen exercising judgment. The praise contained the implication.

The outcome: The new states did not generally establish equal political rights for women. Revolutionary mobilization demonstrated women's political competence and did not translate into citizenship.

About the image: This is a London satire of the women of Edenton, North Carolina, who signed a public pledge to boycott British tea. Look at how it works — the women are ridiculous, a neglected child crawls under the table, a dog misbehaves. The message is that women in politics produce domestic chaos.

Why This Matters

Read the satire as evidence rather than as decoration. Nobody bothers to mock what nobody finds threatening. The energy of the ridicule measures how unsettling women's public political action appeared — which tells you that something real was happening, even though it did not end in formal rights. The phrase on the slide captures it: an opening in political practice, not a completed emancipation.

Phillis Wheatley Claims the Word

  • Enslaved in Boston; published poet; celebrated in London in 1773
  • Her antislavery argument was not borrowed from white patriots
  • She joined biblical deliverance, natural rights, and personal experience
  • An active author who pursued publication and freedom — not a passive prodigy

Read her own words on the next slide — slowly.

The 1773 frontispiece, attributed to the enslaved Black artist Scipio Moorhead — a portrait of authorship, made to certify it

📖 Understanding This Slide

Fourth claim, and here the lecture slows down for one person. Phillis Wheatley was enslaved in Boston, published a book of poetry in London in 1773, and made an antislavery argument that was entirely her own. She is not an illustration of a point. She is a thinker who took the century's most powerful political vocabulary and turned it.

Breaking It Down

Her situation: Wheatley was kidnapped from West Africa as a child, enslaved in the Wheatley household in Boston, educated there, and became a published poet whose work was celebrated in London. Her circumstances were exceptional, and she knew it.

Not borrowed: This is the crucial correction. Her antislavery argument was not simply patriot rhetoric repeated back. She joined biblical deliverance, natural rights, and personal experience into an argument with its own structure and its own authority.

An active author: She pursued publication across an ocean, secured a patron, managed the certification of her authorship, and obtained her freedom. Recovering her as a strategist of her own career — rather than a prodigy displayed by others — is one of the major achievements of recent scholarship.

About the portrait: The 1773 frontispiece, attributed to the enslaved Black artist Scipio Moorhead, shows her seated at a writing desk, quill in hand, mid-thought. It exists because readers had to be persuaded that a Black woman had written the poems. The image is a certificate of authorship — which tells you a great deal about the world she was publishing into.

Why This Matters

Next slide, you read her own words closely. Come to them expecting an argument, not a sentiment.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Vincent Carretta – Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (2011)

Carretta's documentary biography reconstructs Wheatley's authorship, transatlantic publication, manumission, and political voice with close attention to surviving evidence. His most valuable contribution for this lecture is the recovery of her agency — the deliberate work of a writer building a career and pursuing freedom under conditions designed to make both impossible.

Read It as She Built It

"In every human Breast, God has implanted a Principle, which we call Love of Freedom; it is impatient of Oppression, and pants for Deliverance; and by the Leave of our modern Egyptians I will assert, that the same Principle lives in us."
— Phillis Wheatley to Samson Occom, February 11, 1774
Work the passage: Who are the "modern Egyptians"? What does every human Breast do to the patriots' own argument? What authority is she claiming by writing "I will assert"?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Slow down here. This passage is short, and every clause is doing structural work. Wheatley is not expressing a feeling about slavery — she is constructing an argument that traps her readers, and she builds it in three moves.

Breaking It Down

Move one — universality: "In every human Breast, God has implanted a Principle, which we call Love of Freedom." Not in every Englishman. Not in every subject. In every human breast, placed there by God. She has established a premise no Christian patriot could deny without abandoning his own argument, since patriot liberty also rested on rights that came from the Creator rather than from government.

Move two — the naming: "our modern Egyptians." This is the sharp edge. In the Exodus story, the Egyptians are the enslavers whom God judges and whose captives God delivers. Patriots constantly compared themselves to Israel escaping bondage. Wheatley accepts the biblical frame and reassigns the roles: in America, the enslavers are her readers.

Move three — the assertion: "I will assert." Not "I hope" or "I humbly suggest." An enslaved woman claims the standing to state a truth about human nature and to correct the people who considered themselves her superiors. The verb is a claim to interpretive authority.

"By the Leave of": note the deceptive politeness. She asks permission from the very people she is indicting — a formal courtesy that makes the indictment land harder.

Why This Matters

Wheatley did more than accuse individuals of hypocrisy. She universalized the love of freedom and claimed the authority to define revolutionary language herself. This is why "contagion" is the right metaphor for the lecture: a principle proclaimed against Parliament became, in her hands, a claim against masters — using the patriots' own premises, their own scripture, and their own logic.

From Argument to Opportunity

  • Boston-area petitions, 1773–74: enslaved people join natural rights and Christian language to demand freedom
  • November 1775: Dunmore's proclamation offers freedom to enslaved men of rebel masters who join British forces
  • Thousands would later pursue freedom through British lines; others served patriot forces or used wartime disruption to escape
State it precisely: Dunmore's offer was limited, military, and self-interested. It did not abolish slavery and did not apply to people enslaved by loyalists. Freedom entered the war as a weapon, not a principle.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Wheatley made the argument. This slide shows what happened when argument met opportunity — when the disruption of war turned a rhetorical claim into a practical possibility. It also requires the most careful language in the lecture.

Breaking It Down

The Boston-area petitions (1773–74): Enslaved people submitted formal petitions for freedom to the Massachusetts legislature, joining natural rights language, Christian argument, and direct demands. Read the timing: these arrive precisely as white colonists are most loudly denouncing their own "enslavement" by Parliament. The petitioners were listening, and they answered.

Dunmore's Proclamation, November 1775: Virginia's royal governor offered freedom to enslaved men belonging to rebels who would join British forces.

The limits, stated precisely: It was limited (only enslaved men, only those able to reach British lines, only those belonging to rebels), military (a manpower measure in a war), and self-interested. It did not abolish slavery. It did not apply to people enslaved by loyalists. Overstating it is a common error and a serious one.

What people did anyway: Thousands later pursued freedom through British lines. Others served patriot forces. Others used wartime disruption to escape entirely. Enslaved people made their own strategic assessments about which path might lead to freedom — that is the pattern to notice, not the generosity of any proclamation.

Why This Matters

The caution box states it exactly: freedom entered the war as a weapon, not a principle. Neither side was pursuing abolition. Both understood that enslaved people wanted freedom badly enough to be strategically significant — and enslaved people understood that a war between white factions created openings. Remember Dunmore; he returns in Lecture 4, where the Declaration of Independence names him without naming him.

When Liberty Means Invasion

  • Colonial liberty often included the freedom to settle western land
  • From an Indigenous vantage, that freedom meant invasion
  • The Crown — inconsistent and often violent — could still look like a possible restraint on settlers
  • Native nations weighed British, patriot, Spanish, and local threats by their own sovereignty
The patriot nation that emerged claimed the continent — making Indigenous dispossession part of the Revolution's history, not a later deviation from it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The final claim, and it points in a direction that unsettles the whole framework. For every group so far, more liberty for them meant less power for someone above them. For Native nations, colonial liberty meant something categorically different: it meant invasion.

Breaking It Down

What colonial liberty included: freedom to settle western land. This was not a fringe position — it was central to what many colonists believed they were fighting for, especially after the Proclamation Line placed London between them and the Ohio Valley. Recall Lecture 1: the settlers and speculators were one of the four expectations pulling the empire apart.

What that meant from the other side: the freedom colonists demanded was the freedom to take Indigenous homelands. There is no way to reconcile those two positions; they are the same act described by the parties on either end of it.

The strange consequence: the Crown — inconsistent, frequently violent, and never a reliable protector — could still appear as a possible restraint on settler expansion. When patriots demanded liberty from Britain, some Native nations reasonably concluded that a patriot victory would be worse for them.

Independent judgment: Native nations weighed British, patriot, Spanish, and local threats according to their own sovereignty and their own interests. They were not choosing sides in someone else's war; they were pursuing their own objectives in a conflict that had arrived at their borders.

Why This Matters

The callout makes a claim worth taking seriously: the patriot nation that emerged expanded its claims to the continent, which makes Indigenous dispossession part of the Revolution's history rather than a later deviation from its ideals. That is a strong statement, and the evidence supports it. The westward pressure did not begin after the Revolution — it was among the things the Revolution was fought about.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Kathleen DuVal – Independence Lost (2015) and Alan Taylor – American Revolutions (2016)

Both historians insist the Revolution did not present every people with the same choice. DuVal follows the Gulf Coast and interior, where Native nations, enslaved people, loyalists, and imperial rivals pursued entirely different kinds of independence. Taylor's continental synthesis makes liberty geographically uneven: in seaports it meant constitutional resistance and consumer discipline; in the backcountry it might mean access to land; for Native nations it meant settler expansion. Same word, incompatible meanings, depending on where you stood.

Back to Charleston: Your Vote

What Laurens needed

Liberty as a constitutional relationship among British subjects — a word about taxes and charters, safely fenced

What the marchers did

Applied the principle to physical bondage — the most literal oppression in the city

They did not misunderstand the word. They understood it too well — and that is exactly what Laurens could not afford to admit.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Back to Charleston, and back to your vote. Now that you have watched artisans, tenants, Regulators, women, enslaved petitioners, and Native nations all use this word in ways patriot leaders never authorized, reconsider Henry Laurens's claim.

Breaking It Down

What Laurens needed to be true: that liberty described a constitutional relationship among British subjects — a technical term about taxation, representation, and charters, safely fenced off from the institution his fortune depended on.

What the marchers actually did: they applied the principle to the most literal form of unfreedom in the city. If liberty means not being subject to arbitrary power over your person, property, and labor, then the enslaved people of Charleston had the strongest claim of anyone marching that year.

The resolution: they did not misunderstand the word. They understood it too well — and that is precisely what Laurens could not afford to admit, because admitting it would have required him to choose between his politics and his property.

Why This Matters

Notice what this does to the essential question. The lecture asked who possessed the authority to define liberty and decide whose claims counted. The Charleston episode gives you the answer in miniature: the people with power tried to define it narrowly, and could not make the definition hold. Laurens could dismiss the marchers. He could not stop them from hearing what he was saying, or from drawing the obvious conclusion.

If your vote changed during class, that is worth noticing — and worth being able to explain. What evidence moved you?

Answering the Question

The contagion of liberty was not one ideal spreading. It was a multiplication of claims — overlapping, and colliding.
  • Patriots aimed it at Parliament; loyalists at committees; artisans at deference
  • Tenants and Regulators at local elites; women through household and market politics
  • Enslaved people at masters; Native nations defended sovereignty against settler freedom

A society can proclaim a principle broadly while distributing the power to invoke it unevenly.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the synthesis, and its central word is multiplication. The phrase "contagion of liberty" can make it sound like one good idea spreading outward. What actually happened was messier and more interesting: many groups claiming the same word for incompatible purposes, at the same time.

Breaking It Down

Run the list and notice the directions. Patriots aimed liberty at Parliament. Loyalists aimed it at patriot committees. Artisans aimed it at deference and gentry leadership. Tenants and Regulators aimed it at landlords and courthouse elites. Women acted through household and market politics without gaining rights. Enslaved people aimed it at masters. Native nations defended sovereignty against settler freedom.

They collided: These are not variations on a theme. Settler liberty and Indigenous sovereignty cannot both prevail. Patriot enforcement and loyalist security of conscience are directly opposed. The claims genuinely conflicted, and the conflicts had to be settled by power rather than by argument.

Why This Matters

The durable conclusion is the line at the bottom, and it is worth memorizing: a society can proclaim a principle broadly while distributing the power to invoke it unevenly. Universal language and political inclusion are not the same thing. The Revolution produced sweeping statements about human freedom and a political settlement that excluded most of the people who had made those statements their own.

That gap is not a flaw in this lecture's story. It is the engine of American political history for the next two centuries — and you will meet it again in abolition, in Seneca Falls, in Reconstruction, and beyond.

The Question That Stays Open

Should revolutionary language be judged by the exclusions of those who first proclaimed it,
by the institutions they built,
or by the later claims that excluded people made in its name?

Next: everyone is claiming liberty while royal authority dissolves.
Who actually governs?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture closes with a genuine interpretive question rather than a settled answer — because this one is actively debated by serious historians, and you now have enough evidence to think about it.

Breaking It Down

Judge by the exclusions: This reading emphasizes what the founders actually did. They proclaimed universal principles and built a society resting on slavery, property qualifications, and male citizenship. On this view, the gap between rhetoric and practice is the most important fact, and treating the language as noble risks laundering the exclusions.

Judge by the institutions: This reading looks at what was actually constructed — the constitutions, assemblies, and legal structures that emerged. It asks what the Revolution built, not what it said.

Judge by the later claims: This reading emphasizes that the language proved usable by exactly the people it excluded. Wheatley used it in 1774. Abolitionists used it. Seneca Falls used it. Frederick Douglass used it. Martin Luther King Jr. used it. On this view, a principle that can be turned against its authors has done something that mere hypocrisy cannot explain.

Why This Matters

You are not required to pick one — but you should be able to state the strongest version of each and say what evidence would push you toward one or another. That is what historical judgment looks like, and it is more valuable than a memorized verdict.

Looking ahead: Lecture 4 changes the question entirely. Everyone is now claiming liberty, and meanwhile royal authority is dissolving on the ground. When the old government stops functioning, someone has to actually govern — collect taxes, command militia, punish enemies. Who does it, and by what right?

IS 1 · The People Who Chose Neither

  • Travis Glasson, Nobody Men (2025): the Cruger family tried to avoid the patriot–loyalist choice altogether
  • Neutrality was an active, difficult, consequential position — not indifference
  • Treating the Revolution as a civil war changes what "allegiance" even means
Payoff: the sharp patriot/loyalist binary hardened over time — it was not where most people started.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This extension recovers the people the patriot-loyalist binary erases: those who tried, often at great cost, to choose neither side.

Breaking It Down

The Cruger family: Glasson follows a merchant family with transatlantic business ties, relatives on multiple sides, and every practical reason to avoid a permanent commitment. Their story is one of constant negotiation rather than conviction.

Neutrality as action: The central argument is that neutrality was not indifference or cowardice. It required active work — managing relationships, protecting property, avoiding oaths, calculating risk with incomplete information about who would win.

The costs: Neutrals were pressured by both sides, and both sides tended to interpret refusal as covert allegiance to the enemy.

Why This Matters

Payoff: the sharp patriot/loyalist division belongs mainly to the war years and hardened through the conflict. It was not where most people started. When you meet confident percentages of colonial allegiance, remember that they impose a clarity contemporaries did not have — and that the pressure to choose was itself one of the war's coercive features.

IS 2 · Eliza Lucas Pinckney's Liberty

  • Lorri Glover, Eliza Lucas Pinckney (2020): a skilled plantation manager and political observer
  • Her authority and independence rested inside a slave society
  • Complicates any single category of "women's experience" in the Revolution
Payoff: women's expanded political action did not automatically challenge racial slavery or patriarchal law.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Today's slide on women focused on boycott participation. This extension complicates that picture with a woman whose authority was substantial, independent, and completely entangled with slavery.

Breaking It Down

Eliza Lucas Pinckney: managed South Carolina plantations from a young age, experimented with indigo cultivation that became commercially significant for the colony, and was a sharp political observer with an extensive correspondence.

Where her authority came from: her managerial competence was real — and it was exercised over enslaved people whose labor produced the crops and whose lives she directed. Her independence rested on their bondage.

What this complicates: any single category of "women's experience" in the revolutionary era. A Boston artisan's wife spinning homespun and a South Carolina plantation mistress managing an indigo operation were both women, both political actors, and occupied entirely different relationships to freedom.

Why This Matters

Payoff: women's expanded political action did not automatically challenge racial slavery or patriarchal law. Some women's opportunities were built directly on other people's captivity. This is exactly the kind of case that keeps you from reading the widening of politics as a simple story of progress — and it fits the lecture's central claim that liberty's expansion was a multiplication of competing claims, not a rising tide.

IS 3 · Loyalist Liberty in Exile

  • Maya Jasanoff, Liberty's Exiles (2011): loyalist refugees carried their own understandings of British liberty across the empire
  • Loyalism was not the absence of principle — it was a rival version of it
  • Follow where the exiles went, and the Revolution becomes a world event
Payoff: the losers of the Revolution also claimed liberty — and took their claim global.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The losers of the Revolution are usually offstage in American history courses. Jasanoff follows them out of the country and finds that they carried a version of liberty with them.

Breaking It Down

Who left: Tens of thousands of loyalists departed the new United States — to Britain, Canada, the Caribbean, and eventually Sierra Leone. Among them were free Black loyalists who had won freedom by joining British forces, and who had the strongest possible reason to distrust patriot liberty.

Loyalism as principle: Jasanoff's argument is that loyalism was not the absence of political conviction. Loyalists held their own understandings of British liberty — ordered, imperial, protective of minorities and dissenters against local majorities. That is a coherent political vision, not simply timidity.

The global frame: Following the exiles turns the Revolution into a world event with consequences across the British Empire.

Why This Matters

Payoff: this reinforces the lecture's core claim from an unexpected direction. Even the people who lost the argument about the Revolution were using the word liberty — and taking it global. If liberty could be claimed by patriots, loyalists, enslaved people, tenants, and Native nations simultaneously, then the word never belonged to anyone in particular. That is what made it so powerful and so uncontrollable.

IS 4 · Wheatley the Author

  • Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley: Biography of a Genius in Bondage (2011)
  • Reconstructs her authorship, transatlantic publication, manumission, and political voice from surviving evidence
  • Pair with the Occom letter: how did she manage publication across an ocean while enslaved?
Payoff: recovering Wheatley as a strategist of her own career — not a curiosity certified by others.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Class gave you one Wheatley passage. This extension gives you the writer behind it — and asks a practical question that turns out to be revealing.

Breaking It Down

What Carretta reconstructs: Wheatley's authorship, her transatlantic publication, her manumission, and her political voice, all built from close attention to surviving documentary evidence rather than legend.

The question to pursue: How did she manage publication across an ocean while enslaved? Consider what that required — securing a patron (the Countess of Huntingdon), navigating a Boston book market unwilling to publish her, arranging London printing, submitting to an examination by prominent Bostonians who certified that she had genuinely written the poems, and using the resulting fame as leverage toward her own freedom.

The certification detail: That panel of examiners, and the frontispiece portrait, exist because readers would not otherwise believe a Black woman wrote the book. Both are evidence of the obstacles — and of her success in getting past them.

Why This Matters

Payoff: recovering Wheatley as a strategist of her own career rather than a curiosity displayed by others. That reframing matters for the lecture's argument. Her letter to Occom was not a spontaneous outpouring; it was a deliberate intervention by a writer who understood her audience, her sources, and her own position exactly. She was claiming the authority to define liberty, and she knew what she was doing.

Rights of Englishmen vs. Natural Rights

Rights of Englishmen were inherited, historical rights of English subjects — consent to taxation, jury trial, security of property. This language could be conservative: it defended established provincial practice.

Natural rights belonged to all human beings by nature. The move from one to the other was neither linear nor complete — but it made the same word usable by people the first version excluded.

×
Loyalists and Neutrals

Loyalists remained committed to the king and the British Empire — many while resenting London's taxes. Neutrals tried to avoid the choice entirely, often at real cost.

Why it matters here: the sharp patriot/loyalist labels belong to the war years. Earlier allegiance was fluid, shaped by religion, trade, family, occupation, and safety — and it could change as the conflict changed.

×
Regulators

Backcountry movements in the Carolinas protesting fees, taxes, court practices, underrepresentation, and elite corruption. The South and North Carolina movements differed in leadership and outcome, and many of these conflicts predated the imperial crisis.

Why it matters here: the crisis gave older local grievances a broader vocabulary — liberty could point at Charleston and New Bern as easily as at London.

Dunmore's Proclamation (November 1775)

Virginia's royal governor offered freedom to enslaved men belonging to rebels who joined British forces. It was limited, military, and self-interested — not abolition, and not applicable to people enslaved by loyalists.

Why it matters here: it made the link between imperial allegiance and emancipation concrete — and it returns in the next lecture as war remakes the stakes of allegiance.

Did the Reformed Asylums Work?

Assessing nineteenth-century asylum reform: ideals versus outcomes.

The Reformers’ Vision vs. Reality

What Reformers Wanted

  • Humane treatment
  • Medical cure for mental illness
  • Patients restored to their families
  • Small, well-funded institutions
  • Individualized care
→

What Actually Happened

  • Overcrowded warehouses
  • Custodial care, not cure
  • Many died inside institutions
  • Massive, underfunded facilities
  • Assembly-line “treatment”

Compare the two columns, line by line.

Reformers such as Dorothea Dix wanted humane treatment, a medical cure, patients restored to their families, small, well-funded hospitals, and individual care. That was the promise of moral treatment.

What happened? Many asylums became overcrowded warehouses. Care became custodial, meaning patients were kept and fed rather than cured. Many patients died inside. Facilities grew huge and underfunded, and "treatment" became routine handling of crowds.

Why the gap? Lecture 2 gave the causes. Legislatures eager to empty poorhouses sent asylums long-term patients, the elderly with dementia, and people with epilepsy. Funding did not keep up. Moral treatment depended on a superintendent who knew every patient, and that was impossible in a crowded state hospital. The early "cure rates" had also been inflated, as Pliny Earle showed in 1877.

Why this matters: this is the asylum's version of the gap between the reformers' hypothesis and the results.

Achievements

  • End of chaining and caging as standard practice
  • Mental illness recognized as a medical condition
  • Basic shelter, food, and clothing for many indigent patients
  • Some humane treatment some of the time
  • Public awareness and responsibility for the mentally ill

Now be fair to the reformers. The asylums did achieve real things.

They helped end chaining and caging as the standard way to handle mentally ill people. Compare the images of Bedlam and William Norris in Lecture 2.

They helped establish mental illness as a medical condition rather than a sin or a curse.

They gave many poor patients basic shelter, food, and clothing they would not otherwise have had.

They offered some humane treatment some of the time. Notice the careful wording; it is honest about the limits.

And they established public responsibility: states accepted that caring for the mentally ill was a public duty, not only a family burden.

Why this matters: a good historical judgment weighs achievements as well as failures. Dix's crusade did make life better for many people.

Failures

  • Became dumping ground for social problems
  • Cure rates collapsed as chronic cases accumulated
  • Class and ethnic bias in admissions and treatment
  • Restraints and abuse reappeared under pressure
  • Asylums functioned as invisible prisons

Dorothea Dix’s crusade inspired compassion—but also created a system for hiding away society’s “inconvenient” people.

Now the failures.

Asylums became dumping grounds for social problems: poverty, old age, disability, and family conflict. Cure rates collapsed as chronic cases piled up. Class and ethnic bias shaped who was admitted and how they were treated; superintendents often showed open prejudice toward Irish immigrant patients. Restraints and abuse returned under the pressure of crowding. And asylums worked as invisible prisons, holding people out of public sight.

The final line gives the mini-lecture's verdict. Dix's crusade inspired compassion, and it also built a system for hiding away people society found inconvenient.

That is the same double logic you saw in the penitentiary and the common school: compassion and control grew from the same root.

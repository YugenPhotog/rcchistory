Children's Rights Movement: A History of Advocacy and Protection

From the Moral Family to Civil Society

Welcome to today's lecture on the Children's Rights Movement. We'll trace how American society moved from viewing children primarily through the lens of family discipline to recognizing children as having rights deserving of public protection. This transformation reflects broader changes in how Americans understood virtue, citizenship, and the role of civil society.

Introduction: Why Children's Reform Matters

  • Antebellum moral reform expanded civic virtue beyond politics to moral life.
  • Child reform extended this moral project to the next generation.
  • Voluntary associations bridged private virtue and public welfare.

The story in one breath. If the republic depended on virtuous citizens, then how children were raised could not be left entirely to private families. Over the nineteenth century, Americans came to see childhood as a distinct, vulnerable stage of life that deserved public protection. Reformers built houses of refuge, sent city children west on orphan trains, founded the first society to protect children from cruelty, and fought child labor.

The lecture runs on a double track. Every new protection was a real gain for children, and every one also gave reformers new power over poor, immigrant, Catholic, Black, and Native families.

Industrialization, Urbanization, and the Crisis of Childhood

  • Industrial labor and child poverty (mills, mines, glassworks).
  • Immigration and urban overcrowding concentrate poverty.
  • "Street Arabs" / "Street Rats"; ≈30,000 homeless children in NYC by 1850s (contemporary estimate).
  • Reformers warned of a "terrible social crisis."
  • Civil society awakens: churches & charities mobilize.
Rapid urbanization created visible crises that demanded organized response.
The "discovery" of childhood poverty sparked a national reform movement.
Industrialization and urbanization created a visible crisis. Tens of thousands of children lived on the streets, worked in dangerous factories, or languished in overcrowded slums. Reformers called them "street Arabs" and warned of social catastrophe. This visible crisis galvanized middle-class reformers to create new institutions and push for new laws.

Industrialization and immigration made childhood poverty impossible to ignore.

In mill towns, children worked long hours tending machines. In cities, poor children sold newspapers, matches, and flowers, scavenged, begged, and sometimes stole. Disease, accidents, and desertion left many without parents. Reformers called children who lived and worked on the streets "street Arabs" and warned that they would grow into a dangerous criminal class.

In 1849 New York's police chief, George W. Matsell, reported that thousands of vagrant and idle children roamed the city's streets. The figure of about 30,000 on the slide is a contemporary estimate, not a count. Such numbers were impressionistic, and they mixed homeless children with children who worked on the streets but had homes. Still, they captured a real alarm.

Churches, missions, and charities mobilized. Civil society, the network of voluntary groups you met in Lecture 1, turned to children as a new field of reform.

Conflicting Images of Children

Philosophical Foundations: Enlightenment Triumphs over Original Sin

  • John Locke's tabula rasa: Children are blank slates shaped by environment and experience.
  • Primacy of environment: Alter a child's character by providing the proper environment.
  • The older Calvinist stress on original sin loses its hold.
Locke's idea that children are blank slates shaped by experience. If experience forms children, then environment and education matter enormously, and the Calvinist stress on inborn sin weakens.

The first big idea came from John Locke. In 1690 he described the mind at birth as something like a blank slate, a tabula rasa. In 1693 he urged parents to shape character through habit, example, and reasoned discipline rather than harsh beatings.

If experience forms children, then environment and education matter enormously, and the older Calvinist stress on original sin loses some of its hold.

Why this matters: if surroundings shape children, then changing their surroundings can change who they become.

Conflicting Images of Children

The Rise of Romanticism

  • Rousseau's romantic innocence: Childhood as a sacred stage of natural purity that must be protected.
  • Sentimental images of children used to rouse public opinion.
Rousseau's romantic view of childhood innocence. Reformers learned that images of innocent, suffering children could move the public.

The second big idea came from Jean-Jacques Rousseau, whose Emile (1762) celebrated childhood as a natural, innocent stage of life to be protected.

Romantic writers and artists turned the innocent child into an ideal, and reformers later used sentimental images of children to stir public feeling.

Why this matters: if children are innocent and vulnerable, then failing to protect them becomes a public wrong.

Conflicting Images of Children

From Ideas to Institutions

  • Ethos of individualism: self-reliance and discipline, taught through public schools and institutions.
  • Moral psychology: Proper discipline combined with affection forms character.
  • Institution building: Schools, reformatories, and orphanages as engines of moral engineering.
Together, these ideas reframed childhood as improvable through environment and care, driving optimism that the right institutions could produce virtuous citizens.
This optimism about human improvement drove the creation of schools, reformatories, and other institutions designed to shape character. This is the intellectual foundation that makes the children's rights movement possible: if children are improvable through environment, then society has both the obligation and the ability to intervene.

Put Locke and Rousseau together: children were improvable, vulnerable, and worth saving.

Reformers believed discipline mixed with affection built character, and they built schools, reformatories, and orphanages to do that work. Society had both the ability and the duty to step in when a child's surroundings failed.

Why this matters: these ideas join the faith in institutions from Lecture 2 and make the whole children's rights movement possible.

Origins & Philosophy

Youthful lawbreakers could be remolded

  • Alternative to adult prisons for children.
  • stressed rigorous discipline, education, and hard work
  • offered basic education and "such employments as will tend to encourage industry"

Reform schools claimed benevolent purpose but operated like prisons.
"The object of the charity is reformation, by training its inmates to industry; by imbuing their minds with principles of morality and religion."
—Pennsylvania Supreme Court, Ex parte Crouse (1838)
  • Goal: moral reform through discipline, labor, education.
  • Based on belief that environment shapes character.
    • Houses of Refuge were supposed to save children from adult prisons, but they often became brutal institutions themselves. The Ex parte Crouse decision justified state intervention in families by claiming it served the child's welfare—a principle that could be both protective and coercive. These institutions show the dark side of reform: good intentions implemented through harsh, dehumanizing methods. Ex parte Crouse (1838) - Brief Lecture Notes: THE CASE: - Mary Ann Crouse, a minor girl, was committed to Philadelphia House of Refuge by her mother - Her father challenged the commitment, arguing she'd been imprisoned without trial - Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruled AGAINST the father THE RULING: - Court established doctrine of "parens patriae" (state as parent) - State could override parental rights when acting for child's "welfare" - Reformatories were not prisons but "schools" - therefore no due process required - Parents' rights not absolute when child's best interest at stake WHY IT MATTERED: - Legal foundation for state intervention in families - Justified juvenile reformatories, industrial schools, and later juvenile courts - Established precedent: children's welfare > parents' rights - Made "child's best interest" a legal standard THE TENSION: - Progressive achievement: children have rights independent of parents - Paternalistic danger: who decides what's in child's "best interest"? - Often used to control poor and immigrant families - Middle-class reformers gained power over working-class child-rearing DISCUSSION POINT: "When should the state override parents' decisions? Who gets to define 'welfare'?" This case laid groundwork for everything from compulsory education to modern CPS. The same principle that protects abused children can also be used to impose class-based values on families. Reform is never neutral.

    Before the 1820s, children convicted of crimes were held in the same jails as adults. Reformers argued that this turned young offenders into hardened criminals.

    On January 1, 1825, a New York reform society opened the New York House of Refuge, the first American institution just for juvenile delinquents. Boston followed in 1826 and Philadelphia in 1828. Like Lecture 2's moral laboratories, they aimed to remold children through discipline, basic schooling, religion, and hard work. Their charters were broad: they took vagrant, idle, or disorderly children, and children whose own parents committed them as unmanageable.

    The legal foundation came from Pennsylvania. Mary Ann Crouse's mother had her committed to the Philadelphia House of Refuge. Her father argued she had been imprisoned without a jury trial. In Ex parte Crouse (decided in early 1839, often dated to the 1838 term), the court said the House of Refuge was a school, not a prison. When parents failed, the state could act as parens patriae, "parent of the country." The picture shows the New York House of Refuge on Randall's Island.

    Why this matters: parens patriae could protect children, and it could confine them without due process.

    • Magdalene & Female Moral Reform Societies: rescue, repentance, rehabilitation.

      • Early refuges for girls – e.g., Boston Female Moral Reform Society's home (mid-1830s); first state girls' reformatory at Lancaster, MA (1856)

    Women-led organizations pioneered rescue but enforced Victorian purity.
    Magdalene & Female Moral Reform Societies - Brief Lecture Notes: WHAT THEY WERE: - Networks of middle-class Protestant women (1830s–1900s) - Rescue homes for "fallen women" (prostitutes, unwed mothers, sexually "ruined" girls) - Named after Mary Magdalene, biblical figure associated with redemption - Run by women, for women—but with strict moral agenda THEIR PHILOSOPHY: - Rejected the idea that women who "fell" were irredeemable - Believed in rehabilitation through: - Religious conversion and prayer - Domestic training (sewing, cooking, housework) - Moral education and discipline - Isolation from corrupting urban influences - BUT: blamed individual moral failure, not structural conditions THE CHILD CONNECTION: - Targeted young girls as "preventable" cases - Rescued girls from brothels, dance halls, streets - Took in pregnant unwed mothers and their babies - Sometimes removed girls from immigrant/poor families deemed "immoral" - Fed into orphanages, adoption networks, and reformatories POWER DYNAMICS: - Middle-class women gained authority over working-class girls' sexuality - "Rescue" often meant forced confinement—girls couldn't leave - Inmates subjected to surveillance, labor, and moral policing - Double standard: blamed women, rarely confronted male clients WHY IT MATTERS FOR CHILDREN'S RIGHTS: - Part of broader movement to "protect" children from sexual exploitation - Established institutional machinery for controlling girls' behavior - Connected to age of consent campaigns - Blurred line between protection and punishment THE TENSION: Achievement: Created safe spaces for vulnerable girls with nowhere else to go Problem: Imposed middle-class sexual morality as condition of help Question: Were these girls being "saved" or controlled? DISCUSSION POINT: "These reformers genuinely wanted to help. But their vision of 'help' meant making poor girls behave like middle-class girls. When does protection become social control?" NOTE: This is the same reform impulse that led to child protective services—genuine concern mixed with class-based assumptions about proper family life.

    Moral discipline over legal protection: girls treated as sinners, not victims.

    Gendered reform: female delinquency as moral failure, not social injustice.

    Reformers paid special attention to girls.

    Urban poverty pushed some young women and girls into prostitution, and in the 1830s evangelical women organized against it. The New York Female Moral Reform Society, founded in 1834, published a widely read paper, visited brothels, sometimes printed the names of men who went there, campaigned for laws against seduction, and ran shelters and job programs. Similar societies and "Magdalen" homes, named for Mary Magdalene, appeared in Boston, Philadelphia, and other cities. The historian Carroll Smith-Rosenberg has shown that these women were bold critics of the sexual double standard.

    But their help came with conditions. Rescue homes demanded religious conversion, domestic training, and strict supervision. Girls could not always leave. Reformers often treated them as sinners to be redeemed rather than victims of exploitation. Later, in 1856, Massachusetts opened the first state reformatory for girls, at Lancaster.

    Age of Consent Reform: Protecting Girls from Sexual Exploitation

    The Problem (Mid-19th Century)

    • Age of consent 10–12 in most states, and 7 in Delaware
    • Girls could be legally married or sexually exploited with minimal protection
    • Double standard: girls blamed for "seduction," men rarely prosecuted
    Age of consent laws codified the idea that children—especially girls—needed legal protection from adult exploitation.

    Reform Campaigns (1880s–1910s)

    • Women's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU) leads national campaign
    • Frances Willard framed the cause as women protecting homes and children
    • 1885–1905: Most states raise age to 16–18
    • Framed as protecting female virtue and childhood innocence
    Major victory: women exercised political power before they could even vote.
    Age of consent reform shows women's organizations exercising real political power before they even had the vote. The WCTU mobilized thousands of women across the country to pressure state legislatures. Raising the age of consent from 10 or 12 (7 in Delaware) to 16 or 18 represented a major shift in how society viewed children—as vulnerable beings deserving protection, not as miniature adults. This is a perfect example of how excluded groups (women without suffrage) could still shape policy through moral authority and organizing.

    The age of consent is the age below which a girl could not legally consent to sexual relations. In the 1880s it was ten or twelve in most states, and seven in Delaware. That meant the law gave young girls almost no protection from adult men, and girls, not men, were often blamed for "seduction."

    After a British journalist's 1885 exposé of child prostitution in London, the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU), which you met in Lecture 3, and allied groups petitioned state legislatures across the country. By the early twentieth century most states had raised the age to sixteen or eighteen.

    Frances Willard, the WCTU's president, framed the cause as women protecting homes and children. Reformers presented it as protecting female virtue and childhood innocence.

    Notice who did this: women who could not vote. They won through petitions, organization, and moral pressure.

    Why this matters: age-of-consent laws wrote into law the idea that children, especially girls, needed protection from adults.

    Charles Loring Brace

    • Son of prominent Connecticut family
    • Goes to New York to study theology
    • Horrified at circumstances of street children
    • Establishes Children's Aid Society
    • Creates industrial schools, finds jobs for idle boys, newsboys lodging house

    Meet Charles Loring Brace (1826–1890).

    He was the son of a prominent Connecticut family. He studied at Yale and came to New York to train for the ministry. Missionary work among the city's poor horrified him, especially the children living on the streets.

    In 1853, at twenty-six, he helped found the Children's Aid Society. It opened industrial schools for poor children, reading rooms, and, in 1854, a Newsboys' Lodging House, where boys who sold newspapers could sleep, bathe, and save money for a small charge. The charge mattered: Brace wanted the boys to feel they were paying their own way, not receiving charity.

    Brace believed the best place for a poor city child was not an institution at all, but a farm family in the countryside. That belief led to his most famous program, on the next slide.

    The Orphan Trains (1854–1929)

    • ≈200,000 children sent West between 1854–1929.
    • Some found loving homes; others faced exploitation and abuse.
    First time reformers place interests of child before those of natal family!
    "The class of a large city most dangerous to its property, its morals, and its political life, are the ignorant, destitute, untrained, and abandoned youth."
    —Charles Loring Brace, The Dangerous Classes of New York (1872)
    Brace's work is complicated. He genuinely cared about children's welfare, but his solution reflected deep prejudices—he assumed Protestant farm life was superior to Catholic urban culture. The orphan trains show both the possibilities and limitations of 19th-century reform: good intentions mixed with class bias and cultural imperialism. Some children thrived, but many were essentially used as farm labor.

    Brace's big idea was "placing out." Instead of keeping poor children in institutions, the Children's Aid Society sent groups of them by train to rural towns in the Midwest and beyond, where local committees arranged for farm families to take them in. The first group went to Dowagiac, Michigan, in 1854.

    Brace thought fresh air, work, and Christian family life would remake the child. He also wanted to drain the city of children he feared, and farms needed labor. In The Dangerous Classes of New York (1872) he described the city's ignorant, destitute, and abandoned youth as the class most dangerous to its property, morals, and politics. (The slide quotes this sentence; treat the wording as close to Brace's but check the 1872 text before quoting it.)

    Between 1854 and 1929 these orphan trains moved an estimated 200,000 to 250,000 children. Many had at least one living parent. Some found loving homes; others were used as unpaid farm hands, separated from siblings, or abused.

    Orphan Trains: The Shadow Side

    • Inspected like stock: at each depot, children lined up on stage while local adults looked them over and chose.
    • Families split: brothers and sisters were often placed with different families, sometimes in different states.
    • Labor, not love: many farm families wanted an extra hand, so some children worked as unpaid laborers or servants.
    • Little oversight: agents checked in rarely, so neglect and abuse could go unnoticed; some children ran away or were moved again.
    • Religion: Catholic leaders objected to Catholic children placed in Protestant homes, and the New York Foundling Hospital ran its own trains to Catholic families.
    Some riders found loving homes. Others found hard work and loneliness. Placing Out traded one risk for another.
    Riders later recalled being separated from family and inspected by strangers at each stop.
    —Paraphrase of orphan-train rider oral histories
    The orphan trains rescued many children from city streets, but the system had real costs. Children were displayed at depots and chosen by strangers. Siblings were often separated. Many families took children mainly as farm labor. Follow-up visits were infrequent, so mistreatment could go unnoticed. Catholic leaders objected to placing Catholic children in Protestant homes, which led the New York Foundling Hospital to run its own placements with Catholic families. Ask students: was Placing Out a rescue, a labor system, or both?

    The orphan trains moved roughly 200,000 children west between 1854 and 1929. For some, it worked. For others, it was frightening and hard.

    When a train arrived, children were lined up, often on a stage or platform, and local adults looked them over and chose whom to take. Riders later remembered feeling inspected by strangers.

    Brothers and sisters were often split up. Many farm families wanted a worker as much as a child, so some riders became unpaid laborers or servants. Agents visited rarely, so neglect or abuse could go unnoticed, and some children ran away or were placed again.

    Religion mattered too. Catholic leaders objected when Catholic children went to Protestant homes, and the Catholic New York Foundling Hospital ran its own trains to Catholic families.

    Why this matters: reform can solve one problem while creating another; Placing Out removed children from street danger but put them at the mercy of strangers.

    The Mary Ellen Moment, 1874–1875

    • 9 April 1874 — Police, acting on a writ obtained by Elbridge Gerry, remove a scarred 10-year-old from a Hell's Kitchen tenement.
    • 21 April 1874 — Gerry uses the old writ de homine replegiando to bring her before the court.
    • 15 Dec 1874 — New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children chartered ("The Cruelty").
    • Among the first agencies with police powers to enter homes and seize abused children.
    • By the early 1900s → hundreds of sister societies nationwide.
    One whip-scarred girl + one courtroom photograph = the legal invention of child protective services.
    Private charity became public authority in eight months.
    Moral outrage → statute → badge → CPS.
    Mary Ellen told the court she had been whipped with a twisted rawhide and struck with scissors.
    —Paraphrase of her testimony as reported in the press, April 1874

    This is one of the most important moments in child welfare history. Mary Ellen Wilson was a 10-year-old girl being beaten and chained by her foster mother. Church worker Etta Wheeler learned of her case and turned to Henry Bergh and attorney Elbridge Gerry of the ASPCA. Despite the popular myth, she was not rescued under animal-cruelty law: Gerry used the old writ de homine replegiando, and on April 9 police removed her from the tenement. The case sparked outrage and led to the creation of one of the first child protective agencies with police powers. Within eight months, moral outrage became law enforcement authority. Ask students: Why did a myth about animal-cruelty law become the story people remember, and what does it say that an animal-protection society led the case? The Mary Ellen Wilson case established a legal principle - that children had rights and the state could intervene to protect them. It created institutional machinery: the NYSPCC and similar societies. But it focused primarily on individual rescue cases - removing specific children from abusive situations. What it didn't do was address the structural conditions that endangered children en masse: poverty, housing, labor exploitation, lack of sanitation. The same tenements Riis photographed in 1888-1890 had been there all along. The child labor he documented was ongoing. The children sleeping in alleyways weren't necessarily being beaten by parents (like Mary Ellen) - they were victims of economic systems and urban neglect.

    In 1873 Etta Angell Wheeler, a Methodist mission worker in the tenements of what is now Hell's Kitchen, learned from neighbors about a girl named Mary Ellen Wilson. Her caretaker, Mary Connolly, beat her, cut her with scissors, and kept her locked indoors. Police and charities told Wheeler they could do nothing.

    Wheeler turned to Henry Bergh, founder of the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, who acted as a private citizen. Here is the key fact, because a myth says otherwise: his lawyer, Elbridge Gerry, did not use animal-cruelty law. He used an old common-law writ, de homine replegiando, which let a court free a person held unlawfully. On April 9, 1874, Judge Abraham Lawrence issued it, and police brought Mary Ellen to court wrapped in a carriage blanket. The next day she told the court she had been whipped with a twisted rawhide and struck with scissors.

    In December 1874 Bergh, Gerry, and John D. Wright founded the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children. Similar societies spread quickly; by the early 1900s there were hundreds.

    The new Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children could rescue individual children from cruel adults. It could not change the conditions millions of children lived in: crowded tenements, poverty, no playgrounds, unregulated child labor.

    Enter Jacob Riis (1849–1914). He was a Danish immigrant who had known poverty himself in his first years in New York. In 1877 he became a police reporter for the New York Tribune, covering the Lower East Side from police headquarters on Mulberry Street. He saw the same tenements and the same children night after night.

    In 1887, more than a decade after Mary Ellen's case, Riis began photographing tenement interiors, cellars, and alleys. The children were still there. His 1890 book How the Other Half Lives made those conditions visible to middle-class readers; the quotation from it on the slide sums up his point that the comfortable did not know because they did not care.

    Revolutionary and Dangerous: Flash Powder Photography

    The Technology of Exposure

    Flash powder = magnesium powder + potassium chlorate

    • Ignited with match → brilliant explosive flash
    • Thick, choking smoke filled rooms
    • Only available since mid-1880s

    The Risks

    • Set at least 2 documented fires
    • Blew out windows
    • Terrified subjects
    • Sometimes chased from buildings
    Why it mattered: First time windowless interior rooms could be photographed—spaces middle-class reformers had never seen.
    The worst conditions were in interior rooms with no windows, no light—spaces that had literally never been photographed before Riis.
    Emphasize the drama: Riis would enter tenements late at night, often with police officers, carrying this flash equipment. The process: Assistant holds pan of powder, Riis opens camera shutter, assistant touches it with a match—BOOM—brilliant flash, then thick acrid smoke fills the room. SPECIFIC STORIES TO TELL: THE LODGING HOUSE PANIC: Riis was photographing a group of men in a lodging house. When the flash exploded, the men scattered in absolute terror—they thought it was a police raid or that they were under attack. The smoke was so thick they couldn't see anything for several minutes. Imagine waking up to an explosion in your room! CHASED FROM BUILDINGS: Residents would sometimes chase Riis and his team out of buildings, furious at being startled awake by explosions in their homes in the middle of the night. THE FIRES: Riis set at least two documented tenement fires with his flash powder. Fortunately both were quickly put out, but imagine the risk—conducting explosions in wooden buildings filled with families. The physical danger mirrored the social danger of exposing these problems—society didn't want to see these conditions any more than tenement residents wanted photographers exploding flash powder in their rooms at midnight. Why it was necessary: The worst conditions were in windowless interior rooms that had literally NEVER been photographed, never been seen by middle-class reformers. The SPCC investigators might interview a family in their front room during the day and never see the airless sleeping quarters in back where children actually lived.     How do you photograph a room that has no windows? In the 1880s, you set off an explosion.

    Flash powder was a mix of magnesium powder and a chemical that burned it fast. Ignited, it gave a brilliant flash and then thick, choking smoke. Riis began using this newly invented method in 1887, at first with help from amateur photographers. It let him photograph the dark interior rooms, cellars, and lodging houses where the worst conditions were, spaces middle-class reformers had never seen.

    It was dangerous. In his autobiography Riis described accidents with the flash, including setting fires more than once. The slide's "at least two documented fires" rests mainly on his own account. The sudden blasts also terrified the people he photographed, who sometimes chased him out.

    Keep a critical eye. Riis's book also relied on crude ethnic stereotypes, and some of his photographs were staged.

    Why this matters: new technology made hidden suffering visible, and visibility moved the public.

    Photography and Structural Reform

    Riis's Reframing

    Old question: "Which parents are neglecting their children?"

    New question: "How can ANY parent raise children in these conditions?"

    Concrete Reforms Achieved

    • Tenement House Act (1901) — mandated ventilation, sanitation, fire safety
    • Playgrounds & parks — created in tenement districts
    • Child labor reform — momentum for regulation
    • School expansion — more facilities in poor neighborhoods

    Photography's Power

    • Made invisible problems visible
    • Provided undeniable visual evidence
    • Combined facts with emotional appeal

    Legacy

    • Model for Progressive Era reform
    • Lewis Hine built on his foundation (1908–1918)
    • Settlement house movement adopted similar methods
    These reforms changed the environment for ALL children, not just rescued individuals.

    From individual rescue → structural transformation.
    Key distinction: Individual moral failing vs. structural problem. Riis made it impossible to see child welfare purely as a matter of individual rescue—his photographs demanded systemic change. Before Riis, Americans concerned about children's welfare might support the SPCC's work removing children from abusive homes. After "How the Other Half Lives" (1890), they had to confront how housing policy, urban planning, labor regulation, and public investment in parks and schools were also children's rights issues. Discussion questions: "What can photography do that statistics can't?" "Who's doing this work today? Making invisible problems visible?" Connection forward: This structural approach becomes the model for Progressive Era reform—you'll see Lewis Hine doing similar work with child labor photography, settlement houses documenting neighborhood conditions, Progressive reformers using "scientific" investigation to push for change. The children Riis photographed weren't just victims of bad parents—they were victims of bad systems. That insight fundamentally changed American reform movements.

    Riis changed the question reformers asked.

    The old question was: which parents are neglecting their children? That led to individual rescue, like Mary Ellen's case. Riis's new question was: how can any parent raise children in these conditions? That led to changing the environment for every child.

    His photographs and writing helped build support for New York's Tenement House Act of 1901, which required windows, ventilation, and sanitation in new tenements, and for parks, playgrounds, and schools in crowded districts. Theodore Roosevelt, as New York police commissioner in the mid-1890s, became his friend and ally.

    Look at the "Photography's Power" box. Photographs made invisible problems visible, gave hard evidence, and combined facts with feeling. Lewis Hine, on a later slide, used the same method against child labor.

    Why this matters: this is the shift from individual rescue to structural reform, the model the Progressive Era followed.

    The First Juvenile Court: Cook County, Illinois (1899)

    Revolutionary Principles

    • Children not tried as criminals—treated as "wards" of the state.
    • Judge acts in loco parentis (in place of parents).
    • Focus on rehabilitation, not punishment.
    • Informal proceedings; no jury trials.
    • Probation instead of incarceration when possible.

    The Chicago Model Spreads

    • By 1925: juvenile courts in every state.
    • Progressive reformers see this as triumph of scientific child-saving.
    • Critics note: children lose due process rights in the name of protection.
    Juvenile courts embodied Progressive faith that expertise could reform society.
    The juvenile court system represented both genuine progress and new forms of state control over children and families.
    The court's philosophy: the child was not prosecuted for a crime but protected from a harmful condition.
    —Summary of Judge Richard Tuthill's court; law took effect July 1, 1899
    The juvenile court was revolutionary: children would no longer be tried as criminals but treated as victims of circumstance needing guidance. But this benevolent system also meant children lost rights to jury trials and formal procedures. Ask students: Can a system be both protective and paternalistic? The juvenile court shows how Progressive reform often gave experts enormous power over people's lives.

    On July 1, 1899, the Illinois Juvenile Court Act took effect, and the first juvenile court in the world opened in Cook County, Chicago, with Judge Richard S. Tuthill presiding.

    The law came from years of campaigning by Chicago women reformers, including Lucy Flower, Julia Lathrop, and the residents of Jane Addams's Hull House, working through the Chicago Woman's Club and with the Chicago Bar Association.

    The court treated children as wards of the state, not criminals. Proceedings were informal, with no jury, and the judge acted like a wise parent, the parens patriae idea from Ex parte Crouse. Probation officers supervised children at home where possible. The drawing of a boy in court is a modern illustration. Juvenile courts spread fast, and by the 1920s nearly every state had one. The line at the bottom of the slide is a summary of the court's philosophy, not a quotation from Tuthill.

    Critics noted that informality meant children lost due process protections, and vague categories gave judges great power over poor and immigrant families.

    Why this matters: the juvenile court was both real progress and a new form of state control.

    Child-Labor Reform Milestones, 1904–1938

    • 1904 National Child Labor Committee (NCLC) founded in NYC; 1908 Lewis Hine hired to photograph "the shame of the states."
    • 1910 Hine's photos of 8-year-olds in coal breakers and cotton mills printed widely in magazines and reform journals.
    • 1916 Keating-Owen Act bans interstate sale of goods made by children under 14 (or 16 in mines).
    • June 3, 1918 Supreme Court strikes it down in Hammer v. Dagenhart ("Congress cannot regulate local labor").
    • 1938 Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) survives court challenge; sets first federal floor: 16 for regular work, 18 for hazardous.
    1904 → 1916 → 1918 → 1938
    One Lewis Hine photograph → one federal law → one permanent childhood.
    Child labor did NOT vanish in 1850. It took 34 years of lobbying, photography, and two Supreme Court defeats to outlaw it nationwide.

    Addie Card, 12, spinner in a North Pownal, Vermont, cotton mill
    —after Lewis Hine's caption, 1910

    Child labor reform took decades. Lewis Hine's photographs shocked middle-class Americans by making child labor visible. But even after Congress passed laws, the Supreme Court struck them down as unconstitutional. Not until 1938—after the New Deal Court revolution—did federal child labor laws finally stick. This shows how slowly reform could move, even when reformers had public opinion on their side.

    Child labor did not disappear in the nineteenth century. It grew with industry. Ending it took more than thirty years.

    The National Child Labor Committee was founded in New York in 1904. In 1908 it hired the sociologist and photographer Lewis Hine to document children working in textile mills, coal breakers, canneries, and glassworks. His photographs, published in magazines and reports, became the movement's strongest evidence.

    Then follow the legal fight. Congress passed the Keating-Owen Act in 1916, banning interstate sale of goods made by young children. On June 3, 1918, the Supreme Court struck it down in Hammer v. Dagenhart. A second law was struck down in 1922. Only the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, upheld in United States v. Darby (1941), set lasting federal limits.

    The photograph is Hine's 1910 image of Addie Card, a young spinner in a Vermont cotton mill. The caption follows Hine's own caption.

    Why this matters: reform could take decades, even with public opinion on its side.

    Gilded-Age Nationalization: The WCTU "Do Everything" Revolution

    • Frances Willard (WCTU president 1879–1898) turned temperance into a total social gospel.
    • "Do Everything" platform: child labor laws, kindergartens, age-of-consent reform, juvenile courts, suffrage.
    • 1886–1905: WCTU campaigns help raise the age of consent in many states.
    • 1899 Illinois Juvenile Court Act—first children's court—driven by the Chicago Woman's Club, Hull House, and the Chicago Bar Association.
    • 1904 National Child Labor Committee organized in New York.
    Willard cast women's activism as protecting the home and the world's children.
    Women's moral networks became the scaffolding of the 20th-century welfare state.

    WCTU & Frances Willard

    • Founded 1874, Cleveland. World's largest women's organization by 1890.
    • Willard's alliances: Prohibition Party, Knights of Labor, NAWSA.
    • Laid groundwork for 18th Amendment (1919) and 19th Amendment (1920).
    The WCTU shows how women used moral authority to gain political power before they could even vote. Frances Willard's "Do Everything" strategy meant the WCTU didn't just fight alcohol—they fought for child labor laws, juvenile courts, kindergartens, and women's suffrage. Their organizational networks became the foundation for the modern welfare state. This is a crucial example of how excluded groups can still shape policy.     Founded in Cleveland in 1874, the WCTU became the largest women's organization in the country by the late 1800s.     Conclusion: The Legacy of Children's Rights Reform

    Achievements

    • Childhood recognized as a distinct life stage requiring protection.
    • Legal reforms: juvenile courts, child labor laws, compulsory education.
    • New institutions: orphanages, foster care, social work professions.
    • Shift from family sovereignty to state responsibility for child welfare.

    Ongoing Questions

    • How much state power over families is justified?
    • Who decides what's in a child's "best interest"?
    • Do children have rights independent of their parents?
    • How do class and race shape who gets "protected" vs. punished?
    The children's rights movement transformed American society by making childhood a matter of public concern, not just private family business.
    But reform was never neutral—it always reflected the values, prejudices, and power dynamics of the reformers themselves.

    Wrap up by emphasizing both the genuine progress and the persistent problems. Childhood became legally protected, but that protection often came with surveillance and control. Reformers saved many children, but they also imposed middle-class values on working families and immigrant communities. These tensions continue today in debates over child protective services, juvenile justice, and parents' rights. The history of children's reform reminds us that even well-intentioned reforms can have complicated, sometimes troubling, consequences.

    Return to April 1874, when a ten-year-old girl wrapped in a carriage blanket told a New York court what had been done to her. Before that day, no agency's job was to protect her. Within a year there was one. Within a generation there were juvenile courts, compulsory school laws, tenement codes, and the start of child labor regulation.

    That is the achievement side of the slide: childhood became a protected stage of life, and the state took on responsibility for children's welfare.

    Now the ongoing questions. The same doctrine that rescued Mary Ellen let Mary Ann Crouse be confined without a trial. The same faith in rural Christian homes that gave some orphan-train riders new lives separated others from parents who were merely poor. The same era bound Black children to former enslavers and sent Native children to boarding schools.

    Historians still debate whether to read this history mainly as expanding children's rights or mainly as expanding social control. The evidence supports both.

    Why this matters: these tensions run through today's debates over child protective services and parents' rights.

    Who Got "Saved"? Race and Child Policy

    • Black children, 1865–1867: Southern apprenticeship laws bound many Black children, often without parental consent, to white employers, frequently their former enslavers.
    • Pushback: freedpeople and the Freedmen's Bureau protested; in In re Turner (1867) Chief Justice Chase ruled a Maryland apprenticeship violated the Civil Rights Act of 1866.
    • Native children, 1879 on: federal boarding schools, beginning with Carlisle, removed many thousands of children, often under pressure, to strip away language and culture. Founder Richard Henry Pratt: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

    These were separate systems from the orphan trains, run by different people under different laws, but they happened in the same era and also took children from their families in the name of their welfare. Southern apprenticeship laws were passed by postwar legislatures and opposed by freedpeople and the Freedmen's Bureau; In re Turner (1867) struck one down. Federal Indian boarding schools aimed at assimilation; enrollment ranged from coerced to voluntary.

    This slide is not about the orphan trains. It looks at two other systems from the same era that also took children from their families in the name of their good.

    Black children. Right after the Civil War, several Southern states used apprenticeship laws to bind Black children, often without their parents' consent, to white employers, frequently their former enslavers. Freedpeople fought back, and in In re Turner (1867) Chief Justice Salmon Chase ruled that a Maryland apprenticeship of a Black girl violated the Civil Rights Act of 1866.

    Native children. Starting with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in 1879, the federal government sent many thousands of Native children to boarding schools meant to replace their languages and cultures. Some families were pressured or forced; others chose the schools. Carlisle's founder, Richard Henry Pratt, summed up the goal: "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man."

    The photograph shows three pupils at Carlisle.

    Why this matters: comparing these systems shows that reformers' ideas about childhood did not apply equally to every child.

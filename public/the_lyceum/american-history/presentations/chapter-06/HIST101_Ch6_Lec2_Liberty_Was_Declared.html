A Sentence Went Public in 1776

that all men are created equal
Declaration of Independence, 4 July 1776
Declarations do not enforce themselves. So who could actually use this?

Ideals Create Claims. Power Decides Outcomes.

A claim is made → An institution answers → An outcome follows
  • Courts could hear a freedom suit — or refuse it
  • Legislatures could abolish gradually — or not at all
  • Armies could promise freedom — and revoke it
Revolutionary language was a resource, not a gift.

Two Errors to Avoid

“The ideals freed people”

Treats a written principle as if stating it did the work. It ignores who held the courts, the guns, and the property.

“It was all hypocrisy”

Treats the language as empty. But it destabilized old justifications and produced real lawsuits, statutes, and emancipations.

Better question: which claims became enforceable, and why those?

Case One

Black Freedom in a Civil War

When two armies both need you, freedom becomes negotiable

November 1775: An Official Route Opens

“All indented Servants, Negroes, or others, (appertaining to Rebels,) free that are able and willing to bear Arms.”
Lord Dunmore's Proclamation, 7 November 1775

What it was not

  • General abolition
  • Applicable to loyalists' slaves
  • Humanitarian in motive

What it did

  • Made escape a route to legal freedom
  • Terrified slaveholders
  • Turned imperial war into personal opportunity

The Proclamation Is Not the Story

Who moved

  • Men, women, and children
  • Sometimes alone, often as families
  • Carrying knowledge of roads and waterways

What they risked

  • Disease and scarcity in British camps
  • Being treated as captured property
  • Policy that could change without warning
Tens of thousands escaped slavery; roughly 20,000 reached or served within British lines. Several thousand documented evacuees left New York, with others departing southern ports.

Two Armies, Two Offers

British lines

  • 1779: Philipsburg drops the service requirement
  • Freedom for leaving a rebel owner
  • Evacuation lists recorded at war's end
  • Nova Scotia, the Caribbean, Sierra Leone

Patriot forces

  • Policy varied by state and shifted over time
  • Rhode Island raised a regiment of enslaved men
  • Black sailors served in navies and on privateers
  • Some won freedom; some returned to bondage
Service created evidence of a claim on the republic. It did not guarantee the claim was honored.

Watch the Labels

“Black Loyalist” and “Black Patriot” describe which army someone reached. They do not describe what that person wanted.
  • Allegiance was often secondary to freedom
  • Or to keeping a family together
  • Or to food, safety, and the chance to move
  • Opportunity depended on where the armies were

One War, Two Regional Outcomes

North: slow abolition

  • Vermont, 1777: barred adult slavery in principle
  • Pennsylvania, 1780: gradual emancipation
  • Massachusetts: freedom suits made slavery unenforceable

South: destabilized, not abolished

  • Virginia eased manumission in 1782
  • Free Black communities grew
  • No southern state adopted general emancipation
Not the cotton boom. Cotton transformed slavery after the 1790s. In the 1780s tobacco, rice, credit, inherited property, and planter authority were already doing the work.

Making a Constitution Mean Something

  • Massachusetts, 1780: all men born free and equal
  • 1781: Elizabeth Freeman sues for her liberty
  • She wins — and takes the name Freeman
  • Other suits follow; enforcement collapses

Litigants made that sentence operative. It did not apply itself.

Case Two

Native Sovereignty

A different claim entirely

Nations Made Strategic Judgments

The calculation

  • American independence meant faster settler expansion
  • Britain had failed to restrain colonists
  • But Britain at least treated nations as powers
  • Patriot victory removed even that barrier

The variation

  • Oneida and Tuscarora largely with the Americans
  • Many Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, Onondaga with Britain
  • Cherokee and Creek pursued their own territorial aims
There was no “Indian side.” Speaking of one erases councils, debates, rivalries, and choices.

A Confederacy Divided Against Itself

  • The Haudenosaunee split under wartime pressure
  • Missionary ties, old rivalries, local diplomacy
  • 1779: an American campaign burned towns, food stores, orchards
  • Strategy aimed at the material base of resistance

For the Six Nations this was a civil war inside a civil war.

Painted in London in 1776 — a diplomat portrayed as an ally, not a subject

1783: A Line Drawn Over Homelands

From the U.S. view

  • Britain ceded territory to the Mississippi
  • Victory established American sovereignty
  • Western nations were defeated peoples

From Native views

  • No Native nation was at the table
  • Britain gave away land it never owned
  • We were not conquered, so nothing was ceded
Warfare in the Ohio Country continued for over a decade. The peace ended the Anglo-American war and settled nothing in the West.

Two Claims, Not One Experience

Rights within the United States

Include me in the promise. Enforce the sentence you wrote. Make me a full member of this polity.

The right not to be ruled by it

My nation is not inside your polity. Honor the treaty. Recognize a jurisdiction you do not hold.

This is the distinction to carry out of today. Filing both under “minority experience” hides the fact that an inclusive republic can still be a colonial one.

And Everyone Else

Women, Loyalists, Dissenters

Three more tests of the same model

Gained, Lost, Won

Women

  • Ran farms, shops, estates
  • Fed and followed armies
  • Petitioned and boycotted
  • Gained civic significance
  • Usually excluded from formal politics

Loyalists

  • Oaths, confiscation, exile
  • Tens of thousands left
  • Canada, Britain, Caribbean, Sierra Leone
  • The nation defined by expulsion too

Dissenters

  • Establishments weakened
  • Virginia's statute, 1786
  • Compelled support rejected
  • The most durable gain
Republican motherhood enlarged what women meant to the republic while keeping their authority inside the household.

⏸ Which Claim Could Succeed?

Four claimants. Which demand least threatened existing arrangements of property and sovereignty?

  • A Baptist demanding an end to church taxes
  • An enslaved woman suing for her freedom in Massachusetts
  • A Virginia planter's wife asking to vote
  • A Shawnee council demanding the Ohio boundary hold

What does your ranking reveal about whose interests the institutions protected?

The Pattern: Selective Transformation

  • Real freedom won — and slavery constitutionally secured
  • Self-government expanded — and Native jurisdiction denied
  • Conscience protected — and loyalists stripped of rights
  • Women indispensable — and usually formally excluded, with a New Jersey exception
The Revolution widened the arguments available to everyone. It did not redistribute the power to win them.

Which Raises a Prior Question

Every claim today ran into an institution —
a court, a legislature, a treaty, a militia.

But in 1776 those institutions had just been
declared illegitimate.

Independent Study

Going Deeper

After the evacuation • How unity was built on exclusion • The war after the war • Reading the historians

Freedom, Then What?

The achievement

  • Names and claimed status recorded before departure
  • Thousands left New York as free people
  • New communities in Nova Scotia and beyond

The betrayal

  • Land grants delayed or never delivered
  • Discrimination and poverty in the empire
  • Some later emigrated again, to Sierra Leone
Black Loyalists escaped American slavery into British inequality. Both facts belong in the account.

How Thirteen Colonies Became One People

  • Patriot newspapers tied British power to enslaved rebels
  • And to hostile Native nations, and to foreign mercenaries
  • Disparate colonies learned to imagine a shared enemy
  • Racial boundaries hardened in the same breath
Robert Parkinson's argument: universal political language and exclusionary nation-making developed together. Unity was partly manufactured by naming dangerous outsiders.

The West Did Not Accept 1783

  • The United States expected western nations to behave as conquered peoples
  • Confederacies replied that they had never been defeated as a whole
  • Fighting continued in the Ohio Country through the 1780s and 1790s
  • American armies suffered major defeats there
Alan Taylor and Colin Calloway show that the Revolution did not end Indigenous diplomacy. It reconfigured the contest as a settler republic entered continuing British and Spanish imperial rivalries and claimed a continent.

Four Shelves Worth Knowing

Black participation

Benjamin Quarles established it as central. Judith Van Buskirk follows Black Patriots as people rather than policy.

Self-emancipation

Sylvia Frey and Douglas Egerton emphasize resistance, mobility, and Atlantic context.

Native-centered

Calloway organizes the war around communities. Ned Blackhawk places Native nations at the center of U.S. history.

Exile

Maya Jasanoff reads the loyalist diaspora as a global consequence of American independence.

Notice that no shelf treats these people as a footnote to a white political story.

Dunmore's Proclamation

An order issued on 7 November 1775 by John Murray, Earl of Dunmore, the royal governor of Virginia. It declared martial law and offered freedom to indentured servants and enslaved people belonging to rebels who were able and willing to bear arms for the king.

Read the limits carefully: it applied only to people owned by rebels, and it arose from military necessity rather than any commitment to abolition. It matters anyway, because it converted an imperial war into an official route out of slavery — and because slaveholders instantly understood the threat.

The Philipsburg Proclamation, 1779

An order from the British commander Sir Henry Clinton promising protection to people who left rebel owners — without requiring them to serve in the army.

This is the wider door. Dunmore's offer had been tied to bearing arms, which effectively excluded women, children, and the elderly. Philipsburg opened British lines to whole families, and the resulting movement became one of the largest emancipatory processes of the Revolution.

×
Gradual Emancipation

A statute that ends slavery for the future without freeing anyone currently enslaved. Pennsylvania's 1780 act declared that children born to enslaved mothers after a certain date would become free — at age twenty-eight.

Note who paid. Masters kept their existing property and received decades of labor from the children; the enslaved bore the cost of their own emancipation and entered adult life without the inherited property that free white men usually had. The law attacked hereditary lifetime slavery. It did not deliver equality.

×
The Haudenosaunee Confederacy

The league of Six Nations — Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora — often called the Iroquois Confederacy in older textbooks. A long-established diplomatic and political union in what is now upstate New York.

Its wartime division is the point. Oneida and Tuscarora communities generally supported the Americans while many Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga people allied with Britain. The confederacy was not a passive bystander choosing a European patron; it was a polity whose internal politics the war tore open.

Republican Motherhood

The postwar idea that the republic depended on mothers to raise virtuous citizens, giving women a recognized public purpose exercised through the family.

It cuts two ways, and you should be able to say both. It supported expanded education for women and conceded that the republic could not function without their work. It also located that authority firmly inside marriage and motherhood. Most women gained no formal political rights, although New Jersey permitted some property-owning women to vote until 1807.

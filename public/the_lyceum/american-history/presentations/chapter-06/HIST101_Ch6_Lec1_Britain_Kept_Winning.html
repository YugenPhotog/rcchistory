HIST 101, Chapter 6, Lecture 1 — Britain Kept Winning. Why Did the Revolution Not End?

Image description: Emanuel Leutze, Washington Crossing the Delaware (1851), Metropolitan Museum of Art. Washington stands upright in a crowded rowboat pushing through ice floes at dawn, a large American flag raised behind him. Painted seventy-five years after the event and historically inaccurate in many details.

Study prompt. Before reviewing the lecture, ask what this painting makes the Revolution feel like: uncertain, desperate, or destined to succeed. Return to that question after the final classroom slide.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the most famous picture of the American Revolution ever painted, and many of its details are historically inaccurate. The flag shown was adopted after the crossing. The ice resembles the Rhine more than the Delaware, and the standing pose is heroic symbolism rather than a record of ordinary river transport. Emanuel Leutze painted it in 1851 — seventy-five years after the event, in Germany, using American tourists as models.

Hold that thought. Today's lecture is about the gap between how the Revolutionary War actually went and how we remember it going.

Why This Matters

The painting shows a man who looks like he already knows he is going to win. In December 1776 he did not know that at all — he thought he might be watching the end of the whole experiment. Every slide today is trying to get you back into that uncertainty, because you cannot explain why the Revolution survived if you start by assuming it was always going to.

Britain Kept Winning

  • Long Island — August 1776
  • New York City — September 1776
  • Philadelphia — September 1777
  • Charleston — May 1780
  • Camden — August 1780
  • Every major port, at some point
So why was the war still being fought in 1781?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the fact that should bother you. If you made a list of who won the big set-piece battles of the Revolutionary War, Britain would win that list comfortably. So the interesting question is not "how did the plucky Americans beat the mighty British Empire." It is: why did all of that winning not end the war?

Breaking It Down

Long Island, August 1776: Washington split an already outnumbered army, left a mountain pass unguarded, and got flanked. A serious defeat, weeks after the Declaration of Independence.

New York City, September 1776: Britain took the best harbor on the coast and held it for the entire rest of the war. It became their main base.

Philadelphia, September 1777: Britain occupied the rebel capital. Congress packed up and left town. In most eighteenth-century wars, capturing the enemy's capital is roughly where you start writing the peace treaty.

Charleston, May 1780 and Camden, August 1780: Britain captured an entire American army at Charleston and then destroyed the replacement army at Camden. Four years in, and the Americans were arguably doing worse than at the start.

Why This Matters

Notice what this list does to the usual explanations. It cannot be that Britain was incompetent — incompetent armies do not win like this. It cannot be that Americans were better soldiers — they mostly lost. Something other than battlefield performance is doing the work, and finding it is the job of the next slide.

Four Different Things

Win a battle
Defeat an army in the field
Occupy a city
Hold ground with troops
Destroy an army
End its capacity to return
Restore rule
Make people obey again
Britain could reliably do the first two. The war turned on the last two.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the single most useful distinction in the lecture, so if you write down one slide, write down this one. We tend to lump "winning a war" into one idea. It is actually at least four separate achievements, and they get progressively harder.

Think of it like a landlord trying to evict a tenant who refuses to leave. Winning the argument is one thing. Getting a court order is another. Physically removing them is another. Making them stop coming back is another thing entirely.

Breaking It Down

Win a battle: Beat the enemy army on a given day in a given field. Britain could do this almost whenever it chose to.

Occupy a city: Put troops in a place and hold it. Britain could do this too — New York, Philadelphia, Charleston, Savannah. But an army standing in Boston is not standing in the Hudson Valley. Troops are finite, and America was enormous.

Destroy an army: End the enemy's ability to come back next spring. This is the one Britain kept almost doing to Washington and never quite finished.

Restore rule: Get people to obey royal government again after you leave. This is the hardest, and it is not a military task at all — it needs courts, officials, tax collectors, and neighbors willing to keep the whole thing running.

Why This Matters

Britain's war aim was the fourth thing. Britain's capability was the first two. That mismatch is the entire explanation for why a string of victories did not produce a settlement — and it is why the phrase "Britain kept winning" and the phrase "Britain was losing the war" can both be true at the same time.

Section I

Conquest Was Not Restoration

Why an army that wins can still fail to govern

📖 Understanding This Slide

A signpost. The first stretch of the lecture works out why an army that keeps winning can still fail to govern — and why that failure was built into Britain's strategy rather than being a run of bad luck.

Why This Matters

"Conquest" and "restoration" sound like the same thing. They are not. Conquest is something you do to people. Restoration requires something from them. Keep that difference in your head for the next four slides.

The Howes Arrived With Two Jobs

Commanders

  • Overwhelming force at New York
  • Naval control of the coast
  • German auxiliary troops

Peace commissioners

  • Power to pardon rebels
  • Power to restore colonies
  • Goal: obedience, not ruins
Restoring legitimate authority required loyalists to step forward and be counted.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The two Howe brothers arrived in 1776 wearing two hats at once, and the hats did not fit together. Admiral Richard Howe and General William Howe commanded the largest expeditionary force Britain had ever sent anywhere — and they were also officially authorized to negotiate peace and hand out pardons.

Breaking It Down

Why send peace commissioners with an invasion fleet? Because London did not want a conquered province. It wanted its colonies back, functioning, paying, and loyal. Burning the place down would have defeated the purpose.

The logic was not stupid. Independence was only weeks old. Plenty of colonists were undecided, nervous, or actively loyal. A spectacular display of imperial power plus a generous pardon offer might reasonably have persuaded ordinary people that resistance was hopeless and forgiveness available.

The catch: to rebuild royal government you need local people to run it. Which means loyalists have to identify themselves publicly — take an oath, accept an office, supply the army, name their rebel neighbors. That is a lot to ask of someone who has to live in that town afterward.

Why This Matters

This slide sets up the trap on the next one. Every tool Britain had for restoring authority required exposing its own supporters. That is a structural problem, not a tactical mistake, and no amount of battlefield success could solve it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Andrew Jackson O'Shaughnessy – The Men Who Lost America (2014)

For a long time the standard explanation for British defeat was simply that British generals were fools. O'Shaughnessy pushes back hard. He shows that British commanders were working under brutal constraints — enormous distances, slow communications, political instructions from London, an empire to defend elsewhere, and the requirement not to alienate the very population they were trying to win back. Their failures were real, but they were failures inside a mission that may simply have been impossible. That reframing is what this slide is doing.

The Trap Inside the Strategy

Army arrives → Loyalists declare themselves → Army marches on
Patriot militia returns → Oath-takers punished → Nobody declares next time
Every advance created collaborators Britain could not protect.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the trap, drawn as a loop. Read it as a machine that runs the same way every time the British army moves through a region — and notice that each turn of the machine leaves Britain worse off than before.

Breaking It Down

Step one: the army arrives. Overwhelming force, no serious resistance.

Step two: loyalists declare themselves. Some out of genuine conviction. Others because the redcoats are standing in the street and a pardon is being offered. Either way, their names are now known.

Step three: the army marches on. It has to. There is another campaign, another season, another theater. Troops cannot garrison every county in America.

Step four: the Patriot militia comes back. And it wants to know exactly who swore that oath. Fines, confiscation, humiliation, sometimes violence, sometimes flight to British lines.

Step five: nobody volunteers next time. Word travels. In the next county over, the loyalists have watched what happened and they keep their mouths shut.

Why This Matters

Britain was effectively burning through its own political base every time it advanced. The strategy consumed the resource it depended on. This is why the same territory had to be "pacified" over and over, and why by 1777 very few people in New Jersey were willing to publicly declare for the Crown.

The Army Was Disappearing

27,000
Fit for duty, northern theater, September 1776
6,000
With Washington by mid-December — most enlistments expiring
“If this fails us, I think the game will be pretty well up.”
George Washington to Lund Washington, 10–17 December 1776

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two numbers and one sentence. The numbers tell you the army was evaporating. The sentence tells you the commander knew it.

Breaking It Down

About 27,000 down to roughly 6,000 in four months. These figures compare peak northern-theater strength in September with Washington's diminished field army in mid-December, so they are not a strict like-for-like return. Even so, the collapse was real. Enlistments expired, men went home, and others simply left — often taking their muskets with them. The Continental Army was built on short terms of service, which meant it had to be recreated from scratch roughly every year.

And many of those still present were leaving on December 31. That is the detail that turns a bad situation into an emergency. Washington was looking at a deadline after which he might command almost nobody.

"If this fails us, I think the game will be pretty well up." He wrote that to his cousin Lund in mid-December 1776. Read it slowly. This is not stirring wartime rhetoric for public consumption — it is a private admission that the whole thing might be over. Read the letter at Founders Online.

Why This Matters

Compare this slide to the painting on the title slide. Same man, same month. The painting gives you destiny; the letter gives you a man doing arithmetic and not liking the answer. Historians care about this gap because it is the difference between explaining an outcome and simply celebrating it.

What the Army Was For

  • Tied thirteen separate war efforts together
  • Gave Congress an instrument of national policy
  • Kept the rebellion legible as a war between governments
  • Made survival imaginable to foreign courts
The decisive achievement of 1776 was not victory. It was avoiding annihilation.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the counterintuitive claim: the Continental Army mattered most for reasons that were not military. Even when it could not hold ground — and for long stretches it could not — its mere existence was doing enormous political work.

Breaking It Down

It tied thirteen war efforts together. Without a national army you do not have one revolution; you have thirteen separate insurrections that Britain can deal with one at a time.

It gave Congress something to be a government with. A legislature that cannot field a force is a debating society.

It kept the rebellion legible as a war between governments. This one is subtle but important. If the fighting is done by scattered armed bands, Britain gets to call the participants criminals and traitors and hang them. A uniformed army under regular command forces a different category — and different treatment.

It made survival imaginable abroad. No French minister was going to gamble on a country that had no army. Section II picks this thread up.

Why This Matters

So Washington's central achievement in 1776 is a strange one: he mostly avoided losing decisively. That sounds like faint praise until you realize the alternative. If the army had surrendered at New York or dissolved in New Jersey, there would have been no institution left for a French alliance to rescue three years later. Retreat did not win the war. It preserved the possibility of winning it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John Shy – A People Numerous and Armed

Shy's influential argument is that we should stop reading the Revolution as a conventional war with a conventional decisive-battle logic, and start reading it as a contest for the allegiance of an armed population. On that reading, Washington's refusal to risk everything on one big engagement is not timidity — it is the correct strategy for the actual war he was fighting.

Holger Hoock – Scars of Independence: America's Violent Birth (2017)

Hoock insists we take seriously that the Revolution was a civil war full of intimidation, property destruction, and retaliation among neighbors. That matters here in a specific way: a standing Patriot army did not eliminate that violence, but it did put some of the war under regular military command and discipline. Without it, the conflict would have been even more thoroughly a war of neighbors against neighbors.

⏸ Pause & Predict

Washington cannot hold New York.
He cannot hold New Jersey.
He cannot replace the men he is losing.

What does he need most right now —
ground, a visible victory, or a foreign ally?

📖 Understanding This Slide

A thinking break. Before you read on, actually answer it — the slide is designed so that the obvious answer is the wrong one.

Breaking It Down

Ground? Tempting, because that is how we normally score wars. But Washington cannot hold ground, and trying to hold it is exactly what nearly destroyed him at New York.

A foreign ally? Correct eventually — and it is where the second half of the lecture goes. But not yet. No European power allies with an army that is about to stop existing.

A visible victory? This is the answer, and note the word visible. He does not need a strategically significant victory. He needs something that people back home, in the militia, and in Paris can look at and conclude that this cause is still alive.

Why This Matters

This is the moment the lecture switches from military logic to political logic, and it does it by making you feel the switch rather than telling you about it. If your instinct was "ground," that is genuinely useful information about the assumptions you brought into the room.

Trenton and Princeton

Militarily, small

  • An isolated Hessian garrison
  • Nearly 1,000 prisoners taken
  • No British army destroyed
  • Few immediate reenlistments

Politically, large

  • Regular troops shown to be beatable
  • Militia resumed operations
  • British outposts pulled back
  • Patriot authority returned
The campaign changed expectations, not the map.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two columns, and the contrast between them is the whole point. By any conventional military measure, Trenton and Princeton were minor. By political measure they were enormous. Learning to hold both judgments at once is what this slide is training you to do.

Breaking It Down

An isolated garrison. Washington did not attack the main British army. He crossed the ice-choked Delaware on Christmas night and hit a Hessian outpost at dawn, when the defenders were exhausted from weeks of militia harassment and half-blinded by a nor'easter. Nearly a thousand prisoners, at very low cost.

No British army destroyed. The Crown's fighting power was essentially untouched. A week later Cornwallis was in the field with 8,000 men looking for him.

Regular troops shown to be beatable. This is the payoff. The assumption that professional European soldiers simply could not be defeated by Americans took a public hit.

Militia resumed operations, outposts pulled back, Patriot authority returned. Local men who had gone quiet started turning out again. British garrisons withdrew toward New Brunswick and Perth Amboy. Revolutionary committees and courts came back into operation across much of New Jersey.

Why This Matters

The campaign changed expectations, not the map. And in a war where the real prize is civilian allegiance, expectations are the strategic terrain. Note honestly, though, that it did not solve the manpower crisis — the reenlistment surge Washington hoped for did not really materialize. Small victories can change a mood without fixing a problem.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David Hackett Fischer – Washington's Crossing (2004)

Fischer's book is the reason this campaign is now taught as a political event rather than a plucky anecdote about a cold boat ride. His argument is that the victories reorganized what people believed was possible — among soldiers, among New Jersey civilians deciding which way to jump, and eventually among foreign observers. He also emphasizes the New Jersey militia's own initiative in the weeks beforehand, which is why the slide credits harassment for softening the Trenton garrison. The victory was not manufactured by one general's daring alone.

Allegiance Was a Calculation

What people weighed

  • Family and religious ties
  • Local rivalries and debts
  • Fear of punishment
  • Who would rule the county next month

In New Jersey

  • Thousands took the king's oath in 1776
  • Militia returned in 1777 asking who had
  • Fines, seizures, flight to British lines
Careful: “hearts and minds” is a useful phrase, not an eighteenth-century doctrine. Both sides used coercion. Consent and force operated together.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Drop the idea that America contained a fixed population of Patriots and a fixed population of Loyalists who simply needed to be counted. For a great many people, allegiance was a running calculation that got revised whenever the military situation changed.

Breaking It Down

What people weighed. Family and church networks. Who they owed money to and who owed them. A grudge against the loudest committeeman in town. Genuine fear of punishment. And above all a forecast: who will actually control this county next month?

New Jersey is the case study. Thousands took the king's oath during the British sweep in late 1776 — including Richard Stockton, who had literally signed the Declaration of Independence. Then Trenton and Princeton happened, the militia came back, and those same people had to explain themselves. Some groveled. Some paid. Some ran for British lines.

This does not make anybody a hypocrite. That is the important move. Political identity was being produced by events and relationships, not sitting inside people waiting to be discovered.

Why This Matters

The crimson box is a warning about language. "Hearts and minds" helps you see that this war had a political dimension, and that is genuinely useful. But it is a phrase from twentieth-century counterinsurgency doctrine, and neither side in 1777 was running anything of the kind. Both sides used coercion freely — British foraging and property seizures on one side, Patriot committees and loyalty oaths on the other. Consent and force were not alternatives. They operated together, on the same people, often in the same week.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Maya Jasanoff – Liberty's Exiles: American Loyalists in the Revolutionary World (2011)

Jasanoff follows loyalists after the war — to Canada, Britain, the Caribbean, Sierra Leone — and in doing so recovers just how many of them there were and how ordinary most of them were. Her work makes the choice on this slide feel real: these were people with farms and families weighing which side would leave them with a life, and tens of thousands of them guessed wrong and lost everything.

Section II

The War Changes Scale

A colonial rebellion attaches itself to an imperial rivalry

📖 Understanding This Slide

The hinge of the lecture. Everything so far has been inside North America. From here the frame widens, and the question changes from "can the Americans survive?" to "what happens when this rebellion attaches itself to a fight between empires?"

Why This Matters

Watch for a shift in who has agency. In Section I, American choices drive the story. In Section II, a lot of what determines American independence is decided by people in Paris, Madrid, and the Caribbean who are not thinking about American liberty at all.

France Needed Evidence, Not Sympathy

What France wanted

  • Revenge for the Seven Years' War
  • Britain weakened and distracted
  • Strategic position recovered

What France doubted

  • Could the rebellion survive?
  • Would aid be wasted on a collapse?
  • Was another disastrous war worth it?
Americans had to prove they could stay in the field and impose costs.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two columns: what France wanted, and what France was afraid of. The gap between them explains why French help stayed secret and limited for two years, and what had to happen before it became an open alliance.

Breaking It Down

Revenge for the Seven Years' War. France had lost Canada and its position in India barely a decade earlier. That humiliation was recent, and it was personal for French ministers.

Britain weakened and distracted. Note the framing. The goal was not a free America. The goal was a smaller, poorer, more preoccupied Britain.

But: could the rebellion survive? France had already been quietly funneling supplies through a front company since 1776. An open alliance was a different order of risk — it meant war, and war with Britain had recently gone very badly.

So Americans needed evidence, not sympathy. Sympathy was cheap and already available; French officers admired the American cause. What Franklin could not supply by charm was proof that the United States would still exist in two years.

Why This Matters

This inverts the patriotic version of the story, where America's righteousness attracts foreign friends. In the actual sequence, American military credibility attracts foreign self-interest. Which sets up the next slide: Saratoga's importance is not that it was a big win. It is that it was a legible one.

Saratoga, October 1777

  • Incoherent British planning
  • Supply line lengthening from Canada
  • Militia swarming behind the column
  • An entire army surrendered on 17 October

A campaign, not a single battle — and above all, a diplomatic argument.

Trumbull painted this decades later for the Capitol — a commemoration, not a witness

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Saratoga campaign is the one place where an American victory really did destroy a British army — and even here, the reason it mattered most was diplomatic.

Breaking It Down

Incoherent British planning. Burgoyne was sent south from Canada to link up with the New York garrison and cut off New England. Meanwhile the Howes decided to go take Philadelphia instead. Nobody was firmly committed to supporting Burgoyne, which made his whole march strategically pointless.

The supply line stretched. Every mile south from Canada made his logistics worse and his position more exposed.

Militia swarmed behind him. This is the part worth remembering. Local men turned out in large numbers, cut his communications, and made the countryside itself hostile. By the time he reached the Hudson he was outnumbered roughly three to one.

An entire army surrendered on 17 October 1777. Not driven off. Not defeated. Surrendered, as a whole force.

Why This Matters

The caption on the painting is doing work, so read it. Trumbull painted this decades later, on commission, for the U.S. Capitol. It is a national monument in oil paint, not a record of what the field looked like. You will get the same warning again on the Cornwallis painting near the end of class — the lecture keeps asking you to separate evidence about the past from commemoration of the past.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Kevin Weddle – on the Saratoga campaign

Weddle's reassessment stresses the word campaign. Saratoga was not one dramatic afternoon; it was months of marching, supply failure, the costly battles of Freeman's Farm and Bemis Heights, and steadily worsening British odds. His point is that the outcome came from a combination of American operational choices, mass local mobilization, and British misjudgment — not from a single heroic stroke. That matters for the lecture's larger argument, because it means even America's most decisive victory was a collective and partly accidental achievement.

February 1778

What the French alliance actually delivered

Credit
Loans a bankrupt Congress could not raise
Troops
Professional soldiers under Rochambeau
A navy
The one thing Americans could never build
Resist the shortcut: France was not an altruistic champion of republican liberty. Bourbon policy aimed at weakening Britain. Motives need not be idealistic for an alliance to be decisive.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Saratoga convinced Louis XVI that the Americans could win and that intervention was worth the gamble. In February 1778 Franklin and Vergennes signed two treaties. This slide is about what those treaties actually delivered — and the crimson box is about what they did not mean.

Breaking It Down

Credit. Congress could not tax. It had printed money until the currency collapsed. French loans and French guarantees gave the United States access to money it could not raise on its own.

Troops. Professional soldiers under Rochambeau — roughly five thousand of them, and they turn up decisively at Yorktown.

A navy. Underline this one. Americans could build an army, badly and repeatedly. They could never build a fleet capable of contesting the Royal Navy. Every British advantage in this war — moving troops by sea, supplying ports, blockading coasts — ran through naval power. France is the only reason that advantage was ever neutralized, and it was neutralized exactly once, for a few crucial weeks, in 1781.

The alliance treaty also recognized American independence, committed France to fight until Britain conceded it, and disavowed French territorial ambitions on the continent.

Why This Matters

The warning box matters as much as the content. It is tempting to narrate France as a fellow lover of liberty riding to the rescue, especially since figures like Lafayette genuinely were idealistic. But Bourbon France was an absolute monarchy underwriting a republican revolution for reasons of state. Here is the honest and slightly uncomfortable conclusion: an alliance does not need noble motives to be decisive. Historical explanation is not the same as moral endorsement.

Britain Could No Longer Concentrate

1778

  • France enters
  • Philadelphia abandoned
  • Home islands feel exposed

1779

  • Spain enters
  • Gibraltar besieged
  • Gulf Coast contested

1780

  • Dutch conflict opens
  • Caribbean, India, Atlantic trade
  • Recruits harder to find
North America became one theater in a global war.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three years, three columns, one effect: Britain's attention gets split into pieces. This is the answer to the question the lecture opened with, arriving from an unexpected direction.

Breaking It Down

1778 — France enters. George III declared war on France, recalled the Howes, and ordered Philadelphia abandoned. Read that again: Britain gave up the captured rebel capital voluntarily, because it now had bigger problems. It also began to fear invasion at home while its navy was tied up in American waters.

1779 — Spain enters. Not as an American ally — Spain never recognized American independence, and Charles III was not enthusiastic about encouraging colonial subjects to revolt. Spain wanted Gibraltar and Florida. It besieged Gibraltar and took West Florida, and Bernardo de Gálvez ran campaigns along the Gulf Coast.

1780 — the Dutch conflict opens. Now add the Caribbean sugar islands, India, Atlantic trade convoys, and Ireland to the list of things Britain had to worry about. Recruits got harder to find and the national debt soared.

Why This Matters

In 1776 Britain could throw 30,000 men at New York City. By 1780 that kind of concentration was impossible, because North America had become one theater among many. Notice what this does to the shape of the explanation: the Americans did not become strong enough to defeat Britain. Britain became too distracted to finish defeating them.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Piers Mackesy – on the war for America as a global war

Mackesy's classic strategic interpretation makes the point that Britain was fighting a global imperial war even before France formally joined — Canada, the Caribbean, Ireland, the Mediterranean, India, and the home islands were always on the ledger. Once you see the war his way, decisions that look baffling from a purely American vantage point become intelligible as choices made by people rationing finite resources across a planet.

Kathleen DuVal – on the Revolution in the Gulf South

DuVal's work shifts the camera away from the Atlantic seaboard entirely. Seen from the Gulf Coast, this is a struggle among Spanish, British, Native, African, and settler interests in which the thirteen colonies are one factor rather than the protagonist. Her research is the strongest support for the claim on this slide: American independence emerged from a multipolar world, not from a self-contained duel between Britain and its colonies.

⏸ Rank the Causes

Five things had to be true for Cornwallis to surrender. Rank them 1–5.

  • A French fleet reaches the Chesapeake
  • Washington's army still exists in 1781
  • Southern loyalist mobilization failed
  • Cornwallis camps on a peninsula
  • British politics runs out of patience

Defend your first choice in one sentence.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A ranking exercise, and the honest answer is that there is no single correct order. What the activity is really testing is whether you can explain why a cause matters, which is a much more useful skill than memorizing which one historians vote for.

Breaking It Down

A French fleet reaches the Chesapeake. The strongest candidate for first place. Without naval control, Cornwallis gets resupplied or evacuated and there is no siege at all.

Washington's army still exists in 1781. A necessary condition rather than a dramatic one. No army, no allied land force, no coalition operation — and note that this is the payoff of everything in Section I.

Southern loyalist mobilization failed. Britain's whole southern strategy assumed loyalists would hold what the army cleared. Kings Mountain and Cowpens gutted that assumption and pushed Cornwallis northward.

Cornwallis camps on a peninsula. The most contingent item on the list — a decision that only becomes catastrophic because the French fleet arrives. On its own it was a reasonable choice to await reinforcement by sea.

British politics runs out of patience. Arguably the true cause of the war ending, as opposed to the battle ending. Hold this one; it is the subject of a slide in a few minutes.

Why This Matters

Here is the thing to take away. These are not five competing explanations where one wins. They are five conditions that had to hold simultaneously. Remove any single one and Yorktown does not happen, or happens and does not matter. Historians call this overdetermination — and getting comfortable with it is a large part of thinking historically rather than looking for the one true cause.

How the Trap Closed

Cornwallis withdraws to Yorktown → de Grasse sails north
British relief fleet beaten off, 5 September → No rescue, no evacuation
Franco-American armies march 400 miles south → Surrender, 19 October 1781
Naval control, not marksmanship, decided Yorktown.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The mechanics of Yorktown, as a chain. Follow it link by link and notice how much of it is about ships rather than soldiers.

Breaking It Down

Cornwallis withdraws to Yorktown. On Clinton's orders, to a small settlement on a peninsula between the James and York rivers, to wait for the Royal Navy. Reasonable — if the navy shows up.

de Grasse sails north. A powerful French fleet leaves Saint-Domingue for Chesapeake Bay with three thousand soldiers aboard.

The relief fleet is beaten off on 5 September. The Battle of the Capes is the decisive engagement of the campaign, and most people have never heard of it because no famous painting shows it. With the bay closed, Cornwallis cannot be reinforced, resupplied, or taken off.

Franco-American armies march roughly 400 miles south. Washington first feinted at New York to freeze Clinton in place, then moved approximately 5,000 Continentals and 5,000 French troops under Rochambeau toward tidewater Virginia to join Lafayette and other allied forces already assembling there.

Surrender, 19 October 1781. Around eight thousand men.

Why This Matters

The lecture's line is that naval control, not marksmanship, decided Yorktown — and the chain shows why. A peninsula is only a trap if someone else owns the water. This is also the clearest possible illustration of the previous section: the single thing Americans could never provide for themselves is the thing that produced their most important victory.

Britain Still Held New York

Military reality

  • New York, Charleston, Savannah held
  • Canada secure
  • Considerable capacity remaining

Political reality

  • The ministry's strategy discredited
  • Parliamentary opposition strengthened
  • Government fell in 1782
Yorktown was decisive because it changed British politics, not because it exhausted British armies.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Probably the most surprising slide in the lecture. Yorktown is where the story usually stops, so it is easy to assume Britain had been militarily broken. It had not. Look at the left column.

Breaking It Down

New York, Charleston, and Savannah were still held. Canada was secure. The Royal Navy was intact. Britain retained substantial capacity to keep fighting in America, and it went on fighting France in the Caribbean and Spain at Gibraltar for another year and more.

So why stop? Because of the right column. The loss of an entire army discredited the ministry's strategy, strengthened the opposition in Parliament, and brought down the government. Lord North resigned in 1782. George III went so far as to draft an abdication message, though he never released it.

And the war had been deeply unpopular. Trade disrupted, ships lost to privateers, taxes and debt climbing, recruits scarce, invasion feared. By several measures it was one of the least popular wars in British history.

Why This Matters

Wars end when one side decides the cost of continuing exceeds the value of winning. That is a political judgment, not an arithmetic one about remaining regiments. Yorktown was decisive because of what it did in London — which brings us all the way back to the four-part distinction on slide three. Britain lost the ability to sustain the political will to restore rule, long before it lost the ability to fight.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A lesson in reading images as historical claims. Trumbull's canvas hangs in the U.S. Capitol rotunda and shapes how Americans picture the end of the war. Now count what it gets wrong and notice that the errors are not random.

Breaking It Down

French troops fill the left of the canvas. Trumbull is actually being honest here, and it is worth pausing on. In the most famous American painting of the American victory, roughly half the frame is French. The war's coalition character is sitting right there in the composition.

Cornwallis was not present. He claimed illness and sent General Charles O'Hara instead. Washington responded in kind, delegating to his own second-in-command, Benjamin Lincoln — the same officer who had surrendered Charleston in 1780. That detail is a small masterpiece of eighteenth-century status politics.

Trumbull was not present either, and he finished the painting around 1820 — nearly four decades afterward, as one of four monumental Revolutionary scenes commissioned for the Capitol.

Why This Matters

"Commemoration tidies what contingency made messy." A painting made forty years later for a national building has a job: to make the founding look dignified and inevitable. That job is incompatible with showing you a ragged, improvised, ally-dependent, nearly-lost war. Neither painting today is lying, exactly. They are both answering a question — what should Americans feel about this? — rather than the question a historian asks, which is what happened and why?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The payoff. If you can reconstruct these two columns and the box underneath, you can answer the essential question for this lecture and you have the analytical spine of the whole chapter.

Breaking It Down

Britain's problem. Occupation is not obedience. Restoring royal government meant persuading local people to expose themselves as its agents — and the army always had to move on, leaving them to face returning Patriot militias. Britain could take any place in America. It could not hold the political ground.

America's problem. The mirror image. The Revolution had enough strength to survive but nowhere near enough to compel Britain to quit. What survival bought was time, and what time bought was an alliance with a state that had a navy.

Why This Matters

Read the gold box carefully, because it is making a specific methodological claim. It is not saying "lots of factors mattered," which is what a student says when they do not want to commit. It is saying these factors were interdependent: the army's survival only mattered because an alliance eventually arrived; the alliance was only available because the army survived; the alliance only worked because loyalist restoration had failed; British constraints only became fatal because all of the above were true at once. Remove one and the others do less work. That is a real argument, and it is the kind of answer that earns credit on an exam.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Why no single school of interpretation is enough – the historiographical synthesis

Command-centered histories are right that Washington's preservation of the army was indispensable. Social and local histories are right that militias, civilians, loyalists, enslaved people, and Native nations shaped the war's reach and meaning. Imperial and global histories are right that British resources were never unlimited and that French and Spanish intervention was decisive. Political histories are right that the object of the war was legitimate rule rather than possession of ground. The strongest interpretation combines these scales instead of picking a winner — and the Independent Study section lays out the four schools side by side.

"The painting shows a man who already knows he will win." Everything in Leutze's composition says destiny: the upright posture, the light breaking, the flag, the fixed gaze.

"In December 1776 he did not." And we have the letter to prove it, from that same month. If this fails us, I think the game will be pretty well up.

Why This Matters

Historians call the mistake the painting invites hindsight bias — reading an outcome backward as though it had been guaranteed. It is genuinely hard to resist, because we know how the story ends and the participants did not. Resisting it is most of what it means to think historically.

The closing question also sets up your next class. Independence got won. That settles who governs. It does not settle who counts as a member of the thing being governed — and enslaved people, free Black communities, Native nations, women, and loyalists all had very different answers. 📖 Understanding This Slide

The Revolution as most Americans actually experienced it: not battles, but scarcity, requisition, inflation, raids, and captivity. Almost none of this appears in the paintings.

Breaking It Down

About 20,000 Americans became prisoners of war; roughly 8,500 died in British captivity. Many were confined on abandoned and decommissioned ships anchored off New York, where overcrowding, disease, inadequate ventilation, and shortages of food and clothing produced appalling mortality. The totals cover British captivity as a whole, not prison ships alone. The poet Philip Freneau, a former prisoner, accused the British of deliberately killing men they could not subdue. Congress eventually ran squalid prisons of its own, though not on the same scale.

Household income fell by more than forty percent. Sit with that number. It is a Great-Depression-scale collapse sustained across most of a decade.

The Continental dollar fell to only a few cents by 1779 and below one cent by 1781. Congress had been printing money because it had no power to tax. When the printing stopped, the army had to requisition food directly from farmers in exchange for certificates priced far below real inflation — so many farmers simply grew less.

Unpaid regiments mutinied in 1781. Fifteen hundred men of the Pennsylvania Line killed three officers and marched out of winter quarters. New Jersey troops followed; Washington had two of their leaders executed.

Why This Matters

Two things follow. First, more Americans died as prisoners than in combat — which should permanently adjust your mental image of this war. Second, the financial collapse is not a side story. A Congress that could not pay its army or its debts is exactly the problem that produces the constitutional crisis in Lectures 3 and 4. The war's economics are the seedbed of the Constitution.

Sources: National Park Service on prisoners of war; Farley Grubb, The Continental Dollar.

The South: Britain's Best Bet Fails

The plan

  • Take Charleston, then pacify the countryside
  • Recruit loyalists to hold what the army clears
  • Roll authority northward from Georgia

What happened

  • Kings Mountain and Cowpens gutted loyalist units
  • Greene traded ground for British exhaustion
  • Neighbors fought neighbors; malaria wrecked the army
The southern campaign tested the whole theory of loyalist restoration — and broke it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

After France entered, Britain shifted its weight south, where officials expected loyalist support to be strongest. This slide is the plan and the result side by side — and the result is a direct test of the argument from the first half of class.

Breaking It Down

The plan. Take Charleston, use loyalists to pacify the countryside, roll royal authority northward from Georgia, then turn on Washington. Britain actually did restore a royal government with an elective assembly in Georgia between 1780 and 1782, so this was not fantasy.

Kings Mountain and Cowpens gutted loyalist units. At Kings Mountain in October 1780, all but one of the combatants were American colonists; the Loyalist commander, Major Patrick Ferguson, was the sole British regular officer. That near-total predominance of Americans on both sides shows what "civil war" meant concretely. At Cowpens, Daniel Morgan used his militia as skirmishers out front, told them to fire twice and fall back, and destroyed Tarleton's force when it charged into the Continentals behind them.

Greene traded ground for British exhaustion. Greene lost most of his engagements and won the campaign. At Guilford Court House the British held the field and lost a quarter of their army and the strategic initiative. Then Greene went back and took the British outposts one at a time.

Malaria wrecked the army. Not a footnote. Cornwallis told Clinton he had to get to higher country to preserve his troops from sickness — which is part of why he marched north into Virginia and toward Yorktown.

Why This Matters

The southern campaign was the purest test of the theory that loyalists could hold what the army cleared. It failed, and it failed for exactly the reason slide six predicted: exposed loyalists could not be protected once the army moved on. Note too the ugliness. At Waxhaws, Tarleton's force killed many Continentals after their formation collapsed; whether a clear surrender had occurred remains disputed. After Kings Mountain, Patriot officers executed nine Loyalist prisoners following improvised trials amid a wider cycle of retaliation. Both episodes belong to the same brutal civil war, but the second was not simply a direct reply to the first.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

J. R. McNeill – Mosquito Empires: Ecology and War in the Greater Caribbean, 1620–1914 (2010)

McNeill's chapter on Yorktown has genuinely changed how scholars explain Cornwallis's defeat. His argument is that mosquito-borne disease was a strategic actor in the greater Caribbean world. Local populations had acquired resistance; newly arrived European troops had not. So British armies operating in the Carolina lowcountry wasted away in a way American forces did not. It is an excellent example of environmental history reshaping a story everyone thought was settled.

Four Ways to Explain Victory

Command

David Hackett Fischer on Trenton and Princeton; Kevin Weddle on Saratoga as a campaign. Decisions and operations mattered.

Society

Holger Hoock on wartime violence. Militias, civilians, and coercion shaped the war's reach.

Empire

Piers Mackesy and Andrew Jackson O'Shaughnessy: British commanders faced an extraordinarily hard mission, not merely their own incompetence.

Continent

Kathleen DuVal on the Gulf South: the Revolution looks different where Spanish, Native, African, and settler interests met.

The strongest account combines these scales rather than choosing among them.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Four toolkits for answering the same question. This is what a historiographical debate looks like when nobody is simply wrong — the schools are asking about different scales, and the honest answer needs all four.

Breaking It Down

Command. Decisions and operations mattered. Fischer on Trenton and Princeton; Weddle on Saratoga as a sustained campaign. Strongest where contingency and leadership genuinely turned events.

Society. Hoock on the war's violence. The Revolution was a civil war in which intimidation, property destruction, and retaliation shaped what ordinary people did. Strongest at explaining why territory could be taken but not held.

Empire. Mackesy and O'Shaughnessy. Britain was fighting globally under severe constraints, and its commanders were not merely fools. Strongest at explaining British choices that look inexplicable from an American vantage point.

Continent. DuVal on the Gulf South. Viewed from places where Spanish, Native, African, and settler interests met, the thirteen colonies are one factor among several. Strongest at breaking the habit of treating the Atlantic seaboard as the whole world.

Why This Matters

Here is the transferable skill. When you meet competing historical interpretations, the useful question is rarely "which one is right?" It is "what is each one good at explaining, and what does each one leave out?" Combining scales is not fence-sitting. It is what the evidence in this case actually supports — and it is why the closing synthesis slide insisted these factors were interdependent rather than competing.

1783 Settled Less Than It Seemed

  • Britain kept garrisons in western posts
  • Native nations rejected the transfer of their homelands
  • Loyalist refugees rebuilt lives across the empire
  • Congress inherited enormous debts and no power to tax
International recognition arrived. Questions about sovereignty, citizenship, and governmental power did not go away — they organize the rest of this chapter.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Treaty of Paris gave American diplomats almost everything they had asked for: independence, territory east of the Mississippi, fishing rights off Newfoundland. And it left four enormous problems completely unresolved.

Breaking It Down

Britain kept the western posts. Niagara, Detroit, and others sat inside the new American boundary and stayed British, justified by Congress's failure to honor treaty obligations to loyalists and British creditors. They remained a grievance for over a decade.

Native nations rejected the transfer. And why would they accept it? Britain had ceded land it did not own, without consulting the peoples who lived on it and who had not been defeated. Fighting continued in the Ohio Country, and the resulting conflict became the Northwest Indian War, in which American armies suffered major defeats.

Loyalist refugees rebuilt elsewhere. Roughly sixty thousand Loyalists became refugees elsewhere in the British Empire, including white, Black, and Indigenous exiles. In addition, Loyalist enslavers carried away about fifteen thousand enslaved people. The categories must remain distinct: some Black evacuees secured freedom, while others remained enslaved. The free Loyalist exodus amounted to roughly one person in forty and helped transform British North America, especially the future Canada.

Congress inherited enormous debts and no power to tax. Which is the thread that runs straight into the rest of this chapter.

Why This Matters

Peace treaties resolve the questions the signatories care about and postpone the rest. Independence answered "who governs?" and left "how, with what authority, and over whom?" wide open. Those three questions are Lectures 2, 3, and 4 — which is why this slide is the last thing in the deck rather than a footnote.

Source: Maya Jasanoff, Liberty's Exiles.

Colin G. Calloway – The American Revolution in Indian Country (1995)

Calloway organizes the Revolution around Native communities rather than European campaigns, and the effect is to make 1783 look completely different. From Philadelphia the treaty is a triumph. From an Ohio Valley town it is two empires trading away your homeland in a room you were not invited to. Both descriptions are accurate, which is precisely why the boundary drawn in Paris settled nothing on the ground.

Political Legitimacy

The widely shared belief that a government has the right to give orders — so that most people obey out of accepted obligation rather than fear of the nearest soldier.

This is the distinction that organizes today's lecture. Britain could compel obedience wherever its troops physically stood. It could not manufacture the belief that royal government was rightful again, because that required institutions, courts, officeholders, and neighbors willing to sustain it after the army left.

×
The Continental Army as an Institution

A force authorized by the Continental Congress, distinct from the state militias, with longer terms of enlistment and national command.

Treat it as a political fact, not only a military one. As long as it existed, thirteen separate war efforts had a common center, Congress had something to negotiate with, and European courts had a government to recognize. Its destruction would have reduced the Revolution to scattered local uprisings that no alliance could have rescued.

Saratoga

The surrender of General John Burgoyne's army in upstate New York on 17 October 1777, after a campaign that began at Fort Ticonderoga and stalled short of Albany.

The Franco-American Treaties, 1778

Two agreements signed in February 1778: a commercial treaty granting Americans favorable trading terms, and an alliance in which France recognized American independence and undertook to fight until Britain conceded it.

France also disavowed territorial ambitions on the North American continent. Vergennes brought Spain into the war against Britain the following year, though Spain never allied with the United States directly.

The Battle of the Capes

A naval engagement at the mouth of Chesapeake Bay on 5 September 1781, in which the French fleet under the Comte de Grasse drove off a British squadron sent to relieve Yorktown.

This is the hinge of the whole campaign. With the bay closed, Cornwallis could not be reinforced, resupplied, or evacuated. Washington and Rochambeau then had time to bring their armies down from the north and lay siege.

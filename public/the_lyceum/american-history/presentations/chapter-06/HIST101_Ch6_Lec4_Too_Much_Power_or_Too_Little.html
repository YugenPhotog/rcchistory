The Story You Have Probably Heard

Weak Articles → Disorder → Shays's Rebellion → The Constitution saves the nation
Every step names something real. The sequence still hides the argument.

Americans Did Not Agree There Was a Crisis

The same decade looked different depending on where you stood.

Creditors
Paper money and relief laws are theft
Farmers
Hard-money taxes are extraction
Nationalists
A union that cannot act is no union
Calling the 1780s “disorder” adopts one side's diagnosis before examining why others feared the cure.

Section I

Same Facts, Two Readings

Who pays for a revolution once the shooting stops?

Fact One: The Debt Came Due

$15.7m
Requisitioned from the states, 1781–1786
$2.4m
Actually received

Reading A

Without reliable revenue there is no public credit, no diplomacy, no honored commitment to soldiers or lenders.

Reading B

Taxes demanded in scarce specie moved wealth from cash-poor producers to eastern merchants and speculators.

Fact Two: The World Noticed

  • Britain kept the western forts and cited American treaty violations
  • Spain closed the lower Mississippi to American traffic
  • North African corsairs seized American ships
  • States wrote their own trade policies and undercut neighbors

Reading A

Independence without effective sovereignty. The republic cannot defend its own interests.

Reading B

Whose commerce would a national policy serve? Western settlers and eastern diplomats wanted opposite things.

Fact Three: States Acted

What they did

  • Issued paper money
  • Passed stay laws and tender laws
  • Offered debtor relief
  • Adjusted taxes under political pressure

Two names for it

  • Instability that violates contracts
  • Elected governments managing a depression
Some nationalists concluded the danger was not weak government but democratic state government working.

Section II

Massachusetts, 1786–1787

When is resistance a defense of the people — and when is it an attack on them?

The Courts Were the Target

  • Massachusetts taxed hard to restore its credit
  • Payment demanded in coin nobody had
  • Courts processed debt suits, seizures, imprisonment
  • Petitions failed, so Regulators closed the county courts

Shut the court, and no farm can be taken this month.

A hostile 1787 almanac woodcut — it dresses farmers as generals to make them look like a conspiracy

They Had Done This Before

Against Britain, 1774

  • Crowds prevented courts from sitting
  • Officials intimidated into resigning
  • Boycotts enforced by neighbors
  • Claimed to act for the community

Against Massachusetts, 1786

  • Crowds prevented courts from sitting
  • Same tactics, same veterans
  • Same claim to defend the public good
  • Now aimed at an elected government
Governor Bowdoin's reply: resistance appropriate against a tyrant is not appropriate against a government the people elected.

Springfield, January 1787

Congress cannot fund a force → Privately financed militia raised
Insurgents move on the arsenal → Militia fires; several killed → Rising collapses

Nationalists saw

A republic without energy. Washington and Knox feared contagious uprisings against property and credit.

But also happened

Voters removed Bowdoin, returned Hancock, and policy moderated. The political system absorbed much of the grievance.

What the Rebellion Did Not Prove

Do not call it “the first American civil war.” The Revolution itself was a civil war. The phrase inflates a state-level uprising and obscures its actual scale and character.
  • It did not mechanically cause the Constitution
  • The nationalist movement was already underway
  • What it did was crystallize a dispute and supply rhetoric
The Annapolis Convention had already called for a broader meeting in 1786.

Section III

Philadelphia Exceeded Its Instructions

Sent to repair the Articles; proposed something else

Not a Repair. A Replacement.

New powers

  • Tax
  • Regulate commerce
  • Borrow and raise forces
  • Make laws necessary and proper

New structure

  • Independent executive
  • Federal judiciary
  • Federal law supreme in its sphere
  • Acting on individuals, not just states
Under the Articles, Congress asked states. Under the Constitution, the government reaches you.

The Boldest Clause Was About Procedure

13 of 13
States required to amend the Articles
9 of 13
Conventions required to launch the Constitution
They appealed over the existing governments to the people in special conventions — using exactly the lesson Massachusetts had taught in 1780.
An extralegal break with the Articles, defended as an act of popular sovereignty. Whether that is revolution or fidelity depends on your theory of authority.

Slavery Was Structural, Not Peripheral

  • Three-fifths of enslaved people counted for representation — with no rights attached
  • The Atlantic slave trade protected from prohibition until 1808
  • Recovery of people escaping across state lines guaranteed
  • Federal power available to suppress insurrections
Delegates avoided the words slave and slavery. The legal protections were substantive anyway.
Separate two questions. That protecting slavery was necessary to the 1787 bargain is settled. Whether the document is best described as fundamentally proslavery remains genuinely disputed among historians.

Section IV

Two Fears, One Republic

Both sides invoked the Revolution. Both meant it.

Where Does the Danger Come From?

Federalists feared

  • Government too weak to act
  • State rivalry and broken treaties
  • Majority faction; unstable legislation
  • Public credit and commerce collapsing
  • A union unable to defend itself
  • Local majorities violating rights

Anti-Federalists feared

  • Government strong enough to consolidate
  • State autonomy reduced to administration
  • Elite rule insulated from ordinary citizens
  • Taxation serving creditors and merchants
  • Standing armies and coercive executives
  • Distant officials violating rights unaccountably
They disputed the location of danger far more than the value of liberty.

Richmond, June 1788

“Who authorized them to speak the language of, We, the People, instead of, We, the States?”
Patrick Henry, Virginia ratifying convention
“it is of a mixed nature; it is in a manner unprecedented”
James Madison, in reply
Henry pressed a real question. Madison's answer was to deny the choice: not wholly national, not wholly federal. Virginia ratified 89 to 79.

It Was Close, and It Was Negotiated

187–168

  • Massachusetts Federalists won by promising amendments afterward
  • North Carolina rejected first, ratified in 1789
  • Rhode Island held out until a 34–32 vote in 1790
Not a national endorsement of an unmodified document. A politically mediated authorization built on promises.

The Losers Shaped the Settlement

Federalist position

A list of rights is unnecessary, even dangerous: the government holds only delegated powers, and enumerating some rights implies the rest do not exist.

Anti-Federalist reply

Broad clauses and real institutional power require explicit restraints. Write them down.

Congress proposed ten amendments in 1789; they were ratified in 1791. Anti-Federalists lost the vote and still altered the constitutional order.

So Which Threat Was Greater?

The Constitution did not answer that question.

It institutionalized the argument.

  • Power to govern — and enumerated limits on it
  • Popular authorization — and filters on popular action
  • A stronger union — and protections for slavery within it
Better question than democratic-or-not: which forms of popular power did it enable, which did it restrain, and whose security counted as the republic's?

Back to the Painting

Christy painted it in 1940, for an anniversary.

Calm, orderly, finished, inevitable —
every one of those is a choice the painter made.

In 1787 it was contested, close, and unfinished. That is not a flaw in the founding. It is what founding a republic by argument looks like.

Independent Study

Going Deeper

Large states, small states • Federalist No. 10 • The historians' quarrel • What the Bill of Rights did not do

The Fight That Nearly Ended It

Virginia Plan

  • Both houses apportioned by population
  • Legislature choosing executive and judiciary
  • Power to negate state laws

New Jersey Plan

  • One vote per state, as before
  • But Congress gains import duties and trade power

The Connecticut Compromise

  • Population in one house, state equality in the other
  • Two-year House terms, six-year Senate terms
  • An Electoral College borrowed from a Maryland device

Delaware's Gunning Bedford warned that if the large states dissolved the Confederation, small states would find a foreign ally to do them justice.

Ironically, small states then ratified fastest — equality in the Senate made a strong center safe for them.

Madison Inverts Two Thousand Years

Received wisdom

  • Republics must be small
  • Virtue requires close community
  • Size brings corruption and collapse

Madison's claim

  • Small republics are the unstable ones
  • A majority faction forms easily and crushes minorities
  • In a large, diverse republic, no faction assembles a majority easily
Notice what the argument protects: minorities against majorities. Anti-Federalists heard the same argument and concluded it protected elites against ordinary citizens. Both readings are available in the text.

The Historians' Quarrel

Popular conflict

Woody Holton and Terry Bouton: debtor politics and elite reaction. Some nationalists wanted to restrain state-level democracy.

State-building

Max Edling: the Constitution as construction of a fiscal-military state capable of national action.

Real incapacity

George William Van Cleve: the Confederation's structural weakness produced a genuine crisis of governance.

Contingency

Pauline Maier on the breadth of ratification debate; Michael Klarman on the framers' departures from democratic preference; Saul Cornell on Anti-Federalism as a serious tradition.

The Constitution can plausibly be read as both a fulfillment of popular sovereignty and a restraint on popular politics the framers distrusted.

What the Bill of Rights Did Not Do

  • It did not apply to state governments
  • It did not touch slavery, or the three-fifths clause, or the 1808 protection
  • It did not extend the vote to anyone
  • It did not recognize Native sovereignty
What it does demonstrate is that constitutional losers can still reshape a settlement — and that stronger national power became legitimate partly because its opponents forced limits into writing.

Specie

Hard money — gold and silver coin — as opposed to paper currency, promissory notes, or payment in goods and labor.

This is the practical heart of the postwar quarrel. A farm family could be asset-rich and cash-poor: land, livestock, and crops but almost no coin. A tax demanding specie was therefore not merely a bill; it forced a sale, and often a foreclosure. Requiring specie was a policy choice about whose interests the state would protect.

×
Stay Laws and Tender Laws

A stay law postpones the date a debt legally comes due. A tender law requires creditors to accept something other than coin — paper money, or land — in payment.

About half the states issued paper money in the 1780s, and many passed relief measures of this kind. Massachusetts refused both and raised taxes instead, which is why the pressure there turned into an uprising rather than legislation.

×
Regulators

What the Massachusetts insurgents of 1786–1787 generally called themselves. The word matters: to regulate meant to correct a government that had strayed, not to overthrow it.

Daniel Shays became the best-known figure and gave the rising its name, but the movement was much broader than one man and varied considerably from town to town. Job Shattuck led the court closure at Concord in Middlesex County.

The Necessary and Proper Clause

The provision authorizing Congress to make laws necessary and proper for carrying out its listed powers. Often called the catchall clause.

Its ambiguity is the whole fight. Federalists read it as ordinary implementation: you cannot tax without also creating offices to collect. Anti-Federalists read it as an opening through which almost any power could be claimed, since almost anything can be argued to be necessary for something enumerated. Both readings survived into American constitutional politics.

×
The Three-Fifths Clause

Free and slave states agreed to count three-fifths of the enslaved population when apportioning both representation in the House and direct taxes.

Be precise about what this did. It did not declare that enslaved people were three-fifths of a person in some general moral sense. It gave slaveholding states additional representation based on people they denied all political rights — amplifying the political power of enslavers in the national government.

A similar three-fifths idea had been proposed for the Articles of Confederation years earlier and was never ratified.

×
Anti-Federalists

Opponents of ratification — a diverse coalition rather than an organized party, including farmers, rural artisans, militia veterans, and some prominent politicians such as Patrick Henry and George Mason.

The labels invert the plain meaning. The Constitution's supporters took the name Federalists while seeking a substantially more national government; their opponents were often defending the more traditional federal arrangement of sovereign states. The real dispute was between competing forms of federalism.

Saul Cornell's work treats Anti-Federalism as a coherent constitutional tradition defending local knowledge, juries, rotation, and restraints on central power — while also noting that localism could shelter oppressive state practices, including slavery.

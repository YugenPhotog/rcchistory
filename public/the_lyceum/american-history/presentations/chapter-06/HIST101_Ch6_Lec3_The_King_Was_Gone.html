HIST 101, Chapter 6, Lecture 3 — The King Was Gone. Who Had the Right to Rule?

Image description: A page from the manuscript Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776, written in iron gall ink on laid paper in a clerk's hand. Dense eighteenth-century script fills the sheet with no printing, seal, or decoration.

Cold open. Ask one question and let it sit: what makes this piece of paper binding on anybody? Do not accept "because they signed it." Push once — who gave them the authority to sign? That is the lecture. Then advance.

July 1776 Dissolved a Government

What ended

  • Allegiance to the Crown
  • The authority of royal governors
  • Charters as the source of law

What did not begin

  • Any right to tax
  • Any right to punish
  • Any right to conscript
You can prove a government unjust. That does not make the next one rightful.

Elections Do Not Solve It

Suppose you elect the legislature. It can still:

  • Confiscate property
  • Punish dissenters
  • Redraw representation in its own favor
  • Override a minority's rights
If the legislature is sovereign, no authority stands above it. So what does?

Two Diagnoses

Pennsylvania 1776 • Massachusetts 1780

Constitutional design begins with naming your greatest fear

⏸ You Are Writing a Constitution

Pick the danger you would design against first.

Fear A

Rulers who separate themselves from the people and cannot be reached.

Fear B

A momentary majority that acts fast and crushes whoever is in the way.

Hands up for A. Hands up for B. Two states answered differently.

Pennsylvania: Fear the Rulers

Where it came from

  • Committees, militia associations, county meetings
  • Ordinary men already exercising real authority
  • A convention of outsiders, not the old assembly

What it built

  • A unicameral house, elected annually
  • No senate — senates looked like aristocracy
  • A plural executive council, no strong governor
  • Rotation, publicity, a Council of Censors
Liberty protected by responsiveness: keep government close enough to touch.

What It Could Not Do

  • Critics: sudden majorities could legislate without deliberation
  • Supporters: a second chamber only reproduces social hierarchy
  • Politics turned bitter; a new constitution followed in 1790
Deeper problem: the constitution came from the same revolutionary process that elected the legislature — so the people's authority and the legislature's were hard to tell apart.
Resist the shortcut “radical democracy failed.” Pennsylvania had a coherent theory of danger, drawn from real mobilization. Its weaknesses were tradeoffs, not proof that ordinary people cannot govern.

Massachusetts Tried and Failed

1778
The legislature drafts a constitution and sends it to the towns
5 to 1
The margin by which voters rejected it
The objection was procedural: an ordinary legislature has no business writing the fundamental law it will then operate under.

So They Changed the Procedure

Voters elect delegates for one purpose only → Convention drafts
Towns consider it article by article → Constitution takes effect, 1780
Now the constitution comes from an authority the legislature does not possess — and therefore stands above it.

Massachusetts: Fear Concentrated Power

  • A declaration of rights placed first
  • Two legislative chambers
  • An independently elected governor with real powers
  • An independent judiciary
“to the end it may be a government of laws and not of men”
Massachusetts Declaration of Rights, Article XXX, 1780

Rights come before the frame of government — the order is an argument

Same Revolution, Opposite Diagnoses

Pennsylvania, 1776

  • Greatest danger: insulated rulers
  • One chamber
  • Weak plural executive
  • Frequent elections, direct accountability
  • Constitution close to ordinary politics
  • Liberty through responsiveness

Massachusetts, 1780

  • Greatest danger: concentrated power — including legislative
  • Two chambers
  • Independent governor
  • Representation plus institutional checks
  • Constitution made by a separate convention
  • Liberty through balance and higher law
Massachusetts did not abandon popular sovereignty. It made the people sovereign by denying sovereignty to any elected body.

The Idea Worth Keeping

Officials exercise power.

They do not own it.

The people authorize → The constitution creates offices → Officeholders act under limits
This is constituent power — and it is unlike Britain, where no law could invalidate an act of Parliament.

⏸ Pause & Reflect

A constitution is now supposed to bind
the government that operates under it.

In 1780, who enforces that?

Who Counted as “The People”?

Usually inside

  • Free adult men
  • With property, or taxpaying standing
  • Qualifications varying by state

Outside or at the margin

  • Enslaved people
  • Most women
  • Many free Black men
  • Native nations; loyalists under disability
Hold both at once. “The people” was expansive as a theory of authority and narrow as an institution. Neither half is the whole truth.

Scale It Up

Thirteen Sovereigns, One Union

Apply the same fear of distant power to the nation

A Firm League of Friendship

  • One vote per state, whatever its size
  • No national executive, no general judiciary
  • No power to tax individuals
  • No comprehensive power over commerce

Ratified at last on 1 March 1781 — months before the fighting ended.

Printed in 1777 — it took until 1781 for every state to agree

The Clause That Set the Terms

“Each state retains its sovereignty, freedom and independence, and every power, jurisdiction, and right, which is not by this confederation expressly delegated to the United States in Congress assembled.”
Articles of Confederation, Article II
Written by people who had just fought a war against a distant legislature claiming supremacy. This is consistency, not confusion.

It Worked, and It Didn't

What it did

  • Prosecuted the war to its end
  • Negotiated the peace
  • Conducted diplomacy
  • Organized the western domain

What it could not do

  • Compel states to pay requisitions
  • Stop conflicting state trade policies
  • Enforce treaty obligations
  • Prevent foreign powers exploiting division
The institutions best at preventing central tyranny were also worst at acting together. That is a tradeoff, not an oversight.

The Confederation's Best Work

Land Ordinance, 1785

  • Townships six miles square
  • Thirty-six sections of 640 acres
  • Auction from a dollar an acre

Northwest Ordinance, 1787

  • Three to five future states
  • Assembly at 5,000; statehood at 60,000
  • Civil liberties, schools, no slavery
Territories would become equal states, not permanent colonies. Applied to settlers, this rejected colonial dependency outright.

Anti-Colonial and Imperial at Once

For settlers

Temporary territorial status, then admission on an equal footing. No one is ruled as a dependency forever.

For Native nations

The same ordinance surveyed and sold land still occupied and defended by peoples who had never consented to U.S. jurisdiction.

Not a checklist of territorial stages. The Ordinance's real significance is the pairing: equal statehood for settlers, federal authority to distribute Indigenous land.

How Americans Built Governments They Owed Obedience To

  • They separated the act of authorizing from the act of governing
  • They wrote that authorization down and put it above officials
  • They disagreed sharply about which danger to design against
  • They extended the same suspicion to the union itself
The lasting achievement was procedural: a constitution can be made by a distinct act of the people and bind everyone it empowers. The unresolved problem was who the people were.

Two Fears, Still Unsettled

Pennsylvania feared rulers.
Massachusetts feared concentrated power.

By 1786 a third fear arrives:
a government too weak to govern at all.

Next: which fear should a republic take more seriously — and who gets to decide?

Independent Study

Going Deeper

Virginia goes first • Two waves of state constitutions • Rehabilitating the Articles • Franklin, Vermont, and secession

Virginia Went First — and Halfway

June 1776

  • First permanent republican state constitution
  • Declared powers should be separate and distinct
  • Then made the legislature supreme anyway
  • It chose the governor, the council, and the judges

Mason's declaration of rights

  • Life, liberty, property, pursuit of happiness
  • Trial by jury, due process, religious liberty
  • Condemned hereditary privilege
  • Reworded to exclude enslaved people
Legally it was only a statute, no stronger than any other law. It was widely copied anyway — a reminder that constitutional force can lag behind constitutional language.

A Learning Curve, Not a Blueprint

First wave

  • Drafted by legislatures or provincial congresses
  • Weaker checks, dominant assemblies
  • Constitution-making not clearly separated

Second wave

  • Special conventions and popular consideration
  • More elaborate restraints and stronger executives
  • New Hampshire followed the model from 1784
Robert Williams's comparative framework. Gordon Wood traces the same movement as a transformation in how Americans understood sovereignty itself.

Was the Confederation a Mistake?

Merrill Jensen

Read the Articles as a genuine radical republican order in its own right, not a rough draft of something better. Useful for resisting the habit of judging 1781 by 1789.

Jack Rakove

Studies Congress as a working political arena with real coalitions and constraints, rather than a list of formal weaknesses.

The teachable position: the Articles embodied coherent principles, achieved consequential results, and imposed limits that grew harder to manage. All three.

The Union Nearly Came Apart Anyway

  • Tennessee settlers briefly declared a separate state of Franklin
  • Separatist feeling ran strong in Kentucky
  • Western Pennsylvanians talked of going it alone after Spain closed the Mississippi in 1784
  • Vermont, refused admission, sounded out Canadian officials
Neither Congress nor eastern legislatures could solve western problems. Distance was not an abstraction — it was a standing invitation to secede.

Unicameral Legislature

A lawmaking body with a single chamber, rather than two houses that must both agree.

The argument for it in 1776 was social, not merely procedural. Britain had a House of Lords because Britain had a nobility. Pennsylvania radicals reasoned that a republic with no aristocracy needed no second chamber — and that creating one would invent an American upper class with the power to block the people's will.

Constituent vs. Constituted Power

Constituent power is the authority to create a government. Constituted power is the authority a government exercises once created.

Keeping them apart is the whole trick. If the legislature can rewrite the constitution by ordinary law, then the constitution is just another statute and nothing limits the legislature. If a specially elected convention does the writing and the people consider it, the constitution comes from somewhere the legislature cannot reach — which is what makes it higher law.

The Articles of Confederation

The first frame of government for the United States. Completed by Congress in 1777, ratified by the last state and in force from 1 March 1781, superseded in 1789.

Congress could conduct diplomacy, declare war, and manage western lands, but it could only request money from the states and had no general power over commerce. Amendment required unanimous approval — which is why replacing the Articles later had to be done by a route their own text did not allow.

The Northwest Ordinance, 1787

Passed by the Confederation Congress in New York while the Constitutional Convention sat in Philadelphia. It created territorial government north of the Ohio River and a path to admission as states equal to the original thirteen.

It protected civil liberties, provided for education, and prohibited slavery in the territory — while also including a provision for returning people escaping from labor. Southern delegates voted for it despite the antislavery clause, and the timing has long led historians to suspect a bargain with the slavery clauses being written into the federal Constitution at the same moment.

Its declaration that good faith should be observed toward Native peoples sat alongside a federal claim to survey and sell land those nations still held and defended.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Welcome to Lecture 3, the last lecture of Chapter 10. Lecture 1 was about reading and Lecture 2 was about worship. Today is about watching: theaters, minstrel shows, prizefights, and P. T. Barnum’s museum. These stages are where Americans acted out who they thought they were, and who they thought did not belong.

Breaking It Down

The road map: First, how factory time created “leisure time” and how middle-class and working-class people used it differently. Second, the city as a stage and a new, performative public sphere. Third, blackface minstrelsy, the era’s most popular entertainment and a powerful engine of racism. Fourth, blood sports and boxing. Fifth, Barnum and the art of humbug.

Why This Matters

Entertainment can seem like the least serious part of history. This lecture argues the opposite. What people paid to see shows what they feared, wanted, and believed about class, race, gender, and democracy.

The Creation of Leisure Time

Pre-1815: Traditional Work Patterns

  • Seasonal rhythms governed agricultural work
  • Task-based labor in artisan shops
  • Work and life intermingled
  • No clear division between "work time" and "free time"

Post-1815: Industrial Time

  • Factory bells regulate the working day
  • Wage labor creates fixed schedules
  • Separation of work time from free time
  • The working day creates its opposite: Leisure Time

Structured Time Off:

  • Sundays (Sabbath observance)
  • Evenings after work
  • Saturday half-holidays (emerging practice)

New Question: What do you do when you're not working?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Ask a farmer in 1800 what he does in his “free time” and he might look puzzled. This slide shows how factory work invented free time by first inventing unfree time.

Breaking It Down

Before about 1815: Most work followed the seasons and the task. A farmer worked dawn to dusk at harvest and slowed down in winter; a shoemaker worked until the boots were done. Work and home were often the same place, so there was no sharp line between work time and free time.

Industrial time: In mills and shops, bells and clocks set the hours. Workers were paid for their time rather than for finishing a job, and the employer, not the worker, controlled the schedule. Many artisans resented this loss of independence.

Leisure is born: Once the workday had fixed edges, the hours outside it became a separate category. Sundays (with strong pressure to spend them in church), evenings, and, gradually, Saturday half-holidays became “time off.”

The new question: What do you do when you are not working? The answer soon became a marker of class and morality.

Why This Matters

This slide sets up the whole lecture. Once leisure existed, businesses rushed to sell entertainment, and reformers worried about whether people were using their free time “properly.” Arguments about leisure became arguments about who was respectable and what kind of people Americans should be.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

E. P. Thompson – “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism”

Thompson, a British historian, contrasted older task-oriented work with the clock discipline of industry. His 1967 essay explains why factory time felt like a loss of control to workers, a feeling American artisans shared.

Sean Wilentz – Chants Democratic

Wilentz shows New York artisans fighting to keep control of their own time and labor. Their taverns and clubs were places to organize as well as relax.

📖 Understanding This Slide

If leisure became a language, the new middle class wanted to speak it correctly. This slide shows how clerks, shopkeepers, and professionals used “improving” amusements to show the world they were respectable.

Breaking It Down

Who they were: Clerks, shopkeepers, small manufacturers, and professionals such as the doctor in the picture. They worked with their heads more than their hands and had some leisure time and spare money.

Leisure as a status marker: Where you went signaled who you were. A natural-history museum or a lecture said “I am educated and improving myself.” A rowdy theater said something else.

The feminization of respectability: The presence of middle-class women made a place seem safe and proper, so venues advertised that they were suitable for “ladies.” Women’s taste helped draw the line between “refined” and “vulgar.”

Improving amusements and prices: Museums, lyceum lectures, and “respectable” theater. Barnum’s museum cost 25 cents. At a respectable house like the Park Theatre, box seats cost about a dollar and the pit 50 cents, while the cheapest gallery seats were 25 cents. (The slide was corrected: it had listed galleries, the cheapest seats, at 50 cents to a dollar.)

Geography of respectability: Broadway theaters catered to the middle class, and the Astor Place Opera House (opened 1847) was an elite uptown venue that expected patrons to dress formally. In May 1849 it became the site of the deadly Astor Place Riot, a clash fueled partly by these class tensions.

Why This Matters

The closing line is the key idea: respectable recreation fused class aspiration with moral performance, on public display. For the middle class, fun was never just fun. It was a way to prove you deserved your place.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Stuart M. Blumin – The Emergence of the Middle Class

Blumin argues that class was made through everyday practices like housing, dress, and leisure, not just income. Choosing “improving” amusements was one way the middle class drew a line between itself and manual workers.

Lawrence W. Levine – Highbrow/Lowbrow

Levine shows that in the early 1800s Shakespeare was popular entertainment for everyone. The division into “high” and “low” culture was built later, partly by audiences who wanted to set themselves apart.

  • Day laborers: Casual work, no job security

📖 Understanding This Slide

To understand working-class leisure, start with working-class life. This slide shows the crowded, exhausting, and dangerous conditions of the antebellum city.

Breaking It Down

Immigration: In the decade after 1845, roughly three million immigrants arrived, many fleeing Ireland’s Great Famine and economic and political turmoil in the German states. Most arrived poor and crowded into the cheapest housing. (Chapter 14 covers immigration in depth.)

Work: Factory hands worked twelve to fourteen hours around unguarded machines; dockworkers did heavy labor with no guarantee of work the next day; day laborers lived job to job. There was no workers’ compensation, sick pay, or pensions, and many children worked to help their families.

Housing: Families crowded into single rooms in subdivided houses and early tenements. There was little clean water or sewage removal, so cholera, typhus, and tuberculosis spread quickly, and death rates in poor wards were far higher than in wealthier ones.

Why This Matters

Now imagine your few free hours after a fourteen-hour day. You probably want release, company, and fun, not a moral lecture. That is why working-class leisure looked so different from middle-class leisure, and why reformers worried so much about it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Sean Wilentz – Chants Democratic

Wilentz shows how harsh conditions and the decline of independent craft work pushed New York workers toward collective action. Taverns, clubs, and theaters were places where class consciousness, a shared sense of being workers with common interests, could grow.

The Working-Class City:
Bowery · Fire Companies · Five Points

  • Visible working-class culture
  • Bowery Theatre: 12¢ tickets (affordable)
  • Gangs & swagger: The "Bowery B'hoy" style

  • Volunteer brigades: civic service + identity
  • Ward politics: ties to Democratic machines

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide introduces three places where working-class New Yorkers built their own leisure world: loud, physical, proud, and very different from middle-class “improvement.” Each also has a deep-dive slide in the appendix.

Breaking It Down

The Bowery: A street and neighborhood in lower Manhattan that became the capital of working-class culture. The Bowery Theatre’s cheapest seats cost about 12½ cents, and its audiences cheered, booed, and talked back to actors. The “Bowery b’hoy” was a young working man with a flashy style and a swagger.

Fire companies: Before New York created a paid fire department in 1865, volunteer companies fought fires. They were also social clubs and bases of Democratic ward politics, where pride, rivalry, and courage were on display.

Five Points: To middle-class writers, the most infamous slum in America, full of vice and danger. It did have cockfights, dance halls, and saloons, but it was also home to Irish and Black families who built churches and mutual-aid networks to survive.

Why This Matters

These three sites show working-class people creating a culture that was rowdy, public, masculine, and political all at once. That combination is exactly what alarmed middle-class reformers, and the clash between the two cultures runs through the rest of the lecture.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Sean Wilentz – Chants Democratic

Wilentz treats Bowery culture and fire companies as part of working-class politics, not just entertainment.

Lawrence W. Levine – Highbrow/Lowbrow

Levine would remind you that “vulgar” was a label applied by outsiders. The Bowery audience’s noisy participation was an older, democratic way of watching a show.

The Hunger for Spectacle

Urban Density Creates Mass Audiences

Concentration of people in cities produces unprecedented demand for novelty and sensation

Media Saturation

Newspapers and posters saturate public space; the city itself becomes a theater

Performance of Identity

Citizens perform their identity through choices of amusement—where you go and what you see defines who you are

Americans across classes crave spectacle but disagree on what forms are legitimate

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide is a pivot. After two slides about class differences, it points to something everyone shared: a hunger for spectacle, meaning exciting, eye-catching, dramatic entertainment.

Breaking It Down

Urban density: New York grew from about 60,000 people in 1800 to more than 500,000 in 1850. That many people packed together created mass audiences and a constant demand for novelty. Theaters seating thousands could fill night after night.

Media saturation: Penny papers and posters covered the city, hyping shows, scandals, and rivalries. The phrase “the city itself becomes a theater” means daily life felt like a performance.

Performance of identity: Where you went and what you watched announced who you were: the opera house, the Bowery Theatre, or Barnum’s museum.

Why This Matters

The last line is the key tension: Americans of every class craved spectacle but disagreed about which kinds were legitimate. When those disagreements met crowded streets and a sensational press, small cultural differences could explode, as the Astor Place Riot of 1849 showed.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Lawrence W. Levine – Highbrow/Lowbrow

Levine argues that the ranking of spectacles into respectable and disreputable was not natural. It was built over the nineteenth century as elites and the middle class sought to separate themselves from the crowd.

Setting the Stage:
The Urban Public Sphere

📖 Understanding This Slide

This divider opens a short section on the urban public sphere: how the city’s streets, posters, and crowds changed what it meant to take part in public life.

Breaking It Down

Connect to Lecture 1: There, the public sphere was built mostly from print: newspapers and pamphlets read and debated. Here, we add bodies and places: crowds, theaters, and streets.

What to watch for: The next two slides argue that in the 1840s, being seen and judging performances in public became a way of claiming membership in “the people.”

Why This Matters

Keep the word public in mind. Who counts as the public, and where it gathers, will turn out to be a political question, not just a cultural one.

The City as Theater

Media Turn Streets into Stages

Playbills and Posters

Colorful advertisements plaster walls and buildings

Street Performance

Walking Broadway or the Bowery means encountering performance at every turn

📖 Understanding This Slide

Walk down Broadway or the Bowery in the 1840s and you are surrounded by performance: posters on every wall, newsboys shouting, street musicians, and crowds dressed to be noticed. This slide calls the city a theater where everyone is both performer and audience.

Breaking It Down

Playbills and posters: Advertising for theaters, Barnum’s exhibits, lectures, and fights covered buildings in bold color. Cities had never looked so loud.

Newspapers: Penny papers reviewed plays, reported scandals, and created celebrities and rivalries, turning city life into an ongoing story.

Street performance: Broadway was the place to promenade in fine clothes and be seen; the Bowery was where young working men showed off their style. In a city of strangers, people judged one another by appearance and behavior.

Why This Matters

When daily life feels like a stage, identity becomes something you perform, class differences become visible on the street, and public conflicts carry an audience. That helps explain why fights over theaters and amusements in this period became so intense.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Karen Halttunen – Confidence Men and Painted Women

Halttunen shows that middle-class Americans worried a great deal about strangers who might be performing a false identity, such as the smooth-talking con man. Advice books taught readers how to spot sincerity, a worry that makes sense in the city-as-theater.

The Public Sphere Becomes Performative

From rational debate toward spectacle and display

  • Democracy means having the right to watch, judge, and participate in public amusements
  • Citizens act by seeing and being seen, not just by reading and debating
  • Identity is performed through your choice of amusement and your physical presence

New Rules of Citizenship

Citizenship Through Spectatorship

Participating by attending, watching, and judging public entertainment

Identity Through Performance

Your identity is performed through where you go and how you behave there

Representing "The People"

Different entertainment venues = different claims about who represents "the people"

Public culture becomes contested terrain for representing "the people"

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide makes a bold argument: in the 1840s, American democracy itself was becoming more about spectacle and presence and less about quiet reading and debate. It is a theory slide, so take it one box at a time.

Breaking It Down

The traditional ideal: The public sphere (from Lecture 1) was imagined as a space of rational debate in coffeehouses, salons, and newspapers, where the best argument wins.

Democracy as spectacle: In practice, many Americans took part in public life by showing up: at parades, torchlight rallies, and theaters. Being present, watching, and judging became forms of participation. When a Bowery audience cheered or hissed, it acted like a sovereign people.

New rules of citizenship: Citizenship through spectatorship means taking part by attending and judging. Identity through performance means showing who you are by where you go and how you behave. Representing “the people” means different venues made rival claims to speak for the real American public.

Why This Matters

The final line is the stakes: public culture became contested ground for deciding who “the people” were. In the Astor Place Riot of 1849, supporters of the American actor Edwin Forrest attacked the opera house where the British actor William Macready was performing. The militia fired into the crowd and at least 22 people died. A quarrel over actors had become a fight over class, nationality, and democracy.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jürgen Habermas – The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere

Habermas described the ideal of a rational public and feared that mass commercial culture would replace debate with consumption. This slide asks whether that is a decline or just a different, noisier form of democracy.

Lawrence W. Levine – Highbrow/Lowbrow

Levine uses Astor Place to show how audiences split by class and how elites later taught audiences to sit quietly. Taming the audience, he argues, was also a way of limiting who could participate.

Habermas: Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere (1962)

Habermas and The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere

Jürgen Habermas's The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere (1962) is a landmark work in political theory and sociology that traces the rise and fall of what he calls the "bourgeois public sphere" in 18th-century Europe.

Habermas describes the public sphere as a realm between private life and state authority where citizens gather—in coffeehouses, salons, and through newspapers—to rationally debate matters of public concern. This space was revolutionary because people could discuss politics and hold power accountable through reason and argument, not social status.

The transformation Habermas tracks is essentially a decline: as mass media and consumer culture emerged in the 19th and 20th centuries, this rational-critical debate gave way to passive consumption and manipulation. Public discourse became dominated by advertising, entertainment, and manufactured opinion rather than genuine deliberation. Citizens became consumers, and the public sphere lost its critical edge.

The book's influence is enormous—it's foundational for understanding democracy, media, and civil society. Critics have challenged Habermas for idealizing the past and ignoring how the "public sphere" excluded women, workers, and minorities. But his core insight—that democracy depends on spaces for free, rational public conversation—remains vital for thinking about everything from social media to political polarization today.

Origins and Rise of Blackface Minstrelsy

What Was Blackface Minstrelsy?

  • White performers in burnt-cork makeup
  • Mocked Black speech, dance, music, and mannerisms
  • Became the most popular U.S. entertainment form of the 19th century

How It Began

  • Thomas D. Rice performs “Jim Crow” (1820s–30s)
  • Claims to imitate Black performers he observed
  • Virginia Minstrels (1843) create the first full minstrel troupe

Typical Show Format

  • Act I: Semicircle jokes & songs
  • Olio: Variety acts, dancers, novelty pieces
  • Finale: Comic skit or farce

Stock Characters: “Jim Crow” (rural buffoon), “Zip Coon” (urban dandy), “Mammy”.

Historical racist imagery shown for educational analysis.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This section deals with painful material. Blackface minstrelsy was white performers darkening their faces with burnt cork to mock Black people for laughs, and it was the most popular American entertainment of the nineteenth century. We study it, as the slide notes, to analyze how racism was made into entertainment, not to repeat its jokes. The images are historical racist material shown for that purpose.

Breaking It Down

What it was: White men in burnt-cork makeup and exaggerated costumes imitated and ridiculed Black speech, music, and dance, using a fake dialect and grotesque stereotypes.

How it began: Thomas D. Rice became famous in the late 1820s and early 1830s for a song-and-dance routine called “Jump Jim Crow.” Rice and his promoters claimed he copied a Black man he had seen; historians treat that origin story as legend. In 1843 the Virginia Minstrels, four white performers in New York led by Dan Emmett, staged the first full evening-length minstrel show.

The show format: In the first part, the troupe sat in a semicircle trading jokes and songs, with an “interlocutor” (master of ceremonies) in the middle and two “endmen” on the sides. The middle section, the olio, was a variety section of individual acts. The finale was a comic skit, often set on an imaginary plantation or mocking a serious play.

Stock characters: “Jim Crow,” a rural enslaved man shown as foolish and content; “Zip Coon,” a free Black city man mocked for dressing well and using big words; and the “Mammy,” a loyal enslaved caregiver. Each was designed to deny Black people’s intelligence and dignity.

Why This Matters

Minstrelsy turned racism into a night out. Its stereotypes reached millions, North and South, and outlived slavery itself. The name of one character, Jim Crow, later became the name for the system of legal segregation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eric Lott – Love and Theft: Blackface Minstrelsy and the American Working Class

Lott argues that minstrelsy mixed fascination with Black culture (“love”) with ridicule and appropriation of it (“theft”). White audiences enjoyed music and dance they associated with Black people while insisting on Black inferiority.

Why Minstrelsy Mattered

Cultural Function

1) United White Audiences Across Class Lines

  • Working-class and elite venues
  • North and South attended
  • Forged a shared “American” culture through racism

2) Cultural Borrowing with Racist Caricature

  • White performers adopted Black music, dance, and humor
  • Simultaneously portrayed Black people as inferior, childlike, comic
  • Consumed the culture while denying the humanity of its creators

3) Justified Slavery and Racial Hierarchy

  • Stage myths of “happy” enslaved people
  • Normalized claims of “natural” racial inferiority
  • Made racism entertaining—therefore ordinary

📖 Understanding This Slide

Why does a historian care about minstrel shows? This slide gives three reasons. Each shows entertainment doing political work.

Breaking It Down

1) It united white audiences across class: Minstrel shows played in working-class halls and fashionable theaters, North and South. Laughing at the same racist jokes gave white people of different classes a shared identity, an “imagined community” of whiteness.

2) Borrowing with caricature: White performers used music, dance, and humor drawn partly from Black traditions while portraying Black people as childlike and inferior. They consumed the culture while denying the humanity of the people who created it.

3) Justifying slavery and hierarchy: Shows staged the myth of the “happy slave” and the idea that free Black people were ridiculous. Making racism funny made it seem normal.

Why This Matters

Notice the slide’s phrase “comedy as an engine of ideology.” Ideology means a set of beliefs that makes a social order seem natural. Jokes do this quietly: people repeat what made them laugh without examining it. Black Americans saw this clearly. In 1848 Frederick Douglass condemned blackface performers in his newspaper, The North Star, as people who had “stolen from us a complexion denied to them by nature” to make money by pandering to white audiences’ prejudice.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Alexander Saxton – The Rise and Fall of the White Republic

Saxton shows how popular culture, including minstrelsy, helped build whiteness as a political identity tied to the Democratic Party and to the idea that the republic belonged to white men.

David R. Roediger – The Wages of Whiteness

Roediger argues that many white workers, especially immigrants, used racial superiority as a kind of psychological “wage.” Minstrelsy told them that however poor they were, they were not Black.

The Central Contradiction of Minstrelsy

Simultaneous Fascination and Dehumanization

  • White audiences loved Black music and dance
  • But could only enjoy it through racist caricature
  • Cultural borrowing combined with dehumanization

What This Reveals About American Culture

  • White Americans drawn to Black expressive culture
  • But unwilling to treat Black people as equals
  • Pattern continues: jazz, blues, rock, hip-hop — borrowed without equal respect

Historical Significance

  • First truly national mass entertainment (North + South)
  • Template for American variety entertainment (→ vaudeville → Hollywood → TV)
  • Shaped American racial imagination for generations

Mass entertainment and racism emerge together in the U.S.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide names the central contradiction of minstrelsy: white audiences loved Black music and dance but could enjoy them only through racist caricature.

Breaking It Down

Fascination and dehumanization: The attraction was real, and so was the contempt. Minstrelsy let white audiences have the music while denying the musicians equality.

What it reveals: White Americans were drawn to Black expressive culture yet unwilling to treat Black people as equals. The slide suggests the pattern recurs with jazz, blues, rock, and hip-hop, Black-created forms that often earned white performers more money and respect than their creators. Test that claim against evidence as you study later periods.

Historical significance: Minstrelsy was arguably the first truly national mass entertainment. Its format of songs, comedy, and variety acts fed into vaudeville, film, radio, and television, and its stereotypes shaped American racial imagination for generations. Black performers also worked within it; the dancer William Henry Lane, known as “Master Juba,” won fame in the 1840s, sometimes performing with white troupes.

Why This Matters

The bottom line, mass entertainment and racism emerge together in the U.S., is uncomfortable but important. American popular culture was born partly on the minstrel stage, so its history cannot be told honestly without this chapter.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eric Lott – Love and Theft: Blackface Minstrelsy and the American Working Class

Lott’s title captures this contradiction: “love,” the desire for what white audiences imagined Black culture offered, and “theft,” the taking of that culture while degrading its creators.

Blackface Minstrelsy

What Is Minstrelsy?

Blackface minstrelsy: White performers in blackface makeup performing racist caricatures of Black Americans

  • Emerges in the 1830s–1840s
  • Becomes the most popular form of American entertainment by mid-century
  • Performed across class lines—in elite theaters and working-class venues

The Minstrel Show Format

  • Part 1: Semicircle of songs, jokes, and comic banter (interlocutor & endmen)
  • Part 2: "Olio" or variety section with individual acts
  • Part 3: One-act farce or skit
  • Stock characters: Jim Crow (rural slave), Zip Coon (urban dandy)

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide reviews what minstrelsy was and how a show was organized. Use it as a reference card for the section.

Breaking It Down

Definition: White performers in blackface performing racist caricatures of Black Americans. Most performers were men, and their audiences were mostly white.

Timing and reach: The form took shape in the 1830s and 1840s and was the most popular American entertainment by mid-century. It played in elite theaters and in working-class halls alike.

The format: Part 1 was the semicircle of songs, jokes, and comic banter between the interlocutor and the endmen (nicknamed “Tambo” and “Bones” after the tambourine and bone castanets they played). Part 2, the olio, offered variety acts. Part 3 was a one-act farce, often set on a romanticized plantation. (The slide was corrected to match this standard structure; plantation scenes usually came in the closing skit.) Songwriters such as Stephen Foster wrote hits for the minstrel stage, including “Oh! Susanna” (1848).

Stock characters: Jim Crow (the “contented” rural slave) and Zip Coon (the mocked urban dandy). Together they sent a double message: slavery was harmless, and freedom made Black people absurd.

The posters: The two show posters are from later in the century (the 1870s–1890s), when minstrelsy had become big business. The “Zip Coon” sheet music cover dates from the 1830s.

Why This Matters

Seeing the format helps you see the design. Every part of the show, from the endmen’s jokes to the plantation skit, repeated the same lesson about racial hierarchy, packaged as harmless fun.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Robert C. Toll – Blacking Up

Toll’s early history of minstrelsy traced how the shows grew from a few performers into a national industry and how their content shifted with politics, becoming more openly proslavery as sectional conflict rose.

W. T. Lhamon Jr. – Raising Cain

Lhamon argues that early blackface grew out of mixed working-class neighborhoods where Black and white New Yorkers met. That complicates the story but does not excuse what minstrelsy became.

The Ideology of Minstrelsy

Racist Stereotypes

Minstrelsy portrays Black Americans as:

  • Childlike, ignorant, lazy
  • Happy in slavery
  • Incapable of citizenship or self-governance
  • Comic, ridiculous, non-threatening

Political Function

Minstrelsy serves to:

  • Justify slavery by depicting enslaved people as content
  • Oppose abolition by mocking Black claims to freedom and equality
  • Define whiteness by contrast—to be white is to be NOT Black
  • Unite white working and middle classes around shared racial identity

The Paradox of Fascination

White Americans are simultaneously attracted to and repulsed by Blackness

  • Desire: Black music, dance, style, and expressiveness fascinate white audiences
  • Anxiety: This attraction threatens white identity and hierarchy
  • Solution: Minstrelsy allows whites to "play Black" while maintaining racial boundaries

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide moves from what minstrelsy showed to what it did. Its stereotypes were not random; each one supported slavery and white supremacy.

Breaking It Down

The stereotypes: Black people were shown as childlike, ignorant, and lazy; happy in slavery; incapable of citizenship; and comic rather than threatening. If people are imagined as children, then someone else must rule them. That was the proslavery argument in disguise.

Political functions: The shows justified slavery with images of contented enslaved people, mocked abolitionists and free Black people’s claims to equality, defined whiteness by contrast (to be white was to be “not that”), and united white workers and middle-class people around a shared racial identity.

The paradox of fascination: White audiences were attracted to music, dance, and style they associated with Black people, and that attraction made them anxious about their own identity. Blackface offered a “solution”: whites could “play Black” for an evening and then wash off the cork and return to whiteness. The makeup itself marked the boundary.

Why This Matters

This slide teaches that racism is not only individual dislike. It is a system, and culture helps keep it running. Remember too that the real people behind the caricatures were organizing, writing, and resisting at the same time, as Black abolitionists like Frederick Douglass made clear.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eric Lott – Love and Theft: Blackface Minstrelsy and the American Working Class

Blood Sports, Boxing,
and Working-Class Masculinity

📖 Understanding This Slide

This divider opens the section on blood sports and boxing, the roughest corner of working-class leisure and one of the clearest windows into ideas about manhood.

Breaking It Down

Key terms: Blood sports are entertainments built around animals or people fighting and bleeding. Masculinity means a society’s ideas about how men should be and behave.

What to watch for: How did working-class men define manhood differently from the middle-class ideal of the sober, self-controlled “self-made man”?

Why This Matters

Pay attention to the theme of the body. In this section, a man’s body, its courage and toughness, becomes a way to claim worth and respect.

Blood Sports as Rough Amusement

Types of Blood Sports

  • Cockfighting: Roosters fitted with metal spurs fight to the death
  • Dogfights: Pit bulls and terriers battle in underground rings
  • Rat-baiting: Dogs kill as many rats as possible in a pit
  • Bare-knuckle fights: Human boxing without gloves, few rules

Social Meaning

Working-class masculine sociability and defiance of middle-class restraint

  • Held in Five Points and Bowery taverns, cellars, and back rooms
  • Heavy gambling on outcomes
  • Often illegal yet widely practiced
  • Entrepreneurs monetize despite reformers' opposition

Sets the stage for the culture of the prize ring

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide describes amusements that horrified reformers and thrilled participants. Read it as evidence about values, not to celebrate cruelty.

Breaking It Down

The sports: Cockfights (roosters fitted with sharp metal spurs), dogfights, rat-baiting (a terrier killing as many rats as possible in a pit against the clock), and bare-knuckle fights between men with few rules and no gloves.

Where and how: They were held in taverns, cellars, and back rooms in neighborhoods like the Bowery and the waterfront wards. Heavy betting was central. A famous later example was Kit Burns’s Sportsman’s Hall on Water Street in the 1860s.

Social meaning: For participants these events built male friendship and proved toughness. They openly defied middle-class ideals of restraint and sympathy.

Illegal but common: Laws against them were weakly enforced, and proprietors made money from admission, drinks, and bets. In 1866 Henry Bergh founded the ASPCA in New York, and the anti-cruelty movement began targeting these sports.

Why This Matters

Blood sports show that class conflict was about culture, not just wages. Reformers wanted to teach workers sympathy and self-control; many workers rejected that project as an attack on their pleasures and independence. The same values of courage, risk, and loyalty carried straight into the prize ring on the next slide.

Boxing and the Culture of the Prize Ring

The Sporting Fraternity

Bare-knuckle prizefighting: Illegal, staged in secret venues

"The fancy" or "sporting men"—subculture of fight enthusiasts

Who Were the Sporting Men?

Diffuse subculture around prizefighting spanning urban America

  • Working-class men: butchers, mechanics, firemen, tavern keepers
  • Across different ethnic backgrounds but united by sporting culture
  • Found in every major city from New York to New Orleans

Shared Culture of "The Fancy"

  • Sports newspapers: Spirit of the Times and similar publications
  • Tavern gatherings: Discussion and planning of matches
  • Insider slang: Specialized vocabulary marking membership
  • "Flash" style: Distinctive dress and swagger
  • Fan identity: Following fights, knowing fighters, attending matches

Prizefighting creates a working-class male counterpublic—an alternative public sphere with its own values and heroes

📖 Understanding This Slide

Bare-knuckle prizefighting was illegal in most places and enormously popular. This slide shows that it was more than fights. It was a whole subculture, with its own heroes, newspapers, slang, and style.

Breaking It Down

The prize ring: Fights were staged secretly, on riverbanks, barges, or across state lines, to dodge the law. Under the London Prize Ring Rules (1838), a round ended when a man went down, and bouts could last dozens of rounds. In 1849 Tom Hyer beat James “Yankee” Sullivan in Maryland in a fight followed nationwide.

“The fancy”: The fans and backers of prizefighting, or “sporting men,” included butchers, mechanics, firemen, and tavern keepers from many ethnic backgrounds, found in cities from New York to New Orleans.

Shared culture: The Spirit of the Times (founded 1831) and other sporting papers covered fights and horse races. Taverns served as meeting places, insider slang marked membership, and sporting men wore a flashy “flash” style.

Why This Matters

The last line uses a big term: counterpublic, an alternative public sphere built by people outside the respectable mainstream, with its own values and heroes. The sporting world proved that working-class men could build national institutions entirely outside middle-class control, which is exactly why reformers found it threatening.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Elliott J. Gorn – The Manly Art

Gorn shows prizefighting as a ritual of an alternative manhood (courage, loyalty, honor) opposed to the middle-class self-made man.

Nancy Fraser – “Rethinking the Public Sphere”

Fraser, a political philosopher, coined “subaltern counterpublics” for groups who build their own public spaces when excluded from the dominant one. The sporting fraternity fits her model.

P. T. Barnum:
The Art of Humbug

📖 Understanding This Slide

This divider introduces P. T. Barnum, America’s most famous showman, and his specialty, humbug: entertaining deception in which audiences half-know they might be fooled.

Breaking It Down

Who he was: Phineas Taylor Barnum (1810–1891), a Connecticut-born promoter who ran New York’s most popular museum from 1841 to 1865 and later founded a circus.

What to watch for: Barnum mixed education, entertainment, and fraud, and he drew huge cross-class crowds. He also built his early fame on exploiting an enslaved woman and later on displaying people with disabilities.

Why This Matters

Barnum pulls together the lecture’s themes: spectacle, class, race, and the market. Ask throughout whether he expanded democracy or exploited it.

Barnum's American Museum (1841–1865)

Overview

Established in 1841, Barnum's five-story museum drew an estimated 38 million admissions during its 24-year run

What Was Inside?

  • Natural curiosities: Stuffed animals, geological specimens, natural oddities
  • Living "human wonders": People with unusual physical characteristics
  • Theatrical performances: Plays, musical acts, variety shows
  • Scientific lectures: Educational talks (allegedly)
  • Elaborate hoaxes: Fake mermaids, questionable artifacts

Hybrid Institution

A unique combination of:

  • Moral lecture hall
  • Curiosity cabinet
  • Theater
  • "Freak show"

Cross-Class Appeal

  • Broadway location: Respectable enough for middle-class families
  • Low admission (~$0.25): Affordable for working-class visitors
  • Cross-gender appeal: Women and children explicitly welcomed
  • Marketed as educational and moral

📖 Understanding This Slide

Imagine a five-story building on Broadway where, for a quarter, you could see stuffed animals, a living giraffe, a “mermaid,” a little person billed as a general, and a moral play, all in one afternoon. That was Barnum’s American Museum.

Breaking It Down

Overview: Barnum bought an existing museum in late 1841 and reopened it as his own in 1842 at Broadway and Ann Street. It burned in 1865. Estimates put total paid admissions at about 38 million, counting repeat visits, more than the U.S. population of the time. (The slide was corrected from “over 15 million visitors.”)

What was inside: Natural-history specimens; living “human wonders” such as Charles Stratton, billed as “General Tom Thumb”; plays and variety acts in a theater Barnum called the “Lecture Room” so that church-going families would feel comfortable; lectures; and hoaxes.

A hybrid institution: Part moral lecture hall, part curiosity cabinet, part theater, and part “freak show,” all mixed in one space.

Cross-class appeal: A Broadway address and a ban on drinking reassured middle-class families. A 25-cent ticket (children half price) let working people come too. Barnum advertised that the museum was suitable for women and children, and he framed everything as educational.

Why This Matters

Barnum found a way to sell one product to nearly everyone by blending respectability with sensation. That mix became a model for American mass entertainment. But note who was on display: human “exhibits” were often people with disabilities or from other cultures, and the ethics of that business are part of his story.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Neil Harris – Humbug: The Art of P. T. Barnum

Harris describes Barnum’s audiences as enjoying an “operational aesthetic”: the pleasure of figuring out how something worked or whether it was real.

Rosemarie Garland-Thomson – Extraordinary Bodies

Garland-Thomson, a disability studies scholar, argues that freak shows taught audiences to define themselves as “normal” by staring at people presented as abnormal.

What Is Humbug?

Humbug: A blend of education, fraud, and emotional manipulation

  • Not simple deception—audiences know they might be fooled
  • The desire to be deceived is part of the pleasure
  • Spectacle of belief and doubt performed publicly
  • Value measured by experience rather than authenticity

The question isn't "Is it real?" but "Is it worth my quarter?"

📖 Understanding This Slide

Barnum did not just fool people. He invited them to wonder whether they were being fooled and charged admission for the fun of finding out. This slide calls that humbug, and it starts with a case that is anything but fun.

Breaking It Down

Joice Heth (1835): Heth was an elderly, blind, enslaved Black woman. In 1835 Barnum paid $1,000 for the right to exhibit her, in effect buying her, and advertised her as George Washington’s 161-year-old nurse. When interest faded he planted a rumor that she was a machine. After she died in February 1836, he sold tickets to a public autopsy, where a surgeon judged she was probably no older than about 80. This was Barnum’s first big success, built on the exploitation of a Black woman’s body.

The Feejee Mermaid (1842): A dried monkey’s upper body sewn to a fish tail. Barnum’s team planted letters from a fake British naturalist, “Dr. Griffin,” and the debate over whether it was real drew crowds.

What humbug is: Not simple fraud. Audiences knew they might be tricked; the pleasure lay in believing and doubting at once and in arguing about it publicly. Value came from the experience, not from authenticity. Barnum later wrote a book, The Humbugs of the World (1865).

Why This Matters

The slide’s last line, “Is it worth my quarter?”, captures a shift in how Americans thought about truth and value. Humbug could be playful when the object was a stitched-together fish. When the “exhibit” was a human being, as with Joice Heth, the game rested on cruelty and racism.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Benjamin Reiss – The Showman and the Slave

Reiss reconstructs the Heth exhibition and shows how it fed on debates about slavery, race, and memory of Washington. He argues that Barnum’s career began with a spectacle of racial exploitation.

James W. Cook – The Arts of Deception

Cook explains why audiences enjoyed being tricked. In an age when old authorities were weakening, judging a clever fraud let ordinary people feel like expert detectives.

Barnum's Rhetoric and Politics

Democratic Achievements

  • Low prices: Makes culture accessible across class lines
  • Broad access: Welcomes women, children, working class
  • Participatory culture: Audiences actively debate and judge
  • Meritocracy of pleasure: Your enjoyment is what matters, not your refinement

Problematic Aspects

  • Exploitation: Displays people with disabilities as "freaks"
  • Racial mockery: Perpetuates racist stereotypes (Joice Heth)
  • Deception for profit: Capitalizes on gullibility
  • Commercialization: Everything becomes commodity

Barnum represents both the promise and the problems of democratic commercial culture

He democratizes access but also exploits vulnerability. He empowers audiences but also manipulates them. He challenges elite culture but creates new hierarchies of his own.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Was Barnum a democratic hero or an exploitative huckster? This slide sets out both sides and asks you to hold them together.

Breaking It Down

Democratic achievements: Low prices opened culture to many classes; he welcomed women, children, and working people; audiences actively debated and judged; and personal enjoyment, not refinement, became the measure of value.

Problematic aspects: He displayed people with disabilities as “freaks”; he profited from racial mockery and the exhibition of an enslaved woman, Joice Heth; he deceived people for money, including those who truly believed him; and he turned curiosity, wonder, and human beings into products to sell.

Why This Matters

The conclusion box says Barnum democratized access but exploited vulnerability, empowered audiences but manipulated them, and challenged elites while creating new hierarchies of his own (the savvy viewer versus the “sucker”). Can you celebrate the first set of achievements without excusing the second? There is no single right answer, but a strong answer weighs both with evidence.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A closer look at the Bowery b’hoy, a working-class style and attitude so recognizable that it became a stage character.

Breaking It Down

The look: A red flannel shirt (often a fireman’s), black trousers, boots, a tall hat tipped at an angle, a cigar, and hair slicked forward with soap into “soap locks.” His female counterpart, the “Bowery g’hal,” had her own bold style.

The attitude: Swagger, fierce loyalty to his fire company and neighborhood, and open rejection of middle-class respectability. He valued toughness and street smarts over book learning and spent his wages on clothes and fun rather than saving.

On stage: In 1848 Benjamin Baker’s play A Glance at New York, starring Frank Chanfrau as “Mose the fireboy,” opened at Mitchell’s Olympic Theatre and was a huge hit, followed by many sequels. Real b’hoys watched themselves on stage and copied the performance back.

Why This Matters

The b’hoy shows working-class culture as deliberate and self-aware, not just “failed” middle-class culture. He also reminds you that identity can be shaped by entertainment: life imitated theater, and theater imitated life.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Sean Wilentz – Chants Democratic

Wilentz links the b’hoy to the decline of artisan independence. Young men who could no longer expect to become master craftsmen asserted pride through style, loyalty, and toughness.

Christine Stansell – City of Women

Stansell notes that b’hoy culture could also mean control over working-class women. Freedom from middle-class rules did not mean equality at home.

  • Masculine performance: Physical courage, loyalty, and toughness on display

📖 Understanding This Slide

Volunteer fire companies show how leisure, civic duty, politics, and manhood blended together in the antebellum city.

Breaking It Down

Civic service: In crowded wooden cities, fires were frequent and deadly. Volunteer companies, each with a hand-pumped engine and a firehouse, were essential until New York created a paid department in 1865.

Social clubs: Firehouses were places to gather, drink, and belong. Companies had names, uniforms, parades, and fierce loyalties.

Political machines: Companies were tied to Democratic ward politics and Tammany Hall. William M. “Boss” Tweed rose through the Americus Engine Company, “Big Six,” around 1850. Firemen turned out voters and sometimes muscle.

Competition and violence: Rival companies raced to fires and sometimes fought over who would put them out, while buildings burned.

Masculine performance: Running into burning buildings proved courage before an audience of neighbors.

Why This Matters

Fire companies gave working-class men dignity, community, and political power through institutions they ran themselves. When reformers pushed to replace them with a professional department, they were also trying to reduce that independent power.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Amy S. Greenberg – Cause for Alarm

Greenberg shows how volunteer fire companies shaped urban manhood and politics, and how their decline reflected changing ideas of what a respectable man should be.

Paul A. Gilje – The Road to Mobocracy

Gilje traces how crowd violence in New York, including brawls involving fire companies, became a common form of political expression.

Five Points

Five Points: America's Most Infamous Slum

  • Intersection of five streets in Lower Manhattan
  • Home to Irish and Black New Yorkers in extreme poverty
  • Notorious in middle-class imagination as site of vice and danger
  • Actually: Complex community with families, churches, mutual aid societies
  • Underground economy of entertainment and survival

📖 Understanding This Slide

A closer look at Five Points, the neighborhood middle-class Americans loved to fear, and the real community that lived there.

Breaking It Down

The place: In lower Manhattan, the meeting point of Anthony, Orange, and Cross streets (today roughly Worth, Baxter, and Park) formed five corners. The area was built on the filled-in Collect Pond, whose damp ground made housing cheap and unhealthy.

The people: Poor Irish immigrants and Black New Yorkers lived side by side, sometimes in the same buildings, along with other newcomers. Crowding and disease were severe.

The reputation: Journalists, reformers, and tourists described it as a den of vice. Charles Dickens toured it in 1842 and wrote about it in American Notes. Middle-class “slumming” visits turned poverty into a spectacle. In 1850, Methodist women opened a mission there to reform the neighborhood.

The reality: Families, churches, mutual aid, and an informal economy of saloons, dance halls, and small trades. Dance halls such as the one run by Pete Williams hosted Black and Irish dancers, including William Henry Lane (“Master Juba”), whose mixing of styles helped shape American tap dance.

Why This Matters

Five Points teaches you to read sources critically. Most descriptions were written by outsiders with reasons to exaggerate. The neighborhood was poor and often dangerous, but it was also a place where people built lives, culture, and community.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Tyler Anbinder – Five Points

Anbinder argues that the neighborhood’s reputation exceeded its reality and highlights its cultural creativity and the resilience of its residents.

Leslie M. Harris – In the Shadow of Slavery

Harris shows that Black New Yorkers faced both cooperation and hostility from white neighbors, so neither a story of pure solidarity nor one of pure conflict fits.

Physical Mastery as Democratic Metaphor

Virtue Proven in the Ring

Rather than by birth or wealth, a man's worth is demonstrated through physical courage and skill

The Masculine Body as Site of Value

The body becomes the site of value and citizenship—physical prowess equals democratic legitimacy

Rejection of Elite Values

Rejection of "refinement" and self-restraint as elite values—toughness, not polish, defines the man

Democratic Ideology of the Prize Ring

In the ring, all men are equal—only strength, skill, and courage matter. Birth and wealth count for nothing. The working-class body becomes a democratic symbol.

The prize ring offers an alternative vision of American democracy—one based on physical prowess rather than property or education

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide makes prizefighting explicitly political. In the ring, the argument goes, a man’s worth comes from his own body and courage, not from birth, wealth, or education.

Breaking It Down

Virtue proven in the ring: Aristocracies valued birth, and the middle class valued property and self-discipline. The ring valued courage, skill, and endurance, qualities a poor man could have.

The body as a site of value: If physical prowess proves worth, then working men who lack money and schooling can still claim respect. This echoes the older republican ideal of the citizen-soldier who proves his citizenship by risking his body.

Rejecting “refinement”: Toughness, not polish, made the man. Middle-class restraint looked soft or fake from this point of view.

The democratic ideology of the ring: In theory, two men stripped down and faced each other as equals. In practice, training, backers, and promoters mattered, so the “equality” was partly myth, and it was equality for men only.

Why This Matters

The bottom box calls the ring an alternative vision of democracy. Compare it with the middle-class vision from Lecture 2: one measured worth by character, sobriety, and property, the other by courage and toughness. Both left out women entirely.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Elliott J. Gorn – The Manly Art

Gorn reads the prize ring as working-class men’s claim to dignity in an economy that was stripping away their independence.

Michael Kimmel – Manhood in America

Kimmel shows that American men of different classes competed over what manhood meant, setting the self-made man against the tough physical man.

Democratization vs. Exploitation

📖 Understanding This Slide

The last deep-dive slide asks you to judge Barnum’s biggest legacy: did he democratize curiosity and emotion, or commercialize them?

Breaking It Down

Everyone can play: You did not need education or refinement to join the game of belief and doubt. Twenty-five cents and curiosity were enough.

Performing sophistication: Arguing with friends and strangers about whether an exhibit was real let ordinary visitors feel like smart, critical judges.

Commercializing wonder: Barnum sold feelings such as wonder, shock, and amusement. Shared experiences, multiplied by newspapers, created a public of strangers with common reference points.

The persistent tension: Did Barnum empower audiences or manipulate them? Did he open culture to everyone or turn human dignity into a commodity?

Why This Matters

You live in Barnum’s world. Viral hoaxes, reality TV, and influencer culture all mix participation, entertainment, and doubt about what is real. Studying Barnum helps you ask the same questions of today’s media: who profits, who is on display, and is the experience worth what it costs?

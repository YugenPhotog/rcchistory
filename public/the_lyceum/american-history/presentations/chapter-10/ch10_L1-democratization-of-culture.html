Lecture 1

The Democratization of Culture:
Print, Literacy, and the New Public

Popular Culture in America, 1815–1850

📖 Understanding This Slide

Welcome to Lecture 1 of Chapter 10, Popular Culture in America, 1815–1850. Picture the newsroom on this slide: crowded, noisy, full of people who expect to read the news every day. That expectation was new. This lecture tells the story of how cheap print turned ordinary Americans into a reading public, and asks who got left out.

Breaking It Down

The big question: What happened when ordinary people, not just wealthy elites, gained everyday access to newspapers, books, pictures, and public lectures?

The roadmap: We start in 1815, define two key ideas (popular culture and the public sphere), then follow the penny press, cheap books, mass-produced pictures, the lyceum lecture circuit, and party newspapers. We end by asking whom this “democratization” actually reached.

Why This Matters

Everything else in Chapter 10 builds on this lecture. In Lecture 2 the same hunger for feeling and participation shows up in revival religion, and in Lecture 3 it shows up on the stage. Keep one question in your pocket the whole time: democratization for whom?

Key Concepts

What is "Popular Culture"?

  • Culture produced for and consumed by ordinary people (not just elites)
  • Commercially produced and distributed for mass audiences
  • Accessible, affordable, entertaining
  • Distinguished from "high culture" — BUT boundaries are blurring

What is "Democratization of Culture"?

  • Expanding cultural access beyond traditional elite gatekeepers
  • Lower costs, new technologies (steam press), new institutions (lyceums)
  • Culture becomes market-based rather than patronage-based

CRITICAL QUESTION: Democratization for whom?
Who gains access and who remains excluded?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before we tell the story, we need two tools. Think of them as the lenses on a pair of glasses: one helps you see what kind of culture we are talking about, and the other helps you see how it changed.

Breaking It Down

Popular culture: culture made for and enjoyed by ordinary people: cheap newspapers, novels, songs, prints, and shows. It is usually sold in a market, so it has to be affordable and entertaining enough that people will pay for it.

High culture: the arts and learning that elites treated as refined, such as classical learning, fine art, and serious literature. The slide says the boundary was blurring: in this period Shakespeare was popular entertainment, and lecture halls mixed philosophy with stage demonstrations.

Democratization of culture: the widening of access beyond old gatekeepers (wealthy patrons, college-educated ministers, and gentlemen editors). Three forces drive it: lower costs, new technology such as the steam-powered press, and new institutions such as the lyceum. Culture shifts from patronage (a rich sponsor pays) to the market (many buyers pay a little).

Why This Matters

The gold box is the lecture’s critical question. “Democratization” sounds like pure good news, but access was never equal. As you study each slide, ask who could afford it, who could read it, and who was legally or socially shut out.

📊 CLICK FOR STATISTICS

Why would the federal government subsidize newspaper distribution?

📖 Understanding This Slide

In 1815 the War of 1812 had just ended, and Americans felt a surge of national pride. This slide sets the stage: a country that was still mostly rural but already had a large reading population and a remarkable mail system that carried news everywhere.

Breaking It Down

The turning point: Nationalism after the war, rising literacy, growing towns and markets, and faster steam-powered printing all arrived together. Institutions that had served a small elite began opening up.

The Post Office Act of 1792: Congress set very low postage for newspapers (1 cent for up to 100 miles and 1.5 cents beyond, when a letter cost 6 to 25 cents) and let printers exchange papers with each other free. Small-town editors filled their pages with stories clipped from exchange papers, so news from Washington reached frontier towns. That is why the slide calls it an “information superhighway.”

The statistics popup: The network grew from 75 post offices in 1790 to 8,450 in 1830 and 18,417 in 1850. By 1840 post roads stretched more than 155,000 miles. By the 1830s newspapers made up most of the weight of the mail but paid only a small share of its costs, so letter-writers and the Treasury were in effect subsidizing the news.

Why This Matters

The cream box asks why the government would pay to spread newspapers. The founders believed a republic needs informed citizens, so the post office was a deliberate investment in democracy. Notice, though, that it was a government choice: the people who ran the mail could also decide what not to carry, as abolitionists learned in 1835.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard R. John – Spreading the News: The American Postal System from Franklin to Morse

John argues that the post office was the most important branch of the early federal government in most Americans’ lives. For this slide, his point is that the national “public” was built on government infrastructure, not just on private enterprise.

Slide 4 of 15

Understanding the Public Sphere

What is the Public Sphere?

  • A social space where private individuals discuss public matters
  • Distinct from private life (family, home)
  • Distinct from the state (government)
  • Created through communication—newspapers, pamphlets, lectures
  • Access based on literacy and participation (ideally)

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide gives you a key concept you will use all semester: the public sphere. Think of it as an imaginary town square. It is not your home and it is not the government; it is the space in between, where people talk about common problems.

Breaking It Down

A social space for public talk: Private people come together, in person or through print, to discuss public matters such as elections, wars, and moral questions.

Distinct from private life and from the state: Your family dinner table is private; Congress is the state. The public sphere sits between them, where citizens form opinions that the state then has to reckon with. The diagram on the right shows these three zones.

Created through communication: Newspapers, pamphlets, letters, and lectures are what make the public sphere. No print, no shared conversation among strangers.

Access “ideally” open: In theory anyone who could read and argue could join. The word “ideally” is a warning that practice fell short, which is the subject of Slide 6.

Why This Matters

Why use a concept like this? It lets us see that newspapers and lectures were not just entertainment. They were where Americans practiced self-government. When access to print widened, the circle of people who could take part in public debate widened too.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jürgen Habermas – The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere

The German philosopher Habermas described how coffeehouses, salons, and newspapers in eighteenth-century Europe created a space for reasoned public debate. He also argued that commercial mass media later turned citizens into consumers. Keep that worry in mind when we reach the penny press.

Slide 5 of 15

How Print Creates the Public Sphere

The Power of Print

  • Newspapers circulate information to geographically dispersed readers
  • Readers imagine themselves as part of a larger community of "the public"
  • Print enables debate, discussion, and formation of public opinion
  • Penny press makes participation affordable (1¢ vs 6¢)
  • Lyceums bring public lectures to ordinary citizens

CONCRETE EXAMPLE:

A farmer in Ohio and a merchant in Boston both read about the same Senate debate in their local newspapers. Though they've never met, they now share knowledge and can discuss the same political issue. This is the public sphere in action.

📖 Understanding This Slide

How does a stack of newspapers create a public? This slide answers with a simple example: two strangers, hundreds of miles apart, reading the same story on the same day.

Breaking It Down

Circulation to scattered readers: Newspapers carried the same information to people who would never meet.

Imagining a community: Readers began to picture themselves as part of a larger group, “the public” or “the nation,” made up of people reading the same things at the same time.

Debate and public opinion: Once people share information, they can argue about it. Letters to editors, party papers, and tavern conversations turned shared news into public opinion.

The penny press and lyceums: A one-cent paper (instead of six cents) and cheap public lectures made taking part affordable for many more people. Both get their own slides later.

Why This Matters

The farmer in Ohio and the merchant in Boston are the heart of this lecture. Print made it possible for a huge, spread-out country to hold one conversation. That shared conversation is a big part of how Americans came to feel like one nation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Benedict Anderson – Imagined Communities

Anderson argued that nations are “imagined communities”: you will never meet most of your fellow citizens, yet you feel connected to them. He pointed to newspapers as a key reason. Reading the same paper at the same moment is a daily ritual that makes the nation feel real. The Ohio farmer and Boston merchant are a perfect example.

Slide 6 of 15

The Public Sphere: Who's Excluded?

Key Question: Who has access to this "public" sphere?

  • ✓ White men (primary participants—voting rights, literacy, leisure time)
  • ◐ Some middle-class women (limited roles—reading circles, moral reform societies)
  • ✗ African Americans (largely excluded, create separate spheres—Freedom's Journal, 1827)
  • ✗ Enslaved people (legally barred from literacy in most Southern states)
  • ✗ Working poor (economic barriers—time, cost, limited literacy)

"Democratization" is partial and contested—access remains structured by race, class, and gender.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now the hard question: who actually got into this “public” town square? The checkmarks, half-circles, and X’s on this slide sort Americans by how much access they had. The answer is that democratization was real but partial.

Breaking It Down

White men (✓): By the 1830s nearly all white men could vote, most could read, and many had at least some time and money for newspapers. They were the main participants.

Some middle-class women (◐): Women were readers, writers, and organizers of reading circles and moral reform societies, but they could not vote and faced criticism for speaking in public.

African Americans (✗): Free Black Americans were largely shut out of white institutions, so they built their own. Freedom’s Journal (New York, 1827) was the first newspaper owned and edited by African Americans.

Enslaved people (✗): Most Southern states passed anti-literacy laws, especially after David Walker’s Appeal (1829) and Nat Turner’s rebellion (1831), making it a crime to teach enslaved people to read.

The working poor (✗): Long workdays, tight budgets, and limited schooling kept many laborers and poor immigrants on the edges.

Why This Matters

The gold box states the lecture’s thesis in miniature: access was structured by race, class, and gender. Notice that excluded groups did not simply wait. They built counterpublics, their own newspapers, churches, and societies. When you study any “democratization,” always ask who built the doors and who held the keys.

BY THE NUMBERS

📊 VIEW STATISTICS
  • ~1,000 newspapers by 1835
    (vs. ~200 in 1800)
  • ~90% of white adults literate by 1840 (census)
  • Lower in the South; barred for most enslaved people

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide shows the scale of the print revolution: new machines, a new way of paying for newspapers, and a huge jump in the number of papers and readers.

Breaking It Down

Technological innovation: Steam-powered presses printed far faster than hand presses, machine-made paper was cheaper, and stereotyping (click the word on the slide) cast a whole page of type as one metal plate so popular books could be reprinted quickly and cheaply.

Economic innovation: Advertising, not subscriptions, became the main source of income. A paper that sells ads can sell copies cheaply, and more readers means higher ad prices.

Social impact: Print stopped being a luxury and became an everyday commodity, something you buy, read, and toss, like a snack.

By the numbers: The U.S. went from roughly 200 newspapers in 1800 to about 1,000 by 1835. When the 1840 census first asked about literacy, it counted roughly nine in ten white adults as able to read and write, among the highest rates in the world, though lower in the South. Book prices fell from a dollar or more to as little as a quarter for cheap editions. Treat the figures as estimates; nineteenth-century counts were rough.

Key example: Benjamin Day’s New York Sun (1833) proved a one-cent daily could succeed. The next slide explains how.

Why This Matters

The point is that technology and money worked together. Machines made print possible at scale; advertising made it profitable. That combination is the ancestor of nearly every mass medium you use today, from TV to social media, which are also paid for by advertisers.

Slide 8 of 15

The Penny Press: A New Business Model

The Innovation

  • Newspapers sold for 1¢ (not 6¢)—affordable to working-class readers
  • Sold on streets by newsboys, not by subscription
  • Advertising revenue replaces subscriber fees
  • Mass circulation = mass influence

The Players

  • New York Sun (1833) – Benjamin Day
  • New York Herald (1835) – James Gordon Bennett
  • New York Tribune (1841) – Horace Greeley

Why would selling papers on the street change what gets published?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The penny press was a business revolution. Imagine your favorite news site suddenly charging one-sixth of what it used to, and paying for itself with ads and street sales. That is what happened in New York in the 1830s.

Breaking It Down

1¢ instead of 6¢: Older papers cost six cents and were sold by yearly subscription to merchants and lawyers. A penny paper was affordable to clerks, apprentices, and laborers.

Newsboys, not subscriptions: Day hired boys to buy papers in bulk and sell them on street corners, shouting the headlines. The photo of newsboys is from later in the century, but the street-sale model started here.

Advertising pays the bills: More readers meant more advertising revenue.

The players: The Sun (1833, Benjamin Day), the New York Herald (1835, James Gordon Bennett), and the New-York Tribune (1841, Horace Greeley). Bennett loved scandal; Greeley aimed for a more serious, reform-minded cheap paper.

Why This Matters

The cream box asks the key question. If you have to grab a passerby’s attention every single morning, you print what grabs attention: crime, fires, courtroom drama, human-interest stories. Selling on the street changed not just who read the news but what counted as news.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael Schudson – Discovering the News

Schudson argues that the penny press invented modern “news” as a product: timely, about ordinary people, and sold to a mass market. Before the 1830s, papers mostly served parties and merchants. After, they served readers and advertisers, and that shift still shapes journalism.

Sensationalism: Where's the Line?

What the Penny Press Published

  • The Great Moon Hoax (1835): “scientists” discover winged humanoids on the moon
  • Helen Jewett murder case (1836): sex worker’s death becomes daily drama
  • Crime, scandal, spectacle — anything that sells papers

CRITICAL QUESTION:
Where is the boundary between truth and entertainment?

Impact on Democracy

  • Ordinary readers become participants in shared public conversation
  • Journalism shifts from “information” to “performance”
Use mouse wheel or ↑ / ↓ to scroll inside the box

📖 Understanding This Slide

Once papers competed for pennies on the street, they found that sensation sold. This slide gives two famous cases and asks where the line between truth and entertainment falls.

Breaking It Down

The Great Moon Hoax (1835): In late August the Sun ran a series claiming that the astronomer Sir John Herschel had seen forests, animals, and winged, human-like “man-bats” on the moon. It was invented, most likely by reporter Richard Adams Locke. Many readers believed it at first, and the paper’s sales soared.

The Helen Jewett murder (1836): Jewett, a young woman working in a New York brothel, was killed in April 1836. Bennett’s Herald described the scene in vivid detail, and the papers fought for weeks over whether the accused clerk, Richard Robinson, was guilty. A jury acquitted him. A real woman’s death became daily entertainment.

Impact on democracy: Ordinary readers joined a shared public conversation, arguing about these stories everywhere. But journalism also shifted from “information” toward “performance,” news staged to keep an audience hooked.

The images: The scroll box pairs a Moon Hoax illustration with a later crime picture, showing how long this sensational style lasted.

Why This Matters

There is no easy answer to the gold box question, and that is the point. The penny press gave working people a voice and an audience, but it also rewarded exaggeration and turned suffering into a product. You live with the same tension every time a headline is written to be clicked.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Patricia Cline Cohen – The Murder of Helen Jewett

Cohen shows how the Jewett case let editors build mass audiences by mixing crime, sex, and moral lessons. She also recovers Jewett as a real, literate young woman, not just a headline, a good reminder to look for the person behind the spectacle.

Slide 10 of 15

The Book Revolution

Publishing Explosion

  • Harper & Brothers, Carey & Lea pioneer cheap editions
  • James Fenimore Cooper’s Leatherstocking Tales create frontier mythology
  • Serialization in story papers creates new reading habits
  • Books become affordable commodities, not luxury items

IMPACT:

Reading becomes a form of leisure, self-improvement, and identity formation. Americans begin to imagine themselves through the stories they read—whether tales of frontier adventure or sentimental domestic life.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Books went through the same revolution as newspapers. They got cheaper, publishers got bigger, and reading became something ordinary people did for fun and self-improvement.

Breaking It Down

Cheap editions: Publishers such as Harper & Brothers in New York (click the name for a gallery) and Carey & Lea in Philadelphia used new presses to sell books cheaply. Because the U.S. did not protect foreign copyrights, they could also reprint British authors like Walter Scott and Charles Dickens without paying them.

James Fenimore Cooper: His Leatherstocking Tales, including The Last of the Mohicans (1826), made the frontier into a national myth about wilderness and conquest, and about the supposed “vanishing” of Native peoples, a myth that hid ongoing removal.

Serialization: Story papers printed novels in weekly installments, so readers waited eagerly for the next chapter, much like a streaming series today.

Books as commodities: Books became affordable purchases rather than heirlooms.

Why This Matters

The gray box makes the cultural point: reading became a way to relax, to improve yourself, and to figure out who you were. Americans began to imagine themselves through stories, whether frontier adventures or domestic dramas, and those stories shaped what “American” meant.

Sentimental Fiction & Female Readers

The "Scribbling Women"

  • Susan Warner (The Wide, Wide World, 1850)
  • Maria Cummins (The Lamplighter, 1854)
  • E.D.E.N. Southworth (The Hidden Hand, serialized)

Cultural Significance

  • Blend of sentiment and moral didacticism
  • Novels teach "emotional literacy"—feeling becomes virtuous

Link forward: Emotional reading prepares ground for Lecture 2's religious feeling

📖 Understanding This Slide

The best-selling novels of the mid-1800s were often written by women for women. This slide asks why these “sentimental” novels mattered so much, and it links forward to the religious feeling of Lecture 2.

Breaking It Down

The “scribbling women”: The phrase comes from a jealous 1855 letter in which Nathaniel Hawthorne complained to his publisher about a “mob of scribbling women” whose books outsold his. The authors on the slide include Susan Warner (The Wide, Wide World, 1850), Maria Cummins (The Lamplighter, 1854), and E.D.E.N. Southworth (The Hidden Hand, first serialized in 1859). These titles come at or just after the end of our period, so think of them as where the reading culture of the 1830s and 1840s was heading.

Sentiment and moral teaching: Sentimental novels told stories of young heroines who suffer loss and learn faith and self-control. Didacticism means teaching a lesson; these books meant to make readers better people.

“Emotional literacy”: Readers were expected to cry and to feel for the characters. Feeling deeply was treated as a sign of virtue.

Female readers gain authority: When millions of women decided which books sold, they gained real power over American culture.

Why This Matters

Watch the link-forward box. The same idea, that true feeling proves a good heart, powers the revival meetings in Lecture 2. Sentimental reading and emotional religion grew up together.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jane Tompkins – Sensational Designs

Tompkins argues that critics wrongly dismissed these novels as shallow. She says they did serious “cultural work,” teaching readers how to think about family, faith, and even slavery.

Ann Douglas – The Feminization of American Culture

Douglas takes the opposite view, arguing that sentimental culture softened and commercialized American religion and thought. Reading the two together shows you how historians can look at the same books and disagree.

Slide 12 of 15

Visual Print Culture: Images for the Masses

Lithography Revolution (1820s–1830s)

  • Cheap decorative prints enter middle-class homes
  • Currier & Ives (Currier’s firm from 1834; Ives joined 1857) mass-produce American scenes

Political Cartoons & Illustrated Newspapers

  • Visual culture democratizes alongside textual culture
  • Images shape national myths — frontier, domesticity, prosperity

Americans could now see idealized versions of themselves and their nation hanging on their walls.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Pictures went mass-market too. Before this period, most families owned few or no pictures. By mid-century, cheap prints hung in parlors across the country.

Breaking It Down

Lithography (1820s–1830s): Printing from an image drawn in grease on a flat stone. It was much cheaper than engraving metal or carving wood, so prints became affordable for middle-class homes.

Currier & Ives: Nathaniel Currier opened his print business in 1834; James Merritt Ives became his partner in 1857, when the famous name was born. The firm sold millions of cheap prints of disasters, horse races, farm scenes, and politicians. Most of the prints in the gallery (such as Central Park in Winter and Across the Continent) date from the 1860s, after our period, but they show where this visual culture led.

Political cartoons and illustrated papers: Images could carry political messages to people who read little, which made visual culture democratic in its own way.

National myths: Prints celebrated the frontier, the cozy home, and prosperity, idealized pictures that left out slavery, poverty, and conflict.

Why This Matters

The gray box sums it up: Americans could now see idealized versions of themselves on their walls. Images teach values quietly. When the same hopeful farm scene hangs in thousands of homes, it helps define what a “proper” American life looks like.

Slide 13 of 15

The Lyceum Movement: Education, Culture, and Spectacle

What Were Lyceums?

The Lyceum movement began in the 1820s as a network of local associations devoted to adult education, civic improvement, and moral uplift. These organizations hosted lectures, debates, and demonstrations designed to bring knowledge to ordinary citizens.

Founded by Josiah Holbrook in 1826, the lyceum became the backbone of antebellum public culture—where Americans could learn about science, literature, reform, and philosophy outside formal schools.

High Culture and Moral Philosophy

Prominent writers and thinkers like Ralph Waldo Emerson brought Transcendentalist ideas to Lyceum audiences. Their lectures on self-reliance, moral philosophy, and the pursuit of truth helped popularize intellectual and literary culture among the middle class.

Emerson’s tours through small towns and city halls made philosophy a public performance, accessible to tradesmen, teachers, and farmers alike.

Popular Science and Spectacle

Alongside high-minded philosophy, Lyceum stages also hosted demonstrations of phrenology, mesmerism, and electrical experiments—blurring the line between education and entertainment.

Figures like Orson and Lorenzo Fowler delivered engaging phrenology lectures, promising moral and personal insight through the “science” of skull reading. These popular pseudo-sciences reflected Americans’ hunger for self-knowledge and moral order.

Critical Question:

Was the Lyceum a democratic classroom or a performance stage? Did it elevate the public or merely entertain them?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Imagine paying a small fee to hear a famous speaker in your town hall on a winter evening. That was the lyceum. This slide shows how lectures became a mix of school, civic duty, and show business.

Breaking It Down

What lyceums were: Local associations for adult education and self-improvement, named after Aristotle’s school in ancient Athens. They hosted lectures, debates, and science demonstrations.

Josiah Holbrook: He organized the first town lyceum in Millbury, Massachusetts, in 1826. The idea spread quickly across New England, New York, and the Midwest. (The lecture-hall picture on the slide is an AI-generated illustration, not a historical image.)

High culture and moral philosophy: Ralph Waldo Emerson earned much of his living on the lecture circuit; many of his essays began as talks to paying audiences. His themes, such as self-reliance and finding truth for yourself, fit an audience of strivers.

Popular science and spectacle: Audiences also flocked to phrenology, the false claim that you can read character from bumps on the skull, and to mesmerism (putting volunteers into trances). The brothers Orson and Lorenzo Fowler built a phrenology business reading heads for a fee. Today we call these pseudosciences, claims that look scientific but are not. To audiences then, they seemed like exciting new knowledge anyone could judge.

Why This Matters

The gold box asks whether the lyceum was a classroom or a stage. It was both. Paying audiences judged speakers like performers, which made learning democratic and also blurred education with entertainment, just as the penny press blurred news with spectacle.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Angela G. Ray – The Lyceum and Public Culture in the Nineteenth-Century United States

Ray shows that the lyceum created a new kind of public. Ordinary people gathered to listen, judge, and argue, and the lecture hall became a place where Americans practiced being a self-improving democratic audience.

Slide 14 of 15

Print and Politics: From Pamphlet to Partisan Press

Political Mobilization Through Print

  • Broadsides and party newspapers link print to mobilization
  • Jacksonian era's "partisan press" turns voting into spectacle
  • Editorials and cartoons appeal to working-class readers
  • Political participation becomes a form of popular entertainment

KEY CONCEPT: Print makes politics accessible and exciting—but also partisan and emotional

📖 Understanding This Slide

Cheap print also changed politics. Elections became loud, colorful shows, and newspapers were the stagehands.

Breaking It Down

Broadsides and party newspapers: A broadside is a one-sheet poster. Parties used broadsides and their own newspapers to rally voters. Jackson’s supporters built a national network of friendly papers, led by the Washington Globe (1830), and the Whigs built their own.

The partisan press: These papers did not pretend to be neutral. They printed speeches alongside insults, rumors, songs, and cartoons. By the 1830s most states had dropped property requirements, so nearly all white men could vote, and parties needed ways to reach them.

The cartoons: “General Jackson Slaying the Many Headed Monster” shows Jackson fighting the Second Bank of the United States during the Bank War; “King Andrew the First” (1830s) is an opposition cartoon showing Jackson as a tyrant trampling the Constitution. Same president, opposite messages.

Politics as entertainment: In the 1840 election, Whigs staged log-cabin rallies, parades, and songs (“Tippecanoe and Tyler Too”). About 80 percent of eligible voters turned out, a record at the time.

Why This Matters

The key-concept box captures a paradox. Print brought millions of ordinary white men into politics and made elections exciting. It also rewarded slogans, images, and anger over careful argument. Wider participation and sharper partisanship arrived together, and that pattern should feel familiar today.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Sean Wilentz – The Rise of American Democracy

Wilentz argues that Jacksonian-era democracy was built by organized parties and their newspapers, not just by Andrew Jackson himself. The partisan press was how ordinary voters were mobilized and how political identities were formed.

Stereotyping (printing)

Definition: Stereotyping creates a single, durable metal plate of a fully typeset page. Printers set the page in movable type, take a mold (flong), then cast a solid plate (lead alloy) from the mold. The plate prints quickly and can be stored for identical reprints, freeing the handset type for other work.

Very brief history of typesetting (context)

  • Hand composition (movable type): From Gutenberg through the early 1800s, pages were composed by hand from individual sorts.
  • Stereotyping (late 18th – 19th c.): Duplicated an entire page as a single plate—well suited to long runs on steam presses.
  • Electrotyping (from the 1830s–40s): Used electroplating to create high-fidelity copper shells; excellent durability and detail.
  • Linotype (1880s): Mechanized line-casting for composition—post-Civil War innovation, outside the antebellum window.
  • Monotype (1890s): Cast individual characters via keyboard; favored for high-quality book composition (also post-antebellum).

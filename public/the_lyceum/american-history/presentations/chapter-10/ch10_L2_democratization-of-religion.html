Lecture 2: Faith in Action

The Second Great Awakening and the Remaking of American Society

HIST 101: American History to 1865

📖 Understanding This Slide

Welcome to Lecture 2. Last time we watched cheap print turn ordinary Americans into a reading public. Today the same energy moves into religion. Between about 1800 and 1850, revivals swept the country, new churches multiplied, and faith became something ordinary people claimed for themselves.

Breaking It Down

Three parts: (1) the religious revival called the Second Great Awakening, (2) how revival energy became social reform, and (3) how it reshaped the middle-class family and ideas about women’s roles, ending with women who lived outside those ideals.

Key question: Who gets to speak for God, and what happens to a society when that authority spreads from a few trained ministers to millions of ordinary believers?

Why This Matters

This lecture explains why so many nineteenth-century reform movements, from temperance to abolition to women’s rights, spoke the language of sin, conversion, and moral duty. To understand American reform, you have to understand American revivalism.

The Second Great Awakening

A Wave of Revivals

  • Began in the 1790s on the western frontier
  • A series of intense religious revivals
  • Spread throughout the country by the 1850s
  • Became known as the Second Great Awakening

📖 Understanding This Slide

Picture thousands of people camped in a forest clearing, singing, weeping, and shouting as preachers call them to repent. That scene, repeated across the country for decades, is the Second Great Awakening. This slide sets the scene; the next one lists its five main features.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says in full: From the 1790s, a series of intense religious revivals took place on the western frontier, spreading throughout the country by the 1850s. This became known as the Second Great Awakening.

Timing and place: Revivals flared on the western frontier (Kentucky and Tennessee) around 1800 and spread through the whole country over the next half century. (The First Great Awakening was an earlier wave in the 1730s–40s.)

The image: The woodcut shows a Methodist camp meeting at Eastham, on Cape Cod in Massachusetts, around 1850. Notice the tents, the preaching stand, and the crowd. By mid-century, camp meetings had spread from the frontier all the way to New England.

Why This Matters

Why did this happen then? The Revolution had weakened old church establishments (states stopped taxing everyone to support one church), settlers were moving west faster than churches could follow, and many Americans feared the new nation was losing its moral compass. Revival offered community, meaning, and order in a fast-changing country.

The Second Great Awakening (continued)

Main Features

  • Mass revivals and camp meetings: emotional conversions
  • Democratization of religion: ordinary people claim spiritual authority
  • Evangelical denominations: Methodists and Baptists surge ahead
  • Religious innovation: Mormons, Adventists, Shakers
  • Providential history: events as signs of God’s plan

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is your overview of the five main features of the Second Great Awakening. Each one gets its own slide (or several) later in the lecture, so treat this list as a map.

Breaking It Down

Mass revivals and camp meetings: Large, emotional gatherings where people experienced conversion. Huge, emotional outdoor gatherings where people sought a personal experience of conversion, a moment of turning to God.

Democratization of religion: Ordinary people claimed spiritual authority, not just ministers or elites. Ordinary believers, not just college-educated ministers, claimed the right to preach, interpret the Bible, and judge their own salvation.

Rise of evangelical denominations: Methodists and Baptists surged ahead of older churches. Evangelical Protestants stress personal conversion and spreading the faith. Methodists and Baptists grew fastest; by 1850 Methodists were the largest denomination in the country.

Religious innovation: The era saw the birth of new sects like the Mormons, Adventists, and Shakers.

Providential interpretation of history: Events were understood as signs of God’s plan.

Religious innovation and providence: Brand-new movements appeared, and many Americans read events as signs of God’s plan. Both get their own slides.

Why This Matters

Keep these five features in mind as a checklist. When you study any revival, preacher, or new movement in this lecture, ask which of the five it illustrates.

Nathan O. Hatch – The Democratization of American Christianity

Hatch argues that the Revolution’s promise of equality spilled into religion. Upstart preachers told ordinary people they could read the Bible for themselves, and the churches that grew fastest were the most democratic in style.

Mass Revivals and Camp Meetings

Cane Ridge, Kentucky (1801)

  • Often cited as the most dramatic early revival
  • Reports of 20,000 people attending
  • Lasted for several days and nights
  • People traveled hundreds of miles to attend

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide takes you to the most famous early camp meeting, Cane Ridge, the event that made the outdoor revival a national symbol.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: Cane Ridge is often cited as the most dramatic early revival. There were reports of 20,000 people attending; it lasted for several days and nights; and people traveled hundreds of miles to attend.

Cane Ridge, Kentucky (1801): In August 1801, a Presbyterian minister, Barton W. Stone, hosted a communion gathering that grew into a week-long revival. Contemporary estimates ranged from about 10,000 to 25,000 people at a time when Kentucky’s largest town had only a few thousand residents.

Why This Matters

Cane Ridge became a symbol of a new kind of religion: open-air, emotional, and available to anyone. The next slide explains what made meetings like it so distinctive.

Mass Revivals and Camp Meetings (continued)

Key Characteristics

  • Itinerant preachers took center stage, especially Methodists and Baptists
  • Emotional displays: “jerking,” rolling on the ground, ecstatic singing, shouting
  • Outdoor venues: churches couldn’t contain the crowds
  • Multi-denominational cooperation in evangelism

📖 Understanding This Slide

What did a camp meeting actually look and sound like? This slide lists four key characteristics.

Breaking It Down

Itinerant preachers: Itinerant preachers, especially Methodists and Baptists, took center stage. Itinerant means traveling: these preachers moved from place to place instead of serving one settled congregation.

Emotional displays: People were seen “jerking,” rolling on the ground, singing ecstatically, and shouting. Witnesses described people falling to the ground, “jerking,” shouting, and singing for hours. Supporters saw the Holy Spirit at work; critics saw disorder.

Outdoor venues and cooperation: Revivals were often outdoors because churches couldn’t contain the crowds, and the slide notes multi-denominational cooperation in evangelistic efforts. No church could hold such crowds, so meetings were held outdoors. Presbyterians, Methodists, and Baptists often preached side by side, though they later competed fiercely.

Why This Matters

Emotion, mobility, and open access set revival religion apart from the formal, seated, sermon-centered worship of the older colonial churches. That contrast is the heart of the “democratization” you will meet on later slides.

Itinerant Preachers and “Circuit Riders”

Methodist Circuit Riders

  • Usually young men on horseback
  • Traveled vast circuits to frontier communities
  • Simple messages: repentance, personal faith, conversion
  • Plain language: passion over polished theology
  • Accessible to all, regardless of class or education

📖 Understanding This Slide

The circuit rider was the everyday engine of frontier revival. This slide explains who these preachers were and why ordinary people responded to them.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: Methodist ministers, usually young men, traveled vast circuits on horseback, bringing religion to frontier communities.

Circuit riders: Methodist preachers, usually young and unmarried, rode long routes on horseback, preaching in cabins and clearings. They used simple messages (repent, believe, be converted), plain language, and passion rather than polished theology.

Their appeal: simple messages (repentance, personal faith, the conversion experience); plain language (they spoke with passion rather than polished theology); and they were accessible, reaching ordinary people regardless of class or education.

About the image: The picture is a modern AI-generated illustration, not a historical image, so use it for atmosphere only.

Why This Matters

Cane Ridge became a symbol of a new kind of religion: open-air, emotional, and available to anyone. The circuit rider was its everyday engine. Where the old colonial churches waited for people to come to them, Methodists went to the people, and that is a big reason they grew so fast.

Charles Grandison Finney: The Master Revivalist

Background

  • Lawyer turned revivalist preacher
  • Active 1820s–1830s, especially in upstate New York
  • Most influential revivalist of the era
“Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do.”— Charles G. Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835)

📖 Understanding This Slide

If camp meetings were the frontier face of revival, Charles Grandison Finney was its modern, city face. He treated revival almost like a skill that could be learned and practiced.

Breaking It Down

Background: Finney was training as a lawyer in upstate New York when he had a dramatic conversion in 1821. He became the most famous revivalist of the 1820s and 1830s, preaching in towns along the Erie Canal and leading a celebrated revival in Rochester in 1830–31.

The quotation: The blockquote comes from Finney’s Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835): “Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do.” In the same lecture he wrote that a revival “is not a miracle” but the result of using the right means.

Why This Matters

Finney’s message fit a democratic, market-minded society. If salvation is a choice, then every person is responsible for choosing well, and the preacher’s job is to persuade. That idea of personal responsibility spilled directly into the reform movements later in this lecture.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Paul E. Johnson – A Shopkeeper’s Millennium

Johnson studied Finney’s Rochester revival and found that it spread first among the town’s businessmen and their wives. He argues that converted employers then pressed their workers to be sober and disciplined, so revival helped create a new middle-class social order.

Gordon S. Wood – Empire of Liberty

Wood places Finney within a broader democratization of American life after the Revolution: ordinary people were rejecting inherited authority in politics, and in religion they rejected the idea that only an educated clergy could guide souls.

Charles Grandison Finney (continued)

Finney’s Innovations

  • “New Measures”: emotional techniques to induce conversion (critics: manipulative)
  • Anxious bench: public seating for those seeking salvation
  • Protracted meetings: multi-day revival campaigns in cities
  • Women’s prayer groups: encouraged female participation

📖 Understanding This Slide

Finney did not just preach; he changed how revivals were run. This slide lists the methods that made him famous and controversial.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: The “New Measures” were emotionally manipulative techniques to induce conversion (that is how critics described them); the anxious bench was public seating for those seeking salvation; protracted meetings were multi-day revival campaigns in cities; and women’s prayer groups encouraged female participation.

“New measures”: Finney’s methods included the anxious bench (a front seat where people struggling over their souls sat in public view), protracted meetings (nightly services for days or weeks), and prayer meetings in which women prayed aloud in mixed groups. The slide calls these techniques manipulative; that was what his critics said. Finney replied that God works through human means.

The theology behind it: Traditional Calvinism taught predestination, the idea that God has already chosen who will be saved. Finney stressed human free will instead, an idea close to Arminianism: anyone could choose to turn to God, today.

Why This Matters

If salvation is a choice, then a preacher’s methods matter: the right techniques can help people choose. That practical, results-minded approach made Finney the model for later American evangelists, and his encouragement of women’s public prayer opened a door that reformers would push wider.

The “Burned-Over District”

Location & Term

  • Region: upstate New York, especially along the Erie Canal
  • Term: “burned over” by so many revivals that spiritual fuel was exhausted
  • Period: 1820s–1840s

Why This Region?

  • Rapid settlement and growth along the canal
  • Social disruption from the market revolution
  • New England migrants and diverse settlers
  • Anxiety about social change and moral order

📖 Understanding This Slide

Some places caught revival fever again and again. The most famous was western and central New York, nicknamed the Burned-over District. Think of it as a laboratory where Americans experimented with new beliefs and new ways of living.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: The region was upstate New York, especially along the Erie Canal. The term means it was “burned over” by so many revivals that the spiritual fuel was exhausted. The period was the 1820s–1840s.

Location and name: The region along the Erie Canal (finished in 1825), roughly 1820s–1840s. Finney himself called it a “burnt district” because revival fires had swept it so often that, some said, there was no spiritual fuel left. The historian Whitney Cross made the term famous.

Why this region? The slide lists four causes: rapid settlement and economic growth along the Erie Canal; social disruption from the market revolution; a mix of New England migrants and diverse settlers; and anxiety about social change and moral order.

Why here? The canal brought fast growth, new money, and new strangers. Many settlers were transplanted New Englanders raised on Puritan traditions. Rapid change created both opportunity and anxiety, a perfect setting for religious searching.

Why This Matters

Notice the pattern: fast economic change produced both new wealth and new worries, and revival offered a way to make sense of both. The next slide shows what that ferment produced.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Whitney R. Cross – The Burned-over District

Cross’s 1950 study argued that this region was not a backwater of uneducated enthusiasm. Its people were relatively prosperous and literate New Englanders, and their very optimism about human improvement fueled wave after wave of revival and reform.

The “Burned-Over District” (continued)

Religious Movements and Reforms Born Here (click a highlighted term)

  • Mormonism (Joseph Smith, 1830)
  • Seventh-Day Adventism (William Miller’s millennialism)
  • Spiritualism (Fox sisters)
  • Oneida Community (John Humphrey Noyes)
  • Temperance movement (strongest here)
  • Women’s rights activism (Seneca Falls, 1848)

Significance: Both mainstream reform and radical experimentation; a laboratory for democratic religion and social change.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide lists what the Burned-over District produced: four religious movements and two reform causes. Click each highlighted term in class for a one-line definition.

Breaking It Down

What was born here: Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon and organized his church in 1830; William Miller’s predictions of Christ’s return produced the Millerite movement and later Seventh-day Adventism; the Fox sisters’ “spirit rappings” at Hydesville (1848) launched Spiritualism; John Humphrey Noyes founded the Oneida Community (1848). Temperance was strong here, and the Seneca Falls Convention for women’s rights met in 1848.

The popups: Mormonism was founded by Joseph Smith, based on the Book of Mormon and new revelation, and emphasized the restoration of true Christianity. Seventh-day Adventism emerged from William Miller’s prediction of Christ’s imminent return (the Great Disappointment of 1844) and reorganized into the enduring Seventh-day Adventist Church. Spiritualism was a belief system centered on communicating with the spirits of the dead, was popularized by the Fox sisters, and reflected the era’s fascination with the supernatural and direct spiritual experience. The Oneida Community, led by John Humphrey Noyes, practiced “complex marriage” (non-exclusive relationships) and communal living, and believed its members had achieved sinless perfection on Earth.

The significance box in full: The Burned-Over District shows how religious enthusiasm could produce both mainstream reform and radical experimentation. It was a laboratory for democratic religion and social change.

Why This Matters

The significance box says the region produced both mainstream reform and radical experiments. That is the key insight: the same belief that people could perfect themselves led some to join temperance societies and others to found utopian communities or new religions.

Democratization of Religion

Definition: Religious authority, practice, and interpretation shifted from elites to ordinary people

Breaking Down Authority

  • Decline of educated clergy: no formal training needed to preach
  • Emotional over intellectual: direct, plainspoken, passionate preaching
  • Rejection of predestination: free will replaced Calvinist determinism
  • Voluntary association: people “shopped” for churches; congregations voted on leaders

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide defines the lecture’s central idea, the democratization of religion, and shows how it worked. In short, religious authority moved from a small, educated elite to ordinary people.

Breaking It Down

The definition in full: Democratization of religion is the process by which religious authority, practice, and interpretation shifted from elites to ordinary people.

Decline of educated clergy: There was no need for formal education or institutional authority to preach. Colonial Congregational and Episcopal ministers usually had college degrees. Methodist and Baptist preachers often had little schooling; what mattered was a powerful conversion story and a gift for preaching.

Emotional over intellectual: Preaching became direct, plainspoken, and passionate rather than theological. Sermons became direct and passionate. “Heart religion,” what you felt, mattered more than “head religion,” what you could explain.

Rejecting predestination: Free will and individual choice replaced Calvinist determinism. Many preachers taught that salvation was a free choice open to all.

Voluntary association: People “shopped” for churches, and congregations voted on leadership. With no state-supported church, people chose a congregation, and congregations often chose their ministers. Churches had to compete for members, almost like a marketplace.

Why This Matters

Each of these four changes moved power downward, from trained clergy and established institutions to ordinary believers. The next slide shows who stepped into that space.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Nathan O. Hatch – The Democratization of American Christianity

Hatch shows that the fastest-growing movements were led by populist preachers who distrusted elites and trusted ordinary people’s judgment. This is the scholarly source of the slide’s main idea.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Democratization had two sides: old sources of authority broke down, and new groups of people stepped in to participate. This slide covers both.

Breaking It Down

Rise of lay preachers: Circuit riders were often poor, itinerant, and self-taught; what counted was authenticity over credentials.

Personal experience valued: “Heart religion” trumped “head religion”: ordinary people could judge their own salvation.

Accessible to all: Anyone could testify, and anyone could be converted.

Expanding participation, as the slide puts it: Women emerged as exhorters, prayer leaders, and spiritual guides, gaining religious authority despite limited political rights. African Americans established vibrant churches, such as the AME, which grew to 20,000 members by 1850, expanding the Second Great Awakening’s influence and supporting education and moral reform. In lay participation, ordinary believers took active roles in worship, testimony, and church governance, so religion became truly participatory.

Expanding participation: Women became exhorters (people who urged others to convert) and prayer leaders. African Americans built independent churches; the AME Church had roughly 20,000 members by mid-century. Lay people, those who were not ordained clergy, testified and helped govern their churches.

The result box in full: Christianity became more participatory and emotional, broadening religious engagement to foster civic and moral cohesion among diverse groups, including women and African Americans.

Why This Matters

The result box says Christianity became more participatory and emotional. Notice the parallel with Lecture 1: just as cheap print let ordinary people join public debate, revival let them claim religious authority. The same democratic impulse was running through politics, print, and faith at once.

Women and Religious Democratization

Women’s Majority in Churches

  • By the 1830s: 60–70% of church members
  • More likely to convert and attend regularly
  • Dominated prayer meetings and benevolent societies
  • Black women: central to independent Black churches (AME, AME Zion, Baptist)

Why Women?

  • Excluded from formal politics
  • Religion offered authority and voice
  • Community and social networks
  • Moral reform fit domestic roles
  • For Black women: centers of mutual aid, education, resistance

📖 Understanding This Slide

Walk into almost any Protestant church in the 1830s and you would notice that most people in the pews were women. This slide asks why, and what kind of authority religion gave them.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: By the 1830s, women were 60–70% of church members. They were more likely to convert and attend regularly than men, and they dominated prayer meetings and benevolent societies. Black women were central to independent Black churches (AME, AME Zion, and Baptist).

Women’s majority: Church records suggest women made up roughly 60 to 70 percent of members. They were more likely to convert, attend, and lead prayer meetings and benevolent (charitable) societies.

Why women, as the slide lists it: they were excluded from formal political participation; religion offered authority and voice; churches provided community and social networks; and moral reform aligned with women’s domestic roles. For Black women, churches served as centers for mutual aid, education, and resistance.

Why women? Women could not vote or hold office. Church offered a respected voice, community, and networks of friends, and moral reform fit the idea that women were naturally more pious. For Black women, churches were also centers of mutual aid, education, and resistance to racism.

Why This Matters

Church was one of the few public institutions where women were the majority. That gave them numbers, skills, and networks, which the next slide shows them turning into real religious authority.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Mary P. Ryan – Cradle of the Middle Class

Ryan’s study of Utica, New York, found that women often converted first and then brought husbands and sons into the church. Women’s religious networks, she argues, helped create the middle-class family itself.

Women and Religious Democratization (continued)

Women’s Religious Authority

  • Exhorters: spoke at revival meetings
  • Prayer leaders: prayer circles, maternal associations
  • Reformers: founded and led benevolent societies
  • Missionaries: single women sent abroad
  • Writers: religious literature and hymns
  • Black women leaders: teachers, mutual aid organizers, church founders (e.g., Jarena Lee, AME preacher)

📖 Understanding This Slide

Women could not be ordained, but they found many other ways to lead. This slide lists six forms of women’s religious authority and the limits they ran into.

Breaking It Down

Forms of authority, as the slide lists them: exhorters were women who spoke at revival meetings; prayer leaders led prayer circles and maternal associations; reformers founded and led benevolent societies; single women served as foreign missionaries; writers published religious literature and hymns; and Black women leaders were teachers, mutual aid organizers, and church founders (for example, Jarena Lee, a preacher in the AME Church).

Forms of authority: Women served as exhorters at revivals, prayer leaders, founders of benevolent and maternal associations, missionaries, and writers of religious books and hymns. Jarena Lee won Richard Allen’s permission to preach in the AME Church and published her autobiography in 1836.

The limitations box in full: While women gained religious authority, they were still barred from formal ordination (a rare exception: Antoinette Brown, ordained 1853). Their authority was real but constrained by gender norms. Black women faced additional barriers of racism but created powerful networks through churches and reform societies.

Limitations: Almost no denomination ordained women as ministers. A rare exception came in 1853, when Antoinette Brown was ordained by a Congregational church in New York. (The slide was corrected here: the evangelist Phoebe Palmer was highly influential but was never ordained.)

About the image: The portrait of Jarena Lee is a modern AI-generated illustration, not a historical portrait. A real engraved portrait of Lee appeared as the frontispiece of her 1849 autobiography.

Why This Matters

Religion both empowered and limited women. It gave them real influence and organizing experience, which many later used in abolition and women’s rights. But it also reinforced the idea that women’s proper sphere was moral and spiritual, not political. Keep that tension in mind for the domesticity slides.

African American Religious Democratization

Independent Black Churches

  • African Methodist Episcopal (AME): founded 1816 by Richard Allen in Philadelphia
  • AME Zion: founded 1821 in New York
  • Black Baptist churches: grew rapidly in North and South

📖 Understanding This Slide

For African Americans, democratized religion meant something urgent: the chance to worship without being insulted or segregated by white Christians, and to build institutions they controlled.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says: The African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church was founded in 1816 by Richard Allen in Philadelphia; the African Methodist Episcopal Zion Church was founded in 1821 in New York; and Black Baptist churches grew rapidly in North and South.

The AME Church: In Philadelphia, Richard Allen and other Black Methodists walked out of St. George’s Methodist Church after white officials pulled Black worshipers, including Absalom Jones, from their knees during prayer for kneeling in the “wrong” section (traditionally dated 1787; some historians place it in the early 1790s). Allen founded Bethel Church, and in 1816 several Black congregations formed the AME as an independent denomination with Allen as its first bishop.

The AME Zion Church: Organized by Black Methodists in New York in 1821.

Black Baptist churches: Grew in both North and South. In the South, many congregations had to operate under white supervision, and enslaved people also worshiped secretly in the “invisible church” you studied in Chapter 9.

Why This Matters

These three church families gave free Black Americans institutions they controlled. The next slide explains why they broke away and what those churches did.

Albert J. Raboteau – Slave Religion

Raboteau traces how African Americans made Christianity their own, both secretly under slavery and openly in independent churches. His work helps you see that Black churches were not copies of white ones but creations with their own traditions of hope and liberation.

Why did Black Christians build their own churches, and what did those churches become? This slide gives the reasons and the results.

Come-Outers & New Religious Movements

Come-outerism

  • 1830s–40s: withdrawing from “corrupt” churches
  • Seeking a purer, lay-led, perfectionist faith
  • Often linked to radical reform: abolitionism, non-resistance, utopianism

New Religious Movements

  • Out of the same ferment came new religious movements that reshaped the landscape
  • Some behaved like come-outers for a season; others founded entirely new churches

📖 Understanding This Slide

Some believers decided the existing churches were too corrupt to fix, so they left. This slide introduces come-outerism and three new movements that grew from the same religious ferment.

Breaking It Down

What the slide says (the original slide labeled this a “quick note”): Come-outerism was an 1830s–40s posture of withdrawing from “corrupt” churches to seek a purer, lay-led, perfectionist faith, often linked to radical reform (abolitionism, non-resistance, utopianism). Out of the same ferment came new religious movements that reshaped the landscape. Some behaved like come-outers for a season; others founded entirely new churches.

Come-outerism: In the 1830s–40s, radicals urged Christians to “come out” of churches that tolerated sin, especially slavery, and form purer, lay-led fellowships. The phrase echoes a Bible verse, “come out from among them” (2 Corinthians 6:17).

Key terms: Non-resistance was the radical pacifist position, associated with William Lloyd Garrison, that Christians should refuse to use or support any force, even government force. Utopianism meant founding model communities meant to show how a perfected society could live.

Why This Matters

Come-outerism shows how far democratized religion could go: if ordinary believers could judge their own salvation, they could also judge their churches, and leave. The next two slides look at three movements that grew from that spirit.

New Religious Movements

Mormonism (1820s)

  • Restorationist: new scripture (Book of Mormon), prophetic authority
  • Millennial hope; gathered communities; westward migration under pressure
  • Not a classic “come-outer” but a new church

Shakers (peak c. 1800–1850)

  • Communal, celibate, gender-egalitarian; ecstatic worship, simplicity
  • Stood outside mainstream denominations: functional come-outers
  • Renowned craft, order, and discipline

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide compares two of the new movements side by side: one that founded a brand-new church, and one that simply stood apart from all the others.

Breaking It Down

Mormonism (1820s), as the slide puts it: Restorationist (claiming to restore original Christianity), with new scripture (the Book of Mormon) and prophetic authority; millennial expectation, gathered communities, and westward migration under pressure. Note: not a classic “come-outer,” but a new church.

Mormonism (Latter-day Saints): Joseph Smith reported visions in the 1820s, published the Book of Mormon in 1830, and organized a church that claimed to restore original Christianity. Members gathered in communities, faced violent persecution, and after Smith was killed in 1844 most migrated west to Utah (1847). It was a new church, not just a breakaway group.

Shakers (peak growth c. 1800–1850), as the slide puts it: communal, celibate, and gender-egalitarian, with ecstatic worship and simplicity; they stood outside mainstream denominations as functional come-outers, and were renowned for craft, order, and discipline.

Shakers: Founded in England and brought to America by Mother Ann Lee in 1774, they grew during the revivals, living in celibate, communal villages that treated women and men as spiritual equals. Their name came from their ecstatic dancing worship; they are remembered for fine craftsmanship.

Why This Matters

Mormons and Shakers both rejected the ordinary denominations, but in opposite directions: Mormons built a growing new church with new scripture, while Shakers withdrew into small, separate villages.

New Religious Movements (continued)

Millerites → Adventists (1840s)

  • Preached the imminent Second Coming; 1844 “Great Disappointment”
  • Many left home churches in 1843–44 (strong come-outer dynamics)
  • Reorganized as Seventh-day Adventists: prophecy, Sabbath, health reform

Why It Matters

  • Authority shifted from clerical elites to lay-led communities
  • Religious innovation fueled social reform and communal experiments
  • Helped nationalize a grassroots, populist style of American faith

📖 Understanding This Slide

The third new movement, the Millerites, shows come-outer dynamics at their strongest. The “Why it matters” box then sums up all three movements.

Breaking It Down

Millerites → Adventists (1840s), as the slide puts it: imminent Second Coming preaching and the 1844 “Great Disappointment”; many left their home churches in 1843–44 (strong come-outer dynamics); and they reorganized as Seventh-day Adventists, emphasizing prophecy, the Sabbath, and health reform.

Millerites → Adventists: William Miller predicted Christ’s return around 1843–44. When nothing happened on October 22, 1844, the “Great Disappointment” scattered his followers. Some regrouped and eventually organized as the Seventh-day Adventist Church in 1863.

The Providential Worldview

Defined: Daily events and history itself as part of God’s cosmic drama; suffering, disasters, politics read as divine signs

Examples in American Life

  • Disease and disaster: cholera as divine punishment or a wake-up call for reform
  • Territorial expansion: God’s plan, the seeds of “Manifest Destiny”
  • Social reform: temperance, abolition, women’s rights as divine purpose

Consequences: Ordinary Americans saw themselves as actors in a cosmic story; changing society became a moral duty

📖 Understanding This Slide

Imagine believing that nothing happens by accident: a disease outbreak, a good harvest, or an election victory all carry a message from God. That is the providential worldview, and most Americans in this period shared some version of it.

Breaking It Down

The definition box in full: Americans interpreted daily events and history itself as part of God’s cosmic drama. Suffering, disasters, political changes: all were read as divine signs and messages.

Definition: Providence means God’s guidance of history. Americans read personal suffering and national events as parts of a divine drama.

Disease and disaster: Cholera epidemics were seen as divine punishment or wake-up calls for moral reform. During the cholera epidemic of 1832 many ministers called the disease a punishment for drunkenness, Sabbath-breaking, or vice. In 1849, when cholera returned, President Zachary Taylor proclaimed a national day of fasting and prayer.

Territorial expansion: Westward expansion was interpreted as God’s plan, the seeds of “Manifest Destiny.” Many Americans believed God intended the United States to spread across the continent. In 1845 the editor John L. O’Sullivan named this belief “Manifest Destiny.” It also served to justify taking land from Native nations and Mexico.

Social reform: Reform movements (temperance, abolition, women’s rights) were seen as fulfilling divine purpose. If history is God’s story, then fighting drink, slavery, or injustice becomes a sacred duty. Reformers saw themselves as God’s instruments.

The consequences box in full: Ordinary Americans believed they were actors in a cosmic story. This gave urgency to social reform: if history is God’s story, then changing society is a moral duty.

Why This Matters

The consequences box is the payoff: ordinary Americans believed they were actors in a cosmic story. That belief gave reform its urgency, and it also made compromise hard. When both sides of a conflict, such as the coming fight over slavery, believe God is on their side, the stakes feel absolute.

Lowell mill girls, as the slide puts it: young, unmarried women from New England farm families worked in textile mills before marriage, earning wages for dowries, family support, or independence. The Lowell mill girls: Beginning in the 1820s, Massachusetts textile companies recruited young, unmarried women from New England farms. They earned wages (with about $1.25 a week deducted for company boarding houses), worked roughly twelve- to fourteen-hour days, and lived under strict rules. In 1834 about 800 women “turned out” (struck) against a wage cut, and in 1836 about 1,500 struck against higher board charges. In 1845 Sarah Bagley and the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association petitioned the legislature for a ten-hour day. Their magazine, the Lowell Offering, showcased their writing, though it avoided criticizing the companies.

About the images: The group photograph of mill workers dates from well after the 1830s–40s strikes, so use it to picture mill work and mill hands, not as a portrait of the original Lowell strikers.

Why This Matters

The diagnosis

Doctors used “hysteria” as a catch-all diagnosis for women

The word came from the Greek hystera, “womb.” By the 1800s, American doctors blended that old idea with new theories about the nerves.

  • Body: fatigue, insomnia, digestive trouble, pelvic pain
  • Mood: anxiety, grief, melancholy, sudden tears or anger
  • Behavior: resisting what a wife or daughter was “supposed” to do

Where it was treated · Trenton, New Jersey, 1848

Asylums treated hysteria as a nervous and moral disorder

  • Records describe women as “restless” or of “excitable temperament.”
  • Treatment ran from bed rest and moral instruction to baths and sedatives.
  • Family conflict, grief, or a hard marriage could be enough to send a woman here.

The big idea

Hysteria tells us more about society than about any disease

Middle-class women were expected to be pure, calm, and self-controlled. Frustration, ambition, desire, or grief did not fit that picture, so doctors could re-label them as symptoms.

Key point

The diagnosis translated social discomfort into medical language. Calling a woman “sick” could be a way of controlling her.

Change over time

By 1900 the diagnosis had started to fade

Mid-1800s

A disorder of womb and nerves

  • Blamed on the female body
  • Treated with rest, baths, tonics
  • Rarely given to men
→

Early 1900s

A question of mind and trauma

  • New psychiatry spoke of trauma and the unconscious
  • Women’s education and suffrage made the old idea harder to defend

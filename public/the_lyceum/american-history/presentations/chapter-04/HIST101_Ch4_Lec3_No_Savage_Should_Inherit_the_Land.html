HIST 101, Chapter 4, Lecture 3 — No Savage Should Inherit the Land

📖 Understanding This Slide

The opening map places you on the Monongahela battlefield before the lecture tells you what happened there. It invites you to see the campaign as a struggle over land, roads, alliances, and imperial power—not simply as a famous British defeat.

Breaking It Down

The Forks of the Ohio, near present-day Pittsburgh, connected the Ohio, Allegheny, and Monongahela river systems. France and Britain claimed the region, but Native nations possessed their own homelands, diplomatic networks, and political aims there.

A battle plan is also an argument. It makes armies and movements look orderly after the fact. The fighting on July 9, 1755, was far more confused than a clean diagram can show.

Why This Matters

The lecture begins with geography because imperial plans worked only when people could move through contested country—and because control of a road did not automatically mean control of the land around it.

"According to a Captive's Memory..."

"...the English would inhabit & inherit the Land... No Savage Should Inherit the Land."
— reported by Charles Stuart, recounting Shingas's retelling, recorded during Stuart's captivity, November 1755

📖 Understanding This Slide

This quotation presents a remembered warning: British victory would lead not merely to a new flag over the Ohio Country but to settler occupation and Native dispossession. The wording is powerful, but the attribution tells you immediately that it did not come to us as a direct transcript.

Breaking It Down

Charles Stuart was a captive when he recorded what he said the Lenape leader Shingas had reported about Braddock. That creates several steps between the alleged statement and the surviving text.

“Inherit the land” links military conquest to permanent settlement. The warning expresses a central Ohio Country concern: which empire, if either, could be used without surrendering Native land and political independence?

Why This Matters

A source can be uncertain as a word-for-word record yet valuable as evidence of the fears, memories, and political arguments circulating in 1755.

Professor Notes

Pause after the quotation. Ask students what they assume about its speaker before revealing the chain of transmission on the next slide.

[Source caution] Treat the line as Stuart’s report of Shingas’s retelling, not as a verified verbatim statement by Braddock.

Three Layers of Memory

1. Braddock's alleged words
2. Shingas's later retelling of them
3. Stuart's captivity narrative — published from manuscript in 1926 by historian Beverley W. Bond Jr.
No independent record confirms this exchange in precisely this form.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The quotation reached the historical record through three layers: an alleged statement, an oral retelling, and a captivity account. Separating those layers helps you decide what the source can—and cannot—establish.

Breaking It Down

Layer one is what Braddock allegedly said. Layer two is Shingas’s later retelling. Layer three is Stuart’s written account, which a historian published from manuscript long afterward.

None of this makes the account worthless. It means historians should not use it as a stenographic record of a conversation. They can use it more securely as evidence of remembered political meaning: the association between British military power, settlement, and dispossession.

Why This Matters

Historical thinking requires more than choosing between “true” and “false.” It asks how a source was created, transmitted, and preserved—and then matches the claim to the strength of the evidence.

Professor Notes

Emphasize that “published in 1926” does not mean “invented in 1926.” The surviving narrative was edited and published then; the account identifies the captivity setting as November 1755.

Evaluate

Which layer of this account can we verify? Which can't we?
Does that uncertainty make the source useless — or does it just change what we can use it for?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This activity asks you to evaluate evidence rather than accept or reject it all at once. The goal is to identify which claims are directly supported and which remain possible but unconfirmed.

Breaking It Down

We can verify that Stuart’s account contains this report and that the warning fit documented fears about British settlement. We cannot independently verify the precise wording of Braddock’s alleged statement or every step in the oral exchange.

A careful conclusion might be: “The story reveals how British intentions were remembered and interpreted,” not “Braddock definitely spoke these exact words.”

Why This Matters

Calibrating a claim to its evidence is one of the historian’s most important habits. Uncertainty should produce precision, not paralysis.

Professor Notes

Give students about one minute to discuss with a neighbor. Solicit one answer about verification and one about usefulness before advancing.

What the Warning Reveals

  • Fear of settler occupation
  • Disputed and fraudulent land purchases
  • Uncertainty: would British victory leave Native communities a future at all?
Land and liberty, joined as one condition for alliance.

Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo communities did not share one position — their choices reflected local politics, kinship, and rival assessments of both empires.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The warning mattered because land was the material basis of Native political independence. Choosing an ally in 1755 required judging not only who might win a battle but what victory would mean for communities living in the Ohio Country.

Breaking It Down

Land purchases were often disputed because Native and colonial parties did not always agree about who had authority to sell, what boundaries meant, or whether a transaction transferred permanent ownership.

Native communities were not a single bloc. Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo leaders weighed local security, kinship, trade, diplomacy, and the behavior of both empires. Their decisions could change as conditions changed.

Why This Matters

Alliance was not simple loyalty to France or Britain. It was a strategy for preserving land, autonomy, and a future under dangerous circumstances.

From Great Meadows to an Imperial Offensive

  • 1754 — Washington surrenders at Great Meadows
  • Britain sends Major General Edward Braddock, the 44th and 48th Regiments, as commander in chief
  • One column in a four-pronged campaign — Nova Scotia, Lake Champlain, Niagara, Ohio

📖 Understanding This Slide

Washington’s 1754 surrender at Fort Necessity did not end the contest for the Ohio Country. Britain responded by sending regular regiments and planning coordinated attacks across several North American theaters.

Breaking It Down

Great Meadows exposed the limits of a small colonial expedition. In 1755 Britain tried to apply greater imperial resources: regular troops, senior command, artillery, and campaigns against several French positions.

Braddock’s expedition was one part of a larger design. This continental view connects events in the Ohio Country to campaigns in Nova Scotia, the Lake Champlain corridor, and the Niagara region. The National Park Service overview of Braddock’s campaign provides a concise chronology.

Why This Matters

Britain’s response shows expanding imperial ambition, but larger armies did not eliminate the need for local knowledge, supplies, and Native diplomacy.

Professor Notes

Use the Braddock pop-up only if students need a quick identification. Keep the four-pronged plan brief so the lecture remains focused on the two 1755 cases.

Braddock's Road: A Real Achievement

  • Soldiers and laborers widen Nemacolin's path to roughly twelve feet — wide enough for artillery and hundreds of wagons
  • Not a comic-opera army wandering blind — a genuine logistical accomplishment
  • Braddock splits the force: a lighter column advances; Colonel Thomas Dunbar follows with the baggage
A road built to expel the French could just as easily carry traders, land companies, and settlers.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Braddock’s army did not simply stumble through the wilderness. Widening the route across the mountains was a major logistical achievement—but the road also revealed the connection between military movement and future settlement.

Breaking It Down

Nemacolin’s path was an existing Indigenous route, later used and altered by colonial travelers. Braddock’s force widened part of that corridor so artillery, wagons, animals, supplies, and thousands of people could move westward.

Splitting the force was an attempt to balance speed and supply. A lighter advance column moved ahead while Dunbar’s slower baggage column followed. The decision involved real tradeoffs; it was not evidence that the entire expedition lacked planning.

Why This Matters

Infrastructure is never politically neutral. The same road that supplied an army could later accelerate trade, land speculation, migration, and dispossession.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David L. Preston — Braddock’s Defeat

Preston reconstructs the expedition as a serious multinational military operation rather than a caricature of redcoats marching foolishly into a forest. That interpretation makes the campaign’s logistical strengths and diplomatic weaknesses visible at the same time.

Professor Notes

Use the Nemacolin pop-up to reinforce that Braddock improved an existing route. Do not describe the corridor as if Europeans created movement through an empty landscape.

Diplomacy Was the Narrow Supply Line

  • Gift shortages
  • Weak intelligence and mediation
  • Status disputes, competing colonial land ambitions
  • A small number of Native scouts remained — the broad Ohio alliance never came
Britain assembled an army without assembling a coalition.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The expedition’s weakest supply line was diplomatic. Britain could transport weapons and troops, but it failed to secure the broad Native coalition, intelligence network, and mediation that operations in the Ohio Country required.

Breaking It Down

Gift exchange was part of formal diplomacy, not a casual bonus. Shortages and misunderstandings communicated disrespect or unreliability. Disputes over rank and colonial land ambitions further damaged trust.

Some Native scouts remained, so “Braddock had no Native allies” would be inaccurate. The problem was scale: the expedition never built the broad political partnership needed to match its military ambitions.

Why This Matters

In a contested borderland, diplomacy produced practical military power. It supplied intelligence, guides, fighters, communication, and—most importantly—political permission to operate.

Professor Notes

Define gifts as instruments of reciprocal political relationship. Avoid framing Native diplomacy as a side issue or as bribery.

A Memory Written Decades Later

"...the dead, the dying, the groans, lamentations, and crys of the wounded for help."
— Washington, composing his recollections c. 1786–1788
Peale, 1772 — Washington in the same uniform he wore in 1755, painted seventeen years after the battle

📖 Understanding This Slide

Washington’s description conveys the horror of retreat, but it was composed more than thirty years after the battle. The portrait is also retrospective: it shows an older Washington choosing to be represented in the uniform associated with his earlier military identity.

Breaking It Down

Memory is evidence, but not a transparent recording. Later status, later events, and the purpose of recollection can shape which scenes a person preserves and how those scenes are narrated.

The quotation and portrait work together. One remembers trauma; the other constructs continuity between the young Virginia officer and the prominent colonial leader Washington had become.

Why This Matters

The slide returns to the lecture’s opening method: ask not only what a source says, but when, why, and through what medium it says it.

Professor Notes

Do not present the wording as a letter written on the night of the retreat. The deck correctly identifies Washington’s recollections as composed c. 1786–1788.

Predict

Braddock's column: roughly 1,460 regulars and provincials.
The French-led force: about 891.
What do you expect on July 9?

📖 Understanding This Slide

The numbers invite an obvious prediction: the larger, professionally organized British column should win. The purpose of the question is to expose the limits of predicting military outcomes from troop totals alone.

Breaking It Down

Numbers matter, but so do formation, terrain, leadership, morale, intelligence, coalition strength, and the ability to adapt when a plan breaks down.

Both totals are estimates. Rosters and casualty returns do not always align perfectly. The comparison is useful for scale, not as a claim that every participant can be counted with certainty.

Why This Matters

A surprising result should lead to a causal question: what advantages did the smaller coalition create, and what prevented the larger force from using its strength effectively?

Professor Notes

Poll quickly before revealing the outcome. Accept predictions, but ask each student to name the assumption behind the prediction.

A Sudden Encounter, Not a Planned Ambush

  • July 9, 1755 — a few miles from Fort Duquesne, the two forces collide unexpectedly
  • French-led force, ~891: 36 officers, 72 French regulars, 146 Canadian militia, 637 Native warriors
Calling this a scripted ambush imposes an order the encounter never had.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The two forces encountered one another unexpectedly as each moved along the same corridor. The battle became a meeting engagement: confusion and rapid adaptation shaped the fighting more than a prearranged trap did.

Breaking It Down

The French-led force was a coalition. French officers and regulars, Canadian militia, and hundreds of Native warriors brought different military experiences and political reasons for fighting.

“Not a planned ambush” does not mean “no tactical skill.” After the collision, Native warriors and their allies used cover, movement, and the British column’s compression to create a devastating battlefield advantage.

Why This Matters

Correcting the ambush myth restores contingency to the battle. Outcomes emerged from decisions made under pressure, not from an inevitable script.

Professor Notes

Distinguish “surprise” from “ambush.” Both forces were surprised by the encounter; the French-led coalition then adapted more effectively.

Beaujeu Falls. Dumas Holds.

  • French commander Beaujeu is killed early — Captain Jean-Daniel Dumas restores momentum
  • Native warriors move through open woodland, use terrain for cover, press the flanks
  • British units compress along the road — officer losses, smoke, noise, friendly fire

📖 Understanding This Slide

The French-led force survived the early death of its commander because Dumas helped restore momentum and the coalition’s Native warriors fought effectively across the open woodland. British formations, meanwhile, became crowded along the road.

Breaking It Down

Leadership mattered. Beaujeu’s death could have ended the attack, but Dumas helped keep the force engaged. The commander pop-ups identify both officers without interrupting the slide’s main argument.

Battlefield conditions compounded one another. Smoke and noise reduced visibility; officer casualties disrupted command; troops pressed together had difficulty identifying targets; and firing from dense formations increased the danger of friendly fire.

Why This Matters

The result came from coalition resilience and tactical adaptation, not from a simple contest between “civilized” discipline and supposedly instinctive forest warfare.

Professor Notes

Use the Beaujeu and Dumas pop-ups selectively. Keep causal emphasis on the coalition’s response after Beaujeu fell, rather than allowing the narrative to become a two-officer biography.

A Few Dozen

📖 Understanding This Slide

The casualty imbalance was extraordinary. Roughly two-thirds of the engaged British and provincial force were killed, wounded, or missing, while the French-led coalition suffered losses measured in the dozens.

Breaking It Down

Casualty categories differ. “Killed,” “wounded,” and “missing” are not interchangeable, and some later returns revised initial figures. That is why the slide gives a proportion and flags variation among published totals.

The imbalance requires explanation. Once the British column lost cohesion, its numerical advantage could not be translated into effective combat power. Retreat through the corridor intensified the disaster.

Why This Matters

The outcome shattered assumptions about what regular troops and imperial resources could accomplish without a functioning coalition adapted to local conditions.

Professor Notes

Retain the qualifying language. Do not convert the estimates into false precision; published totals depend on which return and which definition of the engaged force an author uses.

This Was Not "European Stupidity"

Resist the old story

Not disciplined lines failing against naturally invisible marksmen. The battlefield was open woodland, not impenetrable forest. Both coalitions adapted in real time. Native tactics were learned military skill — not a racial trait.

A coalition victory: numbers, terrain, and adaptation — not a myth.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Older retellings often reduce the battle to foolish British soldiers standing in rigid lines while Native marksmen disappeared into an impenetrable forest. That story replaces analysis with cultural stereotypes.

Breaking It Down

The terrain was open woodland, not a wall of trees. British troops did attempt to respond, and members of both coalitions adjusted during the fight. Their problem was not an absolute inability to fight outside Europe.

Native tactics were learned expertise. Mobility, use of cover, communication, and coordinated pressure reflected training and experience. Treating those skills as racial instinct denies Native combatants historical agency.

Why This Matters

A better explanation can recognize British mistakes without caricature and Native military achievement without stereotype.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David L. Preston — Braddock’s Defeat

Preston’s reconstruction emphasizes the battle as a multinational collision and shows how the French and Native coalition adapted after an unplanned encounter. His account directly challenges the old “ambush in a trackless wilderness” legend.

Professor Notes

Name the myth explicitly, then replace it with causal factors: coalition composition, terrain, command disruption, compressed formation, and adaptation.

The War Widens

  • Fort Duquesne remains French; the road becomes a corridor for every belligerent
  • By autumn, Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo war parties strike Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia
  • Pennsylvania has no standing militia, and a Quaker-influenced assembly locked in its own fight with the governor

📖 Understanding This Slide

Braddock’s defeat did more than preserve Fort Duquesne. It changed political calculations across the region and helped widen the war into Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia.

Breaking It Down

The road became shared military geography. A corridor built for British advance could also carry retreating troops, scouts, raiders, captives, and information.

War parties had specific political identities. The slide names Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo participants rather than treating them as a single anonymous Native force. Pennsylvania’s divided government then struggled to organize defense and finance.

Why This Matters

A battlefield defeat became a crisis of settlement, governance, and diplomacy. Military outcomes reshaped who believed each empire could protect allies—or threaten homelands.

Professor Notes

Avoid saying that every Lenape, Shawnee, or Mingo community entered the war together. “War parties” and “communities” preserve the political variation established earlier.

The Long Way Back

Real recovery took three more years — and a different kind of campaign.
1758 — General Forbes's expedition, paired with the Treaty of Easton. Britain's road back to the Forks of the Ohio required a bargain, not just a march.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Britain did not take the Forks of the Ohio until 1758. The successful campaign differed from Braddock’s because military movement was paired with diplomacy that reduced support for the French.

Breaking It Down

Forbes’s expedition advanced more deliberately with a combined regular and provincial force. The Treaty of Easton addressed land and diplomatic grievances sufficiently for important Native communities to choose neutrality or step back from support for France.

The bargain was neither a final settlement nor proof of permanent trust. It was a wartime diplomatic achievement with promises and ambiguities that mattered to different participants in different ways. The Treaty of Easton minutes preserve the formal conference record.

Why This Matters

The contrast reveals the lecture’s central claim: imperial armies could not secure the Ohio Country by force alone.

The Other 1755

Same year. A different force. A different kind of power.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture now moves from the Ohio Country to Acadia without leaving the year 1755. The comparison is designed to show that empire operated differently when confronting sovereign Native nations than when governing a conquered civilian population.

Breaking It Down

Same year: both cases grew from the wider Anglo-French struggle for North America. Different force: the Ohio campaign centered on an army and a failed coalition; Acadia moved from military conquest to administrative removal.

Why This Matters

Placing the cases together prevents 1755 from becoming only “the year Braddock lost.” It exposes several tools of empire and several meanings of liberty.

Professor Notes

Use a deliberate pause. Signal that this is a comparison, not a claim that the Battle of the Monongahela and the Acadian deportation were equivalent events.

Fort Beauséjour Falls

  • June 16, 1755 — Beauséjour surrenders; nearby Fort Gaspereau, the next day
  • French power in the region weakens
  • Acadian communities — long navigating between British rule, French ties, and Mi'kmaq alliances — are exposed

📖 Understanding This Slide

The capture of Fort Beauséjour weakened the French military position near the Isthmus of Chignecto. It also left nearby Acadian communities more vulnerable to British officials who already questioned their allegiance.

Breaking It Down

Acadians had lived under British rule since the early eighteenth century, but many maintained family, religious, commercial, and political ties that crossed imperial boundaries. Their relations with Mi’kmaq communities and French authorities varied by place and circumstance.

The fall of the forts changed the balance of power. It did not automatically require civilian deportation. That outcome followed later decisions by British officials. Parks Canada’s Fort Beauséjour history traces the ensuing upheaval.

Why This Matters

Military conquest created the conditions in which administrators could attempt to remake the population of a contested region.

Professor Notes

Do not let “Acadian” become a synonym for uniform political behavior. The slide’s language about communities and navigation is deliberate.

The Oath

  • Many Acadians had long accepted qualified oaths — understood to preserve neutrality
  • British officials now demand unconditional allegiance
  • Real differences among Acadian communities are collapsed into one "problem"

📖 Understanding This Slide

The oath dispute concerned what British subjecthood required during wartime. Many Acadians understood earlier qualified oaths to protect their neutrality, especially from compulsory military service against the French or Mi’kmaq.

Breaking It Down

A qualified oath acknowledged British rule while preserving conditions. An unconditional oath demanded allegiance without those protections. British officials increasingly treated refusal as evidence that Acadians could not be trusted.

That official category flattened real variation. Some Acadians tried to remain neutral; some assisted French forces or Mi’kmaq allies; some cooperated with British authorities; and many made decisions under coercive conditions. Parks Canada’s history of Grand-Pré explains the earlier qualified oath and the 1755 demand.

Why This Matters

An oath converted a complex population into an administrative test: accept the state’s definition of allegiance or be treated as a collective security threat.

Professor Notes

Frame neutrality as a political strategy, not passivity. Avoid implying that every Acadian had sworn the same oath under identical circumstances.

July 28, 1755

The Nova Scotia council orders deportation — an executive wartime decision, not an automatic result of existing law.
  • Justified by claimed military necessity and "unreliable" allegiance
  • Officials understood removal would open Acadian farms to Protestant settlement

📖 Understanding This Slide

On July 28, 1755, the Nova Scotia Council chose deportation. The decision was made by officials exercising wartime executive power; it was not an automatic legal consequence of the Acadians’ status or of Fort Beauséjour’s fall.

Breaking It Down

Military necessity was the official rationale: authorities claimed that a population refusing unconditional allegiance posed an unacceptable risk. That claim should be analyzed, not simply repeated as fact.

Land also mattered. Removal would leave productive farms available for Protestant settlers and help British officials reshape the region’s population. Parks Canada records that the council ordered deportation on July 28 and that forced removals continued for years.

Why This Matters

The distinction between wartime context and deliberate policy preserves human agency: officials selected deportation from among possible responses.

Professor Notes

Stress “decision,” not inevitability. Ask what evidence would be needed to evaluate the military-necessity claim and what interests the policy served beyond immediate security.

Removal and Diaspora

6,000–7,000
deported in the first phase, from the Bay of Fundy region
~10,000
total displaced by the early 1760s

Disease, shipwreck, hunger, and family separation killed many — exact totals remain contested.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Deportation unfolded in phases across several regions rather than in one event on one day. Thousands were forced onto ships, while others fled, hid, resisted, were captured later, or endured repeated displacement.

Breaking It Down

The first figure describes the initial mass removals from the Bay of Fundy region. The larger figure captures a wider chronology and geography extending into the early 1760s. Different definitions of “deported” and “displaced” produce different totals.

Diaspora means the dispersal of a people from a homeland. For Acadians, that dispersal included British mainland colonies, England, France, other parts of North America, and later Louisiana. Family separation, disease, hunger, and shipwreck made removal deadly even when exact mortality cannot be fixed.

Why This Matters

The human consequences exceeded a change of residence. Removal broke households, dispossessed communities, and created a diaspora whose descendants continued to define Acadian identity.

Professor Notes

Preserve the distinction between deported and displaced. Parks Canada reports 6,000–7,000 removed in the early deportation and describes further flight and capture; broader estimates vary with chronology and geography.

What Do We Call This?

Historians debate the term itself — deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing — each carrying different legal and moral weight.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Names do interpretive work. “Deportation” describes forced removal by state authority, “expulsion” emphasizes being driven from a homeland, and “ethnic cleansing” foregrounds the effort to remove a population from territory because of its group identity.

Breaking It Down

These terms overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A historian must explain the criteria being used: official intent, targeted identity, territorial removal, violence, dispossession, and the policy’s implementation.

Legal and historical categories are not always identical. A term may illuminate one feature of an event while obscuring another. Responsible naming therefore requires evidence and a stated definition, not just moral intensity.

Why This Matters

The language historians choose shapes what readers see as the policy’s central feature: administrative action, homeland loss, or demographic transformation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John Mack Faragher — A Great and Noble Scheme

Faragher argues for understanding the removal as an early instance of ethnic cleansing, emphasizing the deliberate expulsion of a distinct population and the redistribution of its land. Other historians debate the label or stress different chronologies and imperial contexts.

Professor Notes

Do not ask students to choose a label by instinct alone. Require a definition and evidence. Reserve the genocide question for the appendix, where distinctions among categories can receive more time.

Two Faces of the Same Year

Ohio Country

Regiments, artillery, engineers — but no coalition. Sovereign Native nations, outside British subjecthood.

Acadia

A fort taken — then administrative force to remake a population. Conquered inhabitants, whose status turned on an oath.

Not equivalent events. Two different ways an empire tried to convert contested land and peoples into governable territory.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The comparison brings the lecture’s two halves together. In the Ohio Country, Britain possessed soldiers and supplies but lacked the coalition needed to operate successfully. In Acadia, British conquest was followed by an administrative effort to remove a civilian population.

Breaking It Down

Ohio Country: Native nations remained sovereign political actors outside British subjecthood. British power depended on alliance and diplomacy even when officials resisted recognizing Native land rights fully.

Acadia: British officials treated Acadians as conquered inhabitants whose right to remain turned on an oath. Once authorities defined the population as collectively unreliable, administrative and military force carried out removal.

Why This Matters

Empire was not one fixed mechanism. It classified peoples differently and used different combinations of negotiation, coercion, law, war, and settlement to make territory governable.

Professor Notes

Keep the caution in the gold box central: this is an analytical comparison of imperial power, not an equation of Native sovereignty with Acadian subjecthood or of battle with deportation.

The Question 1755 Leaves

What kind of power builds roads and moves populations — yet still depends on peoples whose rights it refuses to recognize?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This question asks you to hold imperial capacity and imperial weakness together. Britain could mobilize regiments, build roads, capture forts, and deport communities, yet it still depended on local peoples, knowledge, labor, diplomacy, and land.

Breaking It Down

“Builds roads” points to military logistics and settler expansion. “Moves populations” points to administrative coercion and demographic engineering. “Depends on peoples” recalls Native scouts, allies, negotiators, laborers, colonists, and conquered inhabitants.

Rights were unevenly recognized. Imperial officials often needed Native alliances while contesting Native sovereignty and land claims; they governed Acadians while making continued residence conditional on unconditional allegiance.

Why This Matters

The contradiction between dependence and denial helps explain both the reach and the instability of empire in North America.

Professor Notes

Treat this as synthesis, not a rhetorical flourish. Ask students to answer with one piece of evidence from Ohio and one from Acadia.

Kittanning, 1756

  • Colonial governments offer scalp bounties; militias attack Native towns
  • Colonel John Armstrong's expedition destroys Kittanning — a Lenape town and staging point for raids
Such campaigns could disrupt war parties — and also widen destruction, endangering people who were neutral or seeking peace.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The 1756 attack on Kittanning illustrates how colonial governments responded to frontier war with bounties and raids on Native towns. The expedition struck a real base connected to war parties and captives, but its destruction extended beyond combatants.

Breaking It Down

Scalp bounties turned proof of killing into a state payment and made distinctions among enemy fighters, noncombatants, neutral people, and peace seekers dangerously difficult to maintain.

Kittanning was a Lenape town as well as a staging point associated with raids. Destroying houses and food stores could disrupt military operations while displacing families and intensifying cycles of retaliation.

Why This Matters

Counterinsurgency blurred the boundary between attacking armed opponents and destroying the communities that sustained them. That widening violence could make later diplomacy harder.

Professor Notes

Do not describe Kittanning as either an innocent village untouched by war or a purely military installation. Its mixed functions are central to the ethical and strategic problem.

Pennsylvania's Divided Response

  • No standing provincial army or mature frontier fort system in 1755
  • Quaker-influenced political culture distrusts compulsory militia service
  • Assembly and proprietary governors locked in their own struggle over taxation and authority

📖 Understanding This Slide

Pennsylvania entered the frontier crisis without the institutions needed for a rapid, unified military response. Its difficulty was not simply pacifism; it also reflected disputes over taxation, proprietary power, representation, and who should bear the costs of defense.

Breaking It Down

Quaker political influence encouraged distrust of compulsory military institutions, but Pennsylvania politics included many non-Quakers and changed as the crisis deepened.

The assembly–proprietor conflict mattered because defense required revenue. Representatives resisted tax arrangements that protected proprietary estates or expanded gubernatorial power without adequate legislative control.

Why This Matters

War tested the capacity and legitimacy of colonial government. Arguments over liberty and authority could delay defense even as frontier communities demanded protection.

Professor Notes

Avoid the shorthand “Quakers refused to defend Pennsylvania.” The slide emphasizes institutional immaturity and a multi-sided constitutional dispute.

Forbes, 1758: A Different Strategy

  • A hybrid regular-provincial force, advancing deliberately
  • Paired with negotiated colonial participation and the Treaty of Easton
  • Success required reduced French support, repaired diplomacy, and persuading key communities that neutrality served their interests
See: Michael N. McConnell, To Risk It All (2020)

📖 Understanding This Slide

Forbes’s 1758 expedition succeeded through a combination of military reorganization, deliberate movement, colonial participation, French weakness, and diplomacy. It did not simply repeat Braddock’s campaign with a more competent commander.

Breaking It Down

A hybrid force combined British regulars and provincial troops. The campaign advanced cautiously, built and supplied a new route, and depended on negotiations among colonies as well as military command.

Easton changed the political environment. British representatives addressed grievances and made promises about land, while key Native communities reassessed whether supporting France still served their interests. Neutrality reduced Fort Duquesne’s support and made evacuation more likely.

Why This Matters

Success came from aligning military strategy with diplomacy. The case demonstrates that Native political decisions remained decisive even as Britain increased its imperial resources.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael N. McConnell — To Risk It All

McConnell’s study of Pennsylvania’s wartime soldiers highlights the provincial forces, institutions, and risks behind campaigns often narrated mainly through British commanders.

Professor Notes

Do not imply that Easton alone caused the French withdrawal. Present diplomacy as one necessary part of a larger change that included supply problems, shifting Native support, and British military pressure.

Naming the Acadian Tragedy

  • Deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing, genocide — each is a distinct legal and descriptive category
  • Richard LeBlanc's recent work also extends the chronology backward, into settler-colonial pressures on Acadians before 1755

📖 Understanding This Slide

The terminology debate becomes clearer when the event is placed in a longer chronology. The 1755 order was a decisive turning point, but it emerged from years of settlement pressure, oath disputes, imperial rivalry, and attempts to control Acadian land and mobility.

Breaking It Down

Deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing, and genocide ask different questions about action, intent, identity, territory, and destruction. They should not be arranged as interchangeable words of increasing outrage.

A longer chronology changes causal interpretation. If pressure on Acadian communities predated 1755, then the removal cannot be understood only as an emergency response to one fort’s capture or one refusal of an oath.

Why This Matters

Historical categories become more persuasive when they rest on explicit definitions and a chronology broad enough to reveal policy development.

Professor Notes

Require students to distinguish moral judgment from categorical analysis. The existence of debate does not mean “anything goes”; each label must be defended with evidence about intent, policy, implementation, and outcome.

Further Reading

  • David L. Preston, Braddock's Defeat (2015)
  • Richard S. Grimes, The Western Delaware Indian Nation, 1730–1795 (2017)
  • John Mack Faragher, A Great and Noble Scheme (2005)
  • Geoffrey Plank, An Unsettled Conquest (2001)

📖 Understanding This Slide

These books support the lecture’s two connected inquiries: why Braddock’s campaign failed in the Ohio Country and how British rule culminated in the Acadian deportation.

Breaking It Down

Preston reconstructs the 1755 campaign and battle. Grimes centers western Lenape political history. Faragher offers an extended narrative of the Acadian removal and argues for the ethnic-cleansing framework. Plank places Acadian experience inside the longer history of British conquest and contested rule.

Read the works comparatively. Their subjects, chronologies, and analytical terms differ, and those differences help explain why historians can agree about many events while debating emphasis and classification.

Why This Matters

Historiography is not an ornamental list of expert opinions. It is the continuing process by which historians test evidence, revise inherited stories, and argue about the most useful explanations.

Professor Notes

Independent reading list. Encourage students to choose a title based on the question they want to pursue rather than treating the four books as interchangeable surveys.

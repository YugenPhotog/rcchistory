The Town Called Mose

Classroom title collage: Florida marsh, a small Spanish outpost, a sailing ship, and worn Spanish and British flags, with the lecture title centered.

📖 Understanding This Slide

We open in a salt marsh two miles north of St. Augustine. The ground looks empty and quiet today, which is exactly why it makes a good starting point. In the eighteenth century this was a contested borderland where slavery, freedom, and imperial defense met in a single place — and where the first legally sanctioned free Black town in what is now the United States stood.

Breaking It Down

Mose: Short for Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, founded in 1738 by Spanish Florida’s governor for people who had escaped slavery in British Carolina.

The collage: A marsh, a small Spanish outpost, a ship, and two worn flags. The flags are the argument — this is a story about what happens to people whose freedom depends on which flag is flying.

Why This Matters

This is Lecture 1 of Chapter 4, and it establishes the chapter’s central problem: freedom in colonial North America was not a single thing that people either had or lacked. It was granted, conditioned, and withdrawn by empires acting in their own interests — and it was still real to the people who seized it. Holding both halves of that sentence at once is the intellectual work of this lecture.

Francisco Menéndez

The captain who was freed twice — and sold back

📖 Understanding This Slide

A cold open. Before any argument, you meet a person: Francisco Menéndez, the captain who was freed twice and sold back. That subtitle is the whole lecture compressed into eight words.

Breaking It Down

Who he was: An African man, identified in Spanish records as Mandinga (from the Mandinka people of West Africa), enslaved in British Carolina, who escaped to Spanish Florida, gained legal freedom, and was appointed captain of the free Black militia at Mose.

“Freed twice”: Freedom recognized under Spanish law, lost when a British privateer captured him in 1741 and a British admiralty court refused to acknowledge that freedom, and recovered again when he made his way back to Spanish Florida.

“Sold back”: The two words that matter most. His freedom was legally real — and it was only as durable as the jurisdiction willing to recognize it.

Why This Matters

Watch the video for the person, not for the plot. As you go through the rest of the lecture, keep asking what made his freedom possible and what made it revocable. Those two questions have the same answer: the Spanish Crown’s interest in a contested frontier.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three words to name what you have just seen: conditional, useful, fragile. This slide is teaching you a vocabulary for analyzing freedom rather than simply celebrating it.

Breaking It Down

Conditional: Freedom came with terms — baptism into the Catholic Church, allegiance to the Spanish Crown, and (after 1733) four years of service. It was not unconditional recognition of a right; it was an exchange.

Useful: Every fugitive who reached Florida was labor taken out of Carolina’s fields and added to Spain’s thin frontier population — a shield for St. Augustine and a drain on a rival colony.

Fragile: When Britain took Florida in 1763, the legal order that recognized this freedom disappeared, and the community had to leave for Cuba.

Why This Matters

The essential question at the bottom — how could freedom serve both enslaved people and an empire? — refuses the easy reading in either direction. Spain was not a benefactor, and the people at Mose were not dupes. Both things were happening at once, and the analytical task is to describe how. The callout also warns you that this arrangement was not unique to Spain: England had told the same kind of story about itself a century and a half earlier.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before you conclude that Spain was uniquely generous, meet Francis Drake in 1586, playing the liberator’s part at the same city — and note that he shipped enslaved people himself.

Breaking It Down

1586 at St. Augustine: Drake attacked the town; the Spanish garrison fled; he burned the empty settlement the next day. Africans and Native people in the area were freed from Spanish control in the process.

The story England told: England as Protestant liberator, Spain as Catholic tyrant, rescue as the natural role of English arms. This framing served English purposes in a long religious and imperial rivalry.

The inconvenient fact: Drake had sailed on slaving voyages with John Hawkins, England’s pioneering slave trader. The liberator was a slaver.

Why This Matters

The callout is the takeaway: empires hand out freedom when it serves them. In 1586 England claimed the liberator’s role at St. Augustine; by 1739 the costumes had swapped and Spain was making the offer while England ran the plantations. Neither empire had changed its principles. The strategic situation had changed — and that is what determined who was offering freedom to whom.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Edmund S. Morgan – American Slavery, American Freedom: The Ordeal of Colonial Virginia

Morgan’s central insight was that in colonial America, freedom and slavery grew together rather than opposing each other — the liberty of some rested materially on the bondage of others. This slide extends that logic outward to imperial competition: empires could deploy freedom as a weapon against rivals precisely because neither of them opposed slavery as such.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A before-and-after slide with a sharp final line. The roles reversed within a century and a half, and the explanation is not moral improvement on anyone’s part.

Breaking It Down

The 1580s: England casts itself as liberator, Spain as tyrant. England at this point has almost no plantation economy in the Americas and a great deal of hostility toward Spain.

The 1730s: Spain offers freedom to those who escape; England runs the plantations. By now South Carolina is a slave society with a Black majority, and Spanish Florida is a weak, underpopulated frontier province needing people and defenders.

“Because Spain found a use for freedom”: The explanatory principle of this lecture. Positions on freedom followed strategic interest.

Why This Matters

This is a lesson in reading political rhetoric against material circumstance. When a power champions liberty, the useful question is always: what does that position cost them, and what does it cost their rivals? In 1586 liberating Spain’s captives cost England nothing and hurt Spain. In 1738 offering sanctuary cost Spain little and hurt Carolina badly. The rhetoric changed hands because the interests did.

How the Promise Took Shape

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three dates across nearly fifty years, and the slide wants you to see them as a developing policy rather than as one timeless Spanish promise. Notice especially that the terms get harder over time.

Breaking It Down

1687: Carolina fugitives reach St. Augustine and the governor refuses to return them. This is a local decision, made before any royal policy existed — someone on the ground chose not to extradite.

1693: King Charles II issues a decree granting freedom to fugitives from British colonies who accept Catholicism. The condition is religious conversion, which in Spanish imperial thinking made a person a subject of the Crown.

1733: The Crown renews the offer but now requires four years of service — freedom deferred and paid for with labor.

Why This Matters

The tightening terms tell you this was policy being adjusted to circumstances, not a principle being applied. And the bottom line names the exchange precisely: Catholic allegiance, service to Spain, freedom from an English enslaver. For someone enslaved in Carolina, that was a real and potentially transformative offer. It was also a transaction, and Spain set the price.

📖 Understanding This Slide

1738 is the year the paper promise becomes a place. The slide’s closing line is the historical point: royal decrees had been sitting in the archive for forty-five years, and someone had to act on them.

Breaking It Down

The petition: Menéndez and the Yamasee chief Jorge petitioned Governor Manuel de Montiano to honor the old decrees. This is the crucial detail — the initiative came from the petitioners, not from the governor.

Montiano rereads the orders: He examined the royal decrees and concluded that the Carolina fugitives were legally entitled to freedom. Many had been held in a kind of unofficial servitude in St. Augustine despite the decrees.

The bando: A public proclamation of freedom — announced openly, which also meant announced where Carolina might hear of it.

Fort Mose established: A settlement for the newly freed community, two miles north of the city.

Why This Matters

This slide is about the gap between law and enforcement. The royal decree of 1693 did not free anyone by itself; people had to know it existed, claim it, and find an official willing to act. That pattern — rights on paper requiring pressure from below to become real — recurs constantly in American history, and it is worth recognizing this early instance of it.

Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose

  • Two miles north of St. Augustine
  • Church, council house, earthworks
  • About 100 people
  • Montiano names Menéndez captain of the militia
  • The first legally sanctioned free African settlement in what is now the United States
Mose at its founding — no contemporary eighteenth-century image of the fort survives

📖 Understanding This Slide

The town itself. Note its full name — Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose — a formal Spanish royal designation, not an informal settlement. The naming is itself a form of legal recognition.

Breaking It Down

Two miles north of St. Augustine: Close enough for protection and trade; far enough to be the first thing an army approaching from the north would encounter.

Church, council house, earthworks: The three institutions of a real town — religious, political, and defensive. This was a chartered community, not a camp.

About 100 people: Roughly two dozen households. Small, and its significance is legal rather than demographic.

Menéndez as captain of the militia: A formal military commission from the governor, giving a formerly enslaved African man official rank in the Spanish colonial system.

“First legally sanctioned free African settlement”: Read the qualifier carefully. Free Black communities existed elsewhere; what distinguishes Mose is official legal establishment by a colonial government.

Why This Matters

The caption notes that no contemporary eighteenth-century image of the fort survives, and the archaeology is discussed in the Independent Study section. That absence is worth noticing — a community of this significance left almost no visual record, which tells you something about whose lives the colonial archive was built to document.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jane Landers – Black Society in Spanish Florida

Landers did the archival work that recovered Mose, reconstructing its residents’ lives from Spanish parish registers, militia rolls, petitions, and court records. Her central argument is that Spanish law and institutions — sanctuary, militia service, the church, the right to petition — gave free Black residents of Florida genuine legal standing they used skillfully. She insists this was not benevolence but a legal order that free Black people learned to work, and worked well.

Refuge & Garrison

A refuge

  • Free people
  • Families, church, governance

A garrison

  • Sits on the likely British approach to St. Augustine
  • Its militia would meet the first attack
Ai generated image
Spain deliberately organized this refuge as a military community — both things, at once.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two descriptions of the same town, and the slide insists on both. Mose was a refuge and a garrison — and refusing to choose between those readings is the entire method of this lecture.

Breaking It Down

A refuge: Free people with families, a church, and their own governance. Real community life, legally protected.

A garrison: Positioned directly on the likely British approach to St. Augustine, meaning its militia would absorb the first attack. Spain placed the free Black town where an invading army would hit it first.

“Deliberately organized”: This was not an accident of geography. The site was chosen.

Why This Matters

Hold the discomfort of this slide rather than resolving it. Spain gave these people legal freedom and placed them where they would die first. Both facts are true, and neither cancels the other. This is what historians mean by structural analysis — describing an arrangement accurately, including the parts that do not fit a simple story. And notice that the residents of Mose knew where they were living. They accepted the position, which was itself a choice made with open eyes.

What Was Spain Getting?

Labor
Stolen from Carolina's fields
Fear
Planters unsettled — no Spanish army required
People
More subjects and a militia on a thin frontier
Loyalty
Catholic allegiance and Crown service
Spain did not oppose slavery. It used freedom against a rival — and the freedom was still real when officials honored it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The ledger from Spain’s side. Four returns on a policy that cost the Crown very little, and a closing line that refuses to let the analysis become cynical.

Breaking It Down

Labor: Every person who reached Florida was labor removed from Carolina’s rice fields — a direct economic blow to a rival.

Fear: The mere existence of a known sanctuary unsettled Carolina planters, requiring patrols, vigilance, and expense. Spain achieved this without deploying a single soldier.

People: Florida was chronically underpopulated. Sanctuary produced subjects and a militia on a thin frontier.

Loyalty: Catholic allegiance and Crown service bound the new arrivals to Spain in ways Spain could rely on — they had nowhere else to go.

Why This Matters

Read the last line twice: Spain did not oppose slavery. It used freedom against a rival — and the freedom was still real when officials honored it. That sentence models exactly the balance this course is asking for. Naming Spain’s self-interest does not make the freedom fake. Acknowledging that the freedom was real does not make Spain generous. Most of the hard analytical work in history involves holding two true things that pull against each other.

  • Accept baptism and Crown service

📖 Understanding This Slide

An activity slide, and the framing sentence is the important part: agency at Mose did not mean stepping outside Spanish power. It meant making choices inside it. That distinction is worth more than any single fact in this lecture.

Breaking It Down

The task: Pick one of the four choices, say what the person chose — and then name what Spain still controlled. Both halves are required; an answer with only one is incomplete.

Petition with Jorge: The choice to use Spanish legal channels, pressing officials to honor decrees already on the books. Spain still controlled whether the petition was heard.

Accept baptism and Crown service: The choice to meet the conditions. Spain set the conditions and could change them, as it did in 1733.

Live two miles out as a garrison: The choice to accept a dangerous position in exchange for a town of one’s own. Spain chose the site.

Take a militia command: The choice to accept rank and its obligations. The commission came from the governor and could be revoked.

Why This Matters

Historians use agency to mean the capacity to act meaningfully within constraints one did not choose. It is not the same as freedom or power. Getting this right protects you from two bad habits: treating enslaved and formerly enslaved people as passive objects of history, and pretending their constraints were not real. Practice the two-part answer here; you will use it all semester.

Ai generated image

📖 Understanding This Slide

The formal statement of the agency argument, with concrete evidence attached. And notice the small phrase in the final line: agency operated within — and sometimes against — imperial power.

Breaking It Down

“Not merely imperial instruments”: Spain had purposes for these people. They also had purposes of their own, and the two overlapped without being identical.

They petitioned for rights: The 1738 founding happened because Menéndez and Jorge petitioned. This is documented legal action, not inference.

They built families and institutions: Marriages, baptisms, a church, a council house. Spanish parish records document them — which is how we know about them at all.

They used Spanish law and military service: Turning the conditions of their freedom into tools. Militia service was an obligation; it was also a claim on the Crown’s protection and a source of standing.

Why This Matters

The image shows Menéndez in militia dress with his wife Ana María. That detail matters: a marriage, a household, a family recorded in the parish register. Legal recognition of a marriage was itself a right enslaved people in Carolina did not have. When you assess how much Spanish freedom was worth, count the things that do not appear in the militia rolls.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Ira Berlin – Many Thousands Gone: The First Two Centuries of Slavery in North America

Berlin distinguishes “societies with slaves,” where slavery is one labor form among several, from “slave societies,” where it structures everything. Spanish Florida was the former; South Carolina was decisively the latter. That difference — not Spanish virtue — is much of why a free Black town with its own militia was possible in one place and unthinkable in the other.

Did Carolina Hear the Promise?

  • The 1738 bando made the offer public as war approached
  • Sanctuary was already known in Carolina
  • How far this proclamation traveled cannot be measured
A weapon aimed at a rival empire. Also a promise, heard by people who needed one.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A slide about the limits of evidence, placed deliberately just before the Stono Rebellion. The question is whether enslaved people in Carolina knew about the Spanish offer — and the honest answer is more complicated than either yes or no.

Breaking It Down

The 1738 bando was public: Montiano proclaimed it openly, as war with Britain approached. Publicity was part of the point; a sanctuary nobody knew about would be useless as a weapon.

Sanctuary was already known: Carolina officials had been complaining about fugitives escaping to Florida for decades. Word traveled through sailors, traders, escapees who were recaptured, and the enslaved people who overheard planters worrying.

“How far this proclamation traveled cannot be measured”: We cannot document what any specific person in Carolina knew in 1739. The surviving records were not made to answer that question.

Why This Matters

This is a model of how to state a claim with the right amount of confidence. We can say sanctuary was known in Carolina generally. We cannot say the Stono rebels acted on the 1738 proclamation specifically. On an exam, a careful answer that names that distinction is stronger than a confident one that ignores it. Notice too the closing line: a weapon aimed at a rival empire, and also a promise heard by people who needed one. Both, at once, again.

The Stono Rebellion

September 9, 1739. About twenty enslaved men seize weapons at a store on the Stono River, kill two storekeepers, and move southwest — apparently toward Spanish Florida.

Historical reconstruction from hostile colonial accounts

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Stono Rebellion — the largest slave uprising in the mainland British colonies before the Revolution. Note the caption: historical reconstruction from hostile colonial accounts. Every detail on the following slides comes from people who wanted the rebellion crushed and their society defended.

Breaking It Down

September 9, 1739: A Sunday, when planters were at church and enslaved people had somewhat less supervision. The timing appears deliberate.

About twenty men: They seized weapons and powder at Hutchenson’s store on the Stono River, killed the two storekeepers, and armed themselves.

Moving southwest: Toward Spanish Florida. The direction is the strongest evidence for their destination — but it is inference from a compass bearing, not a recovered statement of intent.

“Apparently”: The slide’s hedge, and it is doing real work. Do not upgrade it.

Why This Matters

The source problem here is severe and permanent. We have no account written by a participant. Everything we know comes from William Bull’s report, colonial newspapers, and later narratives written by men defending a slave society against the charge that it was dangerous. Those sources are still usable — but they must be read as testimony from an interested party, which is what the next several slides will train you to do.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The march itself, given in numbers and a destination. Notice that the numbers are ranges rather than figures — the surviving accounts disagree, and the slide reports the disagreement instead of picking a number.

Breaking It Down

60–100 participants: The group grew as it moved, gathering people from plantations along the road. The range reflects genuine conflict among sources.

More than 20 white colonists killed: Houses were attacked along the Pon Pon Road. Accounts note that some individuals were deliberately spared, which suggests the violence was targeted rather than indiscriminate.

The Edisto River: The immediate objective, and beyond it a long overland route toward Spanish Florida — several hundred miles through hostile territory.

The image: Marching in order, with drums. Whether that ordered column is what an observer actually saw or what a frightened observer reported is itself a question worth holding.

Why This Matters

Using ranges is not vagueness; it is precision about the state of the evidence. When accounts conflict, a historian reports the conflict rather than smoothing it over. If a textbook gives you one confident number for Stono, ask where it came from — and notice that this lecture will not do that to you.

At the Edisto, They Stop

  • Raise banners
  • Beat drums
  • Shout “Liberty”
Ai generated image

📖 Understanding This Slide

At the Edisto River, they stop — and they do the opposite of hiding. Banners raised, drums beating, shouting “Liberty.” This is the slide the rest of the lecture has been building toward.

Breaking It Down

Banners: Flags or standards, reported in several accounts. Carrying a banner is a military and political act, not a practical one.

Drums: Drums coordinate formations and signal over distance. They also announce your presence for miles. West and West Central African military traditions used drums as command instruments.

“Liberty”: Reported as their cry — possibly the Spanish libertad, possibly the English word, possibly rendered into English by a colonist who heard something and translated it.

The oddity: All three are loud and visible. People escaping should be quiet and fast.

Why This Matters

This is the moment where the evidence stops making sense under the obvious interpretation, and that is exactly when history gets interesting. A group running for the border made itself as conspicuous as possible. Hold the puzzle — the next several slides work through it carefully, and the honest answer turns out to be less certain and more interesting than a solution.

Pause & Predict

They are running toward freedom. Why would they stop — and make themselves loud and visible?

Hold your answer. The record has more to say.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A pause-and-predict break. Commit to an answer before you turn the page — the point is not to be right but to notice what you assumed.

Breaking It Down

The question: They are running toward freedom. Why stop, and why become loud and visible?

Possibilities worth weighing: They were recruiting, and needed to be seen and heard by enslaved people on nearby plantations. They were exhausted after a long march in September heat. They were celebrating a victory. They were forming up in a military manner because some of them knew how. Or several of these at once.

Why This Matters

Predicting before you get the answer is not a classroom formality. It surfaces your assumptions so you can examine them — and it makes the evidence that follows land harder, because you have something to compare it against. Notice which explanation occurred to you first and ask why that one.

A Textbook Clue

Some of the rebellion's leaders were Catholics from the African Kingdom of Kongo, where Portuguese missionaries had made numerous converts since the sixteenth century.
— textbook synthesis, not a primary source

Easy to read past. One historian didn't.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One sentence from your textbook, easy to skim past. The slide’s closing line — one historian didn’t — is a small lesson in how historical arguments begin.

Breaking It Down

The claim: Some rebellion leaders were Catholics from the Kingdom of Kongo, where Portuguese missionaries had made many converts since the sixteenth century.

Why it matters that this is textbook synthesis: It is a summary of scholarly conclusions, not a primary source. Someone did the research that produced this sentence, and knowing whose research it was lets you evaluate it.

Why it is easy to read past: It reads like background — a fact about where some people came from. Its significance only appears if you ask what a Kongolese Catholic already knew before arriving in Carolina.

Why This Matters

This slide is teaching you to read for the sentence that does more work than it appears to. Historical insight often comes from noticing that a familiar detail is actually a clue — that people arrived in the Americas with prior lives, prior training, and prior religions, and that those things did not stop mattering on arrival. The next two slides show what one historian built from this observation.

The Kingdom of Kongo

  • Catholic since the late 1400s — a royal, court religion, not a shallow overlay
  • Wars and political conflict sent captives into the Atlantic slave trade
  • Some captives may have carried military experience across the Atlantic
The Kingdom of Kongo, c. 1711 — present-day northern Angola and western DRC

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Kingdom of Kongo, in present-day northern Angola and the western Democratic Republic of the Congo. This slide asks you to take African history seriously as a cause of events in South Carolina.

Breaking It Down

Catholic since the late 1400s: The Kongolese king converted in 1491, and Catholicism became the religion of the court and much of the kingdom — with Kongolese clergy, Kongolese saints’ festivals, and diplomatic relations with the Vatican. The slide is emphatic that this was a genuine, established royal religion, not a thin foreign overlay.

Wars sent captives into the trade: Kongolese civil wars in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries produced war captives who were sold into the Atlantic slave trade. Many arrived in Carolina in exactly the years before 1739.

Military experience: Note the careful phrasing — captives may have carried military experience across the Atlantic. Kongolese armies used infantry formations, banners, and drums. Men taken in war were, by definition, often soldiers.

Why This Matters

This reframes what an enslaved person in Carolina was. Not a blank subject defined only by captivity, but someone with a prior life — a religion, possibly a military training, a language, a political world. American history that begins at the water’s edge cannot explain Stono. History that includes the Kongo can at least propose an explanation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John K. Thornton – Africa and Africans in the Making of the Atlantic World, 1400–1800

Thornton’s broader argument is that Africans arrived in the Americas with fully developed cultures, political ideas, and military skills that shaped what they did there. His work insists that the Atlantic world was made by African actors as well as European ones — and that treating captivity as the beginning of a person’s history makes most of what they did unintelligible.

Freedom Seekers — and Perhaps Soldiers

For participants who were already Catholic, one Spanish condition may have been familiar.
  • One reading: banners, drums, and a public halt suggest organization and recruitment
  • Other possibilities: rest, celebration, tactical delay — or several purposes at once

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the interpretation the previous slides have been assembling — and notice how carefully it is worded. “One reading” and “other possibilities.” This is a hypothesis being offered for evaluation, not a conclusion being handed to you.

Breaking It Down

The familiar condition: Spanish sanctuary required accepting Catholicism. For a Kongolese Catholic, that was not a conversion at all — it was a recognition of a faith they already held. A condition that would have been a serious obstacle for others was, for them, no obstacle.

One reading: Banners, drums, and a public halt suggest organization and recruitment — a formation announcing itself in order to gather more people, in a military idiom its members already knew.

Other possibilities: Rest after miles of marching in September heat. Celebration of what they had accomplished. Tactical delay to gather strength. Or several purposes at once, which is usually how human action works.

Why This Matters

Notice the intellectual structure of these three slides: an odd piece of evidence, a clue from another continent, and a hypothesis that would explain the oddity — presented alongside the alternatives it has to compete with. That is what a historical argument actually looks like. The next slide explains why we cannot simply declare the hypothesis correct.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John K. Thornton – “African Dimensions of the Stono Rebellion,” American Historical Review 96 (1991)

This is the article that changed how Stono is taught. Thornton argued that the rebels’ behavior — the banners, drums, ordered march, and even reported dancing — matched Kongolese military and ceremonial practice, and that many participants were likely Kongolese Catholics with military training. It is a powerful and influential reading. It is also an inference built on circumstantial evidence, which is exactly why the following slide exists.

What the Record Can't Say

Resist the shortcut

Surviving contemporary accounts come from white colonists and officials defending a slave society — not from a careful survey of who the rebels were. We can say some participants likely came from West Central Africa, and some likely had military training. We cannot say every leader was a Kongolese soldier.

That gap between evidence and certainty is historical method.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The most important slide in this lecture for your development as a student of history. It tells you to resist the shortcut — including the shortcut of adopting the interesting new interpretation you just learned.

Breaking It Down

Where the accounts come from: White colonists and officials defending a slave society in the immediate aftermath of an armed uprising. They were not conducting a careful survey of who the rebels were, and they had every reason to shape what they reported.

What we can say: Some participants likely came from West Central Africa. Some likely had military training. Both statements are supported by shipping records, demographic evidence, and the behavior described in the accounts.

What we cannot say: That every leader was a Kongolese soldier. The sources do not identify individuals by origin, and the inference cannot be extended that far.

“That gap between evidence and certainty is historical method”: The lecture’s thesis about how to think, stated outright.

Why This Matters

Thornton’s reading is genuinely illuminating, and adopting it wholesale would be a mistake. Good historical thinking means holding an interpretation as the best available explanation while remembering what it rests on. In your own writing, the phrase “the evidence suggests” is not weakness — it is accuracy, and graders notice the difference.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The suppression, and its legal aftermath. The rebellion lasted less than a day in open form; the hunt continued for a week, and the legislative response shaped South Carolina for generations.

Breaking It Down

Lieutenant Governor William Bull: Encountered the column on the road, escaped, and raised the alarm across the countryside. His report is one of our principal sources — written by a participant with an interest in the account.

The militia: Mounted planters caught the rebels in a field near the Edisto by late afternoon, dismounted, and charged. The rebels fought and then broke.

The week after: Many were killed on the spot; some escaped south; the pursuit continued for days, with executions and displayed bodies intended as warning.

The Negro Act of 1740: South Carolina’s legislative response — restricting assembly, movement, education, and the use of drums, while imposing a duty on new imports from Africa and adding modest limits on masters’ brutality.

Why This Matters

Look closely at what the Negro Act banned: gathering, learning to write, and drums. Legislators had watched a column march behind drums and concluded that drums were a military technology. The law is evidence for how colonists themselves read the rebellion — and it is indirect support for the interpretation you just met. It also shows how a slave society responded to resistance: not by loosening, but by tightening.

What Happened to Mose

  • 1740 — Oglethorpe invades Florida; the first fort is occupied, then destroyed in the counterattack
  • Menéndez's militia fights in that battle; the people live twelve years inside St. Augustine
  • 1752 — they rebuild a second Mose
  • 1763 — Britain takes Florida; the community sails for Cuba
When the flag that recognized their freedom came down, they moved again.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The end of the Mose story, in four dates. The closing line is the sentence to carry out of this lecture: when the flag that recognized their freedom came down, they moved again.

Breaking It Down

1740: James Oglethorpe invades Florida from Georgia. British forces occupy the first Fort Mose; a Spanish and free Black counterattack destroys it. Menéndez’s militia fights in that battle — the garrison function was not theoretical.

Twelve years inside St. Augustine: With Mose destroyed, the community lived in the city, in crowded conditions and reduced circumstances.

1752: A second Mose is built — the fort later located by archaeologists.

1763: Britain acquires Florida in the Peace of Paris. The entire community sails for Cuba rather than live under a government that would not recognize their freedom.

Why This Matters

The 1763 evacuation is where this lecture connects to the rest of Chapter 4. Britain wins the Seven Years’ War, gains Florida — and the only place in eastern North America where an enslaved person could legally become free simply ceases to exist. Lecture 4 will show you colonists celebrating that victory. This slide shows you who paid for it. Their freedom was real, and it was a function of jurisdiction, so when the jurisdiction changed they had to leave.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jane Landers – “The Atlantic Transformations of Francisco Menéndez”

Landers follows Menéndez across the Atlantic world — Africa, Carolina, Florida, capture by a British privateer, the Bahamas, and back to Spanish Florida — and uses his life to show that freedom in this era was not a status a person possessed but a relationship with a particular legal order. Move outside that order and it evaporated. His biography is the argument of this lecture in one life.

Fort Mose Today

  • The 1738 fort's exact footprint has never been located
  • A second fort (1752–1763) was found in a 1986 archaeological dig
  • Now a Florida State Park and National Historic Landmark on the Florida Black Heritage Trail
Explore: Fort Mose Historic State Park, St. Augustine, Florida

📖 Understanding This Slide

What survives, and what does not. The archaeology of Mose is a case study in how historical knowledge gets recovered — and how much of it stays lost.

Breaking It Down

The 1738 fort has never been located: The first Mose, destroyed in 1740, has left no confirmed footprint. Coastal erosion, marsh, and two and a half centuries have taken it.

The second fort was found in 1986: Archaeologists led by Kathleen Deagan located and excavated the 1752–1763 site, recovering building foundations, military artifacts, ceramics, religious medals, and food remains — evidence of a community rather than merely a garrison.

Today: Fort Mose Historic State Park, a National Historic Landmark on the Florida Black Heritage Trail.

Why This Matters

Archaeology can answer questions no document will. Spanish records tell you Mose existed and who lived there; the excavation tells you what they ate, what they built with, and what they carried. It also matters that this happened in 1986 — within living memory. The recovery of Black colonial history is recent and ongoing work, and much of it depended on people deciding these sites were worth looking for.

Menéndez's Second Enslavement

  • Captured by the British privateer Revenge in 1741
  • A British admiralty court in the Bahamas refused to recognize his Spanish freedom
  • Sold into slavery — then back in Spanish Florida by 1759
His freedom was real under Spanish law — and vulnerable wherever British power refused to recognize it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The full arc of Menéndez’s second enslavement, and the sentence at the bottom is the thesis of the entire lecture: his freedom was real under Spanish law and vulnerable wherever British power refused to recognize it.

Breaking It Down

Captured in 1741: The British privateer Revenge took him at sea. He was, at that moment, a legally free man holding a militia commission from a colonial governor.

The admiralty court: A British court in the Bahamas refused to recognize his Spanish freedom. Note what that means — the court did not dispute the facts. It declined to give legal effect to another empire’s law.

Sold into slavery: He was auctioned. A free man with rank and property became property.

Back in Spanish Florida by 1759: How he regained his freedom and returned is not established by the surviving records — an eighteen-year gap the archive does not fill.

Why This Matters

Freedom in the eighteenth-century Atlantic was jurisdictional. It was not a property of a person that traveled with them; it was a relationship with a specific legal order, valid only where that order held force. Cross a maritime boundary and it could vanish. Keep this in mind when you reach the Fugitive Slave Clause and later the Fugitive Slave Act — Americans spent the next century arguing about exactly this question of whether a status granted in one jurisdiction binds another.

1730 — The Great Dismal Swamp

  • Rumor: Britain had freed baptized enslaved people — and Virginia hid the news
  • Several hundred resist or flee; at least 200 enter the swamp's eastern fringe
  • Militia and Pasquotank allies capture some; others remain beyond the archive
A decade before Stono, on a different colony's border — the same underlying pressure.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A test case from a different colony, a different empire, and nine years before Stono. If the pattern holds here too, it is not a Florida story.

Breaking It Down

The rumor, 1730: Word spread among enslaved Virginians that Britain had freed baptized enslaved people and that colonial authorities were suppressing the news. The rumor was false. It was also entirely believable, which is the point.

The response: Several hundred people resisted or fled; at least two hundred entered the eastern fringe of the Great Dismal Swamp, a vast wetland on the Virginia–North Carolina border where maroon communities persisted for generations.

The suppression: Militia forces, with Pasquotank allies, captured some. Others remained beyond the reach of the records entirely — which is why the swamp’s maroon communities are known mainly through archaeology.

Why This Matters

The mechanism is identical to Stono: a belief about what a distant sovereign had decreed produced immediate action on the ground. Enslaved people across the colonies were paying close attention to imperial politics, gathering information through networks colonists could not monitor, and acting on it. The information here happened to be wrong. The political sophistication behind acting on it was not.

Eight Decades of Policy

  • 1687: a Florida governor refuses extradition
  • 1693: a royal freedom decree
  • 1733: a royal renewal with a service requirement
An evolving policy, not three identical royal orders.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A precision slide, and its point is in the final line: an evolving policy, not three identical royal orders. Compressing these decisions into one Spanish promise loses the most interesting thing about them.

Breaking It Down

1687: A Florida governor refuses extradition. This is discretion exercised locally, before any policy exists — and it is what creates the situation the Crown then has to rule on.

1693: A royal freedom decree, conditioned on accepting Catholicism. The Crown converts a local practice into imperial policy, on terms that serve religious and strategic ends together.

1733: A royal renewal that now demands four years of service. The terms have hardened; freedom is no longer immediate.

Why This Matters

Watch the direction of causation. The 1687 governor acted first; the Crown responded. Policy in the Spanish empire, as in most empires, was frequently made by officials on the ground and ratified afterward — which is also why Montiano’s 1738 decision to actually enforce the decrees mattered so much. And note the tightening terms: as sanctuary became more useful, Spain also became more interested in extracting labor from it. Neither generosity nor pure cynicism explains that trajectory. Interest, adjusted over time, does.

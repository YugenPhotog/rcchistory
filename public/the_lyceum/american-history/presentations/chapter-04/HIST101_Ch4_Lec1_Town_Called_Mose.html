The Town Called Mose

HIST 101 · Chapter 4 · Lecture 1

Modern panoramic photograph of the salt marsh at Fort Mose Historic State Park, the site of Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This opening places you in the landscape before asking you to interpret it. The marsh looks quiet today, but in the eighteenth century this ground stood inside a contested borderland where freedom, slavery, and imperial defense met.

Breaking It Down

Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose, usually called Fort Mose, was a free Black community near St. Augustine. The image is modern because no contemporary picture of the fort survives. Historians therefore reconstruct Mose through written records, landscape study, and archaeology; the National Park Service overview introduces that work.

Why This Matters

Beginning with a real place makes the lecture’s central question concrete: under what conditions could formerly enslaved people build a community here, and why did the Spanish Empire help make it possible?

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jane Landers — Black Society in Spanish Florida

Landers treats Mose not as a footnote to English colonial history but as evidence that Black freedom in North America also developed through Spanish law, Catholic institutions, and the choices of freedom seekers themselves.

250 Miles, on Foot, Through Armed Country

CharlestonSouth Carolina → St. AugustineSpanish Florida
Why would anyone try that?

Essential question: How could freedom serve both enslaved people and an empire?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Imagine making a journey of roughly 250 miles on foot while patrols, slave catchers, difficult terrain, and unfamiliar settlements stand between you and the destination. The danger of the route tells you how powerful the possibility at its end must have seemed.

Breaking It Down

Charleston stood at the center of British South Carolina’s plantation slave society. St. Augustine belonged to Spanish Florida, a rival jurisdiction that could assign a freedom seeker a different legal status. The distance is approximate, but the contrast is exact: a person held as property in one empire might be recognized as free in another.

The essential question asks you to hold two perspectives together. Freedom could be life-changing for an enslaved person while also serving Spain’s military and political interests.

Why This Matters

Freedom did not simply lie “north.” In this place and period, geography, law, and imperial rivalry could make southward movement a route toward freedom.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Sections 1 and 3. The approximate distance varies with the reconstructed route; “250 miles” is a classroom-scale estimate.

England Told a Story About Itself

1586 — Francis Drake burns St. Augustine.

  • England as liberator
  • Spain as tyrant
  • Freeing Africans and Indians from Catholic cruelty — so the story went
Sir Francis Drake, 1583 — the Protestant "liberator" of Spanish Florida

📖 Understanding This Slide

Empires do more than fight wars; they tell stories that make their violence look righteous. This slide introduces the English story that Protestant raiders such as Francis Drake were “liberating” people from Catholic Spain.

Breaking It Down

1586 marks Drake’s raid and burning of St. Augustine. English writers could describe England as the liberator and Spain as the tyrant, turning an imperial attack into a moral drama. The phrase “so the story went” is your warning label: this is propaganda to analyze, not a neutral description to repeat.

Why This Matters

The lecture will reverse this familiar script. Once you see that “liberator” and “tyrant” were political labels, you can ask which empire’s policies actually created possibilities for particular people.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 1. Treat “liberator” as English imperial rhetoric, not as a neutral description.

By 1739, the Story Had Flipped

1580s

  • England — liberator
  • Spain — tyrant

1730s

  • Spain — offers freedom
  • England — runs the plantation
Not because Spain became kind. Because Spain found a use for freedom.

📖 Understanding This Slide

By the 1730s, enslaved people in British Carolina could encounter a startling reversal of the older English story: Spain offered a possible route out of English slavery. The reversal came from changing imperial interests, not from a sudden moral conversion.

Breaking It Down

In the 1580s, English propaganda cast England as liberator and Spain as tyrant. In the 1730s, British Carolina operated a plantation economy built on slavery, while Spanish Florida offered conditional freedom to some people escaping it. Spain had “found a use for freedom”: weakening a rival and strengthening its own border.

Why This Matters

The comparison teaches you to judge an empire by how its policies affected people, not by the flattering story it told about itself.

The Offer Took Shape Over Time

1687first documented sanctuary decision → 1693royal freedom decree → 1733renewal + Crown service
Catholic allegiance. Service to Spain. Freedom from an English enslaver.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Spain’s sanctuary policy was not one timeless promise. It developed through decisions made across decades, and its changing conditions show how imperial governments could use freedom selectively.

Breaking It Down

1687 marks the first documented Florida sanctuary decision. 1693 brought a royal freedom decree. 1733 renewed the policy while adding Crown service. Catholic allegiance and service tied freedom to incorporation into Spanish society; the reward was release from an English enslaver and recognition as a Spanish subject.

The National Park Service history of African Americans in St. Augustine provides a concise chronology.

Why This Matters

The timeline prevents a common mistake: treating Spain’s offer as either pure benevolence or meaningless propaganda. It was conditional, unevenly enforced, and still consequentially real.

Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose

  • 1738 — founded two miles north of St. Augustine
  • The first legally sanctioned free African settlement in what is now the United States
  • Church, council house, defensive earthworks
  • Population: roughly 100
Modern interpretive panel, Fort Mose Historic State Park — no contemporary 18th-century image of the fort survives

📖 Understanding This Slide

In 1738, Spain’s freedom policy gained a physical address. Mose was both a community where free Black families lived and a fortified settlement protecting St. Augustine.

Breaking It Down

Gracia Real de Santa Teresa de Mose stood about two miles north of St. Augustine. Calling it the first legally sanctioned free African settlement in what is now the United States emphasizes formal Spanish recognition. Its church, council house, defenses, and population of roughly one hundred point to community life as well as military purpose.

No eighteenth-century image of the fort survives. The modern panel and the archaeological research at Fort Mose help historians visualize what documents alone cannot show.

Why This Matters

Mose turns an abstract promise into institutions, homes, worship, and political organization. Freedom became a place people could defend together.

Landers’s archival work recovers people and legal status; Deagan’s archaeological work reconstructs settlement and material life. Together, they show why historians often need different kinds of evidence to recover communities left at the margins of colonial records.

Refuge & Garrison

A refuge

  • Free people
  • Families, church, governance

A garrison

  • Sits on the likely British approach to St. Augustine
  • Its militia would meet the first attack
Spain deliberately organized this refuge as a military community — both things, at once.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Mose held two identities at once: refuge and garrison. Think of those identities as two sides of the same shield—one protected a free community, while the other protected Spanish St. Augustine.

Breaking It Down

As a refuge, Mose supported free people, families, worship, and local governance. As a garrison, it occupied the likely northern route of a British attack and placed its militia in the path of danger. Spain deliberately connected sanctuary to defense.

Why This Matters

Residents were neither passive recipients of Spanish generosity nor merely tools of empire. They built lives within an arrangement that gave Spain military value and gave them legal freedom, community, and room for political action.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 2. Emphasize that Mose’s residents also pursued family, religious, and community life; they were not merely a Spanish military asset.

Francisco Menéndez

  • Identified in Spanish records as Mandinga
  • Escaped Carolina and fought alongside Yamasee opponents of the English
  • Re-enslaved in Spanish Florida despite the sanctuary policy
  • Petitioned for freedom — then commanded Mose's militia
Captain of the free Black militia at Mose.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Francisco Menéndez’s life shows both the possibilities and the limits of Spain’s promise. He moved from enslavement and warfare to petitioning, freedom, and command—but none of those steps was automatic.

Breaking It Down

Mandinga is the identity Spanish records assigned him; it does not give historians a precise birthplace. After escaping Carolina, he fought alongside Yamasee opponents of the English. Spanish Florida then re-enslaved him despite its sanctuary policy. Menéndez petitioned for freedom and ultimately became captain of Mose’s free Black militia.

The Florida Museum’s St. Augustine history places Menéndez within the colony’s struggle against English expansion.

Why This Matters

His biography replaces a simple rescue story with a history of persistence and political agency. Menéndez had to make Spanish policy answer its own promise.

Jane Landers — “The Atlantic Transformations of Francisco Menéndez”

Landers reconstructs Menéndez across incomplete Spanish and British records. Her careful distinctions—what the archive identifies, what remains uncertain, and what Menéndez’s actions demonstrate—model responsible historical biography.

What Was Spain Getting?

An enslaved population that believes freedom lies south is a constant problem for the colony next door — without Spain ever having to send an army.
A Buffer
A Militia

📖 Understanding This Slide

Spain’s offer worked like a lever: a relatively small settlement could place pressure on a much larger neighboring slave society. Mose helped Spain without requiring Spain to invade Carolina.

Breaking It Down

A buffer is a community placed between a vulnerable center and a likely attacker. Mose guarded the northern approach to St. Augustine. A militia is a locally organized military force; Mose’s free Black residents could defend the jurisdiction that recognized their freedom.

The offer also created uncertainty in Carolina. If enslaved people believed freedom existed to the south, British planters faced the continuing possibility of escape, resistance, and labor loss.

Why This Matters

Spain’s strategic motives and Mose residents’ freedom are not competing explanations. The same arrangement could benefit an empire and create meaningful opportunities for people acting in their own interests.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Sections 1–2. Spain’s strategic gain does not cancel the political agency or legal freedom of Mose’s residents.

The Bando, 1738

  • Florida's governor renews the offer in a public proclamation
  • Issued as war with Britain approaches
  • Sanctuary was already known in Carolina — but circulation cannot be measured
A weapon aimed at a rival empire. Also a promise, heard by people who needed one.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The 1738 bando made Spain’s sanctuary policy public at a moment of rising war tension. It was simultaneously an imperial weapon and a promise that freedom seekers could try to claim.

Breaking It Down

Bando means a publicly announced proclamation. Governor Manuel de Montiano renewed the freedom offer as conflict with Britain approached. People in Carolina already knew that fugitives had reached Spanish Florida, but historians cannot measure how widely this specific proclamation circulated or identify exactly what individual enslaved people heard.

Why This Matters

Good history can place the bando in Stono’s context without claiming a direct connection the surviving evidence cannot prove. Context makes an action understandable; it does not automatically establish causation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John J. TePaske — Spanish Sanctuary Policy

TePaske’s chronology shows that the 1738 proclamation renewed a policy with a long history. That matters because the idea of Spanish sanctuary could circulate even if the text of this particular bando did not.

[Sources] Background essay, Section 3. Spanish sanctuary is essential context for Stono; the surviving record does not prove how widely the 1738 bando circulated or what individual rebels knew about Mose.

About twenty enslaved men seize weapons at Hutchenson's store, kill two storekeepers, and move southwest — apparently toward Spanish Florida.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide begins the Stono Rebellion with documented actions and then labels the interpretation built from them. That distinction helps you see where the record ends and historical inference begins.

Breaking It Down

September 9, 1739: about twenty enslaved men seized weapons at Hutchenson’s store and killed two storekeepers. They then moved southwest. That direction makes Spanish Florida a persuasive intended destination, but no surviving statement in the rebels’ own words confirms it.

The Library of Congress account of Stono summarizes the event and its surviving documentation.

Why This Matters

“Historical reconstruction” does not mean guesswork. It means assembling an interpretation from route, timing, imperial context, and hostile written reports while clearly marking what remains uncertain.

The March

  • 60–100 participants

  • Houses struck along the Pon Pon Road

The destination

  • The Edisto River
  • Beyond it: a long route toward Spanish Florida

📖 Understanding This Slide

The initial group became a moving rebellion. As participants traveled along the Pon Pon Road, their numbers grew, homes were attacked, and the march carried them generally toward Spanish territory.

Breaking It Down

Sixty to one hundred participants is a range because surviving reports disagree. More than twenty white colonists killed likewise avoids pretending the record supplies a perfectly settled total. The southwestward route toward the Edisto River and beyond supports the Florida interpretation even though it does not prove every participant shared one destination or purpose.

Why This Matters

Numbers describe scale; direction helps interpret purpose. Historians use both, but they should communicate uncertainty instead of turning estimates into false precision.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 3. Surviving reports disagree about the rebellion’s maximum size and death toll; use ranges and identify the disagreement as part of the evidentiary problem.

At the Edisto, They Stop

  • Raise banners
  • Beat drums
  • Shout “Liberty”

📖 Understanding This Slide

At the Edisto, the rebels did not behave like a group trying to disappear. Their banners, drums, and public cry of “Liberty” made the rebellion visible and political.

Breaking It Down

Banners can signal collective identity and organization. Drums can coordinate movement, communicate, celebrate, or recruit. “Liberty” named the goal in a word colonial observers understood, although the reports do not tell us what every participant meant by it.

Why This Matters

These details challenge a picture of spontaneous, purely private escape. They point toward collective action—but they still leave more than one possible explanation for why the group stopped.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Sections 3–4; colonial accounts reproduced in Mark M. Smith, ed., Stono. Some sources describe colors, drums, dancing, singing, and calls of “Liberty.”

A Textbook Clue

Some of the rebellion's leaders were Catholics from the African Kingdom of Kongo, where Portuguese missionaries had made numerous converts since the sixteenth century.
— textbook synthesis, not a primary source

Easy to read past. One historian didn't.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The textbook’s short statement contains two important clues: some leaders may have come from Kongo, and Catholicism already had a long history there. John Thornton asks what those clues might help explain about Stono.

Breaking It Down

Kingdom of Kongo refers to a West Central African kingdom, not to a vague idea of “Africa.” Catholic leaders could have encountered Christianity before enslavement and before reaching Carolina. The attribution matters: this sentence is a textbook synthesis, not testimony from a Stono participant.

Why This Matters

A small detail can change the questions a historian asks. Instead of treating rebels as people without prior political or religious histories, Thornton investigates what experiences they may have carried across the Atlantic.

John Thornton's Kongo

  • Wars and political conflict sent captives into the Atlantic slave trade

The Kingdom of Kongo, c. 1711 — present-day northern Angola and western DRC

Thornton restores a history that slavery’s records often hide. Some people forced into Carolina may have arrived with Catholic identities, knowledge of warfare, and memories of African political conflict.

Kongo’s Catholicism reached back to the late fifteenth century and became part of royal and court life; it was not simply a thin European label. Enslavement did not erase the histories people possessed before capture. Those histories may help explain why resistance took particular religious, military, and public forms.

Thornton’s interpretation changes how the halt can look. The participants may have been freedom seekers using organized, public methods familiar from earlier religious or military experience—but the evidence permits other explanations too.

What the Record Can't Say

Resist the shortcut

Surviving contemporary accounts come from white colonists and officials defending a slave society — not from a careful survey of who the rebels were. We can say some participants likely came from West Central Africa, and some likely had military training. We cannot say every leader was a Kongolese soldier.

That gap between evidence and certainty is historical method.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide draws a boundary around the claim. The evidence supports a West Central African and possibly military context for some participants; it does not turn every leader into a documented Kongolese soldier.

Breaking It Down

A hostile archive is a record created mainly by people opposed to the subjects we are trying to understand. White colonists and officials cared about suppressing revolt, not preserving complete African biographies. “Likely” marks an evidence-based probability. “Every” would claim certainty the sources cannot support.

Why This Matters

Historical method is not a choice between certainty and silence. Historians can make careful arguments from incomplete evidence if they label inference honestly and explain the archive’s limits.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Mark M. Smith — Stono

By assembling the surviving documents, Smith makes the evidentiary scarcity visible. Readers can evaluate how much each interpretation explains—and where each one goes beyond direct testimony.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 4; Mark M. Smith, ed., Stono. The written archive is hostile and fragmentary; later Black oral memory adds another perspective but cannot answer every biographical question.

Interpret

Surviving contemporary accounts come from people aligned with the slaveholding regime. What might that record leave out, distort, or never think to ask?

Write one claim and support it with one feature of the archive.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now you apply source criticism. Instead of asking only what the surviving reports say, ask how the reports’ authors, goals, and social position shaped what they recorded.

Breaking It Down

Omission means relevant information is absent. Distortion means an author’s assumptions or interests reshape the account. Unasked questions are possibilities the record’s creators never tried to investigate, such as individual African origins or the meanings participants attached to “Liberty.”

Write one specific claim, then point to one feature of the archive—authorship, purpose, vocabulary, silence, or missing testimony—that supports it.

Why This Matters

Sources do not become useless because they are biased. Bias becomes evidence when you identify how it structures what can and cannot be known.

Professor Notes

Give students one minute to write and two minutes to compare answers. Debrief by separating omission, distortion, and questions the record’s authors never asked.

Which Direction Was Freedom?

Charleston → St. Augustine
South. Conditional. Real — because formerly enslaved people could also defend an empire.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The map of freedom depended on jurisdiction. From Carolina, moving south toward St. Augustine could mean moving from enslavement toward a conditional but legally recognized freedom.

Breaking It Down

South overturns the familiar assumption that freedom always lay north. Conditional names Catholic allegiance, loyalty, service, and uneven enforcement. One Community, Two Forts

1738–1740first Mose → 1740–1752residents in St. Augustine → 1752–1763second Mose
In 1763, the community evacuates to Cuba when Britain acquires Florida.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Fort Mose was not one unchanged settlement lasting from 1738 to 1763. The people and the physical forts followed different timelines, so community continuity must be separated from architectural continuity.

Breaking It Down

1738–1740: the first Mose existed until war destroyed it. 1740–1752: residents lived in St. Augustine while the community and militia continued. 1752–1763: a second Mose was built. When Britain acquired Florida in 1763, the community evacuated to Cuba rather than live under a jurisdiction that did not recognize the same freedom.

The National Park Service Fort Mose history traces these phases.

Why This Matters

The evacuation proves how strongly legal status depended on imperial sovereignty. When jurisdiction changed, residents acted to preserve freedom by moving again.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 5; National Park Service, “Fort Mose.” Distinguish continuity of the community from continuity of the physical settlement.

Menéndez's Second Enslavement

  • Captured by the British privateer Revenge in 1741
  • A British admiralty court in the Bahamas refused to recognize his Spanish freedom
  • Sold into slavery — then back in Spanish Florida by 1759
His freedom was real under Spanish law — and vulnerable wherever British power refused to recognize it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Menéndez’s capture exposes the geographic limits of legal freedom. Spanish law recognized him as free; British power in the Bahamas treated him as enslaveable.

Breaking It Down

1741: the British privateer Revenge captured Menéndez. A British admiralty court refused to recognize the freedom he held under Spanish law and sold him into slavery. By 1759, records place him back in Spanish Florida, but the surviving archive does not explain how he regained freedom or returned.

Why This Matters

The episode separates “conditional” from “unreal.” His Spanish freedom had legal and political force, but a rival empire could deny it when he fell under British jurisdiction.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jane Landers — “The Atlantic Transformations of Francisco Menéndez”

Landers follows Menéndez across jurisdictions whose records assign him conflicting statuses. The biography makes imperial law visible at the scale of one person’s repeated struggles over freedom.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 2; Jane Landers, “The Atlantic Transformations of Francisco Menéndez.” How he regained freedom and returned remains unknown.

1730 — The Great Dismal Swamp

  • Rumor: Britain had freed baptized enslaved people — and Virginia hid the news
  • Several hundred resist or flee; at least 200 enter the swamp's eastern fringe
  • Militia and Pasquotank allies capture some; others remain beyond the archive
A decade before Stono, on a different colony's border — the same underlying pressure.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A decade before Stono, a rumor of hidden British emancipation helped produce resistance and flight in Virginia. The Great Dismal Swamp offered a difficult refuge beyond easy colonial control.

Breaking It Down

The rumor claimed Britain had freed baptized enslaved people and Virginia concealed the news. Several hundred resisted or fled, with at least two hundred entering the swamp’s eastern fringe. Militia and Pasquotank allies captured some; the archive does not resolve every fate.

The Encyclopedia Virginia account of petit marronage explains how the swamp supported both temporary and longer-term refuge.

Why This Matters

Like Spanish sanctuary, the rumor created pressure because enslaved people could imagine another legal or geographic space. Whether a promise was official, rumored, or hidden, belief in possible freedom could shape action.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Encyclopedia Virginia, “Petit Marronage in the Great Dismal Swamp”; Virginia Department of Conservation and Recreation, “Rebellion & Refuge.” Do not use the unsupported figure “29 hanged.”

Eight Decades of Policy

  • 1687: a Florida governor refuses extradition
  • 1693: a royal freedom decree
  • 1733: a royal renewal with a service requirement
An evolving policy, not three identical royal orders.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This final timeline is a precision check. Spain’s sanctuary policy developed through different kinds of decisions whose conditions changed over time.

Breaking It Down

1687 was a Florida governor’s local refusal to extradite fugitives. 1693 was a royal freedom decree. 1733 renewed royal policy while requiring service. The dates belong to one evolving history, but the decisions were not three identical royal orders.

The National Park Service chronology provides additional context for implementation in St. Augustine.

Why This Matters

Chronology protects interpretation. If historians collapse local action, royal decree, and later service requirements into a single promise, they lose the policy’s changing conditions and uneven enforcement.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John J. TePaske — “The Fugitive Slave”

TePaske’s long chronology reveals sanctuary as a developing instrument of intercolonial rivalry. The policy persisted because it repeatedly served Spanish interests, even while freedom seekers pushed officials to honor it.

Professor Notes

[Sources] Background essay, Section 1; John J. TePaske, “The Fugitive Slave: Intercolonial Rivalry and Spanish Slave Policy, 1687–1764”; National Park Service.

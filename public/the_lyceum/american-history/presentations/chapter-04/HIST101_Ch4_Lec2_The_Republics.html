HIST 101, Chapter 4, Lecture 2 — The Republics

📖 Understanding This Slide

The title of this lecture is a deliberate provocation. “The Republics” is not a phrase most students expect to meet in a unit on colonial North America — and that is exactly the point. It is not our word for these communities. It is what French officials on the ground actually called the multiethnic Native towns of the Ohio Country, because those towns had dispersed, negotiated authority rather than a single ruler.

Breaking It Down

Where we are: Chapter 4, Lecture 2. Lecture 1 took you to Spanish Florida and the question of what freedom meant. This lecture takes you to the Ohio Valley and asks a different question: who actually governed the interior of North America in the 1750s?

What a “republic” meant to a French officer: A community where no single authority could simply give orders — where decisions emerged from councils, kinship obligations, and negotiation. Frenchmen used the word because it was the closest thing in their political vocabulary to what they were seeing.

Why This Matters

Most accounts of the French and Indian War treat the Ohio Country as a prize contested by two European empires. This lecture argues that it was a third political world with its own governments, strategies, and interests — and that the war cannot be explained without it. Keep that claim in view; everything else in this lecture is evidence for it.

The Ohio Country was a lived-in political world.

  • Senecas and other Haudenosaunee, claiming authority over the region, though valley residents did not necessarily accept it
  • Miami communities, and Great Lakes peoples with their own histories of displacement

📖 Understanding This Slide

The first line does the work: this was not empty land. The idea of a vacant wilderness awaiting European settlement is one of the most durable and misleading images in American history, and the lecture opens by dismantling it.

Breaking It Down

Shawnee communities: Established in the upper Ohio watershed, with their own towns, farms, and councils.

Delawares (Lenape): Pushed steadily west across a century by colonial expansion in Pennsylvania and New Jersey — including through the notorious Walking Purchase of 1737. They arrived in the Ohio Country as people who had already learned what colonial land dealing looked like.

Senecas and other Haudenosaunee: Claiming authority over the region — a claim valley residents did not necessarily accept. Notice how carefully the slide phrases this. A claim asserted is not the same as authority exercised.

Haudenosaunee vs. Iroquois: Haudenosaunee (“People of the Longhouse”) is the confederacy’s own name; your textbook uses Iroquois or Six Nations for the same people. Both will appear on exams; know that they refer to the same confederacy.

Why This Matters

The closing line is the thesis in miniature: none of them were waiting for Europeans to organize their affairs. These communities had migration histories, diplomatic relationships, economic strategies, and internal disagreements of their own. Once you see that, the war stops being a two-player game.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael N. McConnell – A Country Between: The Upper Ohio Valley and Its Peoples, 1724–1774

McConnell’s title names the argument: the Ohio Valley was a country between empires, belonging to neither, with its own political life. He shows that Delaware and Shawnee migration west was a deliberate strategy to gain distance from both colonial pressure and Haudenosaunee oversight — people building autonomy, not refugees drifting. That reframing is the foundation of this entire lecture.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Meet Logstown and its neighbors — and notice the correction at the bottom of the slide. These were not refugee camps. They were productive towns with farms, trade, families, and politics.

Breaking It Down

Who lived there: Delawares from the east, Shawnees from the south, Mingoes (Haudenosaunee migrants, often called Iroquois in English records), and Great Lakes peoples displaced further west. Several distinct peoples in one town.

How they came together: Migration, displacement, intermarriage, trade, and political choice. That last item is the one most easily overlooked. People chose these towns because multiethnic settlement offered advantages — access to several trade networks, wider kinship protection, and distance from any single authority.

“Not refugee camps”: The distinction matters enormously. A refugee camp is a place of waiting and dependency. Logstown was a functioning community with cornfields, a market in furs and manufactured goods, and diplomats who dealt with two empires.

Why This Matters

If you imagine these towns as desperate collections of the displaced, their subsequent political choices look like flailing. If you understand them as deliberately constructed multiethnic communities, those choices look like strategy — which is what the next several slides will show you.

Kinship Was the Security Policy

Many villages held relatives and allies from several nearby peoples.
To attack Logstown was to attack someone's cousins — who lived in villages you might need tomorrow.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One idea, stated in two sentences, and it is worth pausing over because it explains how a society without police or standing armies kept the peace. Kinship was not sentiment; it was infrastructure.

Breaking It Down

How it worked: In a multiethnic town, marriage and adoption created relatives across national lines. A Shawnee family in Logstown might have Delaware cousins in a village fifty miles away and Mingo in-laws further north.

The deterrent: To attack Logstown was to attack someone’s cousins — and those cousins lived in villages you might need next season for trade, safe passage, or alliance. Violence did not stay local; kinship spread the consequences outward until they reached you.

Why this counts as policy: It was a system that produced predictable results without any central enforcement. That is what a security policy does.

Why This Matters

This slide teaches you to recognize political institutions that do not look like European ones. Europeans arriving in the Ohio Country saw no palaces, no standing armies, no written law, and concluded there was no government. There was government; it ran on obligation and relationship rather than on command. Failing to see it is what allowed Europeans to describe the interior as ungoverned — and to act accordingly.

Historian's Corner
Richard White's Middle Ground describes the Great Lakes region, including the Ohio River Valley, as a place where no single power could simply impose its will. French officials and Native communities built workable relationships through negotiation, adaptation, and reciprocal obligations—though the relationship was never equal.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the word in the lecture’s title, explained. French officials called these towns “republics” because power in them was dispersed — and the second half of the slide shows that relationship beginning to fail.

Breaking It Down

How it worked: Gifts, mediation, ritual reciprocity. French officials took the role of “fathers” — and in Native political language a father was not a commander. A father provided, protected, and settled disputes; he did not give orders to adults. The French accepted those obligations because they had no power to do otherwise.

By the 1740s: Supply disruptions from European wars meant fewer trade goods and fewer gifts. Veteran diplomats who understood the protocols died or rotated out. Their replacements — here is the crucial line — gave orders instead of negotiating.

Why that was fatal: In this system, giving an order to an ally was not merely rude; it announced that you had misunderstood the relationship entirely.

Why This Matters

This is the setup for everything that follows. The French position in the Ohio Country rested on a relationship that required constant, skilled maintenance. When the maintenance stopped, the position weakened — and Native communities began looking for other options, which is exactly what the next slide describes.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard White – The Middle Ground: Indians, Empires, and Republics in the Great Lakes Region, 1650–1815

White’s “middle ground” is the concept the slide is drawing on. It describes a space in which neither Europeans nor Native peoples could impose their will, so both improvised shared practices — mediation, gift-giving, negotiated justice — often based on creative misunderstandings of each other’s customs. It was never equality. It was genuine reciprocity within sharp limits, and it lasted only as long as neither side could dominate.

British Trade Gave the Republics Another Option

📖 Understanding This Slide

A balance sheet, and a disciplined one. The republics had real power — and real limits. Slides that name both sides of a ledger are teaching you to resist the two easy stories: helpless victims, or unstoppable sovereigns.

Breaking It Down

What leverage made possible: Deterring violence by widening the circle of kin who would be drawn in. Negotiating among French, British, and rival Native powers — playing options against each other. Preserving room for local decision-making rather than accepting external direction.

What leverage could not stop: An imperial army that came and stayed. Settler migration backed by land companies with capital and legal claims. Land grants written in London or Williamsburg by men who had never seen the valley.

The British trade option: Pennsylvania traders offered better goods at lower prices than the French could supply. That mattered enormously — it gave Ohio communities a way to reduce dependence on France and improve their bargaining position with both empires.

Why This Matters

Notice the structural insight: the republics’ leverage depended on competition between empires. As long as two European powers wanted their friendship, they had choices. This is worth remembering, because Lecture 4 ends with France removed from the continent — and with it, the entire basis of this leverage.

📖 Understanding This Slide

An activity slide rather than a content slide, and the question it poses is genuinely hard: was the middle ground reciprocity or manipulation? Do not go in expecting a tidy answer.

Breaking It Down

The reciprocity reading: Both sides gained. French officials got allies and trade; Native communities got goods, weapons, and a counterweight to British expansion. The obligations ran in both directions and were genuinely binding.

The manipulation reading: The French were building an empire. Gifts and ceremony were instruments for securing allies at the lowest possible cost, and the “father” language was flattery in service of strategy.

Why sorting the scenarios is hard: Most real cases contain both. A gift can be a genuine obligation and a calculated investment at the same time — and the participants themselves may not have separated the two.

Why This Matters

This exercise trains the skill the entire course depends on: reading evidence that supports more than one interpretation and defending a judgment about which reading is stronger. The categories are tools for thinking, not a puzzle with one correct solution. Pay closest attention to the scenarios you find hardest to sort — those are where the real historical argument lives.

By the Early 1750s: Four Claims, One Valley

France
An imperial corridor linking Canada and Louisiana, reinforced by forts and diplomacy.
Virginia & Pennsylvania

The Haudenosaunee

📖 Understanding This Slide

Four claims to one valley, and notice the order in which they appear. The Valley Itself comes first, before either empire. That is an argument about priority, not just a design choice.

Breaking It Down

The Valley Itself: Communities governed, used, and defended the region through residence, kinship, and local authority. This is the strongest claim by any ordinary standard — they lived there and ran it.

France: An imperial corridor linking Canada to Louisiana, held together by forts, traders, and alliance diplomacy. France wanted a connection, not a settler colony.

Virginia and Pennsylvania: Colonial charters written in the previous century granting land westward, plus traders on the ground and land companies with investors expecting returns. Note that these two colonies were also rivals with each other over the same ground.

The Haudenosaunee: Diplomats asserting suzerainty — overlordship, the right to speak for the peoples of the valley — a claim those peoples contested.

Why This Matters

Four claims, incompatible with each other, none able to eliminate the others by argument. That is the structure of the crisis. And notice a subtlety: the Haudenosaunee claim was useful to Britain, because a British negotiator who could get Iroquois consent to a land transfer could treat the matter as settled — without ever consulting the people actually living there.

The Covenant Chain: An Alliance That Required Renewal

  • The Covenant Chain was the metaphor governing Haudenosaunee–British relations.
  • Alliance required repeated gifts, ceremony, consultation, and attention to grievances.
  • Left untended, the Chain tarnished; neglected long enough, it could break.
Covenant Chain diplomatic exchange

Generated reconstruction of alliance diplomacy

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Covenant Chain is a metaphor, and the metaphor contains the whole theory. A chain of silver tarnishes if it is not polished. Alliance was not a document you signed once; it was a relationship you maintained or lost.

Breaking It Down

What it governed: Relations between the Haudenosaunee Confederacy and the British colonies, especially New York, from the late seventeenth century onward.

What maintenance required: Repeated gifts, formal ceremony, genuine consultation before decisions, and attention to grievances — about land encroachment, dishonest traders, alcohol, and failures of protection.

Tarnish and breakage: Neglect dulled the chain; sustained neglect could break it. The metaphor gave Native diplomats a precise and public vocabulary for complaint, and it put the burden of repair on whoever had been neglectful.

Why This Matters

English officials frequently treated treaties as completed transactions — signed, filed, done. Haudenosaunee diplomats understood alliance as an ongoing obligation requiring continuous renewal. That is not a misunderstanding of the terms; it is a disagreement about what an agreement is. Hold that in mind for the next slide, where a Mohawk leader declares the chain broken.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Meet Theyanoguin, called Hendrik by the English — a Mohawk sachem who had spent decades managing the relationship between his community and the British colonies. By 1753 his patience was gone, and the slide is careful to tell you why.

Breaking It Down

A sachem and spokesman: A civil leader and diplomatic representative, not a king. His authority came from persuasion, reputation, and the confidence of his community.

Decades of navigation: He had traveled to London, met the queen, negotiated repeatedly with New York officials, and understood colonial politics from the inside. This is a sophisticated political actor, not a bewildered outsider.

What he expected: Read the callout carefully. Renewal of the Covenant Chain through gifts and ceremony, protection of Mohawk interests, and redress of grievances over land fraud, dishonest traders, alcohol, and inadequate defense. The complaint was broader than gifts alone. That qualification exists to keep you from reducing his position to a demand for presents.

Why This Matters

The image is labeled an interpretive reconstruction because no verified contemporary portrait survives — a small reminder of how thin the visual archive is for Native leaders even at this level of prominence. It is also a reminder to read the caption on every historical image you encounter.

"We Desire to Hear No More of You"

The Covenant Chain is broken between you and us. So brother you are not to hear of me any more, and Brother we desire to hear no more of you.
— Hendrik (Theyanoguin) to the Governor of New York, 1753

📖 Understanding This Slide

One quotation, given a slide of its own, because it is one of the most consequential sentences spoken in colonial North America. Hendrik is not asking for anything. He is announcing that the relationship is over.

Breaking It Down

“The Covenant Chain is broken”: He uses the established diplomatic vocabulary precisely. Not tarnished, not neglected — broken. Within the shared language of Anglo-Haudenosaunee diplomacy, this is the most severe declaration available.

“You are not to hear of me any more”: A formal severing of communication. He is ending the diplomatic relationship, not opening a negotiation.

Where and when: Delivered to the Governor of New York in 1753 — and he then walked out of the meeting.

Why This Matters

The consequences were immediate and traceable. British authorities in London, alarmed that the Iroquois alliance might collapse just as war with France loomed, ordered the colonies to hold an intercolonial congress to repair relations. That congress met at Albany in 1754. Franklin’s Plan of Union — usually taught as an early stirring of American nationhood — exists because a Mohawk diplomat told New York he was done talking to them. That is a causal chain worth remembering, and it runs the opposite direction from the one textbooks usually draw.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Albany Congress, and notice the first line: Britain ordered New York to host it. This was not a spontaneous colonial initiative; it was an imperial instruction issued because the empire was frightened.

Breaking It Down

Why it happened: Hendrik’s declaration convinced London that the Covenant Chain was genuinely failing at the worst possible moment. Seven colonies sent delegates.

Two purposes: Redress Haudenosaunee grievances, and coordinate colonial defense. The first was the reason London called it; the second is what the delegates spent their energy on.

June 19 to July 11, 1754: Three weeks. Note the timing — the congress was sitting while Washington was in the Ohio Country, and Fort Necessity fell on July 3, in the middle of it.

Why This Matters

The Native diplomacy the congress was called to repair mostly failed — the Haudenosaunee delegates left dissatisfied, and a controversial land purchase made things worse. What the congress is remembered for instead is Franklin’s Plan of Union, which was never enacted. That gap between why a meeting was called and what it is remembered for is itself a lesson in how history gets written: we remember the part that leads toward the story we already want to tell.

Franklin's Snake

  • Franklin published “JOIN, or DIE” in the Pennsylvania Gazette on May 9, 1754.
  • His proposed union included a Crown-appointed President General and an elected Grand Council.
  • The plan would have coordinated taxation, defense, and Native diplomacy.
Pennsylvania Gazette, May 9, 1754

📖 Understanding This Slide

The most famous political cartoon in American history, and it is worth being precise about what it originally meant. “JOIN, or DIE” was not a call for independence. It was a call for the colonies to cooperate within the British Empire against France.

Breaking It Down

May 9, 1754: Published in Franklin’s Pennsylvania Gazette, weeks before the Albany Congress met.

The severed snake: Eight segments for the colonies and regions. The image drew on a folk belief that a snake cut apart could revive if the pieces were rejoined before sunset — so the cartoon is an argument about urgency as much as unity.

The Plan itself: A Crown-appointed President General and a Grand Council elected by the colonial legislatures, with authority over taxation, defense, and Indian affairs across all the colonies.

What it was for: Coordinated management of Native diplomacy and frontier defense — the problem Hendrik had just made unavoidable.

Why This Matters

Be careful with this cartoon. It is constantly reproduced as an image of revolutionary American unity, and in 1754 it meant something close to the opposite: a proposal to make the colonies a more effective and better-coordinated part of the British Empire. It was reused in the 1760s and 1770s for very different purposes. Watching a symbol change meaning is good practice — and the same image serving both imperial cooperation and revolutionary resistance tells you something about how closely those two projects were related.

Adopted at Albany. Rejected Everywhere Else.

  • The Congress adopted Franklin's plan on July 10, 1754.
  • No colonial legislature later adopted the plan.
  • The Crown rejected it in practice by refusing to enact it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The punchline, and it is a deflating one. The Albany Plan was adopted by the congress and then rejected by absolutely everyone with the power to enact it.

Breaking It Down

Adopted July 10, 1754: The delegates at Albany approved it.

No colonial legislature adopted it: Every assembly declined. Their objection was straightforward: the plan would have given an intercolonial council power to tax, and colonial assemblies guarded the power of the purse as the foundation of their own authority. They were not about to hand it to a new body.

The Crown rejected it in practice: London never enacted it. From Whitehall’s perspective, a Grand Council elected by colonial legislatures with real power over taxation and defense looked like a step toward colonial autonomy — exactly what imperial officials did not want.

Why This Matters

The plan died because both sides thought it gave away too much power — and their reasons were mirror images. That double rejection reveals the underlying problem the empire never solved: no one could design an arrangement that satisfied both colonial assemblies’ insistence on local control and London’s insistence on imperial authority. Ten years later Britain stops trying to design and starts issuing commands, and the argument that killed the Albany Plan becomes the argument that breaks the empire.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Timothy J. Shannon – Indians and Colonists at the Crossroads of Empire: The Albany Congress of 1754

Shannon’s study restores what the Albany Congress was actually about. The delegates spent most of their time on Indian affairs, not on Franklin’s plan, and the congress produced a fraudulent land purchase that damaged Anglo-Iroquois relations further. He argues that later generations, looking for the origins of American union, pulled the Plan out of a meeting whose real business was Native diplomacy — and got the story backwards.

📖 Understanding This Slide

An activity slide. The question — who said no to Albany, and why — is more revealing than the fact of the rejection, because each refusal protected a different interest.

Breaking It Down

The method: You are matching actors to the concern each was defending. Do not look for villains. Everyone who rejected the plan was protecting something they had good reason to protect.

What to watch for: Colonial assemblies were defending the power of the purse — the taxing authority that made them meaningful bodies. Why This Matters

Matching actors to interests is one of the most transferable skills in this course. When a proposal fails, the productive question is rarely “who was shortsighted?” but “what was each party protecting, and was that thing worth protecting to them?” Answer that and you can explain the failure without needing anyone to be foolish.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The title is the lesson: it needed no explanation. That phrase captures a failure of imagination with enormous consequences. French officials sent two thousand men to build forts in Native territory and assumed nobody would need to be consulted.

Breaking It Down

Duquesne’s project: In 1753 the governor of New France sent roughly 2,000 Canadians to construct a chain of forts from Lake Erie south toward the Forks of the Ohio.

The French assumption: That forts protected their Native allies and strengthened the alliance — so obviously beneficial that explaining it was unnecessary. This is precisely the shift described earlier: officials giving orders instead of negotiating.

Mingo warning: They told the French plainly not to build in their territory. The claim being asserted here is territorial sovereignty.

The Delaware and Shawnee question: Would the Virginians expel the French and then go home? That is a devastatingly shrewd question. It shows these leaders understood the real threat perfectly — French forts were garrisons that might leave; British settlement was permanent.

Why This Matters

That Delaware and Shawnee question should reframe how you read the whole war. Ohio Valley peoples were not choosing between a good empire and a bad one. They were assessing which threat was more reversible, and asking whether the cure would be worse than the disease. Their eventual answer — and their reasons — explain far more about the war’s course than either empire’s intentions do.

Generated reconstruction of the 1753 mission

📖 Understanding This Slide

George Washington’s entrance into history, at twenty-one, carrying a letter into territory claimed by three powers. Notice how the slide describes his errand: he demanded that the French leave territory Virginia claimed.

Breaking It Down

Governor Robert Dinwiddie: Virginia’s lieutenant governor — and an investor in the Ohio Company, which held a large western land grant. The public mission and the private interest ran together, which was normal and is worth noticing.

The mission: A winter journey of several hundred miles to Fort Le Boeuf to deliver a demand that the French withdraw.

Saint-Pierre’s refusal: The French commander received him courteously and refused entirely, asserting France’s own claim. From the French side, Virginia was the intruder.

What he brought back: Intelligence on French strength, positions, and intentions — and a published journal that made him a minor celebrity and turned a diplomatic dispute into an immediate military crisis.

Why This Matters

Two empires each believed the other was trespassing, and each was acting on documents written thousands of miles away. Meanwhile the people who lived in the valley were watching two outsiders argue over whose paperwork governed their homeland. Keep the phrase “territory Virginia claimed” in mind — the lecture chose those words deliberately, because a claim is not a fact.

Jumonville Glen: May 28, 1754

Open the video on YouTube

📖 Understanding This Slide

A video slide covering the clash at Jumonville Glen on May 28, 1754 — a fifteen-minute skirmish in a wooded ravine that helped ignite a world war.

Breaking It Down

What to watch for: Who is present besides Washington and the French? Tanacharison and his warriors are there, and the Independent Study section argues they had their own objectives entirely.

What remains disputed: The sequence of events, who fired first, and how Jumonville died. Contemporary accounts conflict, and some attribute the killing to Tanacharison after the fighting had largely stopped.

Why the ambiguity survives: The surviving accounts were produced by people with strong reasons to shape the story — Washington defending his conduct, the French building a diplomatic case.

Why This Matters

Hold the uncertainty rather than resolving it. This is a genuine case where the evidence does not settle the question, and the honest historical answer is that we do not know exactly what happened. What we do know is what the event was used for: France called it the assassination of a diplomatic envoy, and that framing shaped everything that followed.

Battle of Fort Necessity in heavy rain

Generated reconstruction; fort and troop positions are interpretive

📖 Understanding This Slide

Six weeks after Jumonville Glen, Washington surrenders in the rain at Fort Necessity — the only surrender of his military career — and signs a document he apparently could not read.

Breaking It Down

The attack: A larger French and Canadian force with Indigenous allies, including Jumonville’s brother, struck Washington’s hastily built stockade at Great Meadows on July 3, 1754.

Why he lost: The fort sat in a low meadow overlooked by wooded high ground. Heavy rain flooded the trenches and soaked the powder; the men were hungry and exhausted. Poor site selection more than poor fighting.

The capitulation document: Written in French, which Washington did not read, translated in the rain by an interpreter of limited skill. It used the word assassinat — assassination — for Jumonville’s death. Washington later insisted the term had not been conveyed to him.

The careful conclusion: Read the slide’s wording. The document made Washington appear to admit responsibility, whether or not he understood it that way. The historical question is what the document did, not what was in his heart.

Why This Matters

France now held a signed confession from a British officer admitting the assassination of an envoy, and published it across Europe. A backcountry skirmish became an international incident with documentary proof. It is a useful demonstration that in the eighteenth century, as now, control of the narrative could matter as much as control of the ground.

From Hendrik's Broken Chain to Fort Necessity, 1753–1754

📖 Understanding This Slide

A timeline slide that compresses two years into a single visual argument. Trace it deliberately, because the causal chain is not the one most textbooks draw.

Breaking It Down

1753: Hendrik declares the Covenant Chain broken. Duquesne begins building forts. Washington carries Virginia’s warning to Fort Le Boeuf.

1754: Britain orders an intercolonial congress; Albany meets in June and July. Franklin publishes the snake in May. Jumonville Glen occurs on May 28. Fort Necessity falls on July 3. The Albany Plan is adopted on July 10 and then rejected everywhere.

The overlap: Notice that the diplomatic and military crises are happening simultaneously, not in sequence. Delegates were debating colonial union while Washington was surrendering in a flooded meadow three hundred miles away.

Why This Matters

The chain runs: a Native diplomat’s ultimatum → imperial alarm → the Albany Congress → Franklin’s Plan of Union. Meanwhile Native territorial assertions and French fort-building → Washington’s missions → open war. Native political decisions are the first cause in both chains. If you can reconstruct this timeline from memory, you have the lecture’s argument.

At the End of 1754: Divided Colonies, Contested North America

  • Native towns and nations governed the Ohio Country; the “republics” used kinship, trade, and imperial competition to preserve leverage, though not complete control.
  • French fort-building, British land claims, the damaged Covenant Chain, and rejection of the Albany Plan exposed competing interests and weak coordination.
  • Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity transformed political and diplomatic conflicts into armed confrontation.
  • Escalation was contingent: these collisions made war more likely, but war was not predetermined.
Next: an army marches into the Ohio Country — and one British general tries to solve a political crisis with force alone.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The summary slide, and the fourth bullet is the most important thing on it. Escalation was contingent. These collisions made war more likely; they did not make it inevitable.

Breaking It Down

Native governance: The republics used kinship, trade, and imperial competition to preserve leverage — though not complete control. Both halves of that sentence matter.

Imperial and colonial failure: French forts, British land claims, a damaged Covenant Chain, and the rejected Albany Plan together show competing interests and weak coordination on every side.

Transformation into violence: Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity converted political and diplomatic conflicts into armed confrontation.

Contingency: The claim is that outcomes depended on choices people actually made, and could have gone otherwise. Historians use this word to resist the illusion that the past was headed where it ended up.

Why This Matters

Contingency is the intellectual habit this lecture most wants you to acquire. It is tempting to read backward from the war and treat every earlier event as a step toward it. But Hendrik could have been satisfied; the Albany Plan could have been adopted; Washington could have been sent elsewhere. Naming the choices is what distinguishes historical explanation from the mere assertion that things had to happen. The next lecture shows what those choices cost.

  • Edwards · Tennent · Davenport · Marrant · Occom
  • Did revival challenge authority, reproduce it, or both?

Five names and one question, pointing forward to the next lecture on the Great Awakening. The instructor is declining to answer on purpose — that refusal is the assignment.

Breaking It Down

Jonathan Edwards: The Northampton theologian whose revival preaching rested on a rigorous Calvinist system — and who defended revival while insisting that emotional display proved nothing.

Gilbert Tennent: Preached that congregations might have to judge whether their own ordained ministers were genuinely converted.

Why This Matters

The classroom hour necessarily moves fast. Three Centers of Power in the Ohio Country

Native North America was not a side waiting to choose between two empires.

Britain

Pressed charter claims, trade, settlement, and land-company expansion.

France

Sought an imperial corridor linking Canada and Louisiana through forts and alliances.

Native political world

Held territory, trade networks, military power, and diplomatic systems of its own.

Neither European empire could control the Ohio Country without Native cooperation.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three centers of power, stated as a structural claim. The first line is the correction: Native North America was not a side waiting to choose between two empires. It was a third power with its own objectives.

Breaking It Down

Britain: Charter claims reaching to the Pacific on paper, active traders, settler migration, and land companies with investors expecting profit. Britain wanted the land itself.

France: A corridor of forts and alliances linking Canada to Louisiana. France wanted the route and the trade, and had neither the population nor the intention to settle the valley.

The Native political world: Territory, trade networks, military capability, and functioning diplomatic systems. Not a resource to be won but an actor with a program.

Why This Matters

The closing line is the operative claim: neither European empire could control the Ohio Country without Native cooperation. That is not a moral statement; it is a description of the balance of force in the 1750s. Both empires knew it, which is why both spent so heavily on diplomacy and gifts. When you read Lecture 4, watch what happens to this three-way structure once France is removed.

The Haudenosaunee Claim Was Real—and Fraying

The claim

  • The Six Nations' council at Onondaga claimed authority over a broad network of dependent peoples.
  • The Ohio Country was central to that claim.
  • For a time, Haudenosaunee diplomacy could force both empires to compete for its support.

The problem

  • Mingos, Delawares, and Shawnees increasingly lived beyond effective Onondaga control.
  • Ohio Valley leaders pursued their own relationships with French and British traders.
  • By the 1750s, Haudenosaunee authority was increasingly contested from within.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide does something valuable: it shows a Native power in internal difficulty. The Haudenosaunee claim to the Ohio Country was real and diplomatically potent — and by the 1750s it was being contested from within.

Breaking It Down

The claim: The Six Nations council at Onondaga asserted authority over a wide network of dependent peoples, with the Ohio Country central to it. For a time this let Haudenosaunee diplomacy force both empires to compete for its favor — genuine leverage, skillfully used.

The problem: Mingos, Delawares, and Shawnees living in the Ohio Valley were increasingly beyond effective Onondaga control. Distance mattered, and so did the fact that many had moved west precisely to gain independence.

Local diplomacy: Ohio Valley leaders dealt directly with French and British traders and officials on their own account, without clearing it through Onondaga.

Why This Matters

This internal tension is exploitable, and the British exploited it. If Onondaga can be persuaded to sign away Ohio Valley land, Britain gets a legal-looking transfer without consulting the people who live there — which is roughly what happened at Albany in 1754 and again at Fort Stanwix in 1768. Recognizing that Native polities had internal politics is not a footnote; it is how you understand the diplomacy that followed.

Tanacharison: The Half King Between Worlds

  • Tanacharison was an Ohio Iroquois leader who lived at Logstown.
  • English colonists called him the “Half King.”
  • He represented Haudenosaunee interests, but he was not an absolute monarch.
  • He was squeezed by French forts, British expansion, and weakening authority over Delawares and Shawnees.
“a Country between”
Neither France nor Britain simply owned the Ohio Country.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Meet Tanacharison, the man the English called the Half King — a title that tells you more about English confusion than about his actual position.

Breaking It Down

Who he was: An Ohio Iroquois (Mingo) leader living at Logstown, representing Haudenosaunee interests in the valley.

“Half King”: An English label reflecting the English assumption that authority must be monarchical — so a leader who could not simply command must be a partial king. He was not a monarch at all; his influence rested on persuasion and on the backing of the Onondaga council, both of which were weakening.

Squeezed from three directions: French forts advancing into the valley, British settlement and land companies pressing from the east, and his own authority over Delawares and Shawnees eroding.

“A country between”: McConnell’s phrase, quoted here. Neither empire owned the Ohio Country, and Tanacharison operated in that gap.

Why This Matters

Understanding his weakness is essential to understanding his actions. A leader whose authority is slipping and whose territory is being occupied has strong incentives to force a crisis — to make the empires fight each other rather than negotiate over his homeland. Hold that thought for two slides.

Tanacharison Was Not Washington's Sidekick

Washington's objective

  • Advance Virginia's claim.
  • Confront French troops.
  • Protect British imperial interests.

Tanacharison's objective

  • Resist French occupation.
  • Preserve Native leverage in the Ohio Country.
  • Use Britain as a counterweight to France.
Washington thought he was recruiting Native allies into Britain's war. Tanacharison was trying to recruit Britain into his struggle.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The single most important reframing in the Independent Study section, delivered in one sentence: Washington thought he was recruiting Native allies into Britain’s war; Tanacharison was trying to recruit Britain into his.

Breaking It Down

Washington’s objectives: Advance Virginia’s claim, confront French troops, protect British imperial interests. He is a very young officer executing a colony’s policy.

Tanacharison’s objectives: Resist French occupation of the valley, preserve Native leverage, and use Britain as a counterweight to France. Every item is about the Ohio Country’s independence, not about British success.

Where they overlap: Both want the French out. That shared interest is enough to produce cooperation — and it conceals the fact that they are pursuing different wars.

Why This Matters

This is a model for reading alliances generally. Allies rarely share goals; they share an obstacle. Once the obstacle is removed, the divergence surfaces — which is precisely what happens after 1763, when France is gone and Britain and its Native allies discover they wanted incompatible things all along. If you carry one idea out of this appendix, make it this one.

Jumonville Glen: A Local Strategy, a Global Crisis

  • Tanacharison guided Washington to the French party on May 28, 1754.
  • Joseph Coulon de Jumonville was killed after the brief clash; the sequence remains disputed.
  • Some contemporary accounts attributed the killing to Tanacharison after the fighting had largely stopped.
  • The French used the death to portray British action as the assassination of an envoy.
Anderson's interpretive question
Did Tanacharison act to make reconciliation between France and Britain harder, forcing Britain to resist French occupation?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Jumonville Glen, re-examined. In the classroom version this is a skirmish that got out of hand. Here it becomes a possible act of Native statecraft — and the slide is scrupulous about labeling that as an interpretive question rather than an established fact.

Breaking It Down

Tanacharison guided Washington: He located the French party and led the Virginians to it. The initiative in finding the French was his.

The disputed sequence: Jumonville was killed after the brief clash; how and by whom remains contested. Several contemporary accounts attribute the killing to Tanacharison after the fighting had largely stopped.

The French use of it: Whatever happened, France portrayed it as the assassination of a diplomatic envoy — and had Washington’s signed capitulation to wave at Europe.

Anderson’s question: Did Tanacharison act deliberately to make Franco-British reconciliation impossible, forcing Britain to commit against French occupation? Note that this is posed as a question, not asserted as a finding.

Why This Matters

If the answer is yes — and the evidence permits but does not compel it — then a Mingo leader with declining authority helped trigger a world war by making a local peace impossible. That is a startling proposition, and the intellectually honest response is to hold it as a live possibility rather than either adopting or dismissing it. Notice how the slide models that stance.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Fred Anderson – Crucible of War: The Seven Years’ War and the Fate of Empire in British North America

Anderson opens his history of the war with Jumonville Glen and gives Tanacharison genuine agency — a leader with his own strategic problem, using the empires against each other. His broader argument is that the war’s origins lie in Native political calculation as much as in imperial ambition. That claim is why this lecture exists in the form it does.

The Payoff: Three Political Projects Collided

The French and Indian War did not begin simply because two European empires wanted the same land.
  • Britain sought western expansion and imperial coordination.
  • France sought forts, trade, and an imperial corridor.
  • Native nations and communities sought sovereignty, security, and room to maneuver.
The war emerged from competing Native and imperial strategies—not from a prewritten script.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The payoff slide, and it delivers the lecture’s thesis in its strongest form: the war did not begin simply because two European empires wanted the same land. Three political projects collided.

Breaking It Down

Britain: Western expansion and imperial coordination — land for settlers, profits for companies, and a more manageable empire.

France: Forts, trade, and a corridor linking its northern and southern colonies.

Native nations and communities: Sovereignty, security, and room to maneuver between rival powers.

“Not from a prewritten script”: The contingency claim again. No one designed this war; it emerged from the interaction of three strategies whose pursuers could not all be satisfied.

Why This Matters

Compare this with the version you most likely learned before: France and Britain both wanted the Ohio Valley, so they fought. That account is not false, but it is thin — it cannot explain why the war started where and when it did, why Fort Duquesne became untenable in 1758, or why 1763 brought immediate Native war rather than peace. Three-actor explanations are harder to hold in your head and they explain far more.

Their Claims Were Not the Same as Anglo-American “Liberty”

The republics sought land, trade, kinship obligations, and diplomatic recognition on their own political terms.

  • The Delawares were not pursuing Anglo-American "liberty"
  • They wanted trade on fair terms, respected land, and honored kinship obligations
Coherent claims on their own terms — not a rough draft of Jefferson.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A caution against a well-meant error. It is tempting to make the Delawares sympathetic by describing them as early advocates of liberty. The slide refuses, and the refusal is a form of respect.

Breaking It Down

What they actually sought: Land held securely, trade on fair terms, kinship obligations honored, and diplomatic recognition as peoples with standing. These are specific, coherent, and political.

What that is not: Anglo-American “liberty” — a set of claims about individual rights, representation, consent to taxation, and the relationship of subjects to a Crown, developed inside English constitutional history.

“Not a rough draft of Jefferson”: The sharpest line on the slide. Reading Native political claims as an early version of American principles makes them count only insofar as they resemble us.

Why This Matters

This is a lesson in intellectual respect that applies to every society you will study. Taking a people seriously means understanding what they wanted in their terms, not grading how closely they anticipated our values. The Delawares had a fully coherent political program. It simply was not ours — and it does not need to be ours to deserve careful study.

A Correction Worth Making

Your textbook says the Ohio Company hired George Washington as its surveyor.

It commissioned Christopher Gist in 1750 to explore, describe, and help survey its western claims. Gist's two journeys produced important geographic intelligence and journals. George Washington's connection was family: his half-brothers Lawrence and Augustine were company investors, and Lawrence later became an important officer. Washington himself went west in 1753 as the governor's emissary, not the company's hired surveyor.

Textbooks compress. This is what checking looks like.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A textbook correction, included for a reason larger than the fact being corrected. The closing line says it: textbooks compress. This is what checking looks like.

Breaking It Down

The textbook claim: The Ohio Company hired George Washington as its surveyor.

What the record shows: The company commissioned Christopher Gist in 1750 to explore, describe, and help survey its western claims; his two journeys produced valuable geographic intelligence and detailed journals.

Washington’s actual connection: Family. His half-brothers Lawrence and Augustine were company investors, and Lawrence became an important officer in it. Washington went west in 1753 as the governor’s emissary, not as the company’s employee.

Why the error is easy: All the elements are true separately — Washington surveyed, Washington went west, Washington’s family was in the Ohio Company. Compression fuses them into a claim the sources do not support.

Why This Matters

This slide is training a skill, not correcting a trivia item. Textbooks summarize, and summary loses distinctions: who was hired versus who was connected, who was commissioned versus who was sent. Learning to notice where a smooth sentence has compressed several different facts — and then checking — is most of what separates a reader of history from a student of it.

Richard White's "Middle Ground"

  • A space where neither side could simply impose its will
  • French officials as "fathers" — obligated to provide, protect, and adjudicate
  • Not equality. Genuine reciprocity, within limits.
White's framework has been debated since 1991 — it remains essential, but it is not beyond challenge.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A reference slide on the concept that underlies this entire lecture — and note the last line, which tells you the framework has been debated for more than thirty years. Even the ideas we build lectures on are arguments, not settled facts.

Breaking It Down

“Neither side could simply impose its will”: This is the precondition. The middle ground exists only where power is genuinely balanced enough that both sides must accommodate. Remove the balance and the middle ground collapses.

French officials as “fathers”: A term both sides used and each understood somewhat differently — and the productive misunderstanding was part of how it worked. To the French it suggested authority; to Native peoples it meant an obligation to provide, protect, and mediate disputes. Both could act on it.

“Not equality. Genuine reciprocity, within limits.”: The precise formulation. The relationship was unequal in resources and unequal in what each side risked. It was nonetheless a relationship of real mutual obligation, and the obligations were enforceable in practice.

Why This Matters

Watch what happens to this framework across Chapter 4. The middle ground depends on imperial competition. In Lecture 4, France is removed from North America, Britain concludes it no longer needs to mediate, and General Amherst cuts the gift-giving that signified the relationship. The middle ground does not fade; it is dismantled — and Native nations answer with war.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard White – The Middle Ground

White introduced the concept in 1991 and has since responded to critics who argue it has been stretched to describe almost any cross-cultural contact. His own position is narrower than the popular use: the middle ground requires a rough balance of power and a shared need for cooperation. Knowing that a famous concept has boundaries — and that its author defends them — is part of using it well.

Inside the Albany Plan

  • A President General, appointed by the Crown
  • A Grand Council, elected by colonial legislatures for three-year terms
  • Authority to tax, raise armies, and manage Indian affairs across all colonies
Some features foreshadow later federal government — but this was an imperial plan, with a Crown-appointed executive, not an early draft of the Constitution.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Albany Plan’s actual contents, and a warning against the most common misreading of it. The resemblance to later American federalism is real; the intention behind it was not.

Breaking It Down

A President General appointed by the Crown: An imperial executive, not an elected one. This is the detail that settles the question of what kind of plan it was.

A Grand Council elected by colonial legislatures: Three-year terms, with representation weighted by each colony’s contribution. Genuinely representative — and answerable to assemblies rather than to voters directly.

Real powers: Taxation, raising armies, and managing Indian affairs across all the colonies. These are substantial, which is exactly why both London and the assemblies balked.

The caution: Some features foreshadow later federal arrangements — but this was an imperial plan with a Crown-appointed executive, not an early draft of the Constitution.

Why This Matters

Historians call the mistake this slide is warning against teleology — reading the past as if it were aiming at the present. Franklin in 1754 was trying to make the British Empire in America work better. Later Americans, looking for their own origins, found the Albany Plan and adopted it as an ancestor. Both things are true, and keeping them separate is the discipline: what the plan was for, and what it was later made to mean.

Hendrik at Lake George

  • 1755 — Hendrik dies fighting alongside British and provincial forces
  • The same alliance his 1753 ultimatum nearly ended
A man who told the empire where it stood — then died holding the line anyway.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A short slide with real weight. Two years after telling New York the Covenant Chain was broken and he wanted to hear no more from them, Hendrik died fighting alongside British and provincial forces at Lake George.

Breaking It Down

1755, Lake George: Hendrik, roughly seventy years old, led Mohawk warriors alongside William Johnson’s provincial force and was killed in the fighting.

The apparent contradiction: The same alliance his 1753 ultimatum nearly ended.

The resolution: The ultimatum was never a bid to leave the alliance. It was a demand that Britain honor it. When British officials responded — through the Albany Congress and, more effectively, through William Johnson’s personal diplomacy — the relationship was repaired well enough for Hendrik to fight for it.

Why This Matters

The slide’s closing line is worth sitting with: a man who told the empire where it stood, then died holding the line anyway. It is a corrective to the idea that Native leaders were either loyal auxiliaries or implacable enemies. Hendrik was a political actor who criticized an alliance because he wanted it to work — which is what serious allies do, and what this lecture has been asking you to recognize throughout.

Breaking It Down

If your grandparents took a survey course, they were taught a different story — one with two empires and a wilderness between them. History is a living field, and this lecture is a report from its current state rather than a fixed body of received facts.

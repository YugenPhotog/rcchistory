This Was Not Empty Land

The Ohio Country was a lived-in political world.

  • Shawnee communities in the upper Ohio watershed
  • Delawares, pushed steadily west for a century
  • Senecas and other Haudenosaunee, claiming authority over the region, though valley residents did not necessarily accept it
  • Miami communities, and Great Lakes peoples with their own histories of displacement
None of them were waiting for Europeans to organize their affairs.
Professor's Reminders
Haudenosaunee is the confederacy's own name. Your textbook calls the same confederacy the Iroquois or Six Nations.
Independent mini-lecture: Native Power and Tanacharison's War

Logstown and Its Sister Villages

  • Delawares from the east
  • Shawnees from the south
  • Mingoes — Haudenosaunee migrants often identified in English records as Iroquois
  • Great Lakes peoples, further displaced
Multiethnic towns were built through migration, displacement, intermarriage, trade, and political choice.
Not refugee camps — productive towns, with farms, trade, and families.

Kinship Was the Security Policy

Many villages held relatives and allies from several nearby peoples.
To attack Logstown was to attack someone's cousins — who lived in villages you might need tomorrow.

Use this as a structural explanation, not a literal claim that every village contained relatives from every neighboring nation. Kinship, marriage, adoption, and alliance created overlapping obligations across communities and made violence politically costly.

The French Called Some Multiethnic Towns “Republics”

The label described communities with dispersed authority — not French settlements

How it worked

  • Gifts, mediation, ritual reciprocity
  • French officials as "fathers" — with real obligations attached

By the 1740s

  • French supply disruptions and personnel turnover strain the relationship
  • Veteran diplomats die or leave office
  • Replacements give orders instead of negotiating
Historian's Corner
Richard White's Middle Ground describes the Great Lakes region, including the Ohio River Valley, as a place where no single power could simply impose its will. French officials and Native communities built workable relationships through negotiation, adaptation, and reciprocal obligations—though the relationship was never equal.

Do not present Louisbourg as causing a uniform, permanent collapse of French trade. The background essay emphasizes uneven effects: wartime disruption, the loss of experienced personnel, and changing French practice weakened relationships, even though Louisbourg returned to France in 1748.

The Republics Had Leverage — But Not Full Control

Kinship, trade, and local autonomy gave the republic towns leverage — but not absolute control.

What it made possible

  • Deterring violence through widening kinship obligations
  • Negotiating among French, British, and Native powers
  • Preserving room for local decision-making

What it could not stop alone

  • Imperial armies and sustained occupation
  • Settler migration backed by land companies
  • Land grants written in a distant capital
British Trade Gave the Republics Another Option
Cheaper goods could loosen French alliance obligations and strengthen local bargaining power.

By the Early 1750s: Four Claims, One Valley

The Valley Itself
Communities governed, used, and defended the valley through residence, kinship, and local authority.
France
An imperial corridor linking Canada and Louisiana, reinforced by forts and diplomacy.
Virginia & Pennsylvania
Charters, traders, and land companies pressed competing colonial claims westward.
The Haudenosaunee
Diplomats asserted suzerainty, though valley peoples contested the claim.

These are four competing claims or political projects, not four unified sides. Each category contained internal disagreements. The Haudenosaunee claim to authority in the Ohio Country was real, but many valley residents contested it; do not imply that all Native peoples acted as one bloc.

The Covenant Chain: An Alliance That Required Renewal

  • The Covenant Chain was the metaphor governing Haudenosaunee–British relations.
  • Alliance required repeated gifts, ceremony, consultation, and attention to grievances.
  • Left untended, the Chain tarnished; neglected long enough, it could break.
Covenant Chain diplomatic exchange

Generated reconstruction of alliance diplomacy

The Covenant Chain is not a literal fifth political party. It is a diplomatic metaphor for a relationship that required repeated renewal through gifts, ceremony, consultation, and the settlement of grievances.

Hendrik

  • Theyanoguin, known to English colonists as Hendrik, was a Mohawk sachem and diplomatic spokesman.
  • He spent decades navigating the relationship between the Mohawk community and British colonial authorities.
  • By 1753, he was deeply frustrated by unresolved grievances and the failure to renew reciprocal obligations.
What did he expect? British officials were expected to renew the Covenant Chain through gifts and ceremony, protect Mohawk interests, and address grievances over land, traders, alcohol, and inadequate protection. The complaint was broader than gifts alone.
Interpretive reconstruction; no verified contemporary portrait of Theyanoguin survives

"We Desire to Hear No More of You"

The Covenant Chain is broken between you and us. So brother you are not to hear of me any more, and Brother we desire to hear no more of you.
— Hendrik (Theyanoguin) to the Governor of New York, 1753

Albany, 1754

  • Britain ordered New York to host an intercolonial congress.
  • Delegates sought to redress Haudenosaunee grievances and coordinate colonial defense.
  • The congress met from June 19 through July 11, 1754.
Albany Congress, 1754

Generated reconstruction of the Albany Congress

Chronology note: Fort Necessity was fought on July 3, while the Albany Congress was still meeting. Do not say that news of Washington's defeat arrived after the conference. The congress had been called because of the preexisting alliance and French-expansion crises; the defeat intensified the emergency during the meeting.

Franklin's Snake

  • Franklin published “JOIN, or DIE” in the Pennsylvania Gazette on May 9, 1754.
  • His proposed union included a Crown-appointed President General and an elected Grand Council.
  • The plan would have coordinated taxation, defense, and Native diplomacy.
Pennsylvania Gazette, May 9, 1754

Adopted at Albany. Rejected Everywhere Else.

  • The Congress adopted Franklin's plan on July 10, 1754.
  • No colonial legislature later adopted the plan.
  • The Crown rejected it in practice by refusing to enact it.

"It Needed No Explanation"

Duquesne's assumption
  • Duquesne sent about 2,000 Canadians to build a line of forts in 1753.
  • French officials assumed the forts strengthened Native alliances and needed no explanation.

  • Delawares and Shawnees asked whether Virginians would expel the French and then go home.

Washington Carries Virginia's Warning

  • In late 1753, Governor Robert Dinwiddie sent the young George Washington to Fort Le Boeuf.
  • Washington demanded that the French leave territory Virginia claimed in the Ohio Country.
  • French commander Jacques Legardeur de Saint-Pierre refused and asserted France's claim.
  • Washington returned with intelligence that made the imperial dispute an immediate military crisis.

Jumonville Glen: May 28, 1754

Open the video on YouTube

Washington and about forty men, guided by Tanacharison, surrounded a French party in a ravine.

Joseph Coulon de Jumonville was killed during the brief clash, and several French soldiers were captured.

The sequence of events and who fired first remain contested in the surviving accounts.

French officials described Jumonville as an envoy who had been assassinated; British accounts presented the encounter as a military engagement.

Conceptual payoff: The political meaning of the clash mattered as much as the gunfire: each side used it to justify its next move.

Fort Necessity: July 3, 1754

  • A larger French and Canadian force with Indigenous allies attacked Washington at Great Meadows.
  • Rain, poor shelter, wet firearms, hunger, and losses forced Washington to surrender.
  • The French capitulation called Jumonville's death an “assassination”; Washington later disputed the translation.
The careful conclusion
The document made Washington appear to admit responsibility for assassinating a French envoy, whether or not he understood it that way. The defeat helped turn the frontier crisis into open war.
Battle of Fort Necessity in heavy rain

Generated reconstruction; fort and troop positions are interpretive

The safest claim is that the capitulation's wording created a damaging French diplomatic narrative. Washington later disputed the translation, but the surviving document does not by itself prove that he was deliberately tricked. Keep the focus on how the document was used politically.

At the End of 1754: Divided Colonies, Contested North America

  • Native towns and nations governed the Ohio Country; the “republics” used kinship, trade, and imperial competition to preserve leverage, though not complete control.
  • French fort-building, British land claims, the damaged Covenant Chain, and rejection of the Albany Plan exposed competing interests and weak coordination.
  • Jumonville Glen and Fort Necessity transformed political and diplomatic conflicts into armed confrontation.
  • Escalation was contingent: these collisions made war more likely, but war was not predetermined.
Next: an army marches into the Ohio Country — and one British general tries to solve a political crisis with force alone.

Closing frame: Emphasize that the war was not preordained. The useful “third power” in this story is Indigenous political society—not a single unified “Indian empire.” The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was one important actor, but Ohio Valley communities also governed themselves and pursued distinct interests. British and French imperial strategies collided with that sovereignty, while fractured colonial coordination and damaged alliances made escalation more likely.

Independent Study

Native Power, Tanacharison, and the Crisis at Jumonville Glen

For the online hour.

Three Centers of Power in the Ohio Country

Native North America was not a side waiting to choose between two empires.

Britain

Pressed charter claims, trade, settlement, and land-company expansion.

France

Sought an imperial corridor linking Canada and Louisiana through forts and alliances.

Native political world

Held territory, trade networks, military power, and diplomatic systems of its own.

Neither European empire could control the Ohio Country without Native cooperation.

This is the mini-lecture's central correction. Anderson's argument is not simply that Indians were a third side in a European war. Native political society was a third center of power, though not a single unified empire. The phrase “third power” is safer and more accurate than “the Indian empire.”

The Haudenosaunee Claim Was Real—and Fraying

The claim

  • The Six Nations' council at Onondaga claimed authority over a broad network of dependent peoples.
  • The Ohio Country was central to that claim.
  • For a time, Haudenosaunee diplomacy could force both empires to compete for its support.

The problem

  • Mingos, Delawares, and Shawnees increasingly lived beyond effective Onondaga control.
  • Ohio Valley leaders pursued their own relationships with French and British traders.
  • By the 1750s, Haudenosaunee authority was increasingly contested from within.

“Aggressive neutrality” describes a strategy, not passivity: Haudenosaunee leaders could threaten to shift toward France or Britain and thereby extract gifts, trade, diplomacy, and concessions. But do not turn this into a story of one unified Iroquois empire. The Confederacy's claim and the Ohio communities' independence existed at the same time.

Tanacharison: The Half King Between Worlds

  • Tanacharison was an Ohio Iroquois leader who lived at Logstown.
  • English colonists called him the “Half King.”
  • He represented Haudenosaunee interests, but he was not an absolute monarch.
  • He was squeezed by French forts, British expansion, and weakening authority over Delawares and Shawnees.
“a Country between”
Neither France nor Britain simply owned the Ohio Country.

Tanacharison is also called Tanaghrisson or Tanacharison in English-language sources. Explain “Half King” as a colonial label for a diplomatic representative, not a translation of an Indigenous constitutional office. His authority was real but negotiated and increasingly precarious.

Tanacharison Was Not Washington's Sidekick

Washington's objective

  • Advance Virginia's claim.
  • Confront French troops.
  • Protect British imperial interests.

Tanacharison's objective

  • Resist French occupation.
  • Preserve Native leverage in the Ohio Country.
  • Use Britain as a counterweight to France.
Washington thought he was recruiting Native allies into Britain's war. Tanacharison was trying to recruit Britain into his struggle.

This is the conceptual payoff: the same alliance could serve different political projects. Avoid implying that Tanacharison spoke for every Ohio Valley community. His actions were strategic, but they also reflected the limits of his authority.

Jumonville Glen: A Local Strategy, a Global Crisis

  • Tanacharison guided Washington to the French party on May 28, 1754.
  • Joseph Coulon de Jumonville was killed after the brief clash; the sequence remains disputed.
  • Some contemporary accounts attributed the killing to Tanacharison after the fighting had largely stopped.
  • The French used the death to portray British action as the assassination of an envoy.
Anderson's interpretive question
Did Tanacharison act to make reconciliation between France and Britain harder, forcing Britain to resist French occupation?

Present this as an interpretation grounded in contested evidence, not as settled fact. The National Park Service notes that accounts differ and that Tanacharison was reported to have killed Jumonville. Anderson's larger point is agency: Tanacharison had reasons of his own for escalating the confrontation.

The Payoff: Three Political Projects Collided

The French and Indian War did not begin simply because two European empires wanted the same land.
  • Britain sought western expansion and imperial coordination.
  • France sought forts, trade, and an imperial corridor.
  • Native nations and communities sought sovereignty, security, and room to maneuver.
The war emerged from competing Native and imperial strategies—not from a prewritten script.

Close by returning students to slide 2: this was not empty land. The important distinction is not “Europeans versus Indians,” but multiple political systems interacting in a densely inhabited region. The Haudenosaunee Confederacy was central, yet Ohio Valley peoples were not merely its subjects or European allies.

Their Claims Were Not the Same as Anglo-American “Liberty”

The republics sought land, trade, kinship obligations, and diplomatic recognition on their own political terms.

  • The Delawares were not pursuing Anglo-American "liberty"
  • They wanted trade on fair terms, respected land, and honored kinship obligations
Coherent claims on their own terms — not a rough draft of Jefferson.

A Correction Worth Making

Your textbook says the Ohio Company hired George Washington as its surveyor.

It commissioned Christopher Gist in 1750 to explore, describe, and help survey its western claims. Gist's two journeys produced important geographic intelligence and journals. George Washington's connection was family: his half-brothers Lawrence and Augustine were company investors, and Lawrence later became an important officer. Washington himself went west in 1753 as the governor's emissary, not the company's hired surveyor.

Textbooks compress. This is what checking looks like.

Richard White's "Middle Ground"

  • A space where neither side could simply impose its will
  • French officials as "fathers" — obligated to provide, protect, and adjudicate
  • Not equality. Genuine reciprocity, within limits.
White's framework has been debated since 1991 — it remains essential, but it is not beyond challenge.

Inside the Albany Plan

  • A President General, appointed by the Crown
  • A Grand Council, elected by colonial legislatures for three-year terms
  • Authority to tax, raise armies, and manage Indian affairs across all colonies
Some features foreshadow later federal government — but this was an imperial plan, with a Crown-appointed executive, not an early draft of the Constitution.

Hendrik at Lake George

  • 1755 — Hendrik dies fighting alongside British and provincial forces
  • The same alliance his 1753 ultimatum nearly ended
A man who told the empire where it stood — then died holding the line anyway.

Mingoes

Iroquois people — primarily Seneca and Cayuga — who relocated westward into the Ohio Country during the 18th century, settling outside the direct authority of the Haudenosaunee Confederacy's core territory in New York.

Their move gave them independence from Confederacy oversight, but also meant British colonial officials sometimes questioned whether Confederacy leaders could speak for them.

Ohio Country

The eighteenth-century region organized around the Ohio River and its tributaries, including parts of present-day Ohio, western Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Kentucky, and Indiana.

It was a populated and contested political world, not an empty frontier awaiting European settlement.

Haudenosaunee

The name used by the Six Nations Confederacy for itself. In this lecture, the term includes the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, Seneca, and Tuscarora nations.

Some Haudenosaunee communities and migrants lived in or claimed authority over parts of the Ohio Country, but they did not always speak with one voice.

Suzerainty

A claim to superior authority over another people while allowing that people some internal independence.

Haudenosaunee diplomats sometimes asserted this kind of authority over Ohio Valley peoples. Those communities did not necessarily accept the claim, which made land cessions and diplomacy contested.

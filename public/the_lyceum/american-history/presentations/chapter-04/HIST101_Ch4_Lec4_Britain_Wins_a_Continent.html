Part Two

Native Nations Reconsider the War

1758: the alliance that stopped paying

France was outnumbered from the beginning. Native allies were how it survived anyway.

An Uneasy Accommodation, 1758

  • British and colonial officials negotiate with western Native peoples
  • Quaker mediators help broker the talks
  • Britain buys peace with one promise:
No seizure of Native land after the war.

Remember that promise. It will matter more in 1763 than it did in 1758.

Why the French Alliance Stopped Paying

  • French goods and gifts run short
  • Native leaders doubt France can win
  • Less willingness to fight other Native peoples for European quarrels

Senecas fighting with France and Mohawks fighting with Britain begin avoiding each other — the first stirrings of a pan-Indian identity.

They did not "switch sides"

Native nations kept pursuing their own interests. Britain was simply, for the moment, the better bet.

France used Native alliances to make up for having so few people.
When the alliances weakened, the arithmetic came back.

Part Four

From the Ohio Valley to a World War

1760–1763

Canada falls — and the war keeps going, because the war was never only here.

Still a World War

The Seven Years' War is being fought across Europe, the Caribbean, Africa, India, and the oceans between them.

Sea power lets Britain attack French possessions anywhere in the world.

Britain gains

  • French claims east of the Mississippi (except New Orleans)
  • Florida, from Spain

France keeps

  • Martinique
  • Guadeloupe

Spain gains

  • New Orleans

France Disappears

France is no longer a territorial power on the North American mainland.

Three earlier wars left this map essentially intact. This one erased a color from it.

Victory Looks Different from the Colonies

British Americans celebrate. They expect:

  • Security on the frontier
  • Prosperity
  • Western land
  • Room to expand
  • Reward for having helped win

Native Nations Reject the Peace

European diplomats treated Native homelands as something France could hand over.

No one had conquered them. The position of Native nations, as the textbook summarizes it

France, they argued, could not give away what France had never owned.

The Old Balance Is Gone

Before 1763

Britain ⟵ Native nations ⟶ France

  • Play one empire against the other
  • Threaten to shift alliances
  • Demand gifts and trade

After 1763

British Empire ⟷ Native nations

  • No second empire to bargain with
  • Far less room to maneuver
British victory made Britain stronger — and removed the rivalry that had helped Native nations protect their independence.

So notice the direction

The continent after 1763 is less stable, not more.

They Won the Same War and Learned Different Lessons

Colonists remember

  • Their soldiers
  • Their money and supplies
  • Years of frontier war
  • Being partners in victory

British officials remember

  • Colonial foot-dragging
  • Fights over men and money
  • Thirteen governments to coordinate
  • That regulars, money, and the navy won it

The Paradox of Victory

Britain's winning strategy ran on cooperation and consent.

Land

Colonists expect to move west. Native nations say the land is theirs.

Security

An enormous new empire to defend, administer, and supply.

Money

The war was ruinously expensive. Holding the winnings will cost more.

Can a larger, more expensive empire keep being run through consent? British policymakers are about to answer no.

Why Was This War Different?

Britain won through organization

Pitt turned money, manpower, colonial participation, and sea power into an imperial war effort.

The continent was transformed

France lost its mainland empire; Native nations lost the diplomatic balance that had protected their independence.

Victory created a new crisis

A larger, costlier empire and rising colonial expectations now had to be reconciled.

Britain won the continent in 1763.

The harder question was what to do with it.

Vocabulary

Forks of the Ohio — Junction of the Allegheny and Monongahela; the strategic prize the war began over.

Fort Duquesne / Fort Pitt — The French post at the Forks, burned in 1758, replaced by a British one.

Provincial troops — Soldiers raised by colonial assemblies, distinct from British regulars.

Louisbourg — French fortress guarding the Gulf of St. Lawrence; taken 1758.

Pan-Indian identity — An emerging sense of shared interests among Native nations across tribal lines.

Plains of Abraham — The plateau above Quebec where the decisive battle was fought, 13 September 1759.

Seven Years' War — The global conflict of which the French and Indian War was the North American theater.

Peace of Paris (1763) — The settlement that removed France as a mainland territorial power.

Every term above has a pop-up definition on the slide where it first appears. Click the gold dotted words.

Forks of the Ohio

The point where the Allegheny and Monongahela rivers join to form the Ohio — today, Pittsburgh.

Why it matters here: whoever held the Forks controlled the water route into the Ohio Valley. This is the ground the war began over in 1754, and Britain finally takes it in November 1758 — four and a half years before the war ends.

Fort Duquesne / Fort Pitt

The French post built at the Forks of the Ohio in 1754. French forces destroyed and abandoned it in November 1758; Britain built Fort Pitt on the site.

Why it matters here: this is the fort the war started over. Britain finally gets it — and the war runs on for five more years. That gap is the puzzle this whole lecture exists to solve.

Provincial troops

Soldiers raised, paid, and officered by individual colonies — not part of the British regular army.

Why it matters here: the friction between provincials and regulars over rank, discipline, and enlistment was one of the things wrecking the British war effort. Pitt's willingness to accommodate provincial officers is exactly what unlocked roughly 20,000 colonial soldiers a year.

Pan-Indian identity

A growing recognition among separate Native nations that they shared interests against European expansion, even when they were formally allied to rival empires.

Why it matters here: Senecas fighting alongside France and Mohawks fighting alongside Britain increasingly declined to fight each other. That is a new political idea forming inside an imperial war — and it will shape what happens after 1763.

Seven Years' War

The wider conflict fought across Europe, the Caribbean, Africa, India, and the world's oceans. What Americans call the French and Indian War is its North American theater.

Why it matters here: it explains why Canada could fall in 1760 and the war still run to 1763 — and why the peace settlement traded Caribbean islands against a continent.

Peace of Paris, 1763

The treaty ending the war. Britain took Canada and French claims east of the Mississippi except New Orleans, plus Florida from Spain; France kept Martinique and Guadeloupe; Spain received New Orleans and Louisiana west of the Mississippi.

Why it matters here: it is the moment the map changes — and the moment Native nations are told their homelands changed hands without them.

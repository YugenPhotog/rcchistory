# Chapter 7 Lecture 1 material to transfer to Lecture 2

This memo records useful material removed from the formal Lecture 1 sequence during the revision toward the new four-lecture arc. Lecture 2 should develop Hamilton and Jefferson's competing visions of what the republic should become.

## Material moved out of the formal Lecture 1 narrative

### 1. Hamilton's problem was public credibility

Former slide title: **“Hamilton's Problem Was Credibility.”**

Useful teaching point: Hamilton's program was not simply “pro-bank” or “pro-rich.” He was trying to make the new federal government credible to creditors and capable of borrowing, raising revenue, and responding to war or crisis. This is a strong opening for Lecture 2's question about what kind of republic Hamilton wanted to build.

Useful language from the former notes:

- A government that cannot pay its debts cannot borrow.
- A government that cannot borrow has little room to survive war or crisis.
- Hamilton's problem was state capacity: public credit, revenue, borrowing, and international credibility.

Source retained from the former deck:

- Alexander Hamilton, “Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit,” Founders Online: <https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-06-02-0076-0002-0001>

### 2. Assumption turned state debt into a national political question

Former slide title: **“Assumption: Debt Becomes National Power.”**

Useful conceptual chain:

**State debts → federal debt → federal revenue → national political loyalty**

The former slide's main claim was that Hamilton was not only paying bills; he was moving political gravity toward the national government. In Lecture 2, connect this policy to the competing question of whether national power would stabilize the republic or subordinate the states.

Useful nuance for later use:

- Soldiers and suppliers had often sold certificates cheaply during hard times.
- Speculators frequently held the certificates when repayment plans were adopted.
- The policy therefore raised a fairness question as well as a credibility question.

Do not present the fairness issue as a settled moral verdict. It works best as evidence that national state-building could distribute benefits and burdens unevenly.

### 3. The Bank debate made implied powers politically consequential

Former slide title: **“The Bank Debate.”**

The removed slide framed the disagreement as a fight over how the Constitution would be read in practice:

- Jefferson: if “necessary” means merely convenient, federal power has no clear limit.
- Hamilton: a government with legitimate ends must be able to choose useful means.

This belongs in Lecture 2 because it is not merely an example of institution-building. It is a direct conflict over the republic's constitutional limits, economic future, and definition of legitimate national power.

Sources retained from the former deck:

- Thomas Jefferson, “Opinion on the Constitutionality of the Bank,” Founders Online: <https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-19-02-0051>
- Alexander Hamilton, “Opinion on the Constitutionality of the Bank,” Founders Online: <https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Hamilton/01-08-02-0060-0003>

### 4. The former Hamilton-versus-frontier activity

Former activity title: **“Make the Case: Hamilton vs. the Frontier.”**

Former task: students wrote Hamilton's strongest argument for reliable revenue and the strongest western argument that a distant government was taxing a local survival economy.

This activity should not be reused unchanged in Lecture 1 because the revised Lecture 1 now treats the Whiskey Rebellion as a test of constitutional enforcement and legitimacy. It could be adapted for Lecture 2 if that lecture explicitly compares Hamilton's national economic vision with western and agrarian fears of concentrated power.

## Material that remains available as optional depth

The revised Lecture 1 keeps these items in the Rabbit Hole rather than teaching them as required content:

- The fairness controversy over original versus current debt holders.
- The assumption-capital bargain and the Potomac location of the future capital.
- Detailed administrative texture about customhouses and federal paperwork.

These items can support Lecture 2, but the formal Lecture 2 sequence should organize them around competing visions rather than present them as a list of Hamilton policies.

## Boundary for Lecture 2

Lecture 1 now teaches that Congress and the Washington administration made the Constitution operational through institutions, routines, rights protections, and enforceable law. Lecture 2 should begin with the political consequence: once the federal government could act, Americans disagreed about what that power should be used to accomplish and how far it should reach.

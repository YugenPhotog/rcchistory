HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 3

Can a Republic Have an Empire?

Jefferson, Louisiana, and the problem of expansion

Opening note: This deck follows the uncoverage principle. We are not covering every part of Jefferson's presidency. We are using Louisiana to uncover one central problem: how can a republic built against empire expand like one?

Formal lecture target: 35-40 minutes with two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper begins after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment only.

One Question, Not Everything

The textbook gives the broad Jefferson story. Today we follow one problem.

What happens when a republic that fears empire suddenly becomes enormous?

This explicitly applies Calder-style uncoverage. Students are reading the textbook for breadth. We are not covering Marbury, Burr, every policy, and every expedition detail. We focus on expansion, power, and sovereignty.

“Port of New-Orleans Shut”

A headline, not a battle, begins the crisis.
In 1802, Spanish officials suspended Americans' right to deposit goods at New Orleans.

What happens to western farmers when someone closes their road to the ocean?

Use this as the inciting incident. The right of deposit was a practical economic issue, not an abstract diplomatic detail. Western farmers needed New Orleans because river commerce connected them to markets.

Source: Library of Congress, Louisiana Purchase legislative timeline and “Port of New-Orleans shut” broadside. Live source

Why One Port Terrified a Republic

Western farms→Mississippi River→New Orleans→World markets
If New Orleans became hostile, western loyalty and national unity could become fragile.

The point is not just economics. The Mississippi connected western settlement to the Union. If another empire controlled the outlet, Americans feared the West could turn away from the Atlantic republic.

Jefferson's Nightmare

“The day that France takes possession of New Orleans...we must marry ourselves to the British fleet and nation.”
Thomas Jefferson to Robert Livingston, 1802
France at New Orleans might force Jefferson into the British alliance he wanted to avoid.

Jefferson's foreign policy sought independence from European entanglements. A strong France on the Mississippi threatens that. This makes the crisis a sovereignty problem.

Source: Thomas Jefferson to Robert R. Livingston, 18 April 1802, Founders Online. Live source

Haiti Changes the Deal

Saint-Domingue

France tries to rebuild a Caribbean slave empire.

Resistance

Black revolutionaries and epidemic disease destroy the project.

Napoleon

Louisiana becomes less useful and harder to defend.

Louisiana became available because empire failed somewhere else.

Avoid monocausality: Haiti alone did not cause the Purchase. But Saint-Domingue belongs inside the story because Napoleon's Caribbean plan and its collapse changed the strategic value of Louisiana.

Teaching point: One revolutionary slaveholding republic expands as the Atlantic world's first independent Black republic emerges.

France Offers More Than Jefferson Asked For

American goal

Secure New Orleans and navigation.

French offer

All of Louisiana for about $15 million.

Livingston and Monroe did not have clear instructions to buy the interior of the continent.

Emphasize surprise and contingency. The envoys wanted New Orleans. They were offered far more than expected and chose to exceed instructions because the chance might vanish.

Would You Take the Deal?

You decide • Constitutional dilemma
You are Jefferson. France offers all of Louisiana, but the Constitution does not clearly say you can buy foreign territory.
A. Take the deal now.
B. Wait for a constitutional amendment.
C. Buy only New Orleans.
D. Reject the offer.
Write one sentence: Which choice best protects the republic, and what risk does it create?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 1 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 3-4 minutes. Ask students to name both the benefit and the risk. The activity frames Jefferson's constitutional dilemma as a real governing problem, not a simple hypocrisy charge.

Historical-thinking moves: Cause, consequence, and contingency.

Jefferson Buys First, Explains Later

Strict principle

Federal power should stay within clearly delegated authority.

Presidential reality

The opportunity was enormous and might disappear.

Jefferson drafted amendment language, then accepted a broader practical reading of treaty power.

Do not reduce this to hypocrisy. Jefferson's dilemma was genuine. He believed the purchase advanced national security and republican future, but he knew the constitutional text did not supply a neat instruction manual.

Source: Jefferson, Draft Amendment on Louisiana, Founders Online. Live source

Why Expansion Looked Like Liberty

Land

Independent farmers need access to land.

Virtue

Property supports independent citizenship.

Scale

A federal republic might spread across a continent.

Jefferson's empire of liberty was not just a slogan. It was a theory of republican survival.

Make Jefferson intellectually legible before complicating him. For Jefferson, land could prevent dependence, crowding, and European-style hierarchy. That is why expansion could look like liberty from inside the settler republic.

Source: Jefferson to Indiana Territory General Assembly, 28 December 1805, Founders Online. Live source

What Did France Actually Sell?

France could transfer its imperial claim. It could not make Native nations disappear.
Treaty claim≠Effective control≠Native consent

This is the map-versus-sovereignty hinge. Recent scholarship treats the purchase as a transfer of European claims and preemption rights, not full ownership of Native homelands. Keep the language clear for first-year students: the map changed faster than power did.

Scholarship: Robert Lee, “Accounting for Conquest,” Journal of American History. Live source

The Map Changed Faster Than Sovereignty

In Paris

A treaty transferred France's claim to the United States.

On the ground

Native nations still governed homelands, trade routes, villages, and diplomatic relationships.

A useful map, if we ask what its colors actually mean.

Use the map as evidence, not decoration. Ask students to interpret the map: what does the colored area mean? It means an American claim, not immediate possession of every acre.

Lewis and Clark Were Not Just Exploring

Map

Rivers, mountains, routes.

Record

Plants, animals, climate, soil.

Report

Native nations, population, alliances, trade.

Announce

A new American diplomatic claim.

The expedition converted American ignorance into American state knowledge.

Keep this concise. This is not an “adventure of discovery” lecture. The expedition's purposes were scientific, commercial, diplomatic, and imperial.

Source: Jefferson, Instructions for Meriwether Lewis, 20 June 1803, Founders Online. Live source

If It's U.S. Territory, Why Are Guns Drawn?

Read the map against the story
In 1804, the expedition nearly came to violence with Teton Lakota leaders on the Missouri River.
The map says: this is now U.S. territory.
The encounter says: American sovereignty is not yet effective here.
Write one sentence: What does this confrontation reveal about the Louisiana Purchase map?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 2 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 4-5 minutes. Students should connect map evidence to encounter evidence. The point is not that the map is useless. It is that students must interpret what the map represents.

Historical-thinking moves: Evidence, comparison, and context.

Source: Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, 25 September 1804, University of Nebraska. Live source

The Expedition Kept Depending on Native Worlds

Mandan and Hidatsa villages

Established agricultural and commercial centers, not empty wilderness.

Sacagawea and Shoshone kinship

Language, relationships, and horses made the route possible.

The journey later symbolized American mastery, but its success repeatedly depended on Indigenous knowledge and diplomacy.

This combines Mandan/Hidatsa and Sacagawea in the formal lecture. Do not overdo expedition stops. The main point is dependence: U.S. claims were weak without Native knowledge, trade, diplomacy, and permission.

Liberty for Whom?

White settlers

Expansion promised land and republican independence.

Native nations

Expansion meant pressure on sovereignty and land.

Enslaved people

Louisiana was already a slave society, and expansion widened slavery's future.

This slide protects against turning “empire of liberty” into a simple triumph. Louisiana already contained slavery, free people of color, and colonial legal orders. The purchase opened future conflicts over slavery and territorial incorporation.

A Republic Acting Like a Power of the Earth

The United States had revolted against empire. Now it was making treaties, claiming territory, sending expeditions, and trying to shape a continent.

Maybe the empire was not outside the republic. Maybe it was inside the republic's idea of liberty.

This is the interpretive synthesis before the final slide. The U.S. saw removal of European power as anti-imperial. Native nations could experience the same act as the arrival of a stronger, closer imperial claimant.

What We Uncovered

Power

Jefferson used national power to secure the republic's future.

Sovereignty

The Purchase changed claims faster than it changed control.

Liberty

Expansion could look like freedom to settlers and dispossession to others.

Next: why did a republic that feared war choose war?

Want to Go Deeper?

The Boundaries Were Unclear

Optional enrichment
No one possessed a neat modern map in 1803.
  • France and the United States did not agree on every boundary.
  • Spain rejected some U.S. interpretations.
  • Ambiguity became part of later diplomacy and conflict.
Big idea: a treaty can create new disputes as well as settle old ones.

Optional only. Useful if students ask whether the Louisiana boundaries were known or fixed at purchase.

The “Great Father” Problem

Optional enrichment
The anti-monarchical republic used paternal imperial language.
  • U.S. officials presented the president as a “Great Father” to Native nations.
  • The phrase borrowed from imperial diplomatic habits.
  • Native leaders could accept medals and gifts without accepting U.S. subordination claims.
Big idea: republican language at home could become imperial language in Native diplomacy.

Optional enrichment from Zachary Conn's 2026 work. Excellent nuance but not required for formal lecture pacing.

Jefferson's Native Policy Was Not Simple Benevolence

Optional enrichment
Trade, debt, “civilization,” and land cessions fit together.
  • Jefferson hoped trade would draw Native communities toward U.S. dependence.
  • Debt could pressure land cessions.
  • Assimilation and dispossession could operate in the same policy world.
Big idea: peaceful language did not remove coercive consequences.

Optional only. Do not assess unless later incorporated formally.

Source: Jefferson to William Henry Harrison, 27 February 1803, Founders Online. Live source

New Orleans Was Already a Slave Society

Optional enrichment
Incorporation did not affect everyone the same way.
  • White residents negotiated law, language, religion, and political rights.
  • Free people of color faced a changing racial order.
  • Enslaved people were incorporated as property, not as citizens.
Big idea: adding territory also meant adding people into unequal legal systems.

Optional enrichment only. The formal lecture flags slavery and incorporation; this adds additional nuance for interested students.

What Happens When the Republic Governs People Who Did Not Consent?

Optional enrichment
Territorial government created a republican contradiction.
  • The treaty promised eventual incorporation.
  • Congress initially governed territories through appointed officials.
  • Residents could be told they were destined for self-government while not yet controlling their government.
Big idea: expansion turned republican self-government into a staged and unequal process.

Optional enrichment only. Useful bridge to future territorial expansion and sectional conflict.

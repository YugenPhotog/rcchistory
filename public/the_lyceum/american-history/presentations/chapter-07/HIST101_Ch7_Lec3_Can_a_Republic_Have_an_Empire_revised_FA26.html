Can a Republic Have an Empire?

Native sovereignty, settler liberty, slavery, and continental power

Opening note: This revised deck follows the uncoverage principle. It asks how territorial growth could look like republican liberty to white settlers while producing dispossession, coercion, and an enlarged future for slavery.

Formal lecture target: Approximately 35 minutes with exactly two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper begins after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment only.

Pacing map: Bridge and Old Northwest 7 minutes; Jeffersonian expansion and New Orleans 7 minutes; Haiti, Purchase, and Activity 1 about 9 minutes; sovereignty, Lewis and Clark, and Activity 2 about 8 minutes; Tecumseh and synthesis about 4 minutes.

Jefferson's Republicans Inherited National Power—and Used It to Expand

Diplomacy

Negotiate with empires and Native nations.

Territory

Acquire and govern land.

Knowledge

Map routes, peoples, and resources.

Administration

Turn claims into policy.

What happens when a republic committed to liberty also wants land?

Continuity from Lecture 2: Do not reteach Jeffersonian ideology. Recall only that Republicans now controlled the national machinery they had once feared. Jefferson did not simply dismantle federal capacity; he used it diplomatically, territorially, scientifically, and administratively.

American Maps Expanded Faster Than American Sovereignty

U.S. claim

The Treaty of Paris placed the Old Northwest inside American boundaries.

Native sovereignty

Native nations governed homelands, towns, trade, and diplomacy across the region.

British influence

British posts and alliances remained part of the contest for the Northwest.

Claim on a map≠Effective control≠Native consent

This establishes the recurring distinction before Louisiana. Avoid saying the land was empty or that all Native nations acted as one. The United States claimed the region, but claims had to confront existing sovereignty and British power on the ground.

Source: National Park Service, “Historical Overview of Fallen Timbers Battlefield and Fort Miamis.” Live source

Native Military Resistance Forced the United States to Fight for the Old Northwest

1790

Native confederacy defeats Harmar's expedition.

1791

Forces led by Little Turtle and Blue Jacket destroy St. Clair's army.

1794

Anthony Wayne wins at Fallen Timbers near a British fort.

1795

Greenville cessions open more Ohio land to U.S. settlement.

The United States did not simply settle western land; it used war and treaties to turn claims into access.

Keep the chronology brisk. The durable lesson is sovereignty, not battle detail. Native confederacy forces won major victories before Wayne's campaign and the Treaty of Greenville imposed extensive land cessions. The treaty created an uneasy peace; it did not end Native sovereignty or resistance.

Sources: National Park Service, “The Treaty of Greenville Creates an Uneasy Peace.” Live source U.S. Army Center of Military History, “Indian Wars.” Live source

Jefferson Believed Land Could Preserve Republican Independence

Land

Independent farmers needed access to land.

Virtue

Property could protect citizens from dependence.

Scale

A federal republic might spread across a continent.

The “empire of liberty” was a theory of republican survival—and a settler vision of the continent.

Make Jefferson's logic intelligible before exposing its costs. From within settler republicanism, abundant land promised independence from wage dependence, crowding, and European hierarchy. The contradiction is that this “available” land was already inhabited and governed.

Source: Jefferson to Indiana Territory General Assembly, 28 December 1805, Founders Online. Live source

New Orleans Tied Western Settlement to the Union

“Port of New-Orleans Shut”
Western farms→Mississippi River→New Orleans→World markets
When Spanish officials suspended the American right of deposit in 1802, an economic outlet became a national crisis.

The right of deposit was practical, not abstract. Western produce moved downriver. If a foreign empire controlled the outlet, western prosperity—and perhaps western loyalty to the Union—looked vulnerable.

Source: Library of Congress, Louisiana Purchase legislative timeline and “Port of New-Orleans shut” broadside. Live source

France at New Orleans Threatened to Push the United States Toward Britain

“The day that France takes possession of New Orleans...we must marry ourselves to the British fleet and nation.”
Thomas Jefferson to Robert Livingston, 1802
The port crisis turned western expansion into a problem of national security and diplomatic independence.

Jefferson hoped to avoid dependence on either European power. A strong France at New Orleans threatened that freedom of action and might force the United States toward Britain.

Source: Thomas Jefferson to Robert R. Livingston, 18 April 1802, Founders Online. Live source

Haiti Helped Destroy Napoleon's North American Strategy

Saint-Domingue

France tried to rebuild a Caribbean slave empire.

Resistance and disease

Black revolutionary resistance and epidemic disease devastated the French project.

Napoleon's calculation

Renewed European war made Louisiana less useful and harder to defend.

A slaveholding republic expanded as the Atlantic world's first independent Black republic emerged.

Avoid monocausality. The Haitian Revolution alone did not cause the Purchase. Revolutionary resistance, disease, renewed European war, and changing French strategy worked together to make Louisiana expendable.

Source: U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, “Louisiana Purchase, 1803.” Live source

France Offered Jefferson More Territory Than He Had Asked to Buy

American goal

Secure New Orleans and navigation, with authority to spend up to $10 million.

French offer

All of Louisiana for approximately $15 million.

Livingston and Monroe exceeded expectations because the opportunity was unexpected and might disappear.

Emphasize contingency. This was not the inevitable culmination of a settled continental plan. The envoys sought New Orleans and West Florida; Napoleon offered the larger territory.

Source: U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, “Louisiana Purchase, 1803.” Live source

Would You Take the Deal?

You decide • Constitutional dilemma
You are Jefferson. France offers all of Louisiana, but the Constitution does not clearly say you can buy foreign territory.
A. Take the deal now.
B. Wait for a constitutional amendment.
C. Buy only New Orleans.
D. Reject the offer.
Write one sentence: Which choice best protects the republic, and what risk does it create?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 1 of 2.

Timing: 3-4 minutes. Ask students to name both benefit and risk. The activity frames the constitutional dilemma as a governing problem rather than a simple hypocrisy charge.

Historical-thinking moves: Cause, consequence, and contingency.

Jefferson Chose Practical National Power Over His Strictest Constitutional Principle

Strict principle

Federal power should remain within clearly delegated authority.

Presidential responsibility

The opportunity could secure the republic's future and might disappear.

Jefferson drafted amendment language, then accepted a broader practical reading of treaty power.

Do not reduce this to hypocrisy. Jefferson believed the Purchase was essential but also thought the Constitution did not provide a neat instruction manual. The deeper issue is what happens when political principle collides with a rare strategic opportunity.

Source: Jefferson, Draft Amendment on Louisiana, Founders Online. Live source

The Louisiana Purchase Enlarged American Claims Faster Than American Sovereignty

In Paris

A treaty transferred France's imperial claim to the United States.

On the ground

Native nations still governed homelands, trade routes, villages, and diplomatic relationships.

Treaty claim≠Control≠Consent
Map unavailable: Louisiana Purchase claim within North America
A useful map—if its colors are read as claims rather than Native consent.

This deliberately echoes the Old Northwest opening. France could transfer its imperial claim, but it could not erase Native sovereignty. Use the map as evidence: the color shows a claim, not immediate possession of every acre.

Lewis and Clark Turned Geographic Knowledge Into State Power

Map

Rivers, mountains, routes.

Record

Plants, animals, climate, soil.

Report

Native nations, population, alliances, trade.

Announce

A new American diplomatic claim.

The expedition converted American ignorance into American state knowledge.

This is not an adventure narrative. The expedition's purposes were scientific, commercial, diplomatic, and imperial. Knowledge helped the government imagine routes, relationships, resources, and future control.

Source: Jefferson, Instructions for Meriwether Lewis, 20 June 1803, Founders Online. Live source

If the Map Shows U.S. Territory, Why Is American Sovereignty Negotiable?

Read the map against the encounter
In 1804, the expedition nearly came to violence with Teton Lakota leaders on the Missouri River.
The map says: the territory belongs to the United States.
The encounter says: passage and authority still depend on power, diplomacy, and Native decisions.
Write one or two sentences: What does the confrontation reveal about the difference between a territorial claim and sovereignty on the ground?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 2 of 2.

Timing: 4 minutes. Students should connect map evidence to encounter evidence. The map is not useless; it represents a legal and diplomatic claim whose enforcement remained incomplete.

Historical-thinking moves: Evidence, comparison, and context.

Source: Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, 25 September 1804, University of Nebraska. Live source

American Expansion Depended on Indigenous Knowledge Even as It Threatened Indigenous Sovereignty

Mandan and Hidatsa villages

Agricultural and commercial centers supplied knowledge, trade, and winter survival.

Sacagawea and Shoshone kinship

Language, relationships, and access to horses made the route possible.

The journey later symbolized American mastery, but its success repeatedly depended on Indigenous diplomacy, kinship, and permission.

Do not overteach expedition stops. The point is Native agency and American dependence. Mandan and Hidatsa villages were established centers, not isolated camps; Sacagawea's relationships mattered within a larger network of Indigenous decisions.

Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa Rebuilt Resistance Against Piecemeal Land Cessions

The challenge

Settlement and repeated treaties kept converting Native homelands into American land claims.

The response

The Shawnee brothers sought broader intertribal resistance to additional cessions and U.S. expansion.

Fallen Timbers and Greenville did not end Native resistance. Expansion generated new coalitions—and renewed connections to Britain.

Use this only as continuity. Do not teach Tippecanoe or the War of 1812 here. Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa show that earlier defeat did not settle sovereignty. Their movement becomes part of Lecture 4 when Native resistance, British influence, and U.S. war aims converge.

Source: National Park Service, “The Battle of Tippecanoe.” Live source

Expansion Promised Liberty to Settlers While Expanding Dispossession and Slavery

White settlers

Land, markets, security, and republican independence.

Native nations

Pressure on sovereignty, homelands, diplomacy, and survival.

Enslaved people

Louisiana was a slave society, and new territory widened slavery's future.

Territorial residents

Incorporation was promised while appointed government delayed full self-rule.

This is the moral and interpretive hinge. Do not flatten the story into triumph or condemnation. The same expansion could materially support white settler independence while dispossessing Native nations, incorporating people unequally, and enlarging the geographic stakes of slavery.

Republican Liberty and Imperial Power Coexisted in Continental Expansion

The United States made treaties, claimed territory, sent armies and expeditions, governed residents, and tried to reshape a continent.

Settlers could understand access to land as liberty while others experienced the same project as imperial power.

Answer the title question with tension, not a slogan. The republic did not cease to be a republic. It exercised imperial power because land was imagined as the material foundation of settler liberty. Native nations could see the removal of distant European claims as the arrival of a stronger and closer claimant.

Expansion Joined Opportunity, National Power, and Dispossession

Opportunity

Expansion promised land, markets, and independence to white settlers.

Power

Treaties, diplomacy, force, exploration, and administration turned claims into control.

Dispossession

Expansion threatened Native sovereignty and widened the territorial future of slavery.

What happens when Native resistance becomes entangled with Britain's continuing presence in North America?

Final formal lecture slide. Stop here unless intentionally entering optional enrichment. The question bridges to Lecture 4 without teaching the War of 1812: continental expansion becomes a foreign-policy crisis when Native resistance and British influence intersect.

Unclear Boundaries Turned the Purchase Into Future Disputes

Optional enrichment
No one possessed a neat modern map in 1803.
  • France and the United States did not agree on every boundary.
  • Spain rejected some U.S. interpretations.
  • Ambiguity became part of later diplomacy and conflict.
Big idea: a treaty can create new disputes as well as settle old ones.

Optional only. Useful if students ask whether the Louisiana boundaries were known or fixed at purchase.

Republican Diplomacy Reused Imperial “Great Father” Language

Optional enrichment
The anti-monarchical republic used paternal imperial language.
  • U.S. officials presented the president as a “Great Father” to Native nations.
  • The phrase borrowed from imperial diplomatic habits.
  • Native leaders could accept medals and gifts without accepting U.S. subordination claims.
Big idea: republican language at home could become imperial language in Native diplomacy.

Optional enrichment from Zachary Conn's 2026 work. Excellent nuance but not required for formal lecture pacing.

Jefferson's Native Policy Joined Trade, Debt, and Land Cessions

Optional enrichment
Trade, debt, “civilization,” and land cessions fit together.
  • Jefferson hoped trade would draw Native communities toward U.S. dependence.
  • Debt could pressure land cessions.
  • Assimilation and dispossession could operate in the same policy world.
Big idea: peaceful language did not remove coercive consequences.

Optional only. Do not assess unless later incorporated formally.

Source: Jefferson to William Henry Harrison, 27 February 1803, Founders Online. Live source

New Orleans Entered the Republic Through an Unequal Racial Order

Optional enrichment
Incorporation did not affect everyone the same way.
  • White residents negotiated law, language, religion, and political rights.
  • Free people of color faced a changing racial order.
  • Enslaved people were incorporated as property, not as citizens.
Big idea: adding territory also meant adding people into unequal legal systems.

Optional enrichment only. The formal lecture flags slavery and incorporation; this adds additional nuance for interested students.

Territorial Government Promised Incorporation While Delaying Self-Rule

Optional enrichment
Territorial government created a republican contradiction.
  • The treaty promised eventual incorporation.
  • Congress initially governed territories through appointed officials.
  • Residents could be told they were destined for self-government while not yet controlling their government.
Big idea: expansion turned republican self-government into a staged and unequal process.

Optional enrichment only. The formal “Liberty for Whom?” slide establishes unequal incorporation; this slide adds the territorial-government mechanics.

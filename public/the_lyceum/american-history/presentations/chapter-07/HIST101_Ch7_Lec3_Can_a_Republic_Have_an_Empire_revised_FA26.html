HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 3

Can a Republic Have an Empire?

Jefferson, Louisiana, and land that already belonged to someone · 1801–1806

AI-generated interpretation · not a historical image

Picture a long wooden keelboat on the Missouri River in the late summer of 1804. Men in linen shirts lean on poles and pull on a towrope along the muddy bank. Up on the bluff, people who have lived on this river for generations watch them come. The men on the boat carry papers that say this river now belongs to the United States. The people on the bluff have never heard of those papers, and they have their own ideas about who decides what happens here.

Today's story runs from 1801 to 1806. It starts with a president who walked to his own inauguration and promised a smaller, plainer government. It ends with that same president owning, on paper, a territory that doubled the size of the country. In between are a slave revolution in the Caribbean, a bargain in Paris, and a two-year journey up the Missouri.

Keep one question in your head the whole time: can a republic, a government of free citizens, also rule an empire?

[Key: Jefferson bought a claim on paper; on the Missouri, Native nations still decided who passed.]

4 March 1801 · Washington City

Jefferson Walked to His Inauguration and Called It a Revolution

The morning: Jefferson walked from his boardinghouse to a half-built Capitol.
The loser: John Adams had left town before dawn. But he left.
The promise: “We are all republicans, we are all federalists.”
The meaning: Jefferson later called his win “the revolution of 1800.”
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide02_Capitol_1800_Birch_revised_FA26.jpg
William Russell Birch, A View of the Capitol of Washington Before It Was Burnt Down by the British, c. 1800. Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

It is March 4, 1801, in Washington City, and the capital is barely a town. There are muddy lanes, tree stumps, and a few thousand residents. Only one wing of the Capitol is finished. Around noon, Thomas Jefferson, fifty-seven years old, tall and plainly dressed, steps out of Conrad and McMunn's boardinghouse and walks up the hill with a small group of friends and militia. No coach. No crown-like ceremony.

The man he defeated is gone. John Adams left the city at four o'clock that morning, bitter and unwilling to watch. But notice what Adams did not do: he did not call out troops or refuse to leave. Power changed hands between enemies without a shot fired.

Inside the Senate chamber, Jefferson spoke so softly that many people could not hear him. His most famous line was a peace offering: “We are all republicans, we are all federalists.” Years later, he called his election “the revolution of 1800,” as real a change in principle as 1776 had been in form.

[Key: a peaceful transfer of power, and a promise of a plainer republic.]

1801–1803 · The President's House

Jefferson Set Out to Shrink the Government He Had Feared

Manners: Handshakes instead of bows. Guests seated “pell-mell,” not by rank.
Money: Congress repealed the whiskey tax; Treasury Secretary Albert Gallatin paid down debt.
Force: The army shrank. The small navy still sailed to fight Tripoli.
The irony: The president of small government was about to double the country.

Pierian Spring: the war with Tripoli →

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide03_Jefferson_Peale_1805_revised_FA26.jpg
Rembrandt Peale, Thomas Jefferson, 1805 (detail). New-York Historical Society. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Once he moved into the President's House, Jefferson tried to make the government look like a republic, not a royal court. Washington and Adams had held formal receptions where guests bowed. Jefferson shook hands. At dinner he seated guests “pell-mell,” meaning wherever they landed, so nobody could claim the seat of honor. In 1803 he greeted the new British minister, Anthony Merry, in an old coat and slippers worn down at the heel. Merry was insulted. Jefferson was making a point.

The point went beyond manners. Jefferson and his Treasury secretary, Albert Gallatin, got Congress to repeal the whiskey tax that had sparked a rebellion in 1794. They cut the army and paid down the national debt. Jefferson did send the small navy to the Mediterranean to fight the pasha of Tripoli, a story you can follow in the Pierian Spring.

Look at Rembrandt Peale's 1805 portrait. Jefferson is in his early sixties, graying, plain. Here is the irony to hold onto: the man who wanted a small, modest government was about to make the largest land deal in American history.

[Barbary War detail: Pierian Spring.]

October 1802 · The Mississippi River

Western Farmers Lived or Died by the Port of New Orleans

The road: Kentucky and Ohio farmers floated grain, pork, and whiskey downriver on flatboats.
The door: Spain let Americans store goods at New Orleans for ocean ships: the “right of deposit.”
The shock: In October 1802, a Spanish official shut that door.
The rumor: Spain had secretly handed Louisiana back to Napoleon's France.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide04_New_Orleans_1803_Woiseri_revised_FA26.jpg
J. L. Boqueta de Woiseri, A View of New Orleans Taken from the Plantation of Marigny, 1803 (detail). Made to celebrate the American purchase. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

To understand why anyone cared about Louisiana, picture the West in 1802. Hundreds of thousands of Americans now lived in Kentucky, Tennessee, and the Ohio country. There were no railroads, and dragging a wagon of corn over the Appalachian Mountains cost more than the corn was worth. So farmers built flatboats, big wooden boxes on the water, piled them with flour, salted pork, and barrels of whiskey, and floated them down the Ohio and Mississippi. At the end sat New Orleans.

New Orleans belonged to Spain. A 1795 treaty let Americans unload their goods there and store them until ocean ships carried them away. That privilege was called the right of deposit. In October 1802, the Spanish official in charge, Juan Ventura Morales, suddenly cancelled it. Crops rotted on the docks. Western newspapers talked about marching on the city. Look at this 1803 view: ships crowd the river, which is exactly what westerners feared losing. The artist added the American eagle to celebrate the purchase that came next.

Worse, word was spreading that Spain had secretly given Louisiana back to France in 1800. Napoleon Bonaparte, the most powerful soldier in Europe, might soon control the mouth of America's main river.

[Key: for westerners, New Orleans was not distant geography. It was their paycheck.]

18 April 1802 · Jefferson to Robert Livingston, U.S. minister in Paris

Jefferson Feared France at New Orleans More Than Britain at Sea

“There is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural and habitual enemy. It is New Orleans… From that moment we must marry ourselves to the British fleet and nation.”
Thomas Jefferson, private letter, 18 April 1802 · Founders Online
Translation: If France takes New Orleans, the United States would have to ally with Britain, the country it had just fought.
The plan: In 1803 Jefferson sent James Monroe to Paris to buy New Orleans, with authority to spend up to about $10 million.

Sources: Jefferson to Livingston, 18 April 1802, Founders Online; Office of the Historian, “Louisiana Purchase, 1803.”

This is Jefferson writing privately to Robert Livingston, the American minister in Paris, in April 1802. Remember who Jefferson is. He loved France. He had lived in Paris and cheered its Revolution. He spent the 1790s accusing Federalists of being too friendly with Britain. Now listen to him: the one spot on earth whose owner must be America's enemy is New Orleans, because, as he wrote in the same letter, the produce of three-eighths of American territory had to pass through it.

Then comes the shocking line. If France takes New Orleans, “we must marry ourselves to the British fleet and nation.” The great friend of France was threatening an alliance with Britain. Jefferson expected the letter to be read by French officials, so this was partly a warning meant for Napoleon's ears.

In January 1803, after the port closed, Jefferson sent James Monroe to join Livingston. Their goal was modest: buy New Orleans and, if possible, West Florida. Congress approved two million dollars, and the envoys were authorized to offer up to about ten million. Nobody was asking for half a continent.

[Key: the original goal was one city and one river, not an empire.]

1802–1803 · Saint-Domingue (today's Haiti)

In Saint-Domingue, Formerly Enslaved Soldiers Destroyed Napoleon's Army

1791: Enslaved people rose up in France's richest sugar colony and won their freedom.
1802: Napoleon sent about 20,000 troops to retake the island. Toussaint Louverture was seized and shipped to a French prison.
1802–1803: Black armies and yellow fever destroyed the expedition.
1 January 1804: Haiti declared independence, the first Black republic.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide06_Toussaint_Louverture_revised_FA26.jpg
Toussaint Louverture, engraving, c. 1802–1810, artist unknown. Rijksmuseum, Amsterdam (RP-P-1878-A-2156). Public domain (CC0), via Wikimedia Commons. Made in Europe, likely by an artist who never saw him.

The reason Napoleon would give up Louisiana was decided far to the south, on a Caribbean island. Saint-Domingue, today's Haiti, was the richest colony in the world, a place of sugar and coffee plantations worked by nearly half a million enslaved Africans. In August 1791 they rose up. After years of brutal war, they ended slavery there, and a formerly enslaved man named Toussaint Louverture became the colony's leader.

Napoleon wanted the colony and its profits back. In early 1802 he sent his brother-in-law, General Charles Leclerc, with about twenty thousand soldiers. In June 1802, French officers lured Toussaint to a meeting, arrested him, and shipped him to a cold prison in the French mountains, where he died in April 1803. As he was taken, Toussaint is reported to have warned that the tree of liberty would spring up again from its roots.

It did. When word came that France had restored slavery on nearby Guadeloupe, Black soldiers under Jean-Jacques Dessalines fought on. Yellow fever killed thousands of French troops, Leclerc among them. Without Saint-Domingue, Louisiana was useless to Napoleon. It was supposed to feed the sugar islands.

[Key: Haitian revolutionaries, not American diplomats, made the deal possible.]

11 April – 2 May 1803 · Paris

Napoleon Sold What He Could No Longer Hold

Asked to sell one city, France offered all of Louisiana: about 828,000 square miles.

Price: $15 million, roughly three cents an acre.

“The noblest work of our whole lives.” — Robert Livingston

AI-generated interpretation · not a historical image

Paris, April 1803. Napoleon has lost an army in Saint-Domingue, and a new war with Britain is coming. Britain rules the seas. If war comes, the British navy could simply sail up the Mississippi and take Louisiana. Napoleon decides to sell before he loses it for nothing, and to put the money toward the war.

On April 11, the French foreign minister, Talleyrand, startled Robert Livingston with a question: what would the United States pay for all of Louisiana? Livingston had come to buy one city. The next day James Monroe arrived from America. For two weeks the Americans bargained with the French finance minister, François Barbé-Marbois. The treaty they signed, dated April 30, 1803, set the price at fifteen million dollars. That is about three cents an acre, for roughly 828,000 square miles stretching from the Mississippi to the Rocky Mountains.

Livingston, shaking hands after the signing, reportedly said, “We have lived long, but this is the noblest work of our whole lives.” There was just one problem, and it was back home in Washington.

[Key: contingency. Nobody planned this; a revolution and a war made it possible.]

Summer 1803 · Monticello and Washington

Jefferson Believed the Constitution Did Not Allow the Deal

His rule: The federal government has only the powers the Constitution lists.
The problem: Nothing in it says the nation may buy foreign land and add its people.
His fix: In July 1803 he drafted a constitutional amendment.
The clock: Word came that Napoleon might change his mind if America delayed.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide08_Jefferson_Drafts_Amendment_revised_FA26.png
AI-generated historical interpretation in a hand-colored copperplate engraving style. Not a historical image or evidence.

When the news reached Washington in July 1803, most Americans cheered. Jefferson was pleased, but troubled. Remember the bank fight in our last lecture. Jefferson had argued that the federal government had only the powers the Constitution clearly lists, and he had called a broad reading of the Constitution dangerous. Now he looked for a power to buy foreign territory, and to make its people citizens, and he could not find one.

So he did what his principles told him to do. He sat down and drafted a constitutional amendment that would make Louisiana part of the United States. In August he wrote to Senator John Breckinridge that by seizing this chance, the government had “done an act beyond the Constitution.”

But an amendment takes months, maybe years. Then a warning arrived from Livingston in Paris: Napoleon was having second thoughts, and any delay might give him an excuse to cancel. Jefferson told his allies that the less said about constitutional difficulties, the better.

So: you are Jefferson. What do you do? That is our first activity.

[Key: principle collided with opportunity.]

Would You Take the Deal?

Activity 1 • Consequence • 4 minutes
August 1803. You are Jefferson. France's offer may vanish. You believe the Constitution does not allow it.
A. Send the treaty to the Senate now; worry about the Constitution later.
B. Wait for a constitutional amendment first.
C. Buy only New Orleans, the part you asked for.
D. Reject the offer.
Write one sentence: Pick one choice and name one consequence it could have for the republic ten years later.

Give students one minute to choose silently, two minutes to write, and one minute to hear two or three answers. Push them to name a consequence, not just a preference.

Here is what each choice risks. Choice A gets the land but sets a precedent: if a president can stretch the Constitution when the prize is big enough, what stops the next one? Choice B keeps faith with the Constitution, but Napoleon might walk away, and France or Britain could end up controlling the Mississippi. Choice C sounds safe, but France had offered the whole package, not a piece. Choice D keeps the republic small and the Constitution clean, and leaves western farmers at a foreign power's mercy.

When someone picks A, ask: who else lived in that territory, and did anyone ask them? Keep that question alive; we will answer it on the Missouri.

Then reveal: Jefferson chose A.

October – December 1803 · New Orleans

Jefferson Took the Deal, and the Flag Went Up at New Orleans

October 1803: Jefferson dropped his amendment. The Senate approved the treaty, 24 to 7.
20 December 1803: The French flag came down in New Orleans' main square; the American flag went up.
The crowd: Many residents spoke French or Spanish. Thousands were enslaved.
The result: Practical power had beaten strict principle.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide10_Flag_Raising_New_Orleans_Thulstrup_revised_FA26.jpg
Thure de Thulstrup, Hoisting of the American Colors over Louisiana, 1902. Painted a century later, so it is an imagined scene. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Jefferson chose to take the deal. He quietly set his amendment aside and sent the treaty to the Senate. Federalists, mostly from New England, protested that he was buying a wilderness and that new western states would drown out their region's voice. They lost. On October 20, 1803, the Senate approved the treaty, twenty-four votes to seven.

On December 20, 1803, in the Place d'Armes, the main square of New Orleans, today's Jackson Square, the French flag came down and the American flag went up. William C. C. Claiborne, the new American governor, stood beside General James Wilkinson. The crowd watching did not look much like the Americans who had bought them. Many spoke French or Spanish. Many were free people of color. Thousands of others in the city and on nearby sugar plantations were enslaved, and they became the property of American citizens overnight.

This painting by Thure de Thulstrup is from 1902, a century later. It is an imagined scene, not a witness's sketch.

[Key: Jefferson chose national power over his own strict reading of the Constitution.]

1803 · West of the Mississippi

The Purchase Bought France's Claim, Not the Land Itself

On paper: France sold its right to rule Louisiana, as against other European empires.
On the ground: Osage, Pawnee, Lakota, Mandan, Hidatsa, Crow, Shoshone, and dozens more nations lived there.
The fine print: Each nation's land still had to be bought by treaty or taken by force.
The paradox: A republic that had rejected a king now claimed rule over nations that never chose it.

Pierian Spring: who joined the republic, and on what terms →

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide11_Louisiana_1804_Map_revised_FA26.jpg
Samuel Lewis, Louisiana, from Arrowsmith and Lewis, A New and Elegant General Atlas, 1804. Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Now look closely at what the United States actually bought. France did not own the Great Plains in any everyday sense. Very few French people lived west of the Mississippi outside a few river towns like St. Louis. What France sold was a claim: the right, recognized among European empires, to be the only outsider who could govern, trade, and make treaties there.

The people who actually lived on the land were never part of the deal. The Osage dominated trade on the lower Missouri. The Pawnee farmed and hunted on the Platte. The Lakota were the rising power of the northern plains. Mandan and Hidatsa towns on the upper Missouri were trading capitals. Further west were the Crow, the Shoshone, the Blackfeet, and many more.

Historian Robert Lee has shown that over the following century the United States spent far more than fifteen million dollars buying this same land again, nation by nation, through treaties often signed under pressure, and took much of the rest by war.

This 1804 map shows how little Americans knew. That is about to change.

[Key: a claim is not control, and control is not consent.]

14 May 1804 · Camp Dubois, near St. Louis

Jefferson Sent the Corps of Discovery to Turn a Claim Into Knowledge

The leaders: Meriwether Lewis, Jefferson's secretary, and William Clark, an army veteran.
The crew: About 45 men in a 55-foot keelboat and two smaller boats.
York: Clark brought an enslaved man, York, who did every task the soldiers did.
The mission: Map the rivers. Record plants, animals, and nations. Tell each nation it had a new “great father.”
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide12_Corps_Leaves_Camp_Dubois_revised_FA26.png
AI-generated historical interpretation in a hand-colored copperplate engraving style. Not a historical image or evidence.

Jefferson had been planning an expedition west even before the purchase. In January 1803, when Louisiana still belonged to France, he secretly asked Congress for $2,500 to send soldiers up the Missouri. He chose Meriwether Lewis, his young private secretary and a former army officer, to lead it. Lewis picked an old army friend, William Clark, as his co-captain.

On May 14, 1804, about forty-five men pushed off from Camp Dubois, across the Mississippi from St. Louis. They traveled in a fifty-five-foot keelboat and two open boats called pirogues, poling, rowing, and dragging them against the Missouri's current. Among them was York, a man Clark had enslaved since boyhood. York hunted, paddled, and hauled like everyone else, and he never received a soldier's pay.

Jefferson's instructions read like a science assignment and a diplomatic mission at once: map the rivers, record soils, plants, and animals, and learn each nation's name, numbers, and trade. Along the way, the captains told every people they met that they now had a new “great father” in Washington.

[Key: knowledge was the first step toward control.]

25 September 1804 · Mouth of the Bad River, today's Pierre, South Dakota

On the Missouri, Teton Lakota Leaders Decided Who Could Pass

The council: Lewis gave a speech, medals, and gifts. The interpreter barely spoke Lakota.
The standoff: Young men seized the boat's rope. Clark drew his sword; bows were strung, a swivel gun loaded.
The decision: Chief Black Buffalo took hold of the rope, then let it go.
The lesson: The Lakota controlled trade on this river and expected payment to pass.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide13_Teton_Lakota_Council_revised_FA26.png
AI-generated historical interpretation in a hand-colored copperplate engraving style. Not a historical image or evidence.

Four months up the river, the Corps reached the mouth of the Bad River, near today's Pierre, South Dakota. This was Teton Lakota country. Jefferson had written to Lewis that the Sioux were the nation he most wanted to impress, “because of their immense power.” The Lakota controlled who traded on this stretch of the Missouri, and traders who passed were expected to pay.

On September 25, 1804, the captains set up a flag and an awning on a sandbar and held a council. Lewis read a speech, but their interpreter, the boatman Pierre Cruzatte, spoke almost no Lakota. They handed out medals, a red coat, a cocked hat. The chiefs wanted more. When Clark took them ashore, three young men grabbed the pirogue's rope. A chief called the Partisan staggered against Clark and told him he could not go on. Clark drew his sword. On the keelboat, Lewis had the swivel gun loaded with sixteen musket balls. On shore, warriors strung their bows.

Then the senior chief, Black Buffalo, took the rope himself, and after hard words, let it go. Nobody died. But the Lakota, not the treaty, had decided.

[Key: sovereignty on the ground.]

The Map Said One Thing. The River Said Another.

Activity 2 • Evidence • 4 minutes
Evidence A · a map of the 1803 Purchase: Everything from the Mississippi to the Rockies is colored as U.S. territory.
Evidence B · Sgt. John Ordway's journal, 25 Sept. 1804: The chief “Seized hold of the cable”… “Sayed he had warriers too and if we were to go on they would follow us.”
Write one or two sentences: Who actually controlled this stretch of the Missouri in 1804? Use one detail from Evidence B, and explain why the map is still useful evidence of something.

Source: Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, 25 September 1804 (Clark, Ordway, Gass), University of Nebraska–Lincoln.

Give students a minute to read both pieces, two minutes to write, and a minute to share. The move here is evidence: what can each source prove, and what can it not?

Strong answers will say the Lakota controlled the river that day. Their leaders decided whether the boats could leave, they demanded goods as the price of passage, and the chief openly said his warriors could follow the Americans and stop them. The Americans got through because Black Buffalo chose to let go of the rope, not because of a treaty signed in Paris.

But do not let students throw the map away. A map is evidence of what a government claims and intends. A map of the Purchase tells us what Washington believed it now had the right to do. The journal tells us what it could actually do in 1804. Both are true at once, and the gap between them is the story of the next hundred years in the West.

One more source caution: these journals were written by the Americans. Clark called the Partisan's behavior insolent. A Lakota account of the same day would describe strangers who came into their country, refused the customary payment, and aimed a loaded swivel gun at their people.

Winter 1804–1805 · Mandan and Hidatsa villages, today's North Dakota

Mandan and Hidatsa Towns Fed the Expedition Through the Winter

The towns: Earth-lodge villages of several thousand people: bigger than St. Louis.
The market: Corn, beans, and squash traded to plains nations, Canadian fur traders, and now Americans.
The hire: Toussaint Charbonneau and his young Shoshone wife, Sacagawea, joined as interpreters.
The cold: At 40 below zero, Mandan corn kept the Corps alive.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide15_Bodmer_Mandan_Village_revised_FA26.jpg
Karl Bodmer, Mih-Tutta-Hangkusch, Mandan Village, aquatint from Maximilian of Wied, Travels in the Interior of North America (1839–43), drawn in 1833–34, about 30 years after Lewis and Clark. Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

By late October 1804, the river was freezing, and the Corps stopped near the villages of the Mandan and Hidatsa, where the Knife River meets the Missouri. These were not camps in the wilderness. They were towns of dome-shaped earth lodges, home to several thousand people, more than lived in St. Louis or Washington City. The women farmed corn, beans, and squash along the river bottoms, and for centuries plains nations and, later, British fur traders from Canada had come here to trade.

The Americans built a log post they called Fort Mandan and settled in. That winter the temperature dropped to forty degrees below zero. The Corps survived by trading with their neighbors: the expedition's blacksmith made metal tools and axes, and the Mandan paid in corn. The Mandan leader Sheheke, called Big White, became a friend.

Here the captains hired a French-Canadian trader, Toussaint Charbonneau, and his teenage wife, Sacagawea, a Shoshone woman who had been captured by Hidatsa raiders years before. In February 1805, she gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste. In April, with the baby on her back, she headed west with the Corps.

[Key: the expedition survived on Native food, Native knowledge, and Native goodwill.]

August – November 1805 · Lemhi Shoshone country to the Pacific

A Shoshone Reunion Bought the Horses to Cross the Mountains

17 August 1805: Sacagawea recognized the Shoshone chief, Cameahwait, as her brother.
The trade: Shoshone horses and a guide carried the Corps over the Rocky Mountains.
The Bitterroots: Starving, the men stumbled down to Nez Perce villages, who fed them.
November 1805: At the Pacific, the Corps voted on where to winter. York and Sacagawea voted too.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide16_Sacagawea_Meets_Cameahwait_revised_FA26.png
AI-generated historical interpretation in a hand-colored copperplate engraving style. Not a historical image or evidence.

By the summer of 1805, the Missouri had shrunk to a creek, and the Rocky Mountains stood in front of the Corps. They could not carry their supplies over on foot. They needed horses, and the people with horses were the Shoshone, Sacagawea's people. Movies often call Sacagawea the expedition's guide. She did recognize some landmarks, but her real importance was as an interpreter, and, as one captain noted, as a sign of peace: a war party did not travel with a woman and a baby.

On August 17, 1805, as the captains met the Shoshone chief Cameahwait, Sacagawea suddenly began to cry and embrace him. He was her brother, whom she had not seen since she was taken as a girl. The Shoshone sold the Corps horses and lent them a guide.

Even so, the crossing of the Bitterroot Mountains in September nearly killed them. They ate candles and colts. The Nez Perce, or Nimiipuu, fed them and kept their horses while they built canoes. In November 1805 they reached the Pacific. When the captains polled everyone on where to spend the winter, York's choice and Sacagawea's were written down too.

[Key: Native diplomacy and kinship carried the Corps across the continent.]

23 September 1806 · St. Louis

The Corps Came Home With Maps, but Not Everyone Came Home Free

The return: After two years and four months, the Corps paddled into St. Louis. Only one man had died.
The maps: Clark's maps turned the West into something Washington could plan around.
The pay: Charbonneau got $500 and 320 acres. Sacagawea got nothing.
York: York asked for his freedom. Clark refused for about ten more years.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Slide17_Lewis_Clark_Track_1814_revised_FA26.jpg
Samuel Lewis, after William Clark, A Map of Lewis and Clark's Track, Across the Western Portion of North America, published 1814. Library of Congress. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

On September 23, 1806, the Corps of Discovery paddled back into St. Louis. People had assumed they were dead. They had been gone two years and four months and had traveled about eight thousand miles. Only one man had died, Sergeant Charles Floyd, probably of a burst appendix, back in August 1804.

What they brought home was knowledge: journals, plant and animal specimens, and above all Clark's maps. This is the map engraved from Clark's work and published in 1814. Compare it with the 1804 map from a few slides ago. The blank space is filling in with rivers, mountain passes, and the names of nations. For the government, that knowledge was the first tool of control.

Rewards were not shared equally. Congress gave the men double pay and land. Charbonneau received about $500 and 320 acres. Sacagawea received nothing. York asked Clark for his freedom as a reward for his service. Clark refused, complained in letters that York had “such a notion about freedom,” hired him out to a harsh master, and did not free him until roughly ten years later.

[Key: the expedition's success was shared; its rewards were not.]

1801–1806 · Back on the river

Jefferson Called It an “Empire for Liberty”

For white farmers, the land meant independence.

For Native nations, it meant a stronger, closer power on their rivers.

For enslaved people, it meant slavery could move west.

Can a republic have an empire? Liberty for whom, and on whose land?

AI-generated interpretation · not a historical image

Let's go back to that keelboat on the river, and to the man who walked to his inauguration. Jefferson never stopped believing he was protecting liberty. To him, cheap land was what kept a republic free: every white family could own a farm, depend on no landlord or factory boss, and vote as an independent citizen. In 1809, just after he left office, he wrote that America could become an “empire for liberty” such as the world had never seen.

But look at who paid for that liberty. The Lakota, the Osage, the Mandan, and the Shoshone had not sold anything; their land had been claimed over their heads, and Jefferson himself wrote privately in 1803 that trading-house debts could push Native leaders to give up land. Thousands of enslaved people in Louisiana became American property overnight, and the new territory gave slavery room to grow.

So, can a republic have an empire? This one did, and it kept calling itself a republic. Your job is to hold both facts at once. Next time: when that expanding republic and Native resistance collide with Britain, the result is war.

[Key: expansion meant liberty for some and conquest for others. Lecture ends here.]

1801–1805 · Tripoli, North Africa

Jefferson's Small Navy Fought a War Against Tripoli

Pierian Spring · independent study

Why: North African states charged ships “tribute” for safe passage. In 1801 Tripoli's ruler demanded more and declared war on the United States.

The disaster: In October 1803 the frigate Philadelphia ran aground; about 300 Americans were captured.

The raid: In February 1804 Stephen Decatur slipped into the harbor and burned the captured ship.

The end: After a march on Derna, a June 1805 treaty freed the captives for a $60,000 ransom.

Connection: even a president who cut the army and navy used force abroad when he thought commerce and honor were at stake.

This story connects to slide 3. Jefferson cut the military, but he still sent warships to the Mediterranean. For years the United States, like European nations, had paid the North African states of Algiers, Tunis, Tripoli, and Morocco to leave its merchant ships alone. In May 1801, Yusuf Karamanli, the pasha of Tripoli, demanded more money; when it did not come, his soldiers chopped down the flagpole at the American consulate, a declaration of war.

In October 1803 the frigate Philadelphia ran aground chasing a ship off Tripoli, and Captain William Bainbridge and roughly 300 sailors became prisoners. In February 1804, Lieutenant Stephen Decatur sailed a captured ketch into the harbor at night, boarded the Philadelphia, and set it on fire so Tripoli could not use it. In 1805, former consul William Eaton and a handful of U.S. Marines led a mostly mercenary force across the desert to capture the town of Derna. A treaty that June ended the war, though the United States still paid $60,000 to free the prisoners. The Marines' hymn line “to the shores of Tripoli” remembers this war.

24 February 1803 · The Supreme Court, Washington

Marbury v. Madison Gave the Court the Power to Strike Down Laws

Pierian Spring · independent study

The setup: In his last days, Adams appointed Federalist judges and justices of the peace, the “midnight judges.”

The case: Jefferson's secretary of state, James Madison, refused to deliver William Marbury's commission. Marbury sued.

The ruling: Chief Justice John Marshall said Marbury deserved his job, but the law that let him sue in the Supreme Court was unconstitutional.

The result: Jefferson won the case; the Court won the power of judicial review.

Connection: Lecture 2 asked who decides when the government goes too far. In 1803, the Court claimed that job.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec3_Pierian_John_Marshall_Inman_revised_FA26.jpg
Henry Inman, John Marshall, 1832. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

This story happened the same spring the Louisiana deal was being made, and it answers a question from our last lecture: who decides whether a law is constitutional? Before he left office in 1801, John Adams and the outgoing Federalist Congress created new judgeships and filled them with Federalists. One of them, William Marbury, was named a justice of the peace, but his commission had not been delivered when Jefferson took office. Jefferson's secretary of state, James Madison, refused to hand it over.

Marbury sued in the Supreme Court. Chief Justice John Marshall, a Federalist and Jefferson's distant cousin and political enemy, was trapped. If he ordered Madison to deliver the commission, Jefferson would ignore him and the Court would look weak. So on February 24, 1803, Marshall ruled that Marbury had a right to his commission, but that the part of the Judiciary Act of 1789 letting him bring the case directly to the Supreme Court went beyond the Constitution, and was void. “It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” he wrote. Marbury never got his job. The Court got the power of judicial review.

27 February 1803 · Jefferson to William Henry Harrison

Jefferson's Indian Policy Used Trade and Debt to Win Land Cessions

Pierian Spring · independent study

The plan: Government trading houses would sell goods to Native leaders on credit.

His words: When debts grew too large, leaders would become “willing to lop them off by a cession of lands.”

The promise: Native families who became farmers on small plots would “need” less land.

The alternative: Any nation that resisted, Jefferson wrote, could be driven across the Mississippi.

Connection: the closing slide's question, “on whose land?”, had an answer in Jefferson's own plans.

This private letter, written to the governor of Indiana Territory in February 1803, two months before the Louisiana deal, shows how Jefferson planned to get Native land east of the Mississippi. It connects to the closing slide of the main lecture.

Jefferson told William Henry Harrison that the government should open trading houses and be glad to see influential Native leaders run up debts, “because we observe that when these debts get beyond what the individuals can pay, they become willing to lop them off by a cession of lands.” He also promoted “civilization”: if Native men became plow farmers and Native women spun and wove, families would need far less hunting land and could sell the rest. And he added a threat. If any nation took up the hatchet, the United States should seize its whole country and drive its people across the Mississippi.

Jefferson spoke publicly of friendship and called himself a “father.” Privately, he designed a system to transfer land. Louisiana gave him somewhere to send nations that refused, an idea that became the policy of Indian Removal a generation later. Source: Jefferson to Harrison, 27 February 1803, Founders Online.

1803–1811 · Territory of Orleans

Louisiana's People Joined the Republic on Unequal Terms

Pierian Spring · independent study

White residents: The treaty promised citizenship “as soon as possible,” but Congress first sent an appointed governor.

Free people of color: Under Spain they had owned property and served in the militia. American rule narrowed their rights.

Enslaved people: Counted as property. Sugar planting and slave sales grew fast.

January 1811: Hundreds of enslaved people led by Charles Deslondes marched on New Orleans in the German Coast Uprising. Militia and soldiers crushed them.

Connection: adding territory meant adding people, and the republic sorted them by race.

This slide connects to slides 10 and 11. Buying Louisiana meant buying its people too, and they entered the United States in very different ways.

The treaty promised that the inhabitants would become citizens “as soon as possible.” But in 1804 Congress created the Territory of Orleans with a governor, William Claiborne, appointed by the president, and an appointed council. Many French-speaking white residents protested that they had been promised self-government and were being ruled like a colony. They won an elected assembly in 1805, and Louisiana became a state in 1812.

Free people of color, a large community in New Orleans, had served in the Spanish militia and owned property. Under American rule their position grew more restricted. Enslaved people were counted simply as property, and as sugar planting boomed, so did the trade in enslaved people. In January 1811, Charles Deslondes led several hundred enslaved people down the River Road toward New Orleans in the German Coast Uprising, the largest slave revolt in U.S. history. Planter militias and federal troops crushed it, killed dozens, and displayed the heads of executed rebels along the road. The memory of Haiti haunted Louisiana's planters.

1803–1819 · West Florida and Texas

Nobody Knew Exactly Where Louisiana Ended

Pierian Spring · independent study

The treaty: France sold Louisiana “with the same extent” Spain had held it, with no map and no clear borders.

Talleyrand's shrug: When asked about the boundaries, he reportedly told Livingston, “I suppose you will make the most of it.”

The disputes: Americans claimed West Florida and parts of Texas. Spain said no.

The settlement: The Adams-Onís Treaty of 1819 finally drew the line.

Connection: a treaty can create new conflicts as well as settle old ones.

This connects to slide 7. The Louisiana treaty never said exactly what was being sold. It transferred Louisiana “with the same extent that it now has in the hands of Spain,” and nobody had ever surveyed that. When Livingston asked Talleyrand where the borders were, the French minister reportedly answered that he did not know, and added, “You have made a noble bargain for yourselves, and I suppose you will make the most of it.”

The Americans did. Jefferson argued that Louisiana included West Florida, the Gulf Coast strip along today's Mississippi and Alabama, and stretched west toward the Rio Grande. Spain disagreed. In 1810 American settlers in West Florida rebelled against Spain and asked to join the United States, and President Madison claimed the region. The western border stayed in dispute until 1819, when Secretary of State John Quincy Adams and Spanish minister Luis de Onís signed a treaty: Spain gave up Florida, and the United States gave up its claim to Texas, for the time being.

1790–1806 · The Ohio country

Before Louisiana, Native Nations Had Already Fought for the Old Northwest

Pierian Spring · independent study

1791: A confederacy led by Little Turtle and Blue Jacket destroyed General Arthur St. Clair's army, killing over 600 soldiers.

1794–1795: General Anthony Wayne won at Fallen Timbers. The Treaty of Greenville gave up most of today's Ohio.

After 1805: The Shawnee brothers Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa, the Prophet, began building a new alliance against land cessions.

Connection: the claim-versus-control problem on the Missouri had already played out in Ohio, and was about to again. See Lecture 4.

This background connects to slide 11. The gap between a map claim and real control was not new in 1803. The 1783 peace treaty with Britain had drawn the Ohio country, the Old Northwest, inside American borders, but the Shawnee, Miami, Delaware, and other nations living there had signed nothing.

When the United States sent armies to force the issue, they lost. In 1790 a confederacy of nations defeated General Josiah Harmar. In November 1791, warriors led by the Miami war leader Little Turtle and the Shawnee leader Blue Jacket destroyed General Arthur St. Clair's army on the Wabash River, killing more than six hundred soldiers, one of the worst defeats in U.S. Army history. Only in 1794, at Fallen Timbers near today's Toledo, did General Anthony Wayne win, and the 1795 Treaty of Greenville forced the nations to give up most of present-day Ohio.

Resistance did not end. Around 1805, a Shawnee prophet named Tenskwatawa and his brother Tecumseh began calling Native peoples to reject American ways and refuse further land sales. Their movement will matter a great deal in Lecture 4.

30 April 1789

Washington Took Office Before the Presidency Had a Routine

Washington described the presidency as a burden of “weighty and untried cares.”
George Washington, First Inaugural Address

The country had a Constitution. It did not yet know how to operate one.

This is the narrative hook. Washington was famous, but the office was new. There was no previous president, no cabinet routine, no settled executive etiquette, and no clear model for how the president should consult Congress.

Source: George Washington, “First Inaugural Address: Final Version, 30 April 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

The Constitution Was Written Before Its Government Had Routines

The Constitution created a framework; the First Congress and the Washington administration had to make it usable.

How do you turn a written Constitution into a government people recognize, obey, and regard as legitimate?

Precedent+Capacity+Legitimacy

Use this as the Calder/uncoverage reset. We are not covering every action of the Washington administration. We are following one problem through selected moments: how precedent, capacity, and legitimacy made the written Constitution operational.

The Constitution Created Offices; Congress Still Had to Build the Machinery

The text

Created offices, powers, and procedures in outline.

The work

Legislators and officials had to create departments, courts, revenue routines, and precedents.

Text→Choice→Precedent→Expectation

The Constitution was not a complete administrative code. Constitutional meaning became practical through legislation, repeated choices, and routines people learned to recognize.

The New Presidency Needed Dignity Without Looking Monarchical

“His Highness the President of the United States of America and Protector of the Rights of the Same”
Senate title proposal, 1789
The House preferred something simpler: President of the United States.

A title communicated what kind of executive the republic was creating. Too little dignity could make the office weak; too much ceremony could make it look monarchical. The Senate proposal was rejected, and the simpler title prevailed.

Source: James Madison, “Title for the President, 11 May 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

Washington's Awkward Senate Visit Helped Create a Treaty Routine

What the Constitution said

Treaties require Senate advice and consent.

What happened

Washington visited the Senate in person. The senators wanted documents, debate, and delay.

Washington left irritated and never again sought treaty advice in person.

The text says “advice,” so Washington tries to get advice. The encounter is awkward enough to help establish a later convention: the president negotiates first and sends the completed treaty to the Senate for consent.

Source context: U.S. Senate history of treaty practice. Live source

Congress Built Departments; Washington Invented the Cabinet

What the Constitution said

It mentioned “executive Departments” and let the president demand written opinions. It never created a cabinet.

What Congress built in 1789

Statutes created State, War, and Treasury. The Judiciary Act added an Attorney General.

Whom Washington appointed

Jefferson (State), Hamilton (Treasury), Knox (War), and Randolph (Attorney General).

How the cabinet emerged

Written opinions came first. Collective meetings grew later as governing got harder.

The cabinet began as Washington’s working habit, not as a constitutional office.

Tell this as a story. Article II assumed departments would exist and allowed the president to require written opinions from their principal officers, but it named no cabinet and prescribed no cabinet meetings. The First Congress filled the gap: Foreign Affairs (soon State) on July 27, 1789; War on August 7; Treasury on September 2. The Judiciary Act of September 24 created the Attorney General as a legal adviser. Washington then staffed the system: Hamilton at Treasury (confirmed September 11, 1789), Knox continuing at War, Jefferson at State (confirmed 1789; arrived from France in 1790), and Edmund Randolph as Attorney General. At first he followed the text and sought advice in writing, then followed up one-on-one. Collective consultation became clearer by late 1791 and especially during the 1793 neutrality crisis. The point for students: the familiar cabinet is an invented routine built on statute and practice.

Sources: Mount Vernon, “A Precedent: The First Cabinet.” Live source National Archives / Founders Online editorial note on Washington to Jefferson, 25 November 1791. Live source Federal Judiciary Act (1789), National Archives. Live source

The First Congress Turned Constitutional Sketches into Working Machinery

Revenue that could reach ports

Tariff and collection laws (1789) created duties, collectors, and customhouses.

Executive partners for the president

Congress created State, War, and Treasury so the executive branch had departments to run.

Courts beneath the Supreme Court

The Judiciary Act of September 24, 1789, built district and circuit courts and defined federal jurisdiction.

Daily rules of office

Oaths, salaries, appropriations, and procedures turned paper powers into routines.

Authority came from the Constitution. The First Congress supplied the machinery that made authority usable.

Keep this slide about building capacity, not about the Bill of Rights (that comes next). Walk the story chronologically through summer and fall 1789: revenue first (Tariff Act of July 4; collection machinery on July 31), then executive departments (Foreign Affairs/State, War, Treasury), then the Judiciary Act that created lower federal courts, marshals, district attorneys, and the Attorney General. Students should leave knowing that “the government” was not finished on inauguration day. Statutes had to invent the offices, money channels, and courts that made constitutional commands real.

The Bill of Rights Helped Make the New Government Legitimate

Ratification fear

Opponents worried that the new national government could become another source of centralized power.

Madison's response

He proposed specified protections without reopening the entire Constitution.

A political settlement

Rights protections answered arguments made during ratification and taught citizens what government could not do.

Capacity preserved

The amendments reassured critics without dismantling the new government's ability to act.

Legitimacy required both effective power and visible limits on that power.

Do not turn this into an amendment-by-amendment lecture. The point is institutional and political: the new government needed to answer ratification-era fears if citizens were to regard it as legitimate.

Sources: National Archives, “The Bill of Rights: How Did it Happen?” Live source James Madison, “Notes for Speech in Congress, ca. 8 June 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

A Small Federal Government Could Still Reach Citizens

Customs

Duties, collectors, customhouses, and paperwork raised revenue.

Courts

Judges, marshals, juries, and jurisdiction made federal law actionable.

Administration

Departments processed information, appointments, treaties, and appropriations.

Force

Diplomacy and military authority gave national decisions consequences beyond paper.

The federal government was small. It was not imaginary.

Keep this high-level. Students need to understand state capacity before the Whiskey Rebellion: revenue, courts, administration, diplomacy, and military authority gave the national government ways to affect ordinary lives.

The Whiskey Excise Made Federal Power Visible at the Still

The excise tax reached western producers directly. Federal marshal David Lenox served process, and revenue inspector John Neville's home at Bower Hill became a flashpoint. Armed resistance, casualties, and the burning of property turned a tax protest into a test of federal law.

1791 · Western Pennsylvania

Hamilton's Whiskey Tax Fell Hardest on Frontier Farmers

Small distillers could pay more than twice per gallon what large distillers paid, and they had to pay in cash.

On the frontier, whiskey was both the crop and the money.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec1_Slide13_Frontier_Farmer_at_the_Still_revised_FA26.png

Story beat 1 of 3 (about 2 minutes): Start with the money. The federal government had taken on the states' Revolutionary War debts, and it needed revenue to pay them. At Hamilton's urging, Congress passed an excise on distilled spirits on March 3, 1791. It was the first nationwide tax on something Americans produced at home rather than imported.

Why the frontier cared: Western farmers could not easily haul bulky grain east over the mountains on poor roads, so many distilled surplus rye and corn into whiskey, which was easier to carry and kept longer. Cash was scarce in the west, so whiskey also worked as a kind of currency. A tax on whiskey felt like a tax on their main crop and their money at the same time.

Why it felt unfair: The 1791 law let distillers choose how to pay. A large, year-round commercial still could pay a flat annual tax based on the still's capacity, which worked out cheaply per gallon. A small farm still that ran only after harvest usually paid by the gallon instead, often more than twice as much per gallon. Rates ran from about 6 to 18 cents a gallon. Payment had to be in cash, which was scarce on the frontier. Anyone charged with not paying could be summoned to federal court in Philadelphia, roughly 300 miles from Pittsburgh.

Resistance began at once: On September 6, 1791, armed men in disguise waylaid Robert Johnson, the revenue collector for Allegheny and Washington counties, at Pigeon Creek. They tarred and feathered him, cut off his hair, and took his horse. Washington issued a proclamation against obstructing the law in 1792, and Congress revised the tax in 1792 and 1794, but collection on the frontier mostly failed.

Ask students: Could a farmer accept that the war debt had to be paid and still think this particular tax was unjust?

July and August 1794 · Bower Hill

In July 1794, Tax Resistance Became Armed Rebellion

Hundreds of armed men attacked tax inspector John Neville's house. Rebel leader James McFarlane was killed, and the house was burned.

Two weeks later, thousands of armed men marched through Pittsburgh.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec1_Slide14_Bower_Hill_Burning_revised_FA26.png

Story beat 2 of 3 (about 2 minutes): In July 1794, U.S. Marshal David Lenox rode through the western counties serving writs that ordered distillers who had not paid to appear in federal court in Philadelphia. John Neville, the regional revenue inspector, guided him. On July 15 they served a writ on farmer William Miller, and armed neighbors confronted them. A shot was fired as they rode off.

Bower Hill: On July 16, a small armed group came to Neville's house at Bower Hill. Neville fired and killed one of them, and enslaved people on Neville's estate also fired on the crowd before it retreated. On July 17 the crowd returned, now several hundred strong (estimates run from about 500 to 700). Neville had slipped away, and about ten or eleven soldiers from the Pittsburgh garrison held the house. James McFarlane, a Revolutionary War veteran leading the rebels, was killed during the fighting. The rebels burned the buildings, and the soldiers surrendered. Lenox was captured and forced to promise not to serve more writs. He and Neville then fled east by a roundabout route.

Braddock's Field: Rebels robbed the Pittsburgh mail and read letters criticizing them. David Bradford of Washington County called the militia to muster at Braddock's Field, about eight or nine miles from Pittsburgh, on August 1. Estimates of the crowd range from about 4,500 (an officer at Pittsburgh's fort) to about 7,000 (later historians). On August 2, Pittsburgh lawyer Hugh Henry Brackenridge helped steer them into a peaceful march through town instead of an attack. Townspeople supplied food and whiskey, several residents were banished, and a barn was burned, but the town survived.

Emphasize: By now this was no longer only a tax protest. Armed men had attacked federal officers and soldiers, men had been killed, and a federal marshal had been driven out of the region.

August and September 1794 · Philadelphia

Washington Tried Law and Talks Before Sending the Militia West

“The very existence of Government and the fundamental principles of social order are materially involved in the issue.”
George Washington, Proclamation, 7 August 1794

First a judge, then a deadline, then peace talks. Only then did the militia march.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec1_Slide15_Washington_Reviews_Militia_at_Carlisle_revised_FA26.png

Story beat 3 of 3 (about 2 minutes): Washington met with his cabinet and Pennsylvania governor Thomas Mifflin on August 2. He could not simply send troops. The Militia Act of 1792 required a federal judge to certify first that the courts and the marshal could not enforce the law. On August 4, Supreme Court Justice James Wilson certified that in Washington and Allegheny counties the law was obstructed by “combinations too powerful to be suppressed by the ordinary course of Judicial proceedings.”

The proclamation: On August 7, Washington ordered the insurgents to “disperse and retire peaceably to their respective abodes” by September 1. The same day, Secretary of War Henry Knox asked the governors of New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia to ready 12,950 militia. Read the quoted line aloud: Washington framed the crisis as a test of whether the government could exist at all, not just whether a tax could be collected.

Talks first: Washington also sent three peace commissioners: Attorney General William Bradford, Senator James Ross, and Pennsylvania Supreme Court justice Jasper Yeates. In late August and early September they negotiated with a committee chosen by a western assembly, including Albert Gallatin and Hugh Henry Brackenridge. The talks did not produce a settlement. On September 24 the commissioners reported that military force was “absolutely necessary,” and the militia began moving west.

Not everyone agreed: A Philadelphia newspaper writer calling himself “Nestor” warned Washington on August 12 that “the milder mode ought to be the first resort” and asked why the government would negotiate with Britain but not with its own citizens. Keep this in mind for the activity that follows.

Hand-off: Stop the story at the march west. The next slides let students reason about resistance first, then return to what the militia actually did.

How Is Resistance Different Under a Constitution?

Write two sentences • Compare contexts
The same word—resistance—could mean liberty or lawlessness depending on the government being resisted.
Revolutionary claim: A tax imposed from far away can be resisted as tyranny.
Constitutional claim: A law made by elected institutions must be obeyed or changed through republican politics.
Write two short sentences: Make the strongest liberty argument for resisting a tax, then name the institutional fact that makes 1794 different from resistance to Parliament.

Revolutionary Resistance Collided with Constitutional Government

Resistance tradition

Americans had celebrated resistance to unjust taxation.

Constitutional government

Federalists argued that laws made by elected institutions had to be obeyed.

The Revolution had not settled when resistance defends liberty and when it destroys self-government.

This is where the activity pays off. Students compare resistance to imperial rule with resistance under a constitutional republic. The vocabulary overlaps; the institutional context does not.

The Government Won the Test—and Created New Fears

Federalist lesson

The republic could tax, enforce law, mobilize militia, and survive resistance.

Republican fear

The same capacity could become coercion against citizens who opposed national policy.

The government became stronger. So did fear of the government.

Do not end as simple triumph. The Constitution had produced a government capable of acting, and the Whiskey Rebellion made that capacity visible. But successful state-building also created the political fear that powers Lecture 2.

Precedent, Capacity, and Legitimacy Made the Constitution Operational

Precedent

The Constitution became real through routines people repeated.

Capacity

Departments, courts, revenue, and force gave the federal government the ability to act.

Legitimacy

Rights, restraint, and consent helped make that power rightful—and still made it frightening.

Next: can citizens organize against the people who control this power without being treated as enemies?

Public Credit Raised a Fairness Question

Optional enrichment
Public credit had a fairness problem.
  • Original holders included soldiers and suppliers who sold certificates cheaply during hard times.
  • Current holders included speculators who bought those certificates.
  • Madison asked about fairness; Hamilton answered with market credibility.
Big idea: the same policy could look like justice, injustice, or survival depending on the principle you emphasized.

What Kind of Republic Did the Revolution Create?

Hamilton, Jefferson, and the invention of loyal opposition

Opening: This revised deck follows the uncoverage principle. It traces one argument: competing visions of republican liberty produced political parties, and foreign crisis forced Americans to decide whether organized opposition could remain loyal.

Protocol: Formal 35-minute lecture with exactly two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper follows only after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment.

Pacing map: Lecture 1 bridge about 1 minute; Hamilton program, Jefferson fear, and Activity 1 about 11 minutes; Bank, parties, Genêt, Jay history and backlash, and Farewell about 7 minutes; Washington's retirement and death plus the apotheosis sequence (Making a Demigod, History vs. Myth, Civil Religion) about 7 minutes; Adams intro about 1 minute; XYZ, Sedition, Activity 2, and Lyon about 9 minutes; Virginia/Kentucky, 1800, and synthesis about 6 minutes.

Federal Power Was Real; Its Purpose Was Still Contested

Tax

Raise national revenue.

Borrow

Use public credit.

Administer

Operate courts and departments.

Enforce

Make federal law effective.

Now that the government had power, what should that power build?

Timing: About one minute. Do not reteach Lecture 1.

Story cue: By 1789-1794 the Constitution was working as a government: Congress could tax and borrow, departments and courts were running, and the Whiskey Rebellion showed federal law could be enforced. The open question for this lecture is not whether power existed, but what kind of republic that power should build.

Arc: Hamilton will answer with credit and national strength; Jefferson and Madison will answer with limited delegated power and citizen independence. That disagreement will produce parties, then a loyalty crisis when foreign war arrives.

Hamilton Believed National Weakness Threatened Independence

The danger

Revolutionary debt, uncertain credit, weak revenue, and dependence on stronger nations.

The answer

A government credible enough to collect, borrow, survive war, and command respect abroad.

A republic that could not survive a crisis could not preserve its independence.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes.

Story: In 1789 Washington made Alexander Hamilton the first Secretary of the Treasury. Hamilton had seen wartime finance collapse under the Continental Congress and Confederation. His starting problem was survival: a republic that could not pay its debts, borrow money, or raise reliable revenue would stay diplomatically and militarily weak. Introduce him as a coherent political thinker, not merely "the bank guy."

Why it matters: If independence depended on creditworthiness, then fiscal design was constitutional politics, not bookkeeping.

Source: Alexander Hamilton, "Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit," Founders Online. Live source

January 1790 · Report on Public Credit

Hamilton's Report Tied Debt, Revenue, and National Survival Together

Fund at face→Assume state debts→Secure revenue
Debt became more than a bill to pay: it tied creditors and revenue to the success of the federal government.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Public_Credit_revised_FA26.png

Name the report: Alexander Hamilton's January 1790 "Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit" (often called the Report on Public Credit). Speak for about 2-3 minutes. Keep the slide thin; put the "why" in notes.

Fund at face: Hamilton wanted the federal government to honor the full face value of its Revolutionary certificates, not a discounted settlement. To him, reliable repayment was the only way markets and foreign governments would trust the new republic enough to lend again.

Assumption: He wanted the national government to take on the remaining state Revolutionary debts. To him, one national debt would bind creditors to the Union, equalize the burden among states, and make the federal government the indispensable fiscal center.

Revenue (tariffs and excises): Funding and assumption only worked if money came in. Customs duties and selective excises, including the whiskey tax, were meant to give the government a steady stream so it could pay interest, keep credit, and survive war without begging the states.

Political point: This was an economic program and a political program. Whoever paid the debt and collected the revenue would become the government that mattered.

Jefferson and Madison Feared Hamilton Was Rebuilding the British System

The danger

Permanent debt, concentrated finance, wealthy interests shaping policy.

The alternative

Delegated federal power, independent citizens, productive agriculture.

Both sides claimed to preserve the Revolution. They feared different ways liberty could be lost.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Agrarian_Republic_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 2 minutes.

Story: In 1790-1791 Thomas Jefferson (Secretary of State) and James Madison (in the House) broke with Hamilton's program. They did not advocate no government. They feared dependence and corruption: permanent public debt, a national bank, and executive patronage could recreate the British fiscal-military system Americans had just rejected. Their agrarian ideal treated independent landholding as a foundation for republican citizenship.

Old allies: Madison had worked with Hamilton on ratification and The Federalist, then came to see Hamilton's reading of federal power as a threat to the constitutional settlement he thought they had made.

Thesis link: Both sides claimed to preserve the Revolution. They feared different ways liberty could be lost, which is why the first party system formed around purpose, not mere ambition.

The Bank Debate Forced a Fight Over "Necessary and Proper"

Necessary and Proper: Congress may choose means that help carry out powers the Constitution already grants.
Jefferson

"Necessary" means indispensable. If another means exists, the bank is not allowed.

Hamilton

"Necessary" means useful and consistent with constitutional ends, not the only imaginable tool.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Bank_Debate_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 2 minutes.

Story: In 1790-1791 Hamilton proposed a Bank of the United States to manage federal funds, issue notes, and stabilize credit. Congress passed a bank bill. Washington asked for constitutional opinions before signing. Jefferson and Madison said nowhere did the Constitution list a bank power. Hamilton answered with the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Teach the clause in one sentence: It lets Congress choose means for powers already listed. Jefferson read "necessary" as indispensable; Hamilton read it as useful and consistent with legitimate ends. Washington accepted Hamilton's opinion; the Bank was chartered in 1791.

Thesis link: This is the clearest continuity with Lecture 1's "instruction manual" theme: same text, opposite republics. Avoid a law-school detour.

Competing Visions of the Revolution Created the First Party System

Federalists

Credit, commercial development, executive energy, and national strength protected independence.

Democratic-Republicans

Limited delegated power, vigilant citizens, and resistance to concentrated wealth protected liberty.

Parties were a product of the fight over what the Revolution meant, not a planned invention.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes. Keep this thin; do not turn it into a theory of parties.

Story: By the mid-1790s Hamilton's supporters and Jefferson/Madison's opponents of the fiscal program sorted into lasting coalitions. Federalists defended credit, commercial development, executive energy, and national strength. Their rivals (contemporaries usually said Republican; "Democratic-Republican" is the modern label) defended limited delegated power, agrarian independence, and vigilance against concentrated wealth.

How it looked: Newspapers, public meetings, and petitions became party tools. Supporters called them watchdogs of liberty; opponents called them faction. Parties were a product of the fight over what the Revolution meant, not a planned invention.

Thesis link: Organized opposition now existed. The next question is whether foreign crisis would treat that opposition as loyal or treasonous.

1793 · Citizen Genêt

Genêt Turned a Foreign Revolution Into a Domestic Loyalty Test

A French envoy appealed to American crowds against Washington's neutrality policy.

Could you cheer a sister revolution and still obey your own government?

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Genet_Welcome_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5 minutes.

Story: In April 1793 Edmond-Charles Genêt arrived as minister from revolutionary France. Washington had just issued the Neutrality Proclamation: the United States would stay out of the European war between France and Britain. Genêt outfitted privateers in American ports, appealed over the president's head to crowds, and tried to mobilize support for France anyway. Washington's cabinet demanded his recall. Republicans often cheered a sister republic; Federalists saw foreign interference and mob politics.

Thesis link: Foreign revolution made domestic disagreement look like disloyalty. Cheering France versus obeying neutrality became a test of what kind of republic Americans thought they had.

1794-1795 · Jay's Treaty

Jay's Treaty Was a Bargain to Avoid War with Britain

The crisis

British posts still held the Northwest. Impressment and trade seizures hit American shipping.

The terms

Britain agreed to evacuate posts and open limited trade. Impressment and seized cargoes stayed unresolved.

Washington and Jay chose an imperfect peace over a war the new republic could not afford.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Jay_Treaty_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. Teach the history first: why the crisis, what the treaty did, and what it left out. British troops still occupied northwestern posts after the Revolution. British naval practice impressed American sailors and seized ships. War with Britain threatened commerce, credit, and survival. John Jay negotiated evacuation of the posts and limited commercial access; impressment and compensation for seizures remained unresolved. Federalists saw necessary survival diplomacy.

Jay's Treaty Turned Foreign Policy Into Party Identity

Federalist defense

An imperfect treaty still averted war and protected credit and commerce.

Republican backlash

A humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France and the Revolution.

Meetings, petitions, and newspaper warfare made diplomacy a domestic loyalty test.

Timing: About 1 minute. Do not re-teach the treaty terms; teach the backlash.

Story: When Jay's Treaty became public in 1795, Republican newspapers and public meetings denounced it as a humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France and the Revolution. Demonstrators burned effigies of Jay; petitions flooded Congress. Federalists answered that an imperfect bargain still averted war and protected credit and commerce. Party identity hardened around foreign policy itself.

Thesis link: Diplomacy had become a domestic loyalty test: which Atlantic power you favored signaled which vision of the republic you held.

Source: U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, "John Jay's Treaty, 1794-95." Live source

1796 · Washington's Farewell Address

Washington Warned Against Permanent Alliances and Party Spirit

Foreign policy

Avoid permanent alliances that pull the republic into European wars.

Domestic politics

Party spirit can put faction above the common good and invite foreign intrigue.

The retiring president named the dangers the 1790s had already made visible.

Watch: Washington's Farewell Address (video)

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes.

Story: In September 1796 Washington published his Farewell Address as he declined a third term. He warned against permanent foreign attachments that could drag the republic into European wars, and against the spirit of party that put faction above the common good and invited foreign intrigue. He was diagnosing the Genêt and Jay crises students just heard, not issuing abstract maxims for a quiet age.

Bridge: The handoff from the Washington era to a contested party succession begins here. The next president inherits the Federalist program and the France crisis without Washington's unique prestige.

1797-1799 · Washington Leaves Office

Washington Gave Up Power, and the Nation Mourned Him as More Than a Man

1797

Retires to Mount Vernon in March; power passes peacefully to Adams.

1798

Briefly recalled to command a new army during the Quasi-War with France.

1799

Dies December 14 of a throat infection, after doctors bleed him heavily.

1801

His will freed the 123 people he enslaved after Martha's death; she freed them early, on January 1, 1801.

Months of national mourning began turning the man into a myth.

Mourning print, Boston, 1801: "There is Rest in Heaven."

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. This slide closes Washington's life and sets up the short apotheosis sequence (three slides) that follows.

Story: Washington refused a third term, attended John Adams's inauguration in Philadelphia on March 4, 1797, and went home to Mount Vernon. That quiet exit mattered: the first transfer of the presidency from one person to another happened peacefully, under the Constitution, and the most powerful man in the country chose to become a private citizen. He did not stay entirely retired. In July 1798, as the Quasi-War with France threatened, Adams named him lieutenant general and commander of a new provisional army. Washington accepted on condition that he would not take the field unless the army was actually needed; Alexander Hamilton did most of the actual organizing, and the army never fought.

Death: On December 12, 1799, Washington rode his farms for hours in snow and freezing rain. The next day he had a sore throat, and early on December 14 he woke struggling to breathe. An overseer and then his doctors bled him four times, removing roughly 80 ounces of blood, about 40 percent of his supply, and gave him purges and blisters. Modern physicians think he had a severe throat infection, probably acute epiglottitis; the treatment, standard for the time, likely weakened him. He died between ten and eleven that night, at 67.

The will: Washington's will, written in July 1799, ordered that the 123 enslaved people he owned outright be freed after Martha Washington's death, that the elderly and sick be supported, and that freed children be taught to read and write and trained in a useful occupation. His valet William Lee was freed at once. He could not free the 153 "dower" slaves who belonged to the estate of Martha's first husband. Martha Washington signed a deed freeing her husband's 123 in December 1800, effective January 1, 1801, a year and a half before her own death. Of the slaveholding presidents of the founding era, he was the only one to free the people he enslaved, and he did it only at his death.

Mourning: Congress heard Henry Lee's eulogy on December 26 and called for national observances on February 22, 1800, Washington's birthday. Towns held mock funerals with empty coffins; hundreds of eulogies and sermons were printed; women wore mourning jewelry and stitched memorial embroideries. The print on this slide, engraved and sold in Boston in 1801, shows three mourners at a monument inscribed "There is Rest in Heaven." The figure with the anchor is Hope. Mourning objects like this are where the apotheosis begins.

Thesis link: Washington's two voluntary exits from power (1783 and 1797) helped make peaceful transfer a republican expectation. His death left the republic without its referee just as party conflict and the France crisis escalated, and it gave Americans a hero they would soon raise to the heavens.

1799-1941 · History to Myth: The Apotheosis of Washington

Mourners, Artists, and Sellers Turned Washington Into a Demigod

Apotheosis

From the Greek for "making a god": raising a person to divine rank.

"First in war, first in peace, and first in the hearts of his countrymen."
Henry Lee, eulogy for Washington, 1799
Printers, potters, painters, and sculptors sold Americans a Washington who rose to heaven.
1 / 8

Timing: About 2 minutes. Click through as many images as time allows; three or four (Barralet, the pitcher, Weems, Brumidi) carry the point. The Prev and Next buttons change the image; the arrow keys still change slides.

From mourning to worship. George Washington died at Mount Vernon on December 14, 1799. The nation's grief was enormous and organized. Congress asked Representative Henry "Light-Horse Harry" Lee, who had served under Washington, to deliver a eulogy, and on December 26 Lee told Congress that Washington was "first in war, first in peace, and first in the hearts of his countrymen." Congress then called for public mourning on February 22, 1800, Washington's birthday, and towns across the country staged mock funerals with empty coffins, processions, and sermons. Hundreds of eulogies were printed. In them Washington was compared to Moses and to Cincinnatus.

What "apotheosis" means. In ancient Rome, the Senate could declare a dead emperor a god; artists showed him carried up to heaven. Americans borrowed that image for Washington. The slideshow follows it for more than a century.

1. Barralet's print (1802). John James Barralet, an Irish-born artist in Philadelphia, published this large engraving in 1802. Father Time and an angel lift Washington from a tomb inscribed "Sacred to the Memory of Washington, Ob. 14 Dec. 1799" toward a beam of light. Below, Liberty with her cap on a pole, a Native American figure, an eagle, and the figures of Faith, Hope, and Charity mourn. The face follows a Gilbert Stuart portrait. Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain (CC0).

2. A pitcher (early 1800s). English potteries in and around Liverpool printed scenes like Barralet's onto cheap creamware pitchers and mugs made for sale in the United States. A family could pour cider from a picture of Washington ascending into heaven. Note the irony: British manufacturers profited by selling the American hero back to Americans. National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian, NPG.2007.250, public domain.

3. Weems's Life (1800 to 1809). Mason Locke Weems, an Episcopal minister and traveling book salesman, rushed out a short life of Washington in 1800 and kept enlarging it. In the fifth edition (1806) he added the cherry tree story. The title page calls him "formerly rector of Mount-Vernon parish," but there was no such parish; Washington's own church, Pohick Church, belonged to Truro Parish. Its verse ends: "Lisp! lisp! his name, ye children yet unborn! / And with like deeds your own great names adorn." The book was written to teach children virtue, not to report facts. Library of Congress, LCCN 2003669684, public domain.

4. Peale's "porthole" portrait (1824). Rembrandt Peale had painted Washington from life in 1795 as a teenager. Decades later he blended his memory with other portraits into one "standard" likeness, framed it in a painted stone oval with an oak wreath, and labeled it "Patriae Pater," Latin for "Father of His Country." The Senate bought it in 1832. Peale painted dozens of copies for sale. U.S. Senate Collection, public domain.

5. Greenough's statue (1840). In 1832, for the centennial of Washington's birth, Congress commissioned Horatio Greenough to carve a statue for the Capitol Rotunda. Greenough modeled it on the ancient Greek sculptor Phidias's statue of Zeus at Olympia: seated, bare-chested, in a toga and sandals, one hand pointing to heaven. Many Americans laughed at a half-naked Washington. The statue was moved from the Rotunda to the Capitol's east lawn in 1843 and to the Smithsonian in 1908. It shows that apotheosis had limits: people wanted a god, but a dignified, clothed one. Smithsonian American Art Museum, 1910.10.3, public domain (CC0); usually displayed at the National Museum of American History.

6. Leutze's painting (1851). You met this painting in Chapter 6. Emanuel Leutze painted it in Düsseldorf, Germany, 75 years after the crossing of December 25 to 26, 1776. Washington stands in a small rowboat in daylight; the real crossing happened at night in sleet, in larger, flat Durham boats, and the Stars and Stripes shown behind him was not adopted until June 1777. The painting turned a desperate gamble into a portrait of destiny. Metropolitan Museum of Art, public domain.

7. Brumidi's fresco (1865). The Italian-born artist Constantino Brumidi painted the canopy of the new Capitol dome in 1865, as the Civil War ended. About 180 feet above the floor, Washington sits enthroned in the clouds between Liberty and winged Victory, surrounded by thirteen maidens for the original states, under a banner reading "E Pluribus Unum." Around the rim, Roman gods help Americans: Minerva teaches Benjamin Franklin and Samuel Morse, and Neptune guards the sea. The Architect of the Capitol describes it as Washington rising to the heavens in glory. Photograph by Carol M. Highsmith, Library of Congress, public domain.

8. Mount Rushmore (1927 to 1941). The sculptor Gutzon Borglum carved Washington, Jefferson, Theodore Roosevelt, and Lincoln into a granite peak in the Black Hills of South Dakota. Washington's face is about 60 feet tall. The Black Hills were guaranteed to the Lakota by the Treaty of Fort Laramie (1868) and taken by the United States in 1877; in 1980 the Supreme Court ruled that the taking was illegal and ordered compensation, which the Lakota have refused. A monument to national heroes stands on land whose history the monument does not tell. National Park Service photo, public domain.

Not shown. Myth and History

History vs. Myth: What the Legend Left Out

"I can't tell a lie, Pa." The boy and the cherry tree

Junius Brutus Stearns, Washington as Farmer at Mount Vernon, 1851. The enslaved harvesters look content; the painting is myth too.

Timing: About 2 minutes. Take the rows in order; the enslaver row matters most.

Why compare? A myth is not always a lie; it is a story a community tells about itself because it teaches something the community values. The Washington myths teach honesty, sacrifice, and humility. History asks a different question: what actually happened, and how do we know? Setting them side by side shows what the myth was for and what it hid.

The cherry tree. Mason Locke Weems added the story to the fifth edition of his Life of Washington in 1806, seven years after Washington died. Six-year-old George barks his father's "beautiful young English cherry-tree" with a new hatchet and confesses: "I can't tell a lie, Pa; you know I can't tell a lie. I did cut it with my hatchet." Weems said he heard it from an elderly woman who had known the family, but he never named her, and no other source supports it. Mount Vernon and the historians at Washington's boyhood home, Ferry Farm, treat it as a myth. It survived because it was a perfect lesson for children, and schoolbooks retold it for generations.

The teeth. Washington began losing teeth in his twenties and had only one of his own left when he became president in 1789. His dentures were never wooden. Surviving sets combine human teeth, cow or horse teeth, ivory (from elephants or hippopotamuses), lead, copper alloy, and silver. The connection to slavery is real but needs care. In May 1784 Mount Vernon's manager, Lund Washington, recorded in George Washington's accounts: "By Cash pd Negroes for 9 Teeth on Acct of Dr Lemoin." The French dentist Jean-Pierre Le Mayeur was treating Washington at the time and bought teeth for transplants. Mount Vernon's historians note that "on account of" the dentist suggests the teeth were for the dentist's practice, and we do not know whether any ended up in Washington's mouth. We also do not know if the unnamed enslaved people could really refuse. Either way, the record shows that enslaved people's bodies were part of the economy around Washington, down to their teeth.

The enslaver. Washington became an enslaver at age 11, when he inherited ten enslaved people from his father. A census he took in 1799 counted 317 enslaved people at Mount Vernon: 123 belonged to Washington, 153 were "dower" slaves from the estate of Martha Washington's first husband (which neither of them could legally free), and 40 were rented from a neighbor. As president in Philadelphia he rotated enslaved workers out of Pennsylvania so that they could not claim freedom under the state's gradual abolition law, and after Ona Judge escaped from the President's House in 1796, he tried for years to recapture her. Over his life his views changed: his will freed his own 123 enslaved people after Martha's death and provided for the elderly and for the education of children. Martha Washington freed them on January 1, 1801. Stearns's 1851 painting, made as the nation argued over slavery's expansion, shows the work as cheerful and the workers as content. It is a myth of benevolent slavery wrapped around the myth of the farmer.

The ambitious man and his image. As a young Virginia officer Washington badly wanted a regular commission in the British army and did not get one. In 1754 he surrendered Fort Necessity to the French. In 1776 he lost New York, and in 1777 he lost at Brandywine and Germantown. His greatness came from persistence, judgment, and keeping the army alive, not from an unbroken string of victories. He also understood public image better than almost anyone. He carefully chose his clothes, his portraits, and his ceremonies, and on December 23, 1783, he resigned his commission to Congress at Annapolis and went home to Mount Vernon. Americans compared him to Cincinnatus, the Roman farmer who was given absolute power in an emergency and then returned to his plow. He served as president-general of the Society of the Cincinnati, the officers' society named for that hero. The painter Benjamin West reported that when George III heard Washington would give up power, the king said that if he did, he would be "the greatest man in the world." The modesty was real, and it was also performed.

Read critically. Notice that the history column does not make Washington small. Giving up power, twice (in 1783 and again in 1797), really was rare and important. The point is that a human Washington, ambitious, defeated, enslaving, and still capable of choosing restraint, is a better guide to the past than a man who never could have done wrong.

Historiography · Nationalism and Civil Religion

Hero Building Made Washington a Saint of American Civil Religion

Why does a nation turn a man into a demigod, and what does the halo hide?

History in service of the nation

Nationalist history glorifies the nation-state and builds heroes with rhetoric, symbols, art, and music. In America's civil religion, the Declaration became scripture and Washington became one of its saints.

The critique

Hero worship distorts by slanting. It hides the enslaved people at Mount Vernon, the defeats, and the ambition, and it turns history into something to revere instead of something to question.

A god cannot teach citizens much. A flawed man who still chose to give up power, twice, can.

Revisit: Civil religion in Chapter 5

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. End the Washington sequence here and move to Adams.

Historiography. Remember that historiography is the study of how history has been written: who told the story, when, for what purpose, and with what evidence. This slide asks why Americans needed a Washington who was more than human.

History in service of nationalism. Nationalism is an ideology: the belief that a people forms a nation that deserves its own state and its loyalty. Nationalists use rhetoric, symbols, art, and music, and they use history to show that the nation has always been united and good. Two common tools are glorifying the nation-state and building heroes. The new United States in 1800 had no king, no established church, no ancient history, and deep divisions between regions and parties. A shared hero helped hold it together. Washington, the one figure nearly everyone had admired, became that hero, and his image was put on prints, pitchers, schoolbooks, paintings, statues, coins, and the Capitol ceiling.

Civil religion. In Chapter 5 ("The Sacred Career of the Declaration of Independence") you met the idea of American civil religion: a set of beliefs, symbols, and rituals that treats the nation itself as sacred. It has scriptures (the Declaration and the Constitution), holy places (the National Mall, Independence Hall), holidays (the Fourth of July, Washington's Birthday), and saints and martyrs (the founders, Lincoln, fallen soldiers). The apotheosis of Washington is civil religion in action: the Capitol dome shows the first president literally in heaven. The sociologist Robert Bellah gave the idea its modern name in his 1967 essay "Civil Religion in America."

The critique. Critics of nationalist history point to several distortions. It slants evidence, keeping what flatters the nation and leaving out what does not. It can be xenophobic, defining the nation against outsiders. And it commits the fallacy of hero worship: treating great individuals as flawless, so that their mistakes, and the people they harmed, disappear. With Washington, hero worship hid the more than three hundred enslaved people at Mount Vernon, the battles he lost, and the ambition behind the modesty. The historian James Loewen calls this process "heroification" and argues that it leaves students with heroes they cannot learn from.

Why it matters. The point is not to tear Washington down. His real achievements, keeping a ragged army together for eight years and then twice giving up power when he could have kept it, are more impressive when you know he was a human being who could have chosen otherwise. A god's choices teach nothing; a person's choices are an example citizens can follow or reject.

Check yourself: What are two ways nationalist history uses the past? What does "civil religion" mean, and where does Washington fit in it? What did hero worship hide about Washington? Can a nation honor its founders without turning them into gods?

The Sedition Act Turned Criticism of Government Into a Federal Crime

It became a federal crime to publish certain "false, scandalous and malicious" writings against the government.
Sedition Act, 1798
Federalists said malicious falsehoods could aid an enemy. Republicans said the governing party was criminalizing criticism.

Timing: About 2 minutes. This is the core law for the formal lecture.

Story: In 1798, during the Quasi-War panic, Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts. For this lecture focus on the Sedition Act: it made it a federal crime to publish "false, scandalous and malicious" writings against the government, Congress, or the president. Federalists said malicious falsehoods could aid an enemy in wartime. Republicans said the governing party was criminalizing criticism and protecting Adams from a free press. Federal prosecutors targeted opposition newspaper editors and politicians; convictions followed in several high-profile cases.

Republican Resistance Defended Liberty and Created a New Constitutional Problem

Madison's Virginia

States should "interpose" against alarming federal violations.

Jefferson's Kentucky

Federal acts beyond delegated powers could be treated as void.

If the federal government violates liberty, who gets to decide that it has gone too far?

Timing: About 1.5-2 minutes. Keep introductory; this is not a full nullification lecture.

Story: In late 1798, Republican-led legislatures answered the Alien and Sedition Acts with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. Madison's Virginia Resolutions argued that states should "interpose" to protect against alarming federal violations of the compact. Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions (he drafted them; the legislature adopted a version) argued that federal acts beyond delegated powers could be treated as void. Few other states joined; Federalist legislatures condemned the idea. The immediate political effect was protest and party mobilization more than successful nullification.

Thesis link: Republican resistance defended liberty against Sedition, but also opened an unresolved problem: if the federal government violates the Constitution, who decides, and how far may a state go?

Federalist warning

Republican warning

Story: By the campaign of 1800 each party had a coherent nightmare. Federalists warned that Jefferson meant French-style radicalism, irreligion, and social disorder. The Jefferson-Burr Tie Exposed a Constitution Not Designed for Parties

73-73
Thirty-five House ballots failed. The thirty-sixth elected Jefferson.
The choice rested with state delegations in the outgoing Federalist House.

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. Avoid excessive electoral mechanics.

Story: Under the original Constitution, electors did not vote separately for president and vice president. Hamilton's opposition to Burr influenced some Federalists, but keep that as a brief aside, not a subplot. Three-Fifths implications stay in optional enrichment.

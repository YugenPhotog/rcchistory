What Kind of Republic Did the Revolution Create?

Hamilton, Jefferson, and the invention of loyal opposition

Opening: This revised deck follows the uncoverage principle. It traces one argument: competing visions of republican liberty produced political parties, and foreign crisis forced Americans to decide whether organized opposition could remain loyal.

Protocol: Formal 35-minute lecture with exactly two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper follows only after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment.

Pacing map: Lecture 1 bridge about 1 minute; Hamilton program, Jefferson fear, and Activity 1 about 11 minutes; Bank, parties, Genêt, Jay history and backlash, Farewell, and Adams intro about 8 minutes; XYZ, Sedition, Activity 2, and Lyon about 9 minutes; Virginia/Kentucky, 1800, and synthesis about 6 minutes.

Federal Power Was Real; Its Purpose Was Still Contested

Tax

Raise national revenue.

Borrow

Use public credit.

Administer

Operate courts and departments.

Enforce

Make federal law effective.

Now that the government had power, what should that power build?

Timing: About one minute. Do not reteach Lecture 1.

Story cue: By 1789-1794 the Constitution was working as a government: Congress could tax and borrow, departments and courts were running, and the Whiskey Rebellion showed federal law could be enforced. The open question for this lecture is not whether power existed, but what kind of republic that power should build.

Arc: Hamilton will answer with credit and national strength; Jefferson and Madison will answer with limited delegated power and citizen independence. That disagreement will produce parties, then a loyalty crisis when foreign war arrives.

Hamilton Believed National Weakness Threatened Independence

The danger

Revolutionary debt, uncertain credit, weak revenue, and dependence on stronger nations.

The answer

A government credible enough to collect, borrow, survive war, and command respect abroad.

A republic that could not survive a crisis could not preserve its independence.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes.

Story: In 1789 Washington made Alexander Hamilton the first Secretary of the Treasury. Hamilton had seen wartime finance collapse under the Continental Congress and Confederation. His starting problem was survival: a republic that could not pay its debts, borrow money, or raise reliable revenue would stay diplomatically and militarily weak. Introduce him as a coherent political thinker, not merely "the bank guy."

Why it matters: If independence depended on creditworthiness, then fiscal design was constitutional politics, not bookkeeping.

Source: Alexander Hamilton, "Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit," Founders Online. Live source

January 1790 · Report on Public Credit

Hamilton's Report Tied Debt, Revenue, and National Survival Together

Fund at face→Assume state debts→Secure revenue
Debt became more than a bill to pay: it tied creditors and revenue to the success of the federal government.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Public_Credit_revised_FA26.png

Name the report: Alexander Hamilton's January 1790 "Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit" (often called the Report on Public Credit). Speak for about 2-3 minutes. Keep the slide thin; put the "why" in notes.

Fund at face: Hamilton wanted the federal government to honor the full face value of its Revolutionary certificates, not a discounted settlement. To him, reliable repayment was the only way markets and foreign governments would trust the new republic enough to lend again.

Assumption: He wanted the national government to take on the remaining state Revolutionary debts. To him, one national debt would bind creditors to the Union, equalize the burden among states, and make the federal government the indispensable fiscal center.

Revenue (tariffs and excises): Funding and assumption only worked if money came in. Customs duties and selective excises, including the whiskey tax, were meant to give the government a steady stream so it could pay interest, keep credit, and survive war without begging the states.

Political point: This was an economic program and a political program. Whoever paid the debt and collected the revenue would become the government that mattered.

Jefferson and Madison Feared Hamilton Was Rebuilding the British System

The danger

Permanent debt, concentrated finance, wealthy interests shaping policy.

The alternative

Delegated federal power, independent citizens, productive agriculture.

Both sides claimed to preserve the Revolution. They feared different ways liberty could be lost.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Agrarian_Republic_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 2 minutes.

Story: In 1790-1791 Thomas Jefferson (Secretary of State) and James Madison (in the House) broke with Hamilton's program. They did not advocate no government. They feared dependence and corruption: permanent public debt, a national bank, and executive patronage could recreate the British fiscal-military system Americans had just rejected. Their agrarian ideal treated independent landholding as a foundation for republican citizenship.

Old allies: Madison had worked with Hamilton on ratification and The Federalist, then came to see Hamilton's reading of federal power as a threat to the constitutional settlement he thought they had made.

Thesis link: Both sides claimed to preserve the Revolution. They feared different ways liberty could be lost, which is why the first party system formed around purpose, not mere ambition.

The Bank Debate Forced a Fight Over "Necessary and Proper"

Necessary and Proper: Congress may choose means that help carry out powers the Constitution already grants.
Jefferson

"Necessary" means indispensable. If another means exists, the bank is not allowed.

Hamilton

"Necessary" means useful and consistent with constitutional ends, not the only imaginable tool.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Bank_Debate_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 2 minutes.

Story: In 1790-1791 Hamilton proposed a Bank of the United States to manage federal funds, issue notes, and stabilize credit. Congress passed a bank bill. Washington asked for constitutional opinions before signing. Jefferson and Madison said nowhere did the Constitution list a bank power. Hamilton answered with the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Teach the clause in one sentence: It lets Congress choose means for powers already listed. Jefferson read "necessary" as indispensable; Hamilton read it as useful and consistent with legitimate ends. Washington accepted Hamilton's opinion; the Bank was chartered in 1791.

Thesis link: This is the clearest continuity with Lecture 1's "instruction manual" theme: same text, opposite republics. Avoid a law-school detour.

Competing Visions of the Revolution Created the First Party System

Federalists

Credit, commercial development, executive energy, and national strength protected independence.

Democratic-Republicans

Limited delegated power, vigilant citizens, and resistance to concentrated wealth protected liberty.

Parties were a product of the fight over what the Revolution meant, not a planned invention.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes. Keep this thin; do not turn it into a theory of parties.

Story: By the mid-1790s Hamilton's supporters and Jefferson/Madison's opponents of the fiscal program sorted into lasting coalitions. Federalists defended credit, commercial development, executive energy, and national strength. Their rivals (contemporaries usually said Republican; "Democratic-Republican" is the modern label) defended limited delegated power, agrarian independence, and vigilance against concentrated wealth.

How it looked: Newspapers, public meetings, and petitions became party tools. Supporters called them watchdogs of liberty; opponents called them faction. Parties were a product of the fight over what the Revolution meant, not a planned invention.

Thesis link: Organized opposition now existed. The next question is whether foreign crisis would treat that opposition as loyal or treasonous.

1793 · Citizen Genêt

Genêt Turned a Foreign Revolution Into a Domestic Loyalty Test

A French envoy appealed to American crowds against Washington's neutrality policy.

Could you cheer a sister revolution and still obey your own government?

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Genet_Welcome_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5 minutes.

Story: In April 1793 Edmond-Charles Genêt arrived as minister from revolutionary France. Washington had just issued the Neutrality Proclamation: the United States would stay out of the European war between France and Britain. Genêt outfitted privateers in American ports, appealed over the president's head to crowds, and tried to mobilize support for France anyway. Washington's cabinet demanded his recall. Republicans often cheered a sister republic; Federalists saw foreign interference and mob politics.

Thesis link: Foreign revolution made domestic disagreement look like disloyalty. Cheering France versus obeying neutrality became a test of what kind of republic Americans thought they had.

1794-1795 · Jay's Treaty

Jay's Treaty Was a Bargain to Avoid War with Britain

The crisis

British posts still held the Northwest. Impressment and trade seizures hit American shipping.

The terms

Britain agreed to evacuate posts and open limited trade. Impressment and seized cargoes stayed unresolved.

Washington and Jay chose an imperfect peace over a war the new republic could not afford.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Jay_Treaty_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. Teach the history first: why the crisis, what the treaty did, and what it left out. British troops still occupied northwestern posts after the Revolution. British naval practice impressed American sailors and seized ships. War with Britain threatened commerce, credit, and survival. John Jay negotiated evacuation of the posts and limited commercial access; impressment and compensation for seizures remained unresolved. Federalists saw necessary survival diplomacy.

Jay's Treaty Turned Foreign Policy Into Party Identity

Federalist defense

An imperfect treaty still averted war and protected credit and commerce.

Republican backlash

A humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France and the Revolution.

Meetings, petitions, and newspaper warfare made diplomacy a domestic loyalty test.

Timing: About 1 minute. Do not re-teach the treaty terms; teach the backlash.

Story: When Jay's Treaty became public in 1795, Republican newspapers and public meetings denounced it as a humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France and the Revolution. Demonstrators burned effigies of Jay; petitions flooded Congress. Federalists answered that an imperfect bargain still averted war and protected credit and commerce. Party identity hardened around foreign policy itself.

Thesis link: Diplomacy had become a domestic loyalty test: which Atlantic power you favored signaled which vision of the republic you held.

Source: U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, "John Jay's Treaty, 1794-95." Live source

1796 · Washington's Farewell Address

Washington Warned Against Permanent Alliances and Party Spirit

Foreign policy

Avoid permanent alliances that pull the republic into European wars.

Domestic politics

Party spirit can put faction above the common good and invite foreign intrigue.

The retiring president named the dangers the 1790s had already made visible.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes.

Story: In September 1796 Washington published his Farewell Address as he declined a third term. He warned against permanent foreign attachments that could drag the republic into European wars, and against the spirit of party that put faction above the common good and invited foreign intrigue. He was diagnosing the Genêt and Jay crises students just heard, not issuing abstract maxims for a quiet age.

Bridge: The handoff from the Washington era to a contested party succession begins here. The next president inherits the Federalist program and the France crisis without Washington's unique prestige.

Source: George Washington, Farewell Address, 19 September 1796, Founders Online. Live source

1797-1801 · John Adams

Adams Inherited a Federalist Republic Already Divided by Party and France

Succession

First contested presidential transfer after Washington.

Inheritance

Federalist program, party newspapers, and a brittle neutrality.

Crisis

Revolutionary France treated Jay's Treaty as betrayal and struck American shipping.

XYZ and Sedition belong to the Adams years, not Washington's.

Timing: About 1-1.5 minutes. Name the president and years before XYZ appears.

Story: John Adams, a Federalist, narrowly defeated Thomas Jefferson in 1796 and took office in March 1797. It was the first contested presidential succession after Washington. Adams inherited Hamilton's institutional program, a partisan press, and a collapsing relationship with France. The French Directory treated Jay's Treaty as American betrayal and began seizing American merchant ships. Students should not still be in Washington's term when Sedition appears: XYZ and Sedition belong to the Adams years (1797-1801).

Thesis link: The republic now had to manage party conflict and foreign crisis without the first president as referee.

Source: U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian, "The XYZ Affair and the Quasi-War with France, 1798-1800." Live source

The XYZ Affair Made Federalist Fear of France Plausible

Ships seized→XYZ demands→Quasi-War
French agents demanded bribes and a loan; diplomacy collapsed into an undeclared naval conflict.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_XYZ_Affair_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 2-3 minutes for XYZ plus Quasi-War. Keep it light enough that Sedition does not feel random.

Story: Adams sent envoys to calm relations with France. In 1797-1798 French agents (later labeled X, Y, and Z in published dispatches) demanded a bribe and a loan before talks would begin. Americans refused; when the dispatches were published, public outrage swung toward the Federalists ("Millions for defense, but not one cent for tribute"). An undeclared naval Quasi-War with France followed (about 1798-1800): American and French warships and privateers fought at sea while Congress expanded the navy and authorized limited hostilities.

Thesis link: French seizures, XYZ, and Quasi-War made Federalist emergency measures feel plausible even if those measures later looked repressive. Fear of France was not invented from nothing.

The Sedition Act Turned Criticism of Government Into a Federal Crime

It became a federal crime to publish certain "false, scandalous and malicious" writings against the government.
Sedition Act, 1798
Federalists said malicious falsehoods could aid an enemy. Republicans said the governing party was criminalizing criticism.

Timing: About 2 minutes. This is the core law for the formal lecture.

Story: In 1798, during the Quasi-War panic, Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts. For this lecture focus on the Sedition Act: it made it a federal crime to publish "false, scandalous and malicious" writings against the government, Congress, or the president. Federalists said malicious falsehoods could aid an enemy in wartime. Republicans said the governing party was criminalizing criticism and protecting Adams from a free press. Federal prosecutors targeted opposition newspaper editors and politicians; convictions followed in several high-profile cases.

Enrichment note: Mention the broader Alien package briefly, but save detailed immigration and naturalization provisions for optional enrichment.

Thesis link: The republic now faced the lecture question in legal form: is organized opposition loyal citizenship or punishable disloyalty?

Source: National Archives, Alien and Sedition Acts (includes Sedition Act text). Live source

Matthew Lyon's Reelection Showed Voters Could Defy Repression

1798

Republican congressman convicted under the Sedition Act.

4

Months in jail.

Wins

Re-elected while imprisoned.

1801

Helps decide Jefferson's election in the House.

Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Seditions_Lyon_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5-2 minutes. Reveal only after Activity 2.

Story: Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was convicted in 1798 under the Sedition Act for published attacks on President Adams. He served about four months in jail and still won reelection while imprisoned. Voters treated the prosecution as partisan repression rather than necessary wartime discipline. Later, in the House contest after the 1800 electoral tie, Lyon cast the deciding vote for his state delegation that helped make Jefferson president.

Thesis link: Human-scale proof that opposition could survive criminalization: the government prosecuted; the electorate answered; the same opposition politician later helped transfer national power.

Republican Resistance Defended Liberty and Created a New Constitutional Problem

Madison's Virginia

States should "interpose" against alarming federal violations.

Jefferson's Kentucky

Federal acts beyond delegated powers could be treated as void.

If the federal government violates liberty, who gets to decide that it has gone too far?

Timing: About 1.5-2 minutes. Keep introductory; this is not a full nullification lecture.

Story: In late 1798, Republican-led legislatures answered the Alien and Sedition Acts with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. Madison's Virginia Resolutions argued that states should "interpose" to protect against alarming federal violations of the compact. Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions (he drafted them; the legislature adopted a version) argued that federal acts beyond delegated powers could be treated as void. Few other states joined; Federalist legislatures condemned the idea. The immediate political effect was protest and party mobilization more than successful nullification.

Thesis link: Republican resistance defended liberty against Sedition, but also opened an unresolved problem: if the federal government violates the Constitution, who decides, and how far may a state go?

Sources: Madison, Virginia Resolutions, Founders Online. Live source; Jefferson draft of Kentucky Resolutions. Live source

By 1800, Each Party Believed Defeat Could Destroy the Republic

Federalist warning

Jefferson meant French radicalism, irreligion, and social disorder.

Republican warning

Federalists meant monarchy, armies, taxes, and repression.

Could either side consent to lose when defeat looked like the end of liberty?

Timing: About 1 minute.

Story: By the campaign of 1800 each party had a coherent nightmare. Federalists warned that Jefferson meant French-style radicalism, irreligion, and social disorder. Republicans warned that Federalists meant monarchy, standing armies, heavy taxes, and Sedition-style repression. Newspapers amplified both stories nationwide.

Thesis link: The historical problem is loser consent: could either side accept defeat when defeat looked like the end of the Revolution itself? That is the emotional setup for the electoral crisis and the peaceful transfer that followed.

The Jefferson-Burr Tie Exposed a Constitution Not Designed for Parties

73-73
Thirty-five House ballots failed. The thirty-sixth elected Jefferson.
The choice rested with state delegations in the outgoing Federalist House.

Timing: About 1.5 minutes. Avoid excessive electoral mechanics.

Story: Under the original Constitution, electors did not vote separately for president and vice president. Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr each received 73 electoral votes, tying for the presidency. The contest went to the outgoing House of Representatives, which voted by state delegation. Thirty-five ballots failed. On the thirty-sixth ballot in February 1801 the deadlock broke and Jefferson was elected. Hamilton's opposition to Burr influenced some Federalists, but keep that as a brief aside, not a subplot. Three-Fifths implications stay in optional enrichment.

Thesis link: The Constitution had not been designed for modern party tickets; party politics exposed a mechanical flaw that nearly produced a constitutional crisis.

Source: U.S. House History, "Hamilton and the House." Live source

The Election of 1800 Established That Opposition Could Become Government

"We are all republicans: we are all federalists."
Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, 1801
The losing party left office. The winning opposition took power. The defeated party remained legal.
Image to comeHIST101_Ch7_Lec2_Slide_Election_1800_revised_FA26.png

Timing: About 1.5-2 minutes.

Story: On 4 March 1801 Jefferson took the oath; Adams left office. In his First Inaugural Jefferson said, "We are all republicans: we are all federalists." Clarify that the line did not erase ideological conflict. The durable precedent was narrower: an organized opposition could criticize, win an election, and take control of the national government while the losing party remained legal and left office without the constitutional system collapsing.

Thesis link: That is the lecture's answer in practice: the Revolution's contested republic learned, under crisis, how loyal opposition could become government.

Hamilton's Credit System Raised a Fairness Question

Optional enrichment
Credibility and fairness did not point to the same answer.
  • Soldiers and suppliers had often sold government certificates cheaply during hard times.
  • Speculators frequently held those certificates when federal repayment plans were adopted.
  • Madison emphasized original holders; Hamilton emphasized reliable market rules.
Big idea: state-building distributed benefits and burdens, not just institutional capacity.

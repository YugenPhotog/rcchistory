HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 4 • 1803–1815

Why Did a Republic That Feared War Choose War?

Sailors, prophets, and the War of 1812

Background: Thomas Birch, Engagement Between the Constitution and the Guerriere, 1813. Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. Public domain.

Look at the painting behind the title. It is August 1812, in the North Atlantic. The American frigate Constitution has just shot away every mast on the British frigate Guerriere. Thomas Birch painted it within a year. They had been told for twenty years that their republic could not stand up to Britain. Here, on canvas, it did.

But hold that picture against what the men who led this country believed. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison built their party on a fear: that war was the one thing most likely to kill a republic. War meant armies, debt, taxes, and presidents with too much power. They had spent the 1790s accusing Hamilton of building exactly that.

So today has one question, and it is on the screen. Why did a republic that feared war choose war in 1812? And what did that war do to the country that came out the other side in 1815? This is the last lecture of Chapter 7, and it closes the story that started with Washington taking an oath on a balcony in 1789.

[Key: The War of 1812 came after five years of trying every other option.]

Cold open · off Cape Henry, Virginia · 22 June 1807

A British Warship Fired Into the USS Chesapeake and Took Four of Her Sailors

The demand: HMS Leopard hailed the Chesapeake and asked to search for British deserters.
The attack: Commodore Barron refused. The Leopard fired broadsides into a ship not ready to fight.
The cost: Three Americans killed, eighteen wounded. The Chesapeake fired one gun, then surrendered.
The seizure: British officers took four sailors. Three were Americans.
Americans were furious. Jefferson could have had a war that summer. He chose not to.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, “Chesapeake-Leopard Affair”; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815.”

It is a calm June morning in 1807, just off the Virginia capes. The frigate Chesapeake is heading out for the Mediterranean. Her decks are cluttered with barrels, cables, and furniture; her guns are not ready. A British warship, HMS Leopard, comes alongside. A lieutenant rows over with a paper: orders to search the American ship for deserters from the Royal Navy.

Commodore James Barron says no. The Leopard answers with broadsides at close range. Splinters fly across the deck. Three men die and eighteen are wounded, Barron among them. The Americans manage to fire exactly one gun, reportedly lit with a hot coal carried up from the galley, and then strike their flag.

British officers board and take four men. One, Jenkin Ratford, really was a British deserter; he was hanged in Halifax that August. The other three, William Ware, Daniel Martin, and John Strachan, were Americans. Martin was a Black sailor from Massachusetts. Jefferson wrote that he had not seen the country so angry since Lexington. Yet war did not come for five more years. Why not, and then why yes? That is today’s story.

[Key: The Chesapeake turned a quiet grievance into a national insult.]

The Republican fear · 1795–1807

Jefferson’s Republicans Believed War Was How Republics Died

Their creed: Armies, debt, and taxes made rulers strong and citizens weak.
In power after 1801: Jefferson shrank the army and navy and paid down the national debt.
The catch: A small, cheap republic had few tools when Britain or France pushed.
“Of all the enemies to public liberty war is, perhaps, the most to be dreaded.”
James Madison, “Political Observations,” 1795

Sources: Madison, “Political Observations,” 20 April 1795, Founders Online; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815.”

To feel the weight of that choice in 1807, you have to know what Jefferson’s party believed. In 1795 James Madison, the quiet Virginian in the portrait, wrote that of all the enemies of liberty, war was perhaps the most to be dreaded. War, he said, is the parent of armies, and from armies come debts and taxes, the tools that put the many under the rule of the few.

Remember Lecture 2. That was the Republican case against Hamilton: a standing army, a national debt, a strong executive, all copied from Britain. When Jefferson won in 1800, he set out to undo it. His Treasury secretary, Albert Gallatin, paid down the debt. The army was cut to a few thousand men. The navy was trimmed, and Jefferson put his faith in small coastal gunboats.

This was not cowardice. It was a theory of how to keep a republic free. But it left a problem. A cheap, small republic had very little to push back with when the two greatest powers on earth started pushing on it.

[Key: For Republicans, choosing war meant risking the very things they had warned against.]

The sailor’s war · Atlantic Ocean · 1803–1812

The Royal Navy Took Thousands of Sailors Off American Ships

The pressure: Britain fought Napoleon from 1803 to 1815 and needed every sailor it could find.
The practice: Boarding officers pulled men off American ships, claiming they were British.
The count: U.S. officials recorded more than 6,000 Americans taken this way.
The insult: Britain held “once an Englishman, always an Englishman.”

Impressment: forcing men into naval service. Britain used it on its own subjects and claimed the right to search neutral ships for them.

Sources: Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815”; Paul A. Gilje, Free Trade and Sailors’ Rights in the War of 1812 (2013).

The Chesapeake was not a one-time accident. It was the most famous case of something that happened all the time. Britain was locked in a war with Napoleon’s France that would run from 1803 to 1815. Its navy was its lifeline, and its ships were always short of men. Life aboard a British warship was brutal, and sailors deserted by the thousands, often to American merchant ships that paid better.

So British captains stopped American ships at sea and lined up the crew. An officer looked each man over and listened to his accent. If he decided a sailor was British, that sailor went into the Royal Navy, sometimes for years. Many of those taken were American-born. U.S. officials counted more than 6,000 cases before 1812. American sailors carried paper “protection certificates” to prove citizenship. Boarding officers often ignored them.

Britain’s rule was simple: once an Englishman, always an Englishman. Americans answered that a free person could choose a new country. That was the whole Revolution in one sentence. So impressment was not just a trade dispute. It asked whether American citizenship meant anything at sea.

[Key: Impressment made national sovereignty personal.]

You’re Jefferson, Summer 1807. What Do You Do?

Activity 1 • Predict the consequence
The Leopard has fired on the Chesapeake. The country wants revenge. Your army and navy are small.
A. Ask Congress to declare war now.
B. Close U.S. ports to British warships and demand an apology.
C. Stop all American trade abroad to squeeze Britain.
D. Keep negotiating and wait.
Write two sentences: Pick one option. Predict one consequence it would have for the republic, at sea or at home.

Stop here and put yourself in Jefferson’s chair. It is July 1807. Newspapers want blood. But you have an army of a few thousand men, a navy of a few frigates and some gunboats, and a party that has spent ten years saying war is the death of liberty.

You have four choices on the screen. War now could be glorious, or it could be a disaster against the world’s strongest navy. Closing the ports and demanding an apology sounds firm, but Britain might simply ignore it. Stopping all trade might hurt Britain, but it would also hurt every American farmer and merchant who sells abroad. Waiting keeps the peace but looks weak.

Your job is not to guess what Jefferson did. Your job is to predict what your choice would do to the country. Pick one, and write two sentences about its consequence. I will give you about four minutes, and then I will tell you what he actually chose.

Reveal after: Jefferson did B first, a proclamation in July 1807 closing American waters to British warships, and then C in December, the Embargo. The next slide shows how it went.

Washington · 22 December 1807

Jefferson Answered With the Embargo: No American Ship Would Sail for Foreign Ports

The plan: “Peaceable coercion.” Starve Britain and France of American goods until they respected American rights.
The bet: Europe needed American grain and cotton more than America needed Europe.
The result: U.S. exports fell from about $108 million in 1807 to $22 million in 1808.
Read the turtle’s name backward: O-grab-me is “Embargo.”

Sources: Office of the Historian, “Embargo of 1807”; Douglas A. Irwin, “The Welfare Cost of Autarky: Evidence from the Jeffersonian Trade Embargo, 1807–09” (2005).

Jefferson’s answer came just before Christmas, on December 22, 1807. Congress passed the Embargo Act. From now on, no American ship could sail for a foreign port. Jefferson called it peaceable coercion. The idea was elegant: Britain and France both needed American food, cotton, and lumber. Cut them off, and they would have to respect American ships and sailors. A republic could win without an army, a war debt, or a single battle.

It did not work that way. Britain found other suppliers, in South America and in its own empire. Napoleon even claimed he was helping enforce the embargo by seizing American ships in his ports. Meanwhile American exports collapsed, from about 108 million dollars in 1807 to 22 million the next year.

Look at the cartoon. Alexander Anderson drew a snapping turtle clamping onto a smuggler as he tries to sneak a barrel to a British ship. The turtle’s name is Ograbme. Read it backward. That was the Federalist joke: the embargo did not bite Britain. It bit Americans.

[Key: Peaceable coercion hurt Americans more than it hurt Britain.]

New England ports · 1808–1809

Peaceable Coercion Became Coercion at Home

Idle ports: Ships sat at the wharves. Sailors and dockworkers lost their jobs.
Smugglers: Goods slipped into Canada by wagon and across Lake Champlain.
Enforcement: The 1809 Enforcement Act let federal officers seize cargoes on suspicion.
Retreat: Congress repealed the Embargo in March 1809, days before Jefferson left office.
To avoid a war abroad, a party that feared federal power used more of it at home.

Sources: Office of the Historian, “Embargo of 1807”; Gautham Rao, National Duties: Custom Houses and the Making of the American State (2016).

Picture Salem or New Haven in the winter of 1808. The masts in the harbor are bare. Sails have been stripped and stored. Sailors walk the docks with nothing to do, and merchants watch their ships sit and rot.

So people cheated. Wagons rolled north into Canada loaded with potash and flour. Rafts crossed Lake Champlain at night. On the Vermont border there was real shooting between smugglers and federal men. In 1808 Jefferson declared the Lake Champlain region to be in a state of insurrection, a remarkable thing for him of all people to say.

Then, in January 1809, Congress passed the Enforcement Act. Customs collectors could seize cargoes on suspicion and call on the army and navy to help. Think about who is doing this. The party that warned against Hamilton’s federal tax collectors in Lecture 1 was now using federal officers to stop Americans from selling their own goods.

It could not last. New England Federalists were furious, and even Republicans had had enough. Congress repealed the Embargo in March 1809, days before Jefferson left office.

[Key: The Embargo failed abroad and made federal power felt at home.]

President Madison · 1809–1811

Madison Tried Every Lever Short of War, and Each One Broke

1809: Non-Intercourse Act. Trade with everyone except Britain and France. Smugglers ignored it.
1809: A British envoy promised a deal. London rejected it. Madison looked foolish.
1810: Macon’s Bill No. 2. Whichever side dropped its rules first would get American trade.
1810–1811: Napoleon pretended to drop his. Madison cut off Britain again.
By 1811 the doors short of war had closed, one after another.

Sources: Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815”; Miller Center, “James Madison: Foreign Affairs.”

In March 1809 James Madison took the oath as president. He inherited the same problem with a smaller toolbox. First came the Non-Intercourse Act. Americans could trade with anyone in the world except Britain and France. It leaked like a sieve; goods simply went through third countries.

Then a moment of hope. In April 1809 the British minister in Washington, David Erskine, promised that Britain would drop its trade restrictions. Madison reopened trade, and hundreds of ships sailed. But Erskine had gone beyond his instructions, and London rejected the deal. Madison had to shut the door again.

In 1810 Congress tried a gamble called Macon’s Bill Number 2. Trade would reopen with both powers, and whichever one dropped its rules first would get America’s business, while the other got cut off. Napoleon saw the opening. His foreign minister sent a letter suggesting France would lift its decrees. France kept seizing American ships anyway, but Madison took the promise and cut off trade with Britain in early 1811.

You do not need to memorize these laws. Count the closed doors instead. Negotiation, embargo, non-intercourse, a bargain. Each one failed.

[Key: By 1811, avoiding war no longer looked like defending American rights.]

Prophetstown · the Wabash River, Indiana · 1805–1808

In the West, a Shawnee Prophet Called Native Peoples to Turn Away From American Ways

The vision: In 1805 a Shawnee man named Lalawethika fell into a trance and woke as Tenskwatawa, “the Open Door.”
The message: Give up whiskey, American goods, and land sales. Return to the Creator’s ways.
The town: In 1808 his followers built Prophetstown where the Tippecanoe meets the Wabash.
This movement grew from Native goals, not British orders.

Sources: National Park Service, “Tenskwatawa”; Adam Jortner, The Gods of Prophetstown (2012).

Now leave the Atlantic and travel west, to the Ohio Valley, where a second crisis was growing at the same time. Remember from Lecture 3 how much land Native nations had already lost after Fallen Timbers and the Treaty of Greenville. Many Shawnee villages were poor, sick, and full of trade whiskey.

In 1805 a Shawnee man named Lalawethika, known mostly as a heavy drinker and a failed healer, fell into a trance so deep his family thought he had died. When he woke, he said he had seen the Master of Life. He took a new name, Tenskwatawa, “the Open Door,” and a new message. Native people must stop drinking, stop depending on American goods, stop selling land, and return to the Creator’s ways. Americans, he taught, were not like the British or French. They came from an evil spirit.

Thousands listened. In 1808 his followers built a town where the Tippecanoe River meets the Wabash, in what is now Indiana. Americans called it Prophetstown. Look at George Catlin’s portrait. Catlin painted it in 1830, when Tenskwatawa was old and his movement long gone.

[Key: Native resistance began as a religious revival with its own goals.]

Vincennes, Indiana Territory · August 1810

Tecumseh Told Governor Harrison That No One Nation Could Sell the Land

The sale: In 1809 Governor William Henry Harrison bought about 3 million acres at Fort Wayne from a few chiefs.
The answer: Tecumseh, the Prophet’s older brother, said the land belonged to all the tribes together.
The plan: He traveled from the Great Lakes to the Creek country to build one confederacy.
“Sell a country! Why not sell the air, the clouds and the great sea, as well as the earth?”
Attributed to Tecumseh, 1810, recorded years later

Sources: National Park Service, “Tecumseh”; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815”; Treaty of Fort Wayne, 1809.

Tenskwatawa had the visions. His older brother Tecumseh had the plan. In September 1809, Indiana’s governor, William Henry Harrison, met a few Miami, Delaware, and Potawatomi chiefs at Fort Wayne and bought about three million acres for cash and yearly payments. The Shawnee were not at the table.

In August 1810 Tecumseh came to Harrison’s capital at Vincennes with several hundred warriors. Their meeting nearly turned violent; men on both sides reached for weapons. Tecumseh’s argument was simple and radical. The land belonged to all the Native nations together, the way air and water do. No single chief could sell it. The famous line on the slide comes to us from accounts written down years later, so treat the exact words with caution. The idea behind it is well documented.

Then Tecumseh went on the road. He traveled from the Great Lakes south to the Creek towns in Alabama, urging nations that had often fought each other to stand as one. The portrait, by the way, is not from life. No confirmed likeness of Tecumseh survives.

[Key: Tecumseh turned a religious revival into a political and military confederacy.]

Prophetstown · 7 November 1811

While Tecumseh Was Away, Harrison Marched on Prophetstown and Burned It

The march: Harrison led about 1,000 soldiers and militia to camp near the town.
The battle: Before dawn, the Prophet’s warriors attacked. About 62 Americans died. Native losses are unknown.
The fire: The next day Harrison’s men burned Prophetstown and its winter food.
The blame: Westerners said they found British-made guns and blamed Britain.
After Tippecanoe, many Americans saw the war at sea and the war in the West as one British war.

Sources: National Park Service, “Tippecanoe Battlefield”; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815.”

In the fall of 1811 Tecumseh was far to the south, visiting the Creeks. He had told his brother not to start a fight. Harrison knew Tecumseh was gone. He marched up the Wabash with about a thousand regular soldiers and Indiana and Kentucky militia and camped within sight of Prophetstown.

Before dawn on November 7, warriors from the town attacked the American camp in the dark and the drizzle. It was a hard, close fight. About sixty-two Americans were killed or died of wounds. We do not know how many Native fighters died. When the warriors pulled back, the people of Prophetstown fled. The next day Harrison’s men burned the town, including the corn its families needed to get through the winter.

Harrison called it a great victory. In truth it was a costly fight that scattered the Prophet’s followers for a while but made many of them angrier. And it changed how the West read the news. Settlers claimed they found British-made muskets and powder. To a Kentucky voter, it now looked like one enemy, Britain, was choking American ships at sea and arming warriors on the frontier.

[Key: Tippecanoe tied the western crisis to the British crisis in American minds.]

The Twelfth Congress · Washington · November 1811

Young War Hawks Took Over Congress and Called Restraint Dishonor

Henry Clay of Kentucky, 34, was elected Speaker on his first day in the House.
Allies: John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, 29; Felix Grundy of Tennessee; Peter Porter of New York.
Their case: Britain insulted American ships and armed Native enemies. Honor demanded war.
The prize: Some hoped to seize Canada, as leverage or for keeps.
“Agrarian cupidity, not maritime right, urges the war.”
John Randolph of Virginia, opposing the War Hawks, December 1811

Agrarian cupidity: greed for farmland.

Sources: U.S. House of Representatives, History, Art & Archives, “Henry Clay”; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815.”

In November 1811, the same week as Tippecanoe, a new Congress gathered in Washington. Many of its members were young, from the South and the West, and they had grown up on stories of the Revolution rather than memories of it. On the very first day, the House elected a 34-year-old Kentuckian named Henry Clay as Speaker. He used the Speaker’s chair to put war men on the key committees.

Around him stood John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, just 29, Felix Grundy of Tennessee, and Peter Porter of western New York. Their opponents nicknamed them War Hawks. Their argument was about honor. A nation that let its sailors be kidnapped and its frontier be armed against it was not really independent. Some called it a second war for independence. Some also talked about taking Canada.

Not everyone bought it. John Randolph of Virginia, a sharp-tongued old Republican, said the real motive was greed for land: “Agrarian cupidity, not maritime right, urges the war.” Keep his charge in mind. You will test it in a few minutes.

[Key: The War Hawks did not create the crisis, but they made war politically possible.]

Washington · 1–18 June 1812

A Divided Congress Declared War on Britain in June 1812

79–49
House, 4 June
19–13
Senate, 17 June
1 June: Madison’s war message listed impressment, ship seizures, and frontier warfare.
The split: Not one Federalist voted for war. Most of New England said no.
The irony: On 16 June, Britain announced it would drop its trade orders. The news crossed the ocean too late.

Sources: Madison, War Message, 1 June 1812, Founders Online; U.S. House, “The House’s First Declaration of War”; U.S. Senate, “Declaration of War with Great Britain, 1812.”

On June 1, 1812, Madison sent Congress a written message. A clerk read it aloud behind closed doors. Madison listed the grievances you now know. British cruisers seizing American sailors. British orders seizing American cargoes. And on the frontier, warfare by Native nations that he blamed on British influence. He did not quite say the word war. He let Congress say it.

The House voted three days later, 79 to 49. The Senate took two weeks and split 19 to 13. It remains the closest vote on a formal declaration of war in American history. Every Federalist voted no. Most of New England voted no, even though New England owned most of the ships and had lost the most sailors. The yes votes came mostly from the South, the West, and Pennsylvania. Madison signed the declaration on June 18.

Now the bitter part. On June 16, two days earlier, the British government had announced it would suspend the Orders in Council, the trade rules Americans hated. There was no telegraph. By the time the news crossed the Atlantic, the war had begun, and impressment was still unsettled.

[Key: “America chose war” is too simple. A divided republic chose war.]

Two Crises or One?

Activity 2 • Weigh the causes
By 1812, Americans faced British pressure at sea and a Native confederacy in the West with British ties.
A. These were separate problems that War Hawk politicians merged to sell a war.
B. These were truly connected parts of one crisis over American independence.
Write two sentences: Which interpretation better explains why Congress chose war? Use one piece of evidence from today, such as Tippecanoe, Randolph’s charge, or the vote.

Here is your second activity, and it is the heart of today’s question. You have now heard two stories that ran side by side. One was at sea: impressment, the Chesapeake, the Embargo, and Madison’s failed bargains. The other was in the West: Tenskwatawa, Tecumseh, the Fort Wayne sale, and the burning of Prophetstown.

Historians still argue about how they fit together. One reading says these were separate problems, and ambitious southern and western politicians stitched them together because a war against Britain could also clear the frontier and maybe take Canada. That is roughly John Randolph’s charge. The other reading says they really were one crisis. Britain’s navy and Britain’s forts in Canada were two arms of the same empire, and both told Americans their independence was only partial.

Here is a clue to weigh. The coastal states that lost the most sailors mostly voted against war. The southern and western states, far from the big ports, voted for it. Does that help A or B? Pick one interpretation and back it with one piece of evidence from today. Either answer can be good if the evidence fits. About four minutes.

The war in brief · 1812–1813

The Invasion of Canada Failed, but Americans Won Lake Erie

Detroit, August 1812: General William Hull surrendered his whole army to the British and Tecumseh without a real fight.
At sea: The Constitution beat the Guerriere. Americans called her “Old Ironsides.”
Lake Erie, September 1813: Oliver Hazard Perry: “We have met the enemy and they are ours.”
The war that was supposed to be quick turned into a long, uneven fight.

Pierian Spring: the war at sea and on the Lakes

Sources: U.S. Army Center of Military History, “War of 1812 Campaigns”; Naval History and Heritage Command, “Battle of Lake Erie.”

Thomas Jefferson predicted that taking Canada would be “a mere matter of marching.” It was not. In July 1812, General William Hull, an aging Revolutionary veteran, crossed from Detroit into Canada, lost his nerve, and pulled back. The British general Isaac Brock and Tecumseh, now fighting beside the British, bluffed him with a show of force. On August 16 Hull surrendered Detroit and his entire army almost without firing a shot. Other invasions fizzled that fall.

The good news came from the sea. Three days after Detroit fell, the frigate Constitution wrecked HMS Guerriere, the painting from our title slide. Sailors said British cannonballs bounced off her oak sides, and she became Old Ironsides.

The turning point in the West came on Lake Erie in September 1813. Oliver Hazard Perry, just 28, fought until his flagship was a wreck, then rowed through the gunfire to another ship and won. He sent Harrison a note: “We have met the enemy and they are ours.” Lake Erie was American, and the British around Detroit had to retreat.

[Skip detail if behind · Key: The war began with failure; Lake Erie turned the West.]

The Thames, Upper Canada · Oct. 1813 · Horseshoe Bend, Alabama · March 1814

Tecumseh Died at the Thames, and His Dream of a Confederacy Died With Him

The Thames: Harrison caught the retreating British and Tecumseh in Upper Canada. Tecumseh was killed.
The South: A Creek civil war ended when Andrew Jackson crushed the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend.
Fort Jackson, 1814: Jackson forced the Creeks, even his Creek allies, to give up about 23 million acres.
Native resistance did not end. But the great confederacy never rose again.

Pierian Spring: the Creek War

Sources: National Park Service, “Indigenous Peoples and the War of 1812”; NPS, Horseshoe Bend National Military Park; Treaty of Fort Jackson, 1814.

After Perry won Lake Erie, the British commander in the West, Henry Procter, retreated east into Canada. Tecumseh begged him to stand and fight. On October 5, 1813, at the Thames River near Moraviantown, Harrison’s army caught them. The British line broke almost at once. Tecumseh’s warriors held on in a swamp until Tecumseh fell. Nobody knows for sure who killed him. Colonel Richard Mentor Johnson of Kentucky later rode the claim all the way to the vice presidency. Look at the print. Nathaniel Currier made it in 1846, more than thirty years later, and it even gets the date wrong. It shows what Americans wanted to remember, not what anyone saw.

In the South, the war merged with a civil war among the Creeks. A faction called the Red Sticks, inspired partly by Tecumseh’s visit, fought Creeks who wanted peace with the Americans. Andrew Jackson’s army, with Cherokee and Creek allies, destroyed the Red Sticks at Horseshoe Bend in March 1814. About 800 died. Then at Fort Jackson that August, Jackson took about 23 million acres in Alabama and Georgia, including land from Creeks who had fought on his side.

[Key: The war broke Native military power east of the Mississippi.]

Washington, D.C. · 24–25 August 1814

British Troops Burned the Capitol and the President’s House

Bladensburg: American militia broke and ran. Officials fled the capital.

That night: British officers ate the president’s dinner, then set the house on fire.

Rescue: Dolley Madison, the enslaved teenager Paul Jennings, and other staff saved Washington’s portrait.

The Taking of the City of Washington in America, wood engraving, London, 1814. A British print celebrating the raid. Library of Congress; public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

By the summer of 1814 the war had turned dangerous. Napoleon had been defeated in Europe that spring, and Britain could finally send veteran troops across the Atlantic. In August, about 4,500 British soldiers landed in Maryland and marched on Washington. At Bladensburg, on August 24, the American militia broke and ran so fast that people mocked it as the Bladensburg Races.

In the President’s House, Dolley Madison was waiting for her husband with dinner ready. Paul Jennings, a fifteen-year-old enslaved by the Madisons, had helped set the table. When the order came to flee, the staff broke the frame of Gilbert Stuart’s portrait of George Washington and sent the canvas off to safety. Dolley got the credit in the newspapers. Decades later Jennings, by then free, wrote that the doorkeeper and the gardener did the work. Notice how the story we remember can leave people out.

That night British officers ate the president’s dinner, then piled up the furniture and set the house ablaze. They burned the Capitol, too, with the Library of Congress inside it. The print on the screen was made in London to celebrate.

[Key: In 1814 the republic that feared war watched its own capital burn.]

Baltimore harbor · 13–14 September 1814

Three Weeks Later, Baltimore Held, and Francis Scott Key Wrote a Poem

The bombardment: British ships fired on Fort McHenry for about 25 hours, through a rainy night.
Dawn: A huge American flag still flew over the fort. The British withdrew.
The poem: Key, held on a ship during the attack, wrote “Defence of Fort M’Henry.”
Later: Set to music, it became the national anthem in 1931.
A defense, not a conquest, gave the war its most lasting symbol.

Pierian Spring: the anthem’s third verse

Sources: National Park Service, Fort McHenry National Monument; Smithsonian National Museum of American History, “The Star-Spangled Banner.”

After Washington, the British turned to Baltimore, one of the largest cities in the country and home to many of the privateers that had been raiding British shipping. This time the Americans were ready. Citizens had dug earthworks for weeks.

On September 13, British warships began firing shells and rockets at Fort McHenry, which guarded the harbor. The bombardment went on through a rainy night, about 25 hours in all. Francis Scott Key, a Washington lawyer, watched from a ship where the British were holding him until the attack was over. He had come to win the release of a captured doctor. All night he could see the flashes and hear the shells. At dawn on the 14th, through the smoke, a giant flag was flying over the fort. It was 30 by 42 feet, sewn by Mary Pickersgill and the women and girls of her household. The British gave up and sailed away.

Key wrote a poem about that morning. Set to a popular tune, it became “The Star-Spangled Banner.” Congress made it the national anthem in 1931, more than a century later.

[Key: Fort McHenry turned survival into a national symbol.]

Ghent · 24 December 1814 · New Orleans · 8 January 1815

Peace Was Signed at Ghent Two Weeks Before the War’s Most Famous Battle

Ghent: The treaty restored the prewar borders. It did not mention impressment.
New Orleans: Andrew Jackson’s mixed army of regulars, militia, free Black soldiers, Choctaws, and pirates held a line of earthworks.
The cost: About 2,000 British casualties. About 70 American.
The catch: No one in America knew the treaty had already been signed.
The battle did not win the war. It won the memory of the war.

Sources: Office of the Historian, “Treaty of Ghent”; National Park Service, Chalmette Battlefield; Naval History and Heritage Command, “The Battle of New Orleans.”

By late 1814 both sides were tired. American and British diplomats had been talking in the city of Ghent, in modern Belgium, since August. On Christmas Eve, 1814, they signed a treaty. The borders went back to the way they were before the war. The treaty did not even mention impressment. With Napoleon beaten, Britain no longer needed to seize sailors anyway.

But news took weeks to cross the ocean. On January 8, 1815, a British army attacked Andrew Jackson’s lines below New Orleans. Jackson had put together one of the most mixed armies in American history: regulars, Tennessee and Kentucky riflemen, two battalions of free men of color, Choctaw warriors, and Jean Lafitte’s Baratarian pirates. The British marched across open ground into the guns. In under an hour they lost about 2,000 men killed, wounded, or captured. The Americans lost about 70.

The painting is by Laclotte, an engineer who was there. The battle changed nothing in the treaty. It changed how Americans remembered the whole war, and it made Jackson famous.

[Key: The war ended in a draw on paper and a victory in memory.]

Hartford, Connecticut · December 1814–January 1815

New England Federalists Protested the War Just as Peace and Victory Arrived

The meeting: 26 delegates from New England met in secret to protest “Mr. Madison’s War.”
The demands: Constitutional amendments, like a two-thirds vote in Congress to declare war.
The timing: Their messengers reached Washington with the news of New Orleans and Ghent.
The result: Republicans called them traitors. The Federalist Party faded away.

Pierian Spring: what the Hartford Convention actually proposed

Sources: National Park Service, “The Influence of Factious Leaders”; Report and Resolutions of the Hartford Convention, 1815.

New England had hated this war from the start. Its merchants traded with Britain, its Federalist leaders thought Madison had blundered into it, and some New England governors refused to send their militia. Trade with the enemy was common. In December 1814, twenty-six delegates from Massachusetts, Connecticut, Rhode Island, and parts of New Hampshire and Vermont met behind closed doors in Hartford.

Some hotheads talked about leaving the Union, but the moderates won. The final report asked for constitutional amendments: a two-thirds vote of Congress to declare war or to admit new states, limits on embargoes, and an end to the three-fifths clause that gave the South extra seats. Remember Lecture 2. This is the same question about where opposition ends and disloyalty begins.

Then came terrible timing. The convention’s messengers arrived in Washington just as the news of Jackson’s victory and the peace treaty arrived. The country was celebrating. The Federalists looked like men who had bet against their own nation. The cartoon mocks them as ready to leap into King George’s arms. The party ran its last presidential candidate in 1816.

[Key: Hartford’s timing helped finish the Federalist Party.]

After the war · 1815–1816

The War Changed Almost Nothing on the Map and a Great Deal Inside North America

Nationalism: Americans came out proud, convinced they had won a second war of independence.
Native nations: Britain stopped backing them. Settlers poured onto their lands.
Parties: The Federalists collapsed. Republicans ran the country almost alone.
A turn: Madison now asked for a national bank, a tariff, and a stronger army and navy.
“The people … are more American; they feel and act more as a nation.”
Albert Gallatin, Treasury secretary and peace negotiator, 1816

Sources: Gallatin to Matthew Lyon, 7 May 1816; Madison, Seventh Annual Message, 5 December 1815, Founders Online; NPS, “Indigenous Peoples and the War of 1812.”

So what changed? On the map, almost nothing. No territory changed hands for good, and no treaty article stopped impressment. But inside North America, a great deal changed.

First, the people who lost the most were the ones who were not at Ghent. British diplomats had once demanded a Native buffer state in the Ohio country. They dropped it. With Tecumseh dead and the Creeks stripped of their land, there was no longer a Native military power east of the Mississippi that could stop American settlement. Former Creek land became cotton country worked by enslaved people.

Second, Americans came out proud. Albert Gallatin wrote in 1816 that the war had renewed the national feeling of the Revolution, that the people were “more American” and acted “more as a nation.”

Third, the Federalists were finished, and the Republicans changed. In December 1815, James Madison, the same man who wrote that war was liberty’s most dreaded enemy, asked Congress for a national bank, a protective tariff, and a stronger peacetime army and navy. Congress chartered a Second Bank of the United States in 1816. Hamilton would have smiled.

[Key: The war strengthened the nation, destroyed a party, and broke Native resistance.]

Boston harbor, July 1812 · and the end of Chapter 7

The Republic That Feared War Survived One and Came Out More National Than Its Founders Planned

1789: Washington wondered whether the Constitution could work at all.
1801: Power passed peacefully from one party to its rivals.
1815: The republic had fought the world’s strongest empire and survived.
The price: Native nations paid for that survival with their land.
Did the War of 1812 complete the Revolution, or turn the republic into what Republicans once feared?

Sources: Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815”; Naval History and Heritage Command, “Chesapeake-Leopard Affair.”

Let’s go back to where we started. Remember the sailors taken from the Chesapeake? Britain finally agreed to send the last two, Daniel Martin and John Strachan, home. In July 1812, five years after they were dragged off that deck, a British schooner delivered them to Boston. By then the two countries were already at war.

Now step back to the whole chapter. In 1789 Washington took an oath and nobody knew whether the Constitution would work. By 1801 one party handed power to its rivals. By 1803 the republic had doubled its size. And by 1815 it had fought the most powerful empire on earth to a draw and come out prouder and more united.

But it was a different republic. The party that feared armies, debt, and a strong central government ended the war asking for a bank and a bigger military. And the price of a secure, expanding nation was paid in Native land. So here is the question to carry out of the room: did the War of 1812 complete the Revolution, or change it?

[Key: The republic survived, but surviving made it more national, more expansionist, and less afraid of power.]

Independent study · Why Britain and not France?

France Seized American Ships Too, but Britain Was the Bigger Threat

Both empires: Britain’s Orders in Council and Napoleon’s Berlin and Milan Decrees (1806–1807) both led to seized neutral ships.
Britain’s reach: Its navy ruled the ocean, it impressed sailors, and it sat next door in Canada.
Party memory: Republicans had distrusted Britain since the Revolution and the Jay Treaty.
Timing: Britain suspended its Orders on 16 June 1812, two days before Madison signed the declaration.
A cause is not just an injury. It is injury plus power, geography, politics, and timing.

Sources: Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815”; J.C.A. Stagg, The War of 1812: Conflict for a Continent (2012).

Students often ask a fair question. If France also seized American ships, why fight Britain? In 1806 and 1807 Napoleon issued the Berlin and Milan Decrees, which said ships that traded with Britain or obeyed British rules could be seized. Britain’s Orders in Council said, in effect, the reverse. American merchants were caught in the middle, and both empires took hundreds of ships.

But Britain could hurt the United States in ways France could not. The Royal Navy ruled the Atlantic, so its seizures happened right off American harbors. Only Britain impressed American sailors. Only Britain had a long land border with the United States, through Canada, and close ties to Native nations in the West. And Republicans already saw Britain as the old enemy, the monarchy whose system Hamilton had copied.

The timing still stings. Britain suspended its Orders in Council in June 1812, partly because of pressure from its own merchants hurt by lost American trade. Had there been a telegraph, the war might have been avoided. Connection to the lecture: this explains why the closing doors on slides 6 through 8 pointed at Britain in particular.

Independent study · The Little Belt affair · 16 May 1811

In 1811 an American Frigate Battered a Small British Warship, and Each Side Blamed the Other

The clash: USS President met HMS Little Belt off the Virginia capes after dark.
The damage: The much larger American ship killed 9 and wounded 23 British sailors.
The meaning: Americans cheered it as payback for the Chesapeake. Britain called it an outrage.
Escalation narrows choices even when no one can agree who fired first.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, “The Little Belt Affair.”

Four years after the Chesapeake, the roles were reversed. On the evening of May 16, 1811, the big American frigate President, commanded by John Rodgers, was looking for a British warship that had impressed a sailor near New York. In the dusk off the Virginia coast, Rodgers met a much smaller British sloop, the Little Belt. Hails were shouted, nobody was satisfied, and someone fired. Each captain swore the other shot first.

The President was far stronger. In a short fight it killed nine British sailors and wounded twenty-three. Many Americans celebrated it as revenge for 1807. In Britain, it looked like American aggression.

Connection to the lecture: by 1811 the crisis was no longer a simple story of Britain acting and America suffering. Tempers were raw on both sides, and every incident made the next step toward war easier.

Independent study · The war at sea and on the Lakes · 1812–1813

“Don’t Give Up the Ship” Began as a Dying Captain’s Order and Became Perry’s Battle Flag

1812: American frigates won several famous single-ship duels, including Constitution over Guerriere.
1 June 1813: HMS Shannon captured the Chesapeake off Boston. Captain James Lawrence was mortally wounded.
10 Sept. 1813: Perry named his Lake Erie flagship Lawrence and flew “DONT GIVE UP THE SHIP.”
The bigger picture: By 1814 the British blockade had bottled up most of the U.S. Navy.
Single-ship victories built pride. The blockade showed who really ruled the sea.

Sources: Naval History and Heritage Command, “Battle of Lake Erie” and “Chesapeake vs. Shannon.”

The tiny U.S. Navy surprised everyone in 1812. Its big frigates, the Constitution, the United States, and others, were heavier than the British frigates they met, and they won a string of one-on-one fights. Americans went wild.

Then on June 1, 1813, the Chesapeake, the same ship from 1807, sailed out of Boston to fight HMS Shannon. In about fifteen minutes the British swept her deck and boarded her. Her captain, James Lawrence, was carried below mortally wounded, calling, “Don’t give up the ship.” The ship was taken anyway.

Three months later on Lake Erie, Oliver Hazard Perry named his flagship the Lawrence and flew a blue flag stitched with those words. When the Lawrence was shot to pieces, Perry took the flag, rowed to the Niagara under fire, and won the battle. Meanwhile, though, the Royal Navy’s blockade slowly closed the American coast. Connection to the lecture: this is the detail behind slide 15.

Independent study · The Creek War · 1813–1814

The Creek War Was a Civil War Before It Was an American War

The split: Red Stick Creeks wanted to resist American ways; other Creeks sought peace and trade.
Fort Mims, Aug. 1813: Red Sticks killed about 250 people, including settlers, Creeks of mixed ancestry, and enslaved people.
Horseshoe Bend, March 1814: Jackson’s army and its Cherokee and Creek allies killed about 800 Red Sticks.
Fort Jackson, Aug. 1814: Jackson took about 23 million acres from friend and foe alike.
Allies and enemies lost land together. That lesson shaped Native politics for a generation.

Sources: National Park Service, Horseshoe Bend National Military Park; Treaty of Fort Jackson, 9 August 1814.

The Creek Nation, in what is now Alabama and Georgia, was already divided before the War of 1812. Some Creeks had taken up cattle, cotton, and even slaveholding, and worked with American agents. Others, inspired partly by Tecumseh’s 1811 visit and by Creek prophets, rejected those changes. They carried red-painted war clubs and became known as Red Sticks.

Fighting among Creeks began in 1813. In August, Red Sticks attacked Fort Mims, a stockade north of Mobile, and killed about 250 people inside. American newspapers called it a massacre, and Tennessee raised an army under Andrew Jackson.

At Horseshoe Bend on the Tallapoosa River, in March 1814, Jackson’s troops and their Cherokee and Creek allies broke the Red Stick fortifications. About 800 Red Sticks died. In the Treaty of Fort Jackson, Jackson demanded about 23 million acres, roughly half of Creek territory, even from Creeks who fought for him. Connection to the lecture: this is the detail behind slide 16.

Independent study · Paul Jennings · Washington, 1814 and 1865

An Enslaved Teenager Saw the Burning of Washington and Later Corrected the Record

1814: Paul Jennings, 15, enslaved by the Madisons, was in the President’s House when the British came.
1847: Jennings bought his freedom with help from Senator Daniel Webster.
1865: He published A Colored Man’s Reminiscences of James Madison, often called the first White House memoir.
Who tells the story shapes who becomes the hero.

Sources: Paul Jennings, A Colored Man’s Reminiscences of James Madison (1865); White House Historical Association, “Paul Jennings.”

Paul Jennings was born into slavery at Montpelier, the Madisons’ Virginia plantation, in 1799. As a boy he became James Madison’s footman and later his personal servant. He was fifteen on August 24, 1814, when word came that the British were close.

Years later, Jennings wrote that the popular story, in which Dolley Madison personally cut Washington’s portrait from its frame, was not true. He said the French doorkeeper, Jean Pierre Sioussat, and the gardener, Magraw, took it down and sent it off in a wagon. Dolley ordered it saved, but others did the work.

After James Madison died, Dolley sold Jennings. With help from Senator Daniel Webster, he bought his freedom in 1847. He is believed to have helped plan a large escape attempt by enslaved people in Washington in 1848. In 1865 he published his memoir. Connection to the lecture: this is a voice from below for slide 17.

Independent study · The anthem and the Colonial Marines

Key’s Anthem Had a Third Verse Many Read as Aimed at Enslaved Men Who Fought for Britain

The offer: In 1814 a British admiral promised freedom to enslaved people who came over to the British.
The Colonial Marines: Hundreds of formerly enslaved men fought in British uniforms in the Chesapeake.
Key’s third verse: “No refuge could save the hireling and slave / From the terror of flight or the gloom of the grave.”
After the war: Britain refused to return most of them. Many resettled in Nova Scotia and Trinidad.
For some Americans, Britain was the side that offered freedom.

Sources: Smithsonian National Museum of American History, “The Star-Spangled Banner”; National Park Service, “The Colonial Marines.”

Few people today know the third verse of “The Star-Spangled Banner,” and almost no one sings it. In it, Francis Scott Key, himself a slaveholder, wrote that “no refuge could save the hireling and slave / From the terror of flight or the gloom of the grave.”

Historians debate exactly whom he meant, but here is the background. In April 1814, British Admiral Alexander Cochrane announced that anyone who wished to leave the United States could join British forces or be settled as free people in British colonies. Thousands of enslaved people in Maryland and Virginia escaped to British ships. Several hundred men joined a unit called the Colonial Marines and fought in the Chesapeake campaign, including the march on Washington.

After the war, the United States demanded them back. Britain refused to return most of them and eventually paid compensation instead. Many settled in Nova Scotia and Trinidad. Connection to the lecture: for these Americans, the War of 1812 was a chance at a freedom the republic denied them.

Independent study · The Hartford Convention · 15 Dec. 1814–5 Jan. 1815

The Hartford Convention Proposed Seven Amendments to Protect New England

On war and trade: Two-thirds of both houses to declare war, ban trade, or admit new states. Embargoes capped at 60 days.
On power: End the three-fifths clause. One-term presidents. No two presidents in a row from the same state.
On newcomers: Bar naturalized citizens from Congress and federal office.
What failed: No amendment passed. Republicans used the meeting as proof of disloyalty.
Every proposal targeted Virginia’s power or a policy that had hurt New England.

Sources: Report and Resolutions of the Hartford Convention, 1815; NPS, “The Influence of Factious Leaders.”

The Hartford Convention met in the Connecticut State House for three weeks, from December 15, 1814, to January 5, 1815. It met in secret, which only made people more suspicious. Its president was George Cabot of Massachusetts, and its best-known voice was Harrison Gray Otis of Boston.

Read the proposed amendments and look for the pattern. Two-thirds votes for war, trade bans, and new states would give New England a veto over the policies that had hurt it. Ending the three-fifths clause would cut the South’s extra power from counting enslaved people. One-term presidents, and no two in a row from the same state, aimed straight at the Virginia dynasty of Jefferson and Madison. Barring naturalized citizens from office reflected Federalist suspicion of immigrant Republicans.

Otis and two others carried the report to Washington in February 1815. They arrived to find the city celebrating New Orleans and the peace. They went home mocked. Connection to the lecture: this is the detail behind slide 20, and it echoes the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions from Lecture 2.

Independent study · Native politics in the Ohio Valley

Prophetstown Was Never One Simple Alliance

Many nations: Shawnee, Kickapoo, Potawatomi, Ho-Chunk, and others gathered there.
Many critics: Miami and Delaware leaders, whose lands were nearby, often opposed the Prophet.
Many choices: Some Native leaders sought accommodation, some resistance, some local advantage.
Native politics were not reducible to “pro-British” or “anti-American.”

Sources: Patrick Bottiger, The Borderland of Fear (2016); Adam Jortner, The Gods of Prophetstown (2012).

It is easy to imagine Native peoples in 1811 as one united front lined up behind Tecumseh. They were not. Prophetstown drew people from many nations: Shawnee, Kickapoo, Potawatomi, Ho-Chunk, Ojibwe, and others. But it sat in country the Miami considered theirs, and many Miami and Delaware leaders distrusted Tenskwatawa. In 1806 the Prophet’s followers had even carried out witch hunts among the Delawares, and several accused people were killed.

Many chiefs who signed the Fort Wayne treaty believed accommodation with the Americans was the best way to protect their people. Others joined Tecumseh. Some switched sides as circumstances changed. When war came, Native nations fought for Britain, for the United States, and against each other.

Connection to the lecture: this complicates slides 9 through 11. The confederacy was powerful precisely because it tried to unite people who were not naturally united.

Independent study · A war without a war machine

Republicans Chose War Without Building the Army or the Treasury to Fight It

Small army: Under 7,000 regulars in early 1812, many of them new recruits.
No bank: Republicans had let the Bank of the United States expire in 1811.
Militia limits: State militias often refused to fight outside the country.
Lesson learned: After 1815 the country kept a larger army and navy and chartered a new bank.
Understanding why war became thinkable is not the same as proving it was wise.

Sources: U.S. Army Center of Military History, “War of 1812 Campaigns”; Office of the Historian, “War of 1812–1815.”

The Republicans chose war, but their own principles had left them unprepared to fight one. In early 1812 the regular army had fewer than 7,000 men, and many of its senior officers were old Revolutionary veterans like William Hull. Congress authorized more troops, but recruiting was slow.

Money was worse. In 1811 Republicans in Congress had refused to renew the charter of Hamilton’s Bank of the United States. When the war came, the government had no national bank to help it borrow, and many New England lenders who opposed the war would not help. By late 1814 the Treasury was nearly broke.

State militias were supposed to make up the difference. But many militiamen believed their duty was to defend their own states, not invade Canada. After the war, leaders drew the lesson. They kept a bigger standing army, built more ships, and chartered a Second Bank in 1816. Connection to the lecture: this is the detail behind the turn on slide 21.

HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 4

Why Did a Republic That Feared War Choose War?

Sailors’ rights, western sovereignty, and the road to 1812

Opening note: This deck follows the uncoverage principle. The textbook gives the broad War of 1812 context, and the major assignment carries the war itself through First Invasion: War of 1812. This lecture stops at the decision for war.

Formal lecture target: 35-40 minutes with two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper begins after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment only.

One Question, Not the Whole War

Today is not a campaign history of the War of 1812.

We are asking why war became thinkable for Republicans who feared armies, taxes, debt, and executive power.

Make the division of labor explicit. Students will encounter the invasion and consequences in the Stream & Discuss assignment. This lecture explains the choice for war.

Republicans Feared War for a Reason

War creates armies

Armies can threaten liberty.

War creates debt

Debt can strengthen financial elites.

War expands government

Taxes, officers, enforcement, and executive power grow.

So choosing war meant choosing a danger Republicans had spent years warning about.

Establish the paradox. Republican fear of war was not pacifism; it was fear that war would transform the republic.

A Sailor on an American Deck

Was he an American citizen or a British subject?
British impressment turned maritime policy into a question of sovereignty and citizenship.

Humanize impressment. This is not just “trade policy.” The Royal Navy claimed the right to recover British subjects, including alleged deserters, from neutral vessels. Americans increasingly saw the sailor as a citizen whose liberty represented national honor.

Scholarship: Paul A. Gilje, Free Trade and Sailors’ Rights in the War of 1812. Live source

A Treaty That Wasn’t Good Enough

Monroe & Pinkney

Negotiate with Britain in 1806.

Jefferson’s objection

The treaty had “no provision against impressments.”

Before embargo and war, the United States tried diplomacy and found the central problem unresolved.

This is the first closing door. It prevents the story from sounding like Jefferson never tried negotiation.

Source: Thomas Jefferson, Notes on Jay's and Monroe's Treaties, 31 December 1806, Founders Online. Live source

June 22, 1807: Britain Fires

Chesapeake
HMS Leopard fired into USS Chesapeake, killed Americans, wounded more, and seized sailors.

Many Americans expected war. Jefferson refused.

Tell this as the first dramatic act. The attack condensed years of maritime grievance into one humiliating scene. Jefferson still chose not to fight.

Source: Naval History and Heritage Command, USS Chesapeake-HMS Leopard Affair. Live source

Would You Fight Back?

You decide • Presidential dilemma
A British warship has fired into an American warship and taken sailors. You are Jefferson.
A. Declare war now.
B. Ban British ships and demand apology.
C. Stop American trade to pressure Britain.
D. Keep negotiating and avoid escalation.
Write one sentence: Which choice best protects the republic, and what risk does it create?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 1 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 3-4 minutes. The point is not to find one obvious answer. The point is to make students feel why every option carried danger.

Historical-thinking moves: Cause, consequence, and contingency.

Jefferson Tries to Fight Without Fighting

Peaceable coercion
The Embargo tried to use American trade as a weapon without creating a war state.
No war+No submission=Economic pressure

Make the Embargo intellectually legible. It was not simple cowardice or isolationism. It was an attempt to defend sovereignty without war.

Peaceable Coercion Became Coercive at Home

Exports collapse

American commerce suffers badly.

Smuggling grows

Especially in commercial regions.

Enforcement expands

Customs officials and federal authority become more intrusive.

This connects back to Lecture 1. Commercial coercion required state capacity. Republicans used federal enforcement at home to avoid war abroad.

Scholarship: Gautham Rao, National Duties. Live source

Madison Inherits Closing Doors

Diplomacy

Unreliable and incomplete.

Embargo

Failed abroad, bitter at home.

Non-intercourse

Too weak to force change.

Macon’s Bill

Let Napoleon manipulate the crisis.

By 1811, avoiding war no longer looked like protecting sovereignty.

Do not over-teach every statute. This is a closing-doors slide. Madison cycles through alternatives and the menu narrows.

May 16, 1811: America Fires Back

Little Belt
USS President battered HMS Little Belt. Each side said the other fired first.

The maritime crisis was no longer simply Britain acting and America enduring.

This is the mirror image of Chesapeake. It helps students see escalation. British public opinion could now imagine its own Chesapeake-like grievance.

Source: U.S. Navy, “The Little Belt Affair,” Naval History and Heritage Command. Live source

The West Had Its Own Crisis

Tenskwatawa

Religious renewal and rejection of American dependency.

Tecumseh

A political effort to resist further land cessions.

Native resistance was not simply a British puppet show.

Keep Indigenous agency central. Britain mattered, but Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa had their own project. Also avoid presenting Native politics as completely unified.

Scholarship: Adam Jortner, The Gods of Prophetstown. Live source; Patrick Bottiger, The Borderland of Fear. Live source

Tippecanoe Made Two Crises Look Like One

Sailors' rights+Western conflict+British influence?=Sovereignty crisis
After Tippecanoe, western Republicans increasingly saw Native resistance and British pressure as connected.

Tippecanoe is not being taught as a full battle history. It is the convergence point: maritime and western threats begin to look like one challenge to sovereignty.

Two Crises or One?

Connect the causes • Make the argument
By 1811, Americans faced British maritime pressure and a western Indigenous resistance movement with British connections.
Interpretation A: These were separate problems that politicians merged.
Interpretation B: These were connected parts of one sovereignty crisis.
Write one sentence: Which interpretation better explains why war became thinkable?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 2 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 4-5 minutes. Students can choose either side if they make a historically grounded argument. The point is multicausal reasoning, not memorizing a causes list.

Historical-thinking moves: Cause, comparison, and evidence.

The War Hawks Raised the Temperature

Henry Clay

Becomes Speaker and gives militant Republicans institutional power.

Canada

Not the only cause, but a possible way to gain leverage against Britain.

War Hawks did not invent the crisis. They helped make war politically possible.

This is the guardrail against the old “War Hawks wanted Canada” single-cause story. Canada mattered strategically; it was not the whole explanation.

Madison Puts the Case Before Congress

At sea

Impressment and attacks on neutral commerce.

In the West

Native warfare and British influence.

After alternatives

Negotiation and economic pressure had failed.

Use Madison's message as the primary-source synthesis. The administration did not present war as a first choice. It presented war after a sequence of failed alternatives.

Source: James Madison to Congress, War Message, 1 June 1812, Founders Online. Live source

The Vote Was Divided

79–49
The House approved war. The Senate followed 19–13.

“America chose war” is too simple. A divided republic chose war.

The vote is the climax, not a footnote. It shows that grievance and policy are different. Many Americans opposed British policy but did not accept war as the answer.

Sources: U.S. House of Representatives, “The House's First Declaration of War.” Live source; U.S. Senate, “Declaration of War with Great Britain, 1812.” Live source

What We Uncovered

Sovereignty

Neutral rights and sailors' rights became tests of national independence.

Failed alternatives

Republicans tried to avoid war until avoidance looked like weakness.

Convergence

Maritime and western crises made war seem like the lesser danger.

Now the documentary asks what happened when that gamble became a war.

Want to Go Deeper?

Why Britain and Not France?

Optional enrichment
France violated American commerce too.
  • Britain's navy had greater power to enforce its policies.
  • Britain's Canada and Great Lakes connections made it a continental problem.
  • Republican political culture was already deeply suspicious of British influence.
Big idea: cause is not just injury; it is injury plus power, geography, politics, and timing.

Optional enrichment only. This is an obvious student question, but the formal lecture has enough to imply the answer.

The Orders in Council

Optional enrichment
Trade rules became a sovereignty issue.
  • Britain restricted neutral trade with Napoleonic Europe.
  • American merchants were caught inside an imperial war economy.
  • Britain suspended the Orders just before news of the U.S. declaration arrived.
Big idea: timing can make historical decisions look tragically avoidable.

Optional only. Stagg stresses Orders in Council strongly, but the formal lecture keeps them under the broader “neutral rights” umbrella to protect pacing.

The John Henry Affair

Optional enrichment
Foreign-meddling fear sharpened partisan suspicion.
  • A former British agent claimed to have observed New England separatist sentiment.
  • Madison publicized the materials in 1812.
  • Republicans saw another sign that Britain encouraged domestic disunion.
Big idea: foreign policy and partisan mistrust fed each other.

Optional enrichment only. Useful for Reeder's political-psychology argument but not essential to the main classroom spine.

Prophetstown Was Not One Simple Alliance

Optional enrichment
Native politics were divided and local.
  • Prophetstown stood in Miami homeland.
  • Native communities disagreed over Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa's movement.
  • Some sought accommodation, some resistance, and some local advantage.
Big idea: Indigenous politics were not reducible to “pro-British” or “anti-American.”

Optional enrichment from Bottiger. Keep this out of formal lecture unless students ask.

A Gamble on a Weak State

Optional enrichment
Republicans chose war without building a strong war machine.
  • The army was small.
  • Militia politics were complicated.
  • Canada looked easier to attack than it was.
Big idea: understanding why war became thinkable is not the same as proving it was a wise choice.

Optional only. The documentary assignment can carry the consequences of this gamble.

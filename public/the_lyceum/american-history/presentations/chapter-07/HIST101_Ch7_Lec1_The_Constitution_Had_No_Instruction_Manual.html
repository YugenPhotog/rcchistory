HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 1

The Constitution Had No Instruction Manual

How a blueprint became a government

Opening mood: Begin at Federal Hall, not with a policy list. Students should feel that the Constitution did not spring to life automatically.

Image description: Federal Hall National Memorial seen from Broad Street. The current building stands at the site where the first federal government under the Constitution met in New York.

Source: National Park Service image via Wikimedia Commons. Live source

April 30, 1789

Washington described the presidency as a burden of “weighty and untried cares.”
George Washington, First Inaugural Address

The country had a Constitution. It did not yet know how to operate one.

This is the narrative hook. Washington was famous, but the office was new. There was no previous president, no cabinet routine, no settled executive etiquette, and no clear model for how the president should consult Congress.

Source: George Washington, “First Inaugural Address: Final Version, 30 April 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

The Problem

Americans had created a stronger national government because the Confederation had been too weak.
But they had just fought a revolution against concentrated power.

How do you make government strong enough to act without making it look like monarchy?

This is the lecture question. Do not frame it as “Hamilton versus Jefferson” yet. Frame it first as a governing problem. Every episode today is an attempted answer.

A Government Mostly on Paper

Revenue

How will the federal government collect money?

Courts

What will federal justice look like outside the Constitution’s few words?

Departments

Who actually runs foreign affairs, war, and finance?

Rights

How do you reassure people without reopening ratification?

The First Congress did not simply pass laws. It built machinery.

Emphasize that the First Congress is not housekeeping. The tariff, collection system, executive departments, Judiciary Act, and Bill of Rights all made constitutional government practical.

Precedent Became Part of the Constitution

Some answers survived because people repeated them.

Text→Choice→Precedent→Expectation

This is where Jonathan Gienapp belongs in the background, not as a named historiographical detour for students. The constitutional system was still being fixed through practice.

First Test: What Do We Call Him?

“His Highness the President of the United States of America and Protector of the Rights of the Same”
Senate title proposal, 1789
The House preferred something simpler: President of the United States.

Do not treat this as silly. A title communicated what kind of executive the republic was creating. Too little dignity could make the office weak. Too much ceremony could make it look monarchical.

Source: James Madison, “Title for the President, 11 May 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

Dignity Without Kingship

Washington had to look presidential without looking royal.
Too ordinary?

The office could seem weak at home and unimpressive abroad.

Too grand?

The president could look like an elective king.

Washington managed ceremonies, receptions, travel, dress, and access. This was not vanity. It was the creation of republican executive culture.

Image source: Gilbert Stuart portrait of George Washington, Wikimedia Commons. Live source

The Senate Experiment

What the Constitution said

Treaties require Senate advice and consent.

What happened

Washington visited the Senate in person. The senators wanted documents, debate, and delay.

Washington left irritated. He never again sought treaty advice this way.

This is a great scene. The text says “advice,” so Washington tries to get advice. The actual encounter is awkward enough that it helps create the later norm: the president negotiates first and sends the completed treaty for consent.

Source context: U.S. Senate history of treaty practice. Live source

The Cabinet Was Not in the Constitution

State

Treasury

War

Washington slowly discovered that governing required advisers in the same room.

The first full cabinet meeting was not immediate. Washington experimented with written opinions, individual advice, and consultation with senators before regular collective consultation developed.

You’re Washington. What Do You Do?

Write one sentence • Reconstruct the decision
Washington needs advice, but no one knows the routine yet.
A. Ask the Senate in person
B. Ask the Senate in writing
C. Ask department heads together
D. Decide alone, then seek approval
Write one sentence: Which option best balances effective government with republican accountability? Why?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity. Visible slide title intentionally uses student-facing language.

Activity goal: This is the first designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activity. It asks students to reconstruct a real institutional problem rather than merely recall the outcome.

Timing: 3-4 minutes. Let students write first, then ask for two or three answers. Emphasize that no option is perfect because the Constitution did not specify the operating routine.

Historical-thinking moves: Consequence and comparison. Students compare possible institutional routines and predict what each might do to executive effectiveness and republican accountability.

Notebook connection: Later, the Historian's Notebook should provide a space for this one-sentence decision judgment.

Rights: Reassurance Without Reopening Everything

Anti-Federalist fear

The new government might become too powerful.

Madison's task

Protect civil liberties without dismantling the new constitutional structure.

The Bill of Rights limited power and helped legitimate the new government.

The Bill of Rights was not just a list of liberties. It was a political settlement. Madison answered public anxiety while keeping the Constitution's new fiscal and administrative powers intact.

Source: James Madison, “Notes for Speech in Congress, ca. 8 June 1789,” Founders Online. Live source

Hamilton's Problem Was Credibility

Hamilton wanted the new government to be trusted by creditors.
A government that cannot pay its debts cannot borrow.
A government that cannot borrow has little room to survive war or crisis.

Avoid reducing Hamilton to “pro-bank” or “pro-rich.” His problem was state capacity: public credit, revenue, borrowing, and international credibility.

Image source: John Trumbull portrait of Alexander Hamilton, Wikimedia Commons. Live source

Who Gets Paid?

Original holders

Soldiers and suppliers often sold government certificates cheaply during hard times.

Current holders

Speculators bought the certificates and expected full payment.

Madison asked about fairness. Hamilton answered with market credibility.

This is the human moral dilemma behind funding the debt. Madison's discrimination proposal was not absurd. Hamilton's rejection was not simply greed. They disagreed about what public faith required.

Source: Alexander Hamilton, “Report Relative to a Provision for the Support of Public Credit,” Founders Online. Live source

Assumption: Debt Becomes National Power

State debts→Federal debt→Federal revenue→National loyalty

Hamilton was not only paying bills. He was moving political gravity toward the national government.

Assumption transferred obligations to the federal government and linked creditors to national fiscal stability. This is why opponents saw the plan as political, not merely financial.

The Bank Debate

Jefferson

If “necessary” means convenient, federal power has no clear limit.

Hamilton

A government with legitimate ends must be able to choose useful means.

This was a fight over how the Constitution would be read in practice.

This section explicitly ties back to Lecture 1's title. The Constitution had powers, but the scope of implied means had to be argued into practice.

Sources: Jefferson, “Opinion on the Constitutionality of the Bank.” Live source Hamilton, “Opinion on the Constitutionality of the Bank.” Live source

A Thin State With Real Reach

At the ports

Customhouses, collectors, paperwork, duties, remittances.

At the courts

Federal process, marshals, judges, jurisdiction.

At the frontier

Treaties, military posts, Native diplomacy, contested claims.

At the still

Internal excise taxation reaches western producers.

The federal government was small. It was not imaginary.

Use this as a bridge into the Whiskey Rebellion. The federal state was strategically concentrated in customs, finance, courts, diplomacy, and territorial enforcement.

The Tax Finds the Frontier

Treasury view

The whiskey excise funds public credit and proves the internal taxing power works.

Western view

A distant government taxes a local product in a cash-poor region.

The same law could look like national survival from Philadelphia and political extraction in western Pennsylvania.

Explain why whiskey mattered: grain was bulky, roads poor, cash scarce, and whiskey was portable value. The excise hit different communities differently.

Make the Case: Hamilton vs. the Frontier

Two-sided argument • Compare the claims
The whiskey excise looked different depending on where you stood.
Hamilton's claim: Without reliable revenue, public credit fails.
Western claim: A distant government is taxing a local survival economy.
Write two short sentences: First, make Hamilton's strongest argument. Then make the strongest western argument.

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity. Visible slide title intentionally uses student-facing language.

Activity goal: This is the second designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activity. It prepares students for the Whiskey Rebellion by making them inhabit the competing claims before the confrontation turns violent.

Timing: 4-5 minutes. Students write both claims, then compare what each side considered at stake. Push them beyond “taxes good” or “taxes bad.”

Historical-thinking moves: Comparison, cause, and context. The same excise becomes evidence of national survival in one context and extraction in another.

Notebook connection: Later, the Historian's Notebook should include a two-perspective response space for this activity.

1794

Bower Hill Burns

Tax resistance becomes a constitutional test

Image description: A historical painting of Washington reviewing troops during the Whiskey Rebellion. Use it to frame the confrontation, while clarifying that Washington did not personally lead a battle in western Pennsylvania.

Federal marshal David Lenox served process. Revenue inspector John Neville's home, Bower Hill, became a flashpoint. Armed resistance, casualties, and the burning of property turned a tax protest into a test of federal law.

Image source: “The Whiskey Rebellion,” Wikimedia Commons. Live source

Was This Liberty or Lawlessness?

Resistance tradition

Americans had celebrated resistance to unjust taxation.

Constitutional government

Federalists argued that laws made by elected institutions had to be obeyed.

The Revolution had not settled when resistance defends liberty and when it destroys self-government.

This is where the deeper historical thinking appears. Students can compare Revolutionary resistance to resistance under a constitutional republic. Same vocabulary, different institutional context.

Washington Chooses Overwhelming Force

13,000
militia called out
0
major battles
2
treason convictions, then pardons
Federal power appeared, dispersed the resistance, and then stepped back from mass punishment.

Be careful here. Washington accompanied and reviewed the militia, then returned east. Henry Lee commanded the force operationally, with Hamilton accompanying. The ending matters: enforcement, legal process, and pardons all shaped the precedent.

Source: George Washington, “Proclamation, 7 August 1794,” Founders Online. Live source

What Did the Whiskey Rebellion Prove?

Federalist lesson

The republic could tax, enforce law, mobilize militia, and survive resistance.

Republican fear

The same capacity could become coercion against citizens who opposed national policy.

The government became stronger. So did fear of the government.

Do not end as simple triumph. The government proved it could act. That was the point of Lecture 1. But successful state-building created the political fear that powers Lecture 2.

The Bridge to Lecture 2

If the national government can tax, borrow, prosecute, mobilize, and enforce...

Can citizens organize against the people who control it without being treated as enemies of the republic?

This is the transition into “When Does Opposition Become Treason?” The question of capacity becomes the question of opposition.

What We Learned

Institutions

The Constitution became real through laws, offices, routines, and precedents.

Capacity

Hamilton's system built fiscal credibility and federal reach.

Legitimacy

Power that saved the republic could also make citizens fear it.

The first Washington administration did not finish the Revolution. It institutionalized its central dilemma.

Final wrap. Do not overload. Let the three words be the memory structure: institutions, capacity, legitimacy.

Who Can the President Fire?

Optional enrichment
Another missing instruction manual problem: removal power
  • The Constitution said the president appoints officers with Senate consent.
  • It did not clearly say who could remove those officers.
  • The 1789 debate helped build a working presidency by practice, not just by text.
Big idea: constitutional meaning was being made through ordinary governing decisions.

The Decision of 1789 is not necessary for the formal lecture because Senate advice and the Cabinet already carry the institutional-precedent argument. It is excellent optional depth for students who want one more example of constitutional practice filling textual gaps.

Dinner, Debt, and the Potomac

Optional enrichment
The assumption-capital bargain
  • Assumption turned state debts into national obligations.
  • Some Virginians resisted paying for other states' debts after Virginia had already paid much of its own.
  • The bargain helped move the future capital to the Potomac while allowing assumption to pass.
Big idea: state-building also happened through bargaining, geography, and political trust.

Use only as optional enrichment. The formal lecture already teaches what assumption did. This slide supplies the famous bargain and geographic politics without adding a required item to downstream assessments.

What Did the Federal Government Actually Look Like?

Optional enrichment
Not a giant bureaucracy, but not nothing either
  • Customhouses collected most federal revenue.
  • Collectors, surveyors, clerks, forms, ports, courts, and inspectors made federal power visible.
  • The early state was thin, but it could still reach commerce, debt, land, taxes, and war.
Big idea: institutions can be small and still matter.

This preserves the customhouse material from the background essay without slowing the formal lecture. It should not become an exam or notebook requirement unless later promoted into the formal sequence.

A Government on Paper Meets a Native Continent

Optional enrichment
Claiming territory was not the same as controlling it
  • Native nations still exercised sovereignty across much of the continent.
  • British posts and influence remained important in the Northwest.
  • Federal taxes, credit, diplomacy, and military capacity were connected to the problem of making national claims real.
Big idea: the new federal state was being tested far beyond Philadelphia.

This is probably the strongest optional enrichment item. It plants a seed for later lectures on empire, Native sovereignty, and federal reach. Guardrail: because it appears only here, do not assess it unless it is later moved into the formal lecture or assigned elsewhere.

HIST 101 • Chapter 7 • Lecture 2

When Does Opposition Become Treason?

How Americans learned that an opponent could be partisan and loyal

Opening: This revised deck follows the uncoverage principle. We are not covering every political event in the 1790s. We are using selected events to uncover one central problem: can organized opposition be legitimate in a republic?

Protocol: Formal 35-40 minute lecture with two designated In-Class Historical Thinking Activities. Want to Go Deeper follows only after the formal lecture and is optional enrichment.

Last Time

The federal government proved it could act: tax, borrow, administer, and enforce law.

Now comes a harder problem: what happens when citizens organize to defeat the people running that government?

Bridge from Lecture 1. The new government has capacity. Now students ask whether opposition to the people using that capacity is legitimate or dangerous.

One Big Problem

Modern assumption

Parties are normal. Opposition is part of democratic politics.

1790s fear

Parties looked like factions: organized groups willing to sacrifice the common good.

Disagreement→Faction?→Corruption?→Treason?

This slide replaces several conceptual setup slides. Do not over-teach eighteenth-century republican theory. Give the key mental shift: organized opposition was not yet obviously normal or loyal.

Old Allies Become Enemies

Hamilton

Credit, finance, executive energy, national power.

Madison

Former ally who comes to fear Hamilton's system.

Jefferson

Sees British-style corruption hiding inside republican forms.

Each side believed it was defending the Revolution.

Keep the balance. Federalists feared instability and weakness; Republicans feared aristocracy and monarchy. This is not a villain/hero setup.

A Printing Press Can Become a Party

Federalist press

Defend the administration. Build national support.

Republican press

Expose corruption. Organize opposition.

Newspapers did not merely report party conflict. They helped create it.

This is enough print culture for the formal lecture. Fenno, Freneau, Bache, and Duane can live in optional enrichment unless you want an example. The theme is infrastructure: opposition needed communication.

Watchdog or Faction?

Choose a side • Make the case
A group starts a newspaper, holds public meetings, circulates petitions, and tries to defeat the administration.
Watchdog: citizens protecting liberty from power.
Faction: an organized party trying to overawe elected government.
Write one sentence: Which label is stronger in a republic, and why?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 1 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 3-4 minutes. Do not let this become a long debate. It prepares students to understand Democratic-Republican societies, Jay Treaty petitions, and Washington's warning.

Historical-thinking moves: Comparison and context.

France Turns Argument Into Danger

Republicans saw

A sister revolution against monarchy.

Federalists saw

Violence, radicalism, and foreign manipulation.

Genêt showed

A foreign revolution might mobilize American citizens against American policy.

Foreign policy became domestic identity.

This slide compresses French Revolution, Genêt, and Democratic-Republican societies into one functional point: foreign crisis made domestic opposition look more dangerous. Mention “self-created societies” orally if useful, but do not dwell on it.

Jay's Treaty: Politics Moves Into the Streets

1795

Treaty with Britain reduces danger of war.

But

Republicans see betrayal of the Revolution.

Crowds

Meetings, petitions, demonstrations.

Warning

Washington sees party spirit already emerging.

Opposition was becoming organized, public, and national.

This combines Jay's Treaty and Washington's Farewell warning. If time permits, tell the story of Hamilton being shouted down. Keep the payoff: the system Washington warned against already existed.

Source: George Washington, Farewell Address, 1796, Founders Online. Live source

1796: The Constitution Meets Parties

Winner

John Adams, Federalist president.

Runner-up

Thomas Jefferson, Republican vice president.

The opposition leader now sat inside the executive branch.

This covers both the constitutional mechanics and Adams's difficult position. Do not linger on electoral details. The point is that institutions built without parties now have to operate with parties.

XYZ Makes Fear Look Reasonable

1798
French agents demanded concessions before negotiations would proceed. Americans exploded in anger.
Ships seized→Diplomatic insult→Quasi-War

This is an interpretive balance slide. Federalist fears were not invented. French attacks on shipping, XYZ, and the Quasi-War made emergency politics plausible.

The Sedition Act Made Criticism a Crime

It became a federal crime to publish certain “false, scandalous and malicious” writings against the government.
Sedition Act, 1798
Federalists said lies could aid an enemy. Republicans said criticism was being criminalized.

This is the core law for the formal lecture. Mention that the Alien Acts are part of the larger crisis, but save details about immigration and belonging for optional enrichment.

Source: National Archives, Sedition Act text. Live source

Matthew Lyon Wins From Jail

1798

Republican congressman convicted under the Sedition Act.

4

Months in jail.

Wins

Re-elected while imprisoned.

1801

Helps decide Jefferson's election in the House.

Do not cut this. This is the human story that makes the Sedition Act concrete. The government prosecutes an opposition politician; voters return him to office; he later helps decide control of that government.

Where Is the Line?

Sort the cases • Draw the boundary
A republic has to protect itself. But it also has to allow opposition.
A newspaper attacks the president.
A public meeting denounces a treaty.
A state declares a federal law unconstitutional.
A party tries to block a rival's election.
Write one sentence: Which case is closest to legitimate opposition, and which is most dangerous? Why?

Internal category: In-Class Historical Thinking Activity 2 of 2. Visible title uses student-facing language.

Timing: 4-5 minutes. Keep responses quick. This prepares students for Virginia/Kentucky and the election crisis without requiring a detour through every related episode.

Historical-thinking moves: Evidence, comparison, and consequence.

Republicans Answer From the States

Madison's Virginia

States should “interpose” against alarming federal violations.

Jefferson's Kentucky

Federal acts beyond delegated powers could be treated as void.

Defending liberty raised another question: who decides when the federal government has gone too far?

Keep this concise. It is not a full nullification lecture. It shows that Republican resistance defended speech but also opened another constitutional danger.

Sources: Madison, Virginia Resolutions, Founders Online. Live source; Jefferson draft of Kentucky Resolutions. Live source

1800: Losing Felt Like the End

Federalist warning

Jefferson means atheism, French radicalism, and social disorder.

Republican warning

Federalists mean monarchy, armies, taxes, and repression.

What happens if each side thinks losing will destroy the republic?

Set emotional stakes. Do not make it sound like a normal campaign with colorful insults. The point is loser’s consent under conditions of fear.

73–73: The System Freezes

35
Thirty-five House ballots failed to choose a president.
The decision sat with the outgoing Federalist House, inside a constitutional system shaped by slave-state representation.

This combines the tie, House procedure, repeated ballots, and the Three-Fifths warning into one slide. Avoid too much mechanics. Students need to feel the freeze.

Source: Jefferson correspondence during the House deadlock. See Jefferson to Monroe, 15 February 1801. Live source

The Transfer Became the Lesson

“We are all republicans: we are all federalists.”
Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, 1801
The losing party left office. The winning opposition took power. The defeated party remained legal.

Clarify: Jefferson's line does not mean parties vanished or harmony suddenly arrived. It means the defeated side was not outlawed, and opposition could become government.

Source: Thomas Jefferson, First Inaugural Address, 4 March 1801, Founders Online. Live source

What We Uncovered

Opposition

A republic needed organized criticism, even when it felt dangerous.

Fear

Foreign crisis made domestic opponents look like enemies.

Precedent

1801 taught that losing power did not have to mean losing the republic.

Optional Enrichment

Want to Go Deeper?

Protocol guardrail: This begins optional enrichment after the formal lecture. Material appearing only in this section is not required course content and should not be assessed unless first promoted into the formal instructional stream.

The Alien Acts Asked: Who Belongs?

Optional enrichment
Security fears became membership rules.
  • The Naturalization Act lengthened the path to citizenship.
  • Federalists associated immigrant radicals with Republican opposition.
  • Republicans feared that political opponents were being treated as foreign threats.
Big idea: the crisis was about speech, but also about who counted as safe enough to belong.

Optional enrichment only. The formal lecture mentions the broader Alien and Sedition crisis but focuses on Sedition because it most directly answers the lecture question.

Source: National Archives, Alien and Sedition Acts. Live source

Fries's Rebellion: When Resistance Became Treason

Optional enrichment
Tax resistance forced Adams to draw the line.
  • Resistance to a federal direct tax erupted in Pennsylvania.
  • John Fries helped free detained tax resisters.
  • Fries was convicted of treason, and Adams later pardoned him.
Big idea: Adams signed repressive laws, but he also resisted defining treason too broadly.

Moved out of formal lecture to protect pacing. It is excellent material but not necessary once Sedition/Lyon carries the central treason question.

Source: John Adams, Questions on the Cases of Fries, Heaney, and Gettman, 20 May 1800, Founders Online. Live source

How Ugly Did the Newspapers Get?

Optional enrichment
Print culture was personal, vicious, and political.
  • Editors attacked character as well as policy.
  • Reputation still mattered in a culture of honor.
  • A newspaper could become a local party headquarters.
Big idea: parties grew through print before they had modern party machinery.

Optional enrichment only. Useful if students are curious about the mechanics of early party organization.

Was Jefferson Too Dangerous to Elect?

Optional enrichment
Some Federalists warned Jefferson would destroy religion.
  • Election rhetoric linked Jefferson to French radicalism and irreligion.
  • Fear made ordinary defeat feel like civilizational disaster.
  • This helps explain why “loser’s consent” mattered so much.
Big idea: the election was not just partisan. For many voters it felt moral and religious.

Optional enrichment only. This material is valuable but not needed in the formal line of argument.

The Three-Fifths Problem

Optional enrichment
The transfer of power happened through an unequal system.
  • Enslaved people had no political rights.
  • Their numbers increased representation for slaveholding states.
  • That shaped the Electoral College and the House.
Big idea: 1800 was a democratic milestone built inside an undemocratic constitutional structure.

Optional enrichment only. The formal lecture flags this, but this slide gives curious students more time with it.

Why Adams Matters

Optional enrichment
The president who signed repression also chose restraint.
  • Adams signed the Alien and Sedition Acts.
  • He resisted High Federalists who wanted a larger conflict with France.
  • He pardoned Fries and accepted the transfer of power.
Big idea: historical actors rarely fit cleanly into simple hero/villain categories.

Optional enrichment only. Useful for interpretive balance, especially if students reduce Adams to a villain.

  • Emerged in mid-19th century USA, primarily among Democrats.
  • Advocated for the expansion of American democracy and liberal reforms.
Domestic Policies
  • Supported free trade, opposed monopolies, and promoted infrastructure development.
  • Encouraged a distinct American cultural identity through arts and literature.

The Young America Movement was a political and cultural attitude in the mid-19th century United States that embraced a belief in manifest destiny, the expansion of American democracy, and a liberal and reformist approach to domestic policies. Here's a detailed analysis and discussion of its key aspects:

Origins and Ideology: Emerging primarily among the Democratic Party, the movement was a response to the changing social and economic conditions of the time. It advocated for the expansion of American democracy and values, viewing the United States as a beacon of liberty and progress. The movement drew inspiration from the European revolutions of 1848, favoring liberal reforms and the spread of democratic ideals.

Manifest Destiny: A central tenet of the Young America Movement was the belief in Manifest Destiny, the idea that the United States was destined to expand across the North American continent. This expansionist mindset fueled support for territorial acquisition and was a driving force behind American foreign policy in the mid-19th century.

Domestic Policies: Domestically, the movement supported a range of liberal reforms. These included advocating for free trade, opposing monopolies, and supporting infrastructure development. The Young America Movement was also noted for its support of the arts and American literature, promoting a distinctly American cultural identity.

Impact on Slavery: The movement's stance on slavery was complex. While some members opposed slavery on moral grounds, others saw it as a necessary institution for the economic and political growth of the nation. This division reflected the broader national debate over slavery during this period.

Influence on American Politics: The Young America Movement had a significant impact on the Democratic Party and American politics in general. Its ideals influenced several political figures of the time, including President James K. Polk, who pursued an aggressive expansionist policy.

Legacy and Critique: The movement's legacy is mixed. While it promoted the idea of American exceptionalism and progress, its association with manifest destiny and expansionism also contributed to conflicts, including the Mexican-American War. Moreover, its ambiguous position on slavery is seen by some historians as a missed opportunity to take a firmer stand against the institution.

In summary, the Young America Movement represented a blend of expansionist, liberal, and democratic ideals that significantly influenced American politics and culture in the mid-19th century. Its impact was felt in both domestic policies and foreign affairs, reflecting the dynamic and often contentious nature of this period in American history.

Manifest Destiny is a term that encapsulates a complex set of ideas, attitudes, and policies that greatly influenced the United States' expansionist agenda in the 19th century. It's a multifaceted concept with significant historical, cultural, and political dimensions. Here's a detailed analysis and discussion:

Definition and Origins:

Manifest Destiny was a widely held belief that American settlers were destined by God to expand across the continent.

Coined in 1845 by journalist John L. O'Sullivan, the term encapsulated existing ideas about American exceptionalism, divine providence, and the mission to spread democracy and capitalism.

Ideological Foundations:

Rooted in a sense of American exceptionalism, the belief held that the United States was unique and superior in its political and social systems.

Religious overtones: Many adherents believed that this expansion was not only justified but also preordained by God.

Impact on Expansion:

Manifest Destiny was a driving force behind the westward expansion of the United States.

It fueled the annexation of Texas, the Oregon Territory, and the Mexican-American War, which resulted in the acquisition of territories like California and New Mexico.

Native American and Mexican Relations:

The ideology often led to the justification of the displacement and harsh treatment of Native Americans and Mexicans.

Policies like the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the ensuing Trail of Tears were partly rationalized through Manifest Destiny.

Interplay with Slavery:

The expansionist impulse intensified the debate over slavery, as new territories raised the question of whether they would be slave-holding or free states.

This contributed to tensions that eventually led to the American Civil War.

Criticism and Legacy:

The concept has been criticized for promoting cultural and racial superiority, leading to imperialism and colonialism.

Its legacy is controversial, as it highlights the complex interplay between American idealism and the darker aspects of expansionism.

Cultural and Political Influence:

Manifest Destiny influenced art, literature, and the national identity of the United States.

Politically, it was used to justify not only territorial expansion but also later interventions in foreign affairs.

In conclusion, Manifest Destiny was a potent and controversial idea that played a central role in shaping the United States' history in the 19th century. It reflected a complex mix of idealism, religious fervor, and national ambition, while also contributing to some of the darkest chapters in American history, particularly in terms of its treatment of indigenous peoples and its role in the slavery debate.

Early U.S. Settlement in Texas under Mexico

Explain that initially, Mexican leaders encouraged American settlers to move into the Texas territory to promote development and secure the region.

Highlight the incentives offered to settlers, such as land grants, and how this led to a significant influx of Americans into Texas.

Background Context

Mexican Independence: Mexico gained independence from Spain in 1821. The new government sought to populate the northern territories, including Texas, to strengthen its borders.

Early Policies Encouraging Settlement

Mexican Colonization Laws: Initially, Mexico enacted laws to encourage settlement in Texas, offering land grants to empresarios (land agents) who would bring settlers from the U.S. Stephen F. Austin was among the most notable empresarios.

Shift in Policy and Restrictions

Change in Mexican Attitudes: Over time, the Mexican government grew wary of the increasing American presence in Texas. Concerns included the settlers' failure to integrate (many didn't learn Spanish or convert to Catholicism, as required by law) and their continued ties to the United States.

Law of April 6, 1830: A critical turning point was the Law of April 6, 1830, enacted by Mexican President Anastasio Bustamante. The law:

Prohibited Further U.S. Immigration: Aimed to stop the influx of Americans into Texas.

Suspended Empresario Contracts: This halted the provision of land grants.

Imposed Customs Duties: New tariffs were introduced, affecting trade and economic interests of Texan settlers.

Promoted Mexican and European Settlement: To balance the demographics, the law encouraged settlement from other areas, not just the U.S.

Resulting Tensions

Texan Response: The settlers, both American and Tejano (Mexicans living in Texas), were angered and felt their rights and livelihoods were being threatened.

Cultural and Economic Frictions: The restrictions exacerbated cultural differences and economic frustrations among the settlers.

Political Discontent: Increasing centralization of power in Mexico under President Santa Anna further alienated the Texan settlers, who felt their autonomy was being eroded.

Escalation to Conflict

Early Skirmishes: Tensions eventually led to confrontations, such as the Anahuac Disturbances in 1832 and 1835, which were early signs of rebellion.

Road to Revolution: The cumulative effect of these restrictions and tensions contributed significantly to the Texan settlers’ decision to seek independence, leading to the Texas Revolution.

In summary, the Mexican government's shift from encouraging U.S. settlement to imposing strict restrictions was a pivotal factor in the rising tensions in Texas. These tensions, driven by cultural, economic, and political grievances, set the stage for the Texas Revolution and the eventual declaration of Texan independence.

The Alamo

Background

The Alamo, originally a mission in San Antonio, Texas, became a key battleground during the Texas Revolution.

In December 1835, Texan forces had captured it from the Mexican troops.

Siege and Battle (February 23 – March 6, 1836)

General Antonio López de Santa Anna, the Mexican President and General, laid siege to the Alamo in an effort to regain control.

The Texan defenders, numbering around 200, included famous figures like James Bowie, William B. Travis, and Davy Crockett.

Despite being vastly outnumbered, the Texan forces held the Alamo for 13 days against Santa Anna's army, which was estimated to be over a thousand strong.

Outcome and Significance

The battle ended in the fall of the Alamo and the death of all the Texan defenders.

"Remember the Alamo" became a rallying cry for Texan forces, symbolizing heroic resistance and the sacrifice for independence.

The loss at the Alamo galvanized Texan support for the war and became a symbol of their struggle for independence.

Battle of San Jacinto

Prelude

Following the fall of the Alamo, Texan forces continued to gather under General Sam Houston.

Despite pressure to confront Santa Anna, Houston conducted a strategic retreat to train and better prepare his army.

The Battle (April 21, 1836)

The decisive battle took place near present-day Houston at San Jacinto.

The Texan army, numbering about 900, launched a surprise attack on the Mexican camp during siesta time.

The battle was short, lasting only 18 minutes, but it was brutal and decisive.

Outcome and Significance

The Texan army won a stunning victory, capturing General Santa Anna the following day.

Santa Anna signed the Treaties of Velasco, granting Texas its independence, although the Mexican government did not recognize this.

The victory at San Jacinto was pivotal; it effectively ended the military phase of the Texas Revolution and led to the establishment of the Republic of Texas.

Conclusion Both the Alamo and the Battle of San Jacinto are crucial chapters in the story of Texas's fight for independence. While the Alamo stands as a symbol of defiance and sacrifice, San Jacinto represents a strategic triumph and the realization of Texan independence. These events are deeply ingrained in Texan and American history, epitomizing the spirit of determination and resilience.

Lecture Notes on the Compromise of 1850:

Context of the Debates:

The ongoing debates were primarily focused on two major issues: the status of fugitive slaves and how to manage the territories won from Mexico following the Mexican-American War.

The North and South held deeply conflicting views on these matters, particularly on whether new territories should permit slavery.

Henry Clay's Role:

Senator Henry Clay, known as the "Great Compromiser," stepped forward with the intention of resolving these critical disputes.

His aim was to create a balance that would quell the tensions and provide a framework for governance in the new territories.

Congressional Activity:

Clay's proposals and the associated debates gave rise to a series of significant speeches within Congress.

These speeches were not only pivotal in terms of their content but also in terms of the political weight carried by their orators.

John C. Calhoun, Daniel Webster, and William H. Seward were among the most prominent speakers. Each brought forth powerful arguments reflective of their regional and political affiliations.

Legislative Process:

Initially, Clay attempted to pass a comprehensive bill that would address all the concerns in a single legislative package.

However, this omnibus approach faced significant opposition as legislators were unwilling to support the entire package due to specific provisions they opposed.

Strategic Voting:

As a result of the opposition, senators and representatives employed strategic voting. They voted against the omnibus bill, not necessarily in opposition to the entirety of its content but to prevent certain clauses they found objectionable from passing.

Outcome:

This strategy led to the disintegration of the original omnibus bill into separate pieces of legislation, each addressing a distinct aspect of the larger issues at stake.

These separate bills, when considered individually, were able to pass and collectively came to be known as the Compromise of 1850.

Lecture Notes: Resistance to the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

Slide Title: Opposition and Resistance

Abolitionists’ View

Overview: Abolitionists were among the loudest voices opposing the Fugitive Slave Act. They argued that it was:

Immoral: The law forced free states to participate in slavery by capturing and returning escaped slaves, undermining moral opposition to the institution.

Unconstitutional: Abolitionists claimed the law violated due process rights, as fugitives were denied a jury trial and presumed guilty.

Draconian: Severe penalties were imposed on those who helped fugitives, including heavy fines and imprisonment, stifling acts of humanity.

Example:

William Lloyd Garrison, a leading abolitionist, wrote fiery editorials denouncing the law as a betrayal of freedom and justice, calling it "the most infamous law ever enacted in the history of America."

Frederick Douglass, a formerly enslaved man and abolitionist leader, spoke out against the law in his public lectures, urging resistance and labeling it a tool of oppression.

Resistance in the North

Community Efforts to Hide Fugitives

Northern communities, both black and white, often banded together to protect escaped slaves from capture.

Underground Railroad: A secret network of safe houses and routes helped fugitives escape to Canada. Key figures like Harriet Tubman risked their lives to guide dozens of individuals to freedom.

Northern cities like Boston and Philadelphia became hubs of resistance, where abolitionists organized "vigilance committees" to warn fugitives of impending danger.

Example:

Shadrach Minkins: In 1851, Shadrach, an escaped slave, was arrested in Boston. A group of black men stormed the courthouse, freed him, and helped him escape to Canada.

Legal Challenges and Public Protests

Legal Challenges: Lawyers and activists filed court cases to challenge the constitutionality of the Fugitive Slave Act or delay proceedings to buy time for fugitives to escape.

Example: In Boston, abolitionists repeatedly tried to legally block the return of Anthony Burns in 1854, though they were ultimately unsuccessful.

Public Protests:

Protests often erupted when fugitives were captured. Crowds would gather to denounce federal enforcement of the law.

Example: During the Anthony Burns case, over 50,000 Bostonians lined the streets, waving flags hung upside-down as a symbol of protest.

  • Harriet Beecher Stowe writes Uncle Tom’s Cabin in 1851
    • Response to the injustices of the Fugitive Slave Act
    • Direct attempt to mobilize the Northern Public against the inhumanity of slavery
    • becomes runaway best-seller – 500,000 copies sold in US and Britain in the first year

By mid 19th century American literature was coming unto its own. None was as famous or influential as Uncle Tom’s Cabin. Originally published serially in the National Era, it appeared in book form on March 20, 1852 selling 10,000 copies in its first week and 300,000 by the end of the year. Sales in Britain were even higher, 1,200,000 within a year.

Harriet Beecher Stowe, from Connecticut, only direct experience of slavery was a short visit to Kentucky, a border state. She seems to have got most of her information about slavery from black women servants, especially her cook and from abolitionist literature. Southerners were outraged at the depiction of slavery – argued that it had no factual basis. Helped by her brother – she combed through newspaper reports of actual legal cases. Her A Key to Uncle Tom’s Cabin was published proving that the cruelties and injustices of which the novel complained were, in reality, far more severe than she had imagined.

Book had become an international best seller and a phenomenon. The book was turned into statues, toyes, games, handkerchiefs, wallpapers, plates, etc. Turned into songs and dramatized versions.

Widely believed that Mrs. Stowe was responsible for Lincoln’s election, and so for the chain of events which led to the bombardment of Fort Sumter. When Presiden Lincoln met Stowe he said to her: So you’re the little woman who wrote the book that started this great war.”

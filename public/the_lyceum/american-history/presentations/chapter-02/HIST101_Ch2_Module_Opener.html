Founding the Colonies
Four Questions About How English America Began

Two groups of English settlers land on the same Atlantic coast, in the same century, speaking the same language. One builds a plantation society growing tobacco with unfree labor. The other builds a tight-knit religious commonwealth of towns and churches.

Why did the same country produce two completely different Americas? And who — Crown, colonists, or the Native nations already living there — actually controlled any of it?

This unit doesn’t just tell you what happened. It teaches you to ask why — one question at a time.

How This Unit Thinks

Chapter 2 is built as four big questions, one per lecture. Each lecture names its question out loud at the start, works through the answer in a few clear steps, and returns to answer it at the end. You’re not memorizing a list of colonies — you’re learning to explain why early America turned out the way it did.

The questions build on each other. First why the English came at all; then why their colonies diverged so sharply; then what one of those societies actually built, and who it left out; and finally, who really held power on a continent that was never only English to begin with.

A Thread Running Underneath
Who got to take part — and who was left out?

As the four questions unfold, keep asking who counted as a full member of these new societies. That question of participation and exclusion runs quietly through the whole chapter, and it will come back in later units.

How This Unit Works

The textbook gives you the facts. The lectures give you the meaning. Your chapter reading covers the who, what, and when. The four lectures don’t repeat that — they dig into why it matters and how it connects. You need both.

Each lecture is organized around a small number of key moves — "Four Forces," "Four Words," "Three Moves," "Three Voices" — so the structure of the argument is always visible on screen.

Watch for the Pause & Reflect and Pause & Predict slides. Those are the moments to stop and think, predict what comes next, or commit to an answer — they’re where the ideas actually stick.

The Four Questions

Each lecture is a question. Answer all four and you understand how English America began — and why "English America" was always an overstatement.

LECTURE 1 · MOTIVE
Why Colonize?
What drove England across the Atlantic? Not one reason but four forces — religion, rivalry, profit, and opportunity — converging in a single generation. We start with England fifty-seven years apart and ask what changed.

LECTURE 2 · DIVERGENCE
Why Did the Colonies Become Different?
Same country, same coast, same century — so why did Virginia and New England become such different worlds? The answer runs through four words that set each colony on its path. If the settlers were all English, why didn’t all English colonies turn out the same?

LECTURE 3 · WHAT THEY BUILT
Building a Puritan Society
"They came for religious freedom" — true enough, but what did they actually build once they arrived, and who did that building include or exclude? Three moves turn a persecuted movement into a governing society. Escaping persecution and creating freedom are not the same thing.

LECTURE 4 · POWER  |  CHAPTER FINALE
Who Controlled America?
Three voices each claim the continent — the Crown, the colonists, and the Native nations already living there. Who actually held power? The honest answer is that control was contested, layered, and shared far more than any one voice admitted. The question isn’t who owned America — it’s who really governed it.

As You Go Through This Unit, Watch For…

  • The question at the top. Every lecture opens by naming its question and closes by answering it. If you can answer all four, you understand the chapter.
  • The numbered structure. "Four Forces," "Three Moves," "Three Voices" — the shape of the argument is always on screen. Use it to organize your notes.
  • Who was included — and who wasn’t. As these societies take shape, keep asking who counted as a full member and who didn’t. That thread runs through the whole unit.
  • Causes are usually plural. Notice how the lectures resist single-cause answers — "four forces," not one. Real history is almost always about several causes at once.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide is doing something sneaky: it is taking away all the easy answers before you can reach for them. Look at the two columns. English settlers, Atlantic coast, same century — identical on both sides. If everything that obviously matters is the same, then whatever made Virginia and New England different has to be something else. That "something else" is the whole lecture.

Breaking It Down

Why this framing matters: Students often assume colonies differed because different kinds of people founded them — as if Virginians were greedy and New Englanders were pious, and that settled it. That explanation feels satisfying and is mostly wrong. Both groups were English, Protestant, monarchist, and convinced of their own superiority over the peoples they encountered.

The essential question: If the settlers were English, why didn't all English colonies become the same kind of society? Hold onto this sentence. Everything today is an attempt to answer it, and the last few slides return to it directly.

Why This Matters

Historians call this a comparative method — you hold most variables constant so the ones that actually caused the difference stand out. It is the same logic a scientist uses in a controlled experiment. By the end of class you should be able to name four variables that did the work.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jack P. Greene – Pursuits of Happiness (1988)

Greene pushed back hard against the habit of treating New England as the "real" America and the South as a deviation from it. He argued the opposite: the Chesapeake pattern — commercial, dispersed, hierarchical, oriented toward private gain — was actually closer to the norm across British America, and New England was the outlier. Whether or not you end up agreeing, his point matters for this slide: do not assume one colony is the standard and the other needs explaining. Both need explaining.

Four Words

By the end of today, you should be able to explain the difference using four words.

Goals
Why was the colony founded?
Environment
What did the land make possible?
Migration
Who actually came?
Labor
Who did the work, and on what terms?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is your roadmap, and it is deliberately short. Four words. If you remember nothing else from today, remember these four and what each one asks. Think of them as four dials — turn them differently and you get a different colonial society out the other end.

Breaking It Down

Goals: Why was the colony founded in the first place? A colony launched to earn money for investors will be organized differently from one launched so a congregation can worship together. Founding purpose shapes who gets recruited, how land is distributed, and what counts as success.

Environment: What did the land and climate actually permit? This is not about the environment "determining" everything — people made choices. But you cannot grow tobacco profitably in Massachusetts, and that constraint has consequences that ripple for a century.

Migration: Who actually got on the ships? A colony that receives mostly single young men in their late teens and twenties develops very differently from one receiving whole families with children and grandparents. Sex ratios, ages, and family structure shape population growth, inheritance, community stability, and social order.

Labor: Who does the work, and on what terms? This is the dial that ends up mattering most, and it is where today's lecture spends the most time. The difference between household labor, temporary bound labor, and hereditary racial slavery is the difference between three fundamentally different societies.

Why This Matters

Notice that these four are not a list of topics — they are connected. Goals shape who migrates. Environment shapes what economy is possible. Economy shapes labor demand. Labor shapes everything else. You are learning a model of causation, not four separate facts. That is why you can carry it forward and apply it to colonies we have not discussed yet.

Section I

Virginia Began as a Business

What happens when a colony is founded to make money?

Jamestown Was an Investment

What the investors wanted

  • A joint-stock company sends settlers, 1607
  • Settlement on the James River
  • Return on investment — eventually

What actually happened

  • Food production proved difficult
  • Disease and mortality were severe
  • Leadership was unstable
  • The colony nearly collapsed
Jamestown was designed first as an investment — not as a self-sufficient family community.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The single most useful thing to know about Jamestown is that it was a business venture, not a refuge, a utopia, or a homestead. Once you know that, most of what went wrong in the first years stops looking like bad luck and starts looking like predictable consequences of the founding design.

Breaking It Down

Joint-stock company: A business where many investors buy shares and pool their money for a venture too expensive or risky for any one person, then split profits and losses. The Virginia Company was chartered in 1606 and landed settlers at Jamestown in 1607. It is essentially a seventeenth-century startup with shareholders expecting returns.

Why that shaped everything: The Company recruited for extraction and trade, not for farming. Early passenger lists carry a striking number of gentlemen, goldsmiths, and jewelers — men equipped to find precious metals and open trade, not to clear fields and plant corn. There was no expectation of feeding themselves indefinitely; supply ships were supposed to handle that.

What went wrong: Food production faltered. Disease and mortality were brutal — the site sat on brackish tidal water. Leadership was divided among a quarreling council rather than concentrated in one authority. Supply ships came late and undersupplied. The colony came close to complete collapse more than once.

Why This Matters

This is your first look at how Goals — the first of the four words — cascade into everything else. A settlement organized as an investment was badly organized for survival. Hold that thought, because the moment Virginia finally discovers how to actually make money, the investment logic does not disappear. It intensifies, and it becomes far more dangerous for everyone living nearby.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Karen Ordahl Kupperman – The Jamestown Project (2007)

Kupperman resists the old story that Jamestown nearly failed because the settlers were lazy aristocrats who would not work. She argues the problems were structural: divided authority, an investor model that demanded quick returns, genuine ignorance of the environment, and dependence on trade with Native neighbors whose own harvests were strained by drought. Her larger point is that England learned colonization the hard way, through failure, and that the lessons of Jamestown shaped everything England attempted afterward.

Someone Else's Political World

Jamestown stood inside Tsenacommacah — the territory of the Powhatan Confederacy.

  • Communities and trade routes
  • Alliances and rivalries
  • Established political authority
  • Existing patterns of land use

The English did not arrive on politically empty land.

English map of Virginia, engraved by William Hole — note the density of Native towns

📖 Understanding This Slide

This may be the most important correction of the day. The English did not sail into an empty wilderness and begin building. They sailed into the middle of an existing state — one with borders, a capital, a ruler, tributary provinces, a tax system, and its own foreign policy. Jamestown was not a beginning. It was an intrusion.

Breaking It Down

Tsenacommacah: The Algonquian name for coastal eastern Virginia, roughly meaning "densely inhabited land." Notice what that name tells you. The people who lived there did not think of it as sparsely settled frontier. They thought of it as crowded.

The Powhatan Confederacy: A paramount chiefdom of roughly thirty tributary communities, unified under Wahunsonacock — the leader the English called Powhatan, after the name of his people. Tributary communities paid him goods and deferred to him in war and diplomacy. This is a political structure, not a scattering of villages.

Read the map as evidence: The engraving beside the text is English, made to accompany John Smith's account. Yet look at what it records — over two hundred small circles marking Native towns, and Powhatan himself seated in his longhouse in the upper corner. The English mapmakers could not help documenting how thoroughly occupied this land was, even while making a case for English claims to it. That tension is what makes it useful evidence.

Why This Matters

If you start the story with an empty landscape, everything that follows looks like settlement and growth. If you start it correctly — with the English entering someone else's state — then everything that follows looks like an escalating territorial conflict between two political powers. Those are not the same story, and only one of them fits the evidence.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Helen C. Rountree – The Powhatan Indians of Virginia (1989)

Rountree spent a career reconstructing Powhatan society on its own terms rather than as a backdrop to English arrival. She shows a complex political system with hereditary leadership, tribute obligations, priestly authority, and sophisticated seasonal use of land and water. Her work makes it impossible to describe Powhatan communities as "simple" or "wandering" — categories the English used precisely because they justified taking the land.

Karen Ordahl Kupperman – Indians and English: Facing Off in Early America (2000)

Kupperman argues that in the earliest years neither side held decisive advantage, and both were trying to read the other, size up their intentions, and figure out how to make use of them. Powhatan may well have seen the English as a potentially useful tributary group — a source of metal tools and weapons against his rivals. That is a very different picture from helpless natives meeting inevitable conquerors.

Section II

Tobacco Changes Everything

The relationship becomes far more dangerous once Virginia discovers how to make money.

One Crop, Six Consequences

Tobacco  →  Profit  →  More land
More labor  →  More expansion  →  More inequality and conflict

The crop is not the point. The chain is the point.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the engine of the entire lecture. Do not memorize it as a list — memorize it as a chain, where each link pulls the next one into motion. If you can reproduce this chain on an exam and explain why each arrow points where it does, you have understood the Chesapeake.

Breaking It Down

Tobacco → Profit: John Rolfe introduced a sweeter West Indian tobacco strain around 1612 that Europeans actually wanted to buy. Suddenly Virginia had something salable. The Virginia Company's original problem — no return on investment — was solved.

Profit → More land: Tobacco is hard on soil. Fields lose productivity after a few crops, so profitable planting means constantly acquiring fresh acreage. Success does not let you settle down; it forces you outward.

More land → More labor: Land without workers produces nothing. Tobacco is labor-intensive at every stage — seeding, transplanting, topping, worming, cutting, curing, packing. More acres demands more hands, permanently and urgently.

More labor → More expansion → More inequality and conflict: Meeting that labor demand reshapes who comes to Virginia and on what terms. Meeting the land demand pushes settlement into territory that already belongs to somebody. Both pressures generate conflict — with Native nations over land, and within colonial society between those who own and those who work.

Why This Matters

Here is the crucial move: the crop is not the point; the chain is the point. Tobacco is just the specific thing that got the sequence going in Virginia. Later this semester you will watch the identical logic run with sugar in the Caribbean, rice in South Carolina, and cotton across the Deep South. Learn the chain now and you will recognize it every time it reappears — including in the decades leading to the Civil War.

Tobacco Creates a Land Problem

  • Tobacco exhausts the soil
  • Planters push outward for fresh acreage
  • That land was not empty
  • Expansion threatens Powhatan communities and sovereignty
Economic success made Native conflict worse, not better.

The 1622 attack as English readers were shown it — engraving, 1628

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the counterintuitive claim, and it is the one worth arguing about: Virginia's economic success is what made relations with Powhatan communities catastrophically worse. Not failure. Not starvation. Prosperity. A colony that could not feed itself was a nuisance to its neighbors. A colony that had found a profitable export was an existential threat.

Breaking It Down

Soil exhaustion: Tobacco strips nutrients quickly. Seventeenth-century planters typically got a few good years from a field before yields dropped, then cleared new ground and let the old field revert. This was not incompetence — it was rational when land seemed abundant. But it means the plantation frontier had to keep moving.

The land was not empty: Every "new" field came from somewhere. Often it came from land Powhatan communities used for their own cultivation, hunting, or gathering. English livestock roaming unfenced made it worse, trampling Native cornfields and driving off game.

What that produced: In March 1622, Opechancanough — Wahunsonacock's successor — launched a coordinated attack on English plantations spread along the James River, killing roughly a quarter of the colonial population. The attack succeeded precisely because tobacco had scattered the English across so many isolated, indefensible sites.

About the image: Look at it critically. It is a 1628 engraving made in Europe to illustrate an English account — a print, not a photograph or an eyewitness record. It shows Powhatan warriors as treacherous killers ambushing innocents, which is exactly how English readers were meant to understand the event. It is genuine evidence, but of two things at once: that the attack happened, and that the English worked hard to narrate it as unprovoked savagery rather than as a response to their own expansion.

Why This Matters

This slide is where Environment and Goals collide and produce violence. It also models a habit of mind worth developing: when a source shows you an event, ask who made the source and what they wanted you to conclude. The English print wants you to see savagery. The land record shows you a cause.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Frederic W. Gleach – Powhatan's World and Colonial Virginia (1997)

Gleach makes an argument that surprises most students: the 1622 attack was probably not intended to exterminate the English. In Powhatan political practice, a sharp, limited strike was a way to correct a subordinate group that had overstepped — to reestablish proper relations, not to annihilate. The English, operating from entirely different assumptions about war, read it as total war and responded with campaigns aimed at destroying villages and food supplies. Two political cultures misreading each other, with devastating results.

Section III

Labor Systems Build Different Societies

Who is going to do the work?

The First Answer: Indentured Servitude

A laborer works a fixed number of years in exchange for passage to America — and eventual freedom.

Why migrants accepted it

  • Passage was otherwise unaffordable
  • Freedom came at the end of the term
  • Land and independence seemed possible

Why planters wanted it

  • Labor was urgently needed
  • Servants were available from England
  • Employers controlled their labor for the term
Servants were unfree during their term — but indentured servitude was not hereditary racial slavery.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Virginia needed workers desperately and had almost no way to pay for them. Indentured servitude was the solution — a system that let people who could not afford the voyage trade several years of their labor for it. Think of it as a mortgage where the collateral is your own body and the payments are years of your life.

Breaking It Down

Indentured servant: A person who signed a contract — an indenture — to work for a master for a fixed term, usually four to seven years, in exchange for ocean passage, food, and shelter, plus "freedom dues" of goods, tools, or sometimes land at the end. Perhaps half or more of all European migrants to the colonies south of New England arrived this way.

Why migrants accepted it: Passage across the Atlantic cost more than a laboring family in England could save. England in this period had a surplus of young people, uncertain wages, and limited access to land. A young person facing that could reasonably gamble several hard years against the possibility of land and independence afterward — a prospect essentially unavailable at home.

Why planters wanted it: Labor was the bottleneck on profit, servants were available from a crowded English labor market, and the master controlled the servant's labor completely for the duration of the term.

The qualification that matters: Servants were genuinely unfree during the term. Contracts were bought and sold without the servant's consent. Masters could beat them within legal limits. Running away brought punishment by extending the term. Many died before finishing. But — and this is the crucial distinction — the term ended, the status was not inherited by children, and it was not assigned by race. Those three differences separate indentured servitude from the slavery that followed.

Why This Matters

Get this distinction precise, because a great deal of confused argument depends on blurring it. Indentured servitude was harsh, exploitative, and sometimes lethal. It was not slavery. Understanding what changed between the two — and it did change, deliberately, over decades — is the work of the next several slides.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Lois Green Carr and Russell R. Menard – Chesapeake social history

Carr and Menard reconstructed servant life chances from county records with painstaking care. Their findings cut both ways. In the earlier decades, a servant who survived the term had a genuine shot at acquiring land and even minor office — the gamble could pay. But as the century wore on, mortality, falling tobacco prices, and the engrossment of good land by established planters closed that window. The same bargain that had been a real opportunity in 1640 was much closer to a dead end by 1680. That deterioration matters enormously for the next slide.

Opportunity and Inequality Grow Together

What expands

  • Landowners accumulate more land
  • Successful planters accumulate wealth
  • Migration continues

What narrows

  • Servants supply the labor
  • Access to land grows less equal over time
  • The gap between planter and laborer widens
A theme that will return all semester: expanding opportunity for some people can coexist with — or depend upon — restrictions placed on others.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Most of us carry an assumption that opportunity and equality travel together — that a society getting richer and freer for some people is generally getting richer and freer overall. This slide asks you to consider that they can move in opposite directions, and that sometimes one is built on the other.

Breaking It Down

What expands: Established planters accumulate land, often the best-watered and best-situated acreage. Wealth concentrates among those who arrived early or arrived with capital. Migration keeps flowing in, because the promise of opportunity remains real enough to keep recruiting.

What narrows: The people supplying the labor are not the people accumulating the land. As prime land gets claimed, freed servants find the good ground taken and the remaining land distant, expensive, or dangerously exposed on the frontier. The distance between planter and laborer widens into something closer to a permanent social gap than a temporary stage of life.

The tension this creates: A society producing large numbers of armed, landless, disappointed free men who were promised independence and did not get it is a society with a stability problem. Keep that in the back of your mind — it becomes explosive later in the century, and you will meet the consequences in a future lecture.

Why This Matters

This is one of the recurring threads of the entire course, so I want to name it plainly: expanding opportunity for some people can coexist with, and sometimes depend upon, restrictions placed on others. You will see this pattern in westward expansion, in industrial growth, in immigration policy, and in the relationship between American freedom and American slavery. It is not a cynical claim that opportunity was fake. It is a historical claim that both things were real at the same time, for different people.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Edmund S. Morgan – American Slavery, American Freedom (1975)

This is one of the most influential books ever written about early America, and it argues something genuinely unsettling. Morgan asks how Virginia — the colony that produced Jefferson, Madison, and the most eloquent American language of liberty — was also the colony most thoroughly committed to slavery. His answer is that these were not a contradiction but a relationship. Replacing a restless, landless, temporarily-bound white servant class with a permanently enslaved Black labor force removed the danger that poor white men would rebel, and allowed white Virginians of every rank to understand themselves as sharing a common freedom defined against those who had none. Equality among whites, in his account, was purchased with the permanent unfreedom of others. You do not have to accept Morgan's argument wholesale, but you should be able to state it, because most subsequent scholarship on this question is a response to him.

Racial Slavery Was Constructed Over Time

Early Virginia

  • Africans present by 1619
  • Legal status less uniformly defined than it later became
  • Some conditions resembled servitude; some people became free

Across the century

  • Colonial law hardens the line between European servants and Africans
  • Slavery becomes lifelong, inheritable, and defined by race

Enslaved labor on a Virginia tobacco plantation, ca. 1670

Resist the shortcut — “Africans arrived in 1619, and slavery began.” The mature system was built gradually.
→ Independent Study: 1619 and the Historians' Debate

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is the claim to take away: American racial slavery was built. It was not imported fully formed, not established by a single decision, and not present from the first moment Africans arrived. It was assembled piece by piece, mostly through law, over roughly two generations. That is harder to remember than a single date — and far more accurate.

Breaking It Down

Early Virginia: Africans were present in Virginia by 1619, when roughly twenty people taken from a Portuguese slaving vessel were traded to the colony. Their legal status was less uniformly defined than it later became. Some appear in records in terms resembling servitude. A documented minority became free, acquired land, sued in court, and in some cases held servants themselves.

Across the century: Colonial statutes progressively hardened the line. Laws established that a child's status followed the mother's condition — making slavery inheritable. Laws specified that Christian baptism did not confer freedom — closing a route out. Laws restricted manumission, movement, assembly, weapons, and testimony. By the early eighteenth century Virginia had a comprehensive slave code defining a lifelong, hereditary, racially assigned status.

The shortcut to resist: "Africans arrived in 1619, and slavery began." That sentence compresses decades of legal construction into a single moment and makes the outcome look automatic. The evidence does not support it — and the red box on the slide is there to keep you from reaching for it.

Why This Matters

Why insist on this? Because "it was built" and "it just happened" imply different things about human responsibility. Systems that get constructed by identifiable people making identifiable choices, recorded in statutes with dates attached, are systems someone chose. That is a historical judgment, not a moral lecture — and it is what the documentary record actually shows.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Ira Berlin – Many Thousands Gone (1998)

Berlin offers a distinction worth memorizing: a society with slaves versus a slave society. In the first, slavery is one labor arrangement among several and does not organize everything else. In the second, slavery is the central institution around which economy, law, politics, and social identity are built. Early Virginia was the former; by the eighteenth century it was decisively the latter. Berlin also describes the "charter generations" — Atlantic creoles with multilingual, cross-cultural backgrounds who navigated early colonial society with more room to maneuver than their descendants would have.

T. H. Breen and Stephen Innes – "Myne Owne Ground" (1980)

Breen and Innes followed free Black landowners on Virginia's Eastern Shore in the mid-1600s — men like Anthony Johnson, who owned land, livestock, and servants. Their existence is not a feel-good story about early tolerance; it is evidence that the racial order was not yet fixed. Watching that window narrow and close over the following decades is watching racial slavery being constructed in real time.

Why Did the Labor System Change?

There is no single moment when Virginia “chose” slavery. Several developments interact.

  • Demand for labor keeps rising
  • English servants become less available later in the century
  • Atlantic slave-trading networks expand
  • Planters prefer permanent labor to temporary labor
  • Colonial law increasingly defines Africans and their descendants as enslaved
A process — not an inevitable outcome.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Students almost always want a single cause here — one reason, one moment, one decision. Resist that. The honest answer is that several developments were pushing in the same direction at once, and their interaction produced the outcome. Think of it less like a switch being flipped and more like a current gradually turning.

Breaking It Down

Rising labor demand: The tobacco chain from earlier never stopped pulling. More land under cultivation meant permanently more hands needed.

Fewer English servants: Conditions in England shifted later in the century — population growth slowed, wages improved, and other colonies competed for the same migrants. The stream of young English laborers willing to indenture themselves thinned.

Expanding slave trade: English participation in the Atlantic slave trade grew substantially, especially after the Royal African Company's founding in 1672 and the later opening of the trade to independent merchants. Enslaved Africans became more available and, over time, more affordable to mainland planters.

Planter preference for permanent labor: A servant's term expires; an enslaved person's does not. A servant's children are free; an enslaved woman's children were legally enslaved. From the planter's economic standpoint, permanent inheritable labor was simply a better asset — and that cold calculation is exactly what makes it worth examining.

Law hardening: Colonial assemblies — composed of planters — wrote the statutes that made this status permanent, heritable, and racial. The law did not passively record a change. It accomplished one.

Why This Matters

Notice the shape of the explanation: multiple causes, interacting, over decades, with human decisions at every step. That is what most serious historical explanation looks like. Single-cause stories are easier to remember and almost always wrong. If you can hold five interacting causes in your head at once, you are doing history rather than reciting it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Winthrop D. Jordan – White Over Black (1968)

Jordan argued that English attitudes toward Africans — associations with blackness, heathenism, and savagery — existed before large-scale slavery and shaped how the institution developed. He famously called the result an "unthinking decision": no one sat down and chose racial slavery, yet prejudice and economic self-interest reinforced each other until the outcome looked natural to those living inside it.

Anthony S. Parent Jr. – Foul Means (2003)

Parent pushes back on "unthinking." He argues that a relatively small planter elite acted deliberately and self-consciously between roughly 1660 and 1740 — seizing land, importing enslaved Africans, and writing the statutes that locked the system in place. Where Jordan sees cultural drift, Parent sees policy. Put Jordan and Parent side by side and you have a live scholarly debate about how much of this was chosen. That disagreement is exactly what the phrase "a process, not an inevitable outcome" is pointing at.

Push the Model Further: The West Indies

Sugar  →  Enormous profit
Heavy capital investment  →  Large plantations
Massive reliance on enslaved African labor
Virginia: the plantation society develops gradually.
Barbados: sugar drives the same pattern faster and harder from the 1640s.

Barbados divided into plantation parcels

📖 Understanding This Slide

If the four-word model is any good, it should work somewhere other than Virginia. So let's test it. Take the same variables to the Caribbean, swap tobacco for sugar, and watch what happens. The answer: the same pattern, running faster and harder — which is the best evidence that we have identified something real rather than just describing Virginia.

Breaking It Down

Sugar → enormous profit: Sugar was the most valuable commodity in the seventeenth-century Atlantic. European demand seemed bottomless, and small islands could generate wealth dwarfing anything the mainland colonies produced.

Heavy capital investment → large plantations: Here is the mechanism that makes sugar different from tobacco. Cut cane begins losing sugar content within hours, so it must be crushed and boiled almost immediately. That requires a mill, a boiling house, and a curing house on site. Tobacco needs a barn; sugar needs a factory. Only operators with serious capital could afford the plant, so small farmers sold out and estates consolidated rapidly.

Massive reliance on enslaved labor: Sugar work was brutal and lethal — punishing field labor plus dangerous work around boiling houses. Death rates were high enough that the enslaved population often could not sustain itself without continuous new importation. Barbados shifted to sugar from the 1640s and became majority-enslaved within a few decades.

The comparison: Virginia's plantation society developed gradually across most of a century. Barbados compressed a comparable transformation into roughly a generation. Same variables, different speed and intensity.

Why This Matters

Two payoffs here. First, it confirms the model travels. Second, and less obvious: the English Caribbean was not a sideshow to mainland history. Barbados ran short of land quickly, and its planters, capital, and fully-developed plantation practices moved outward — most consequentially into South Carolina. Caribbean slavery shaped mainland slavery directly.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard S. Dunn – Sugar and Slaves (1972)

Dunn's study of the English West Indian planter class documents how fast and how completely sugar reorganized these societies — land consolidated, small holders squeezed out, and an enslaved majority governed by a small, wealthy, frequently absentee elite. He also stresses the demographic catastrophe underneath the wealth: these were societies where both the enslaved and, to a lesser degree, the English died at extraordinary rates.

Sidney W. Mintz – Sweetness and Power (1985)

Mintz, an anthropologist, connects the plantation to the consumer. He traces how sugar moved from luxury to daily necessity for European working people, and argues the Caribbean plantation was an early form of industrial organization — regimented, time-disciplined, and coordinated — decades before factories in England. His point for you: the enslaved worker in Barbados and the English laborer taking sugar in tea were two ends of a single system.

Virginia and New England

Virginia

  • Commercial founding
  • Tobacco
  • Warmer, disease-prone
  • Many young migrants
  • Heavy demand for bound labor
  • Scattered plantations

New England

  • Religious and community migration
  • Farms and mixed economy
  • Healthier environment
  • More family migration
  • Greater reliance on family labor
  • Town-centered settlement
These are broad patterns, not absolute rules. Virginia had families. New England traded. The chart is a model for explaining divergence — not a description of every household.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now the payoff. Everything we have built for Virginia gets set beside New England, and the differences line up column by column. Read across the rows rather than down the lists — each pair is one of our four variables producing a different result.

Breaking It Down

Goals: Virginia was founded by a company seeking profit. New England migration was driven substantially by Puritan congregations seeking to build a godly society together — which meant recruiting whole communities rather than individual laborers.

Environment: The Chesapeake supported a high-value export staple and also carried heavy disease burdens in its tidal lowlands. New England's colder climate blocked staple agriculture but proved remarkably healthy — clean water, fewer mosquitoes, and a mortality rate so low that life expectancy rivaled or exceeded England's.

Migration: Virginia drew heavily on young single men, producing a skewed sex ratio and a population that grew mainly by continued immigration. New England received far more complete families, producing balanced sex ratios and rapid natural population growth — New England's population multiplied largely by having children who survived.

Labor: Chesapeake planters needed bound labor at scale and moved toward indentured servitude and then racial slavery. New England farms could largely be worked by families and their own children, which is a labor system requiring no bound workforce at all.

Settlement pattern: Virginia spread into dispersed plantations along navigable rivers, since each planter shipped from his own landing. New England settled in compact towns organized around a church and a common — which is why New England developed town meetings and Virginia did not.

Why This Matters

Now read the red box, because it matters as much as the chart. This is a model, not a description of every household. Virginia had families, churches, and towns. New England had merchants, servants, and — importantly — enslaved people and deep commercial ties to Caribbean slavery. Simplified comparisons are useful precisely because they give you somewhere to attach complexity later. They become a problem only when you mistake them for the whole truth.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Philip J. Greven Jr. – Four Generations (1970)

Greven reconstructed families in Andover, Massachusetts across four generations and found something striking: extraordinary longevity and large surviving families, with fathers retaining control of land — and therefore of sons' marriages and independence — well into old age. Compare that with the Chesapeake, where high mortality meant many children lost a parent before adulthood and few grandparents were around at all. Demography is not a dry topic here; it produced two different kinds of family authority and two different social orders.

Goals + Environment + Migration + Labor = Colonial Society

Goals

Virginia sought commercial profit; New England increasingly emphasized building religious communities.

Environment

Tobacco thrived in the Chesapeake; New England's conditions supported mixed farming and healthier family growth.

Migration

Virginia received many young laborers; New England received proportionately more families.

Labor

The Chesapeake turned to bound labor and ultimately racial slavery; New England relied far more on household labor.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the answer to the question we opened with. If someone asks you on an exam why English colonies became different societies, this slide is your response — four variables, each with a concrete example on both sides. Learn it as an argument you can deliver in about ninety seconds.

Breaking It Down

Goals: A colony founded to return profit to investors recruits different people, distributes land differently, and measures success differently than one founded so a congregation can build a godly community.

Environment: Tobacco thrived in the Chesapeake and nothing comparable grew in New England — which pushed one region toward staple export and the other toward mixed farming, fishing, and trade. Disease environments differed just as sharply, with enormous demographic consequences.

Migration: Young single laborers versus families. This single variable drives sex ratios, birth rates, family stability, inheritance, and how quickly a settled society could form.

Labor: The Chesapeake's demand for bound labor led to indentured servitude and then to hereditary racial slavery. New England's household farms could largely be worked by families. This is the variable with the longest reach — it is still shaping American life two centuries later.

Why This Matters

Notice these four are not independent. Goals shaped who migrated; environment shaped what economy was possible; economy shaped labor demand; labor shaped everything downstream. Explaining how they interact is what separates a good answer from a list.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David Hackett Fischer – Albion's Seed (1989)

Fischer would push back on how much weight we just placed on environment and economy. He argues that four distinct waves of British migrants — East Anglian Puritans, southern English gentry and servants to Virginia, Quakers to the Delaware Valley, and North Britons to the backcountry — carried different regional folkways with them: distinct speech, building styles, family patterns, attitudes toward authority and violence. In his account, colonies differed largely because the migrants were already culturally different before they left England. Many historians think he overstates it, but it is a serious challenge to any explanation resting mainly on soil and climate — and it is worth asking yourself which account you find more persuasive, and what evidence would settle it.

The Point Worth Keeping

Colonists did not simply reproduce England in America.

The places they entered, the people who migrated, the economies they created, and the labor systems they adopted transformed English settlers into different colonial societies.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One sentence to carry out of the room. If you remember the four words but forget everything else, this slide tells you what they were for.

Breaking It Down

"Colonists did not simply reproduce England in America": This is the claim being rejected. Colonies were not transplanted copies of the home country that slowly diverged by accident. Something actively transformed them.

The places they entered: Not empty land — occupied territory with existing politics, which shaped every subsequent relationship.

The people who migrated: Who boarded the ships determined what kind of society could form on the other side.

The economies they created: A staple export economy and a mixed farming economy generate different needs and different social structures.

The labor systems they adopted: Adopted is a deliberate word. These were choices made over time, not conditions that simply appeared.

Why This Matters

There is a broader habit of mind here worth taking with you. When you encounter a society and want to know why it looks the way it does, ask what it produces, who does the producing, on what terms, and who was already living there. Those four questions will take you a long way — not just in this course, and not just in American history.

📖 Understanding This Slide

We have used New England as a comparison all day — a useful foil for explaining Virginia. Next time it stops being the comparison and becomes the subject.

Breaking It Down

What we established: New England's difference came substantially from family migration and religious goals. But saying "religious goals" is a placeholder, not an explanation. It names a cause without unpacking it.

The question that opens: What did those settlers actually think they were building? Not merely a place to worship freely — that was available in the Netherlands, where some of them had already gone. They wanted something far more ambitious: an entire society ordered by their understanding of God's will.

Why the distinction matters: Crossing an ocean to practice a religion is a request to be left alone. Crossing an ocean to organize an entire society around one is a claim to authority over everyone in it. That difference explains an enormous amount about New England — including how it treated people who disagreed.

Why This Matters

Come to the next class with the four words in hand. We will be running the same model on New England, and you will find that "Goals" carries far more weight there than it did in Virginia — which is itself a finding worth noticing.

Why Jamestown Nearly Failed

Structural problems

  • Settlers recruited for profit, not farming
  • Brackish water and marshland siting
  • Supply ships arrived late and light
  • Authority divided among a quarrelsome council

The winter of 1609–10

  • Trade with Powhatan communities broke down
  • Mortality reached catastrophic levels
  • Survivors nearly abandoned the site entirely
The lesson is not the horror of the winter. It is that a colony organized as an investment was badly organized for survival.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is enrichment — you are not required to master it for the main argument, but it makes the main argument concrete. The question is not "why did settlers suffer" but "why was this settlement so badly designed for survival," and the answer takes you straight back to the investment model.

Breaking It Down

Recruited for profit, not farming: Early passenger lists include an unusual concentration of gentlemen and skilled craftsmen suited to finding precious metals and establishing trade. Very few had agricultural experience, and gentlemen were culturally exempt from manual labor.

Brackish water and marshland siting: Jamestown Island was chosen for defensibility against Spanish attack — a reasonable priority for a company worried about rival empires. But its water was tidal and brackish, contaminating with waste and salt, and the surrounding marsh bred mosquitoes. Tree-ring evidence indicates the colony's early years coincided with severe drought, which worsened both water quality and everyone's harvests, including Powhatan harvests.

Late and light supply ships: The colony was designed as dependent on resupply. When ships were delayed or wrecked, there was no self-sufficient fallback.

Divided authority: Governance sat with a quarreling council rather than a single executive, and the sealed instructions naming its members were not opened until arrival — a procedure almost designed to produce factional conflict.

The winter of 1609–10: The "Starving Time". Trade with Powhatan communities collapsed, the settlement was effectively besieged, and mortality reached catastrophic levels — most of the colony died. Survivors boarded ships to abandon Virginia entirely and were turned back only by the chance arrival of a relief fleet in the river.

Why This Matters

Note the lesson the slide draws, because it is the analytical point rather than the drama: a colony organized as an investment was badly organized for survival. The suffering was real, but it was a symptom. The founding design was the cause.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Karen Ordahl Kupperman – The Jamestown Project (2007)

Kupperman argues the early crisis was a failure of organization and expectation rather than of individual character. She also places Jamestown in a wider frame: England was improvising colonization with no successful model, and the painful lessons learned in Virginia — about food self-sufficiency, unified authority, and land ownership as an incentive — shaped every English colony that followed.

The Anglo-Powhatan Wars

Three phases of conflict across roughly forty years — each one tracking tobacco's expansion.

  • Early skirmishing and hostage-taking as the English press for food
  • A negotiated pause, sealed by the marriage of Pocahontas and John Rolfe
  • A coordinated Powhatan attack in 1622 after tobacco expansion accelerates
  • English retaliation aimed at destroying settlements and food crops
  • A final war in the 1640s ending with formal subordination of Powhatan communities
Read the wars as a land conflict driven by an export crop, not as a story of temperament.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Enrichment material, and the payoff is a way of reading conflict. Rather than three disconnected episodes of violence, watch these wars track the expansion of tobacco cultivation. When planting pushes outward, fighting follows.

Breaking It Down

Early skirmishing: The first years brought raids, hostage-taking, and coerced trade as the English pressed Powhatan communities for food they could not grow themselves — during a drought that left little surplus to give.

The negotiated pause: The capture of Pocahontas and her subsequent marriage to John Rolfe in 1614 produced several years of relative peace. Both sides had reason to accept it. Note the timing, though — this pause coincides almost exactly with tobacco becoming profitable, which means the peace was purchased just as the pressure behind the conflict was intensifying.

The 1622 attack: Opechancanough coordinated simultaneous strikes on plantations along the James, killing roughly a quarter of the colonists. Tobacco had dispersed the English into isolated sites — the economic pattern created the military vulnerability.

English retaliation: The response targeted villages and food supplies — burning cornfields, destroying settlements — a strategy aimed at making the land uninhabitable for its inhabitants. English writers also used the attack to argue that coexistence was impossible and dispossession justified.

The final war: In the 1640s an aged Opechancanough launched one more large attack. It failed. The 1646 treaty formally subordinated Powhatan communities, set boundaries, and imposed tribute — converting an independent power into a dependent one.

Why This Matters

Read these as a land conflict driven by an export crop, not as a story about temperament or inevitable cultural clash. And notice the shape of it, because you will see it again: expansion, resistance, retaliation framed as justified defense, treaty, further expansion. That sequence recurs across the continent for the next two and a half centuries.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Frederic W. Gleach – Powhatan's World and Colonial Virginia (1997)

Gleach reads Powhatan military action through Powhatan political logic: a sharp limited strike as a way of correcting a subordinate that had overstepped, not a bid for extermination. The English, whose assumptions about war were entirely different, read it as annihilation attempted and responded in kind. Cultural misreading with catastrophic consequences.

Helen C. Rountree – Pocahontas, Powhatan, Opechancanough (2005)

Rountree tells this history through three Powhatan lives, insisting on Native leaders as strategists making calculated decisions under worsening constraints — not as figures in an English story. Her Pocahontas in particular bears little resemblance to the popular version, and the difference is instructive about how national myths get built.

1619 and the Historians' Debate

What is documented

  • Africans taken from a Portuguese slaving vessel arrive in Virginia in 1619
  • Records describe some as servants; a few later appear as landholders
  • No Virginia statute yet defines lifelong hereditary slavery

What historians dispute

  • Whether racial prejudice preceded slavery or hardened alongside it
  • How much early legal ambiguity actually protected anyone
  • When the system becomes recognizably “slavery” rather than extreme servitude
Both the “it began in 1619” story and the “nothing was settled for decades” story can flatten the evidence. The defensible claim is that the system was built.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide shows you something valuable: historians genuinely disagreeing, in print, for decades, about a question the evidence does not fully settle. That is not a flaw in the discipline. That is the discipline.

Breaking It Down

What is documented: In 1619 roughly twenty Africans, taken from a Portuguese slaving vessel intercepted by English privateers, were brought to Virginia and traded for supplies. Surviving records describe some in terms used for servants. A documented minority later appear as free landholders. No Virginia statute at that date defines lifelong hereditary slavery.

What is disputed — did prejudice come first? Some scholars argue English racial attitudes preceded slavery and shaped it. Others argue the labor system came first and racial ideology developed afterward to justify it. This is close to a chicken-and-egg problem, and the evidence supports partial versions of both.

What is disputed — how much did early ambiguity protect anyone? Free Black landowners existed. But a handful of documented cases across a large population may indicate a genuinely open window, or an exception that a few unusual people managed to exploit. Historians read the same records and reach different conclusions.

What is disputed — when does it become "slavery"? If a person is held for life without a contract but no statute yet exists, is that slavery or extreme servitude? Where you draw that line determines what date you give.

Why This Matters

Both simple stories distort. "Slavery began in 1619" compresses decades of legal construction into a single arrival and makes it look automatic. "Nothing was settled for decades" can soften a reality that was already brutal for most Africans in Virginia from the beginning. The defensible claim — the one the evidence supports — is that the system was built, deliberately, over roughly two generations.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Oscar and Mary Handlin – "Origins of the Southern Labor System" (1950)

The Handlins argued that slavery grew out of servitude as planters gradually extended terms and degraded conditions for Africans, and that racial ideology followed as justification rather than leading the way. Economics first, racism second.

Winthrop D. Jordan – White Over Black (1968)

Jordan replied that English culture already carried powerful negative associations with blackness and with non-Christian peoples before mass slavery existed, and that these attitudes shaped the institution from the outset. His "unthinking decision" formula tries to capture something subtle: prejudice and profit reinforcing one another so steadily that no single moment of choice is visible.

Ira Berlin – Many Thousands Gone (1998)

Berlin reframes the whole question by distinguishing a society with slaves from a slave society. Asking "when did slavery begin" may be the wrong question; the better one is when slavery moved from being one arrangement among several to the institution organizing everything else. For Virginia, that transition happens well after 1619.

The Barbados Sugar Revolution

Why sugar concentrated wealth

  • Cane must be milled and boiled within hours of cutting
  • That requires a mill, boiling house, and curing house on site
  • Only large operators could afford the plant
  • Small farmers sold out; estates consolidated

Why it mattered on the mainland

  • Barbados ran short of land within a generation
  • Planters and their capital moved outward
  • Caribbean plantation practice shaped mainland English colonies
Sugar shows the plantation-labor model in its most intensive early English form — the same variables as Virginia, turned up.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Enrichment, and it answers a question the main lecture raises without fully explaining: why did sugar concentrate wealth and land so much faster than tobacco? The answer is essentially industrial, and once you see it the whole pattern makes sense.

Breaking It Down

The processing constraint: Cut sugarcane starts losing sugar content within hours. It must be crushed and the juice boiled almost immediately, which means processing has to happen on the plantation itself.

The capital requirement: That means a mill to crush cane, a boiling house with a battery of copper kettles, and a curing house — an integrated industrial facility, plus the skilled labor to run it. This is enormously expensive compared with tobacco, which needs a drying barn and little else.

Consolidation: A small farmer could grow tobacco profitably. A small farmer could not build a sugar works. So small holders sold out to those who could, and land concentrated into large estates with startling speed. Within roughly a generation, Barbados transformed from a place of modest farms into an island of large plantations worked by an enslaved majority.

The mainland connection: Barbados is small and ran out of land quickly. Planters, younger sons, capital, and — critically — a fully developed set of plantation practices and slave codes moved outward. A substantial share of early Carolina settlers came from Barbados, arriving with the plantation model already assembled rather than developing it from scratch.

Why This Matters

Sugar shows the plantation-labor model in its most intensive early English form — the same four variables as Virginia with every dial turned up. And the mainland connection matters for the rest of the course: when South Carolina develops the harshest slave regime on the mainland, it is not inventing something new. It is importing something already built in the Caribbean.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Richard S. Dunn – Sugar and Slaves (1972)

Dunn documents how completely and how quickly sugar reorganized English Caribbean society, and insists on the demographic reality beneath the spectacular wealth: these were societies of extraordinary mortality, sustained only by continuous importation of enslaved people.

Russell R. Menard – Sweet Negotiations (2006)

Menard complicates the idea of a sudden "revolution," arguing the shift was more gradual and more the product of accumulated local experiment — including the credit networks and Dutch commercial connections that made the capital available. A useful reminder that even our best models smooth out a messier reality.

Indentured Servant

A person who signed a contract — an indenture — agreeing to work for a master for a set number of years, usually four to seven, in exchange for ocean passage, food, and shelter, with a payment of goods or land at the end.

During the term the servant was genuinely unfree: the contract could be sold to another master, and running away was punished by extending the term. But the term ended, the status was not inherited by children, and it was not assigned by race. Those three differences are what separate it from the slavery that followed.

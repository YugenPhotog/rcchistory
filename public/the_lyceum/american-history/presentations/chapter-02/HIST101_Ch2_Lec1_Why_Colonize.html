Fifty-Seven Years Apart

England, 1550

  • No American colonies
  • Religious identity unsettled
  • Little Atlantic power

England, 1607

  • Firmly Protestant
  • A major rival of Spain
  • Jamestown established
Why did England begin establishing permanent colonies in North America?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two snapshots of the same country, fifty-seven years apart. In 1550 England has no American colonies, no settled religious identity, and very little presence in the Atlantic. By 1607 it is firmly Protestant, locked in rivalry with Spain, and planting a colony at Jamestown. Nothing on this slide explains the change — that is deliberate. The gap between the columns is the question.

Breaking It Down

No American colonies (1550): This is easy to forget because we know how the story ends. But at mid-century England was a second-rank power watching Spain grow rich off an American empire it had held for decades.

Religious identity unsettled: England had changed its official religion three times in about twenty years. Which church you belonged to depended on which monarch was alive.

Little Atlantic power: English ships traded and fished, but England had nothing resembling Spain’s network of ports, fleets, and colonial administration.

Why This Matters

Notice the shape of the question. It is not “when did England colonize?” or “who founded Jamestown?” It asks why — and that means the answer will be a set of causes, not a date. Everything in this lecture is an attempt to fill the space between those two columns. Keep the question in front of you; you will be asked to answer it before class ends.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is your framework for the entire lecture: four forces, each with a question attached. Think of them as four lenses. Most historical events look different depending on which one you hold up, and the honest answer to “why did England colonize?” requires more than one.

Breaking It Down

Religion — what England came to believe, and how belief became bound up with being English.

Rivalry — the competition with Catholic Spain that pulled England into the Atlantic.

Profit — what there was to gain, and how the shape of the profit changed over time.

Opportunity — why ordinary people would actually board a ship, which is a different question from why a government wanted colonies.

Why This Matters

The line “there is no single answer” is not a hedge. It is the argument. Students often want history to work like a math problem with one correct cause. What you are being trained to do instead is hold several causes at once and explain how they interacted. When these four words come back at the end of the lecture, you should be able to say something specific under each one.

Section I

Religion Remade England

What happens to a country when it changes its church?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section I takes up the first of the four forces: religion. The divider question — what happens to a country when it changes its church? — is worth sitting with. In the sixteenth century a church was not simply a place of worship. It held land, ran courts, shaped education, and told people what their monarch’s authority rested on. Changing it changed everything attached to it.

Four Monarchs, One Generation

Henry VIII breaks with Rome  →  Edward VI goes further
Mary I returns to Rome  →  Elizabeth I settles it after 1558
The Tudor family drama is not the point. The English Reformation tied religion to national identity.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Four monarchs, one generation, and a country that reverses its official religion repeatedly before settling. The chain on the screen is compressed on purpose. What matters is not memorizing which Tudor did what, but seeing how fast a fundamental part of English life changed — and what that speed did to how the English understood themselves.

Breaking It Down

Henry VIII breaks with Rome: He separated the English church from papal authority and made the monarch its head. His motives were largely dynastic and political rather than theological — a point worth remembering, because it means the English Reformation did not begin as a popular religious awakening.

Edward VI goes further: Under Henry’s young son, England moved substantially further toward Protestant belief and practice.

Mary I returns to Rome: Henry’s eldest daughter restored Catholicism. This reversal matters — it shows the outcome was genuinely uncertain.

Elizabeth I settles it after 1558: Under Elizabeth a durable Protestant settlement took hold. “Durable” is the operative word: it lasted, and that endurance is what let religion harden into identity.

Why This Matters

The callout says the Tudor family drama is not the point, and it means it. The point is that within roughly one generation, being English and being Protestant became difficult to pull apart. Once that fusion happens, a conflict with Catholic Spain is no longer just a dispute over trade routes or territory. It becomes a conflict about what England is. That is what turns religion into a cause of colonization rather than a private matter.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Eamon Duffy – The Stripping of the Altars

Duffy pushed back hard on the tidy version of this story. He argued that traditional Catholicism in England was vigorous and popular right up to the Reformation, and that the change was imposed from above and resisted from below far longer than older accounts admitted. Hold his argument alongside this slide: the chain on screen shows the official religion changing quickly, but Duffy would remind you that what ordinary people actually believed changed much more slowly and unevenly.

Diarmaid MacCulloch – The Reformation: A History

MacCulloch places England inside a Europe-wide upheaval rather than treating it as a national curiosity. That framing helps here, because it explains why the English Reformation immediately had foreign-policy consequences. England was not choosing a religion in isolation; it was picking a side in a continental conflict that already had armies attached to it.

Religion Becomes Foreign Policy

  • Catholic
  • Extremely powerful
  • Dominant in the Americas
Protestant England increasingly defined itself against Catholic Spain.

Elizabeth's hand rests on the globe — the claim is deliberate

Puritans — Protestants who thought the English church was not reformed far enough. England's settlement stayed contested. We stop here; their story is Lecture 3.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is where religion stops being an internal English matter and becomes a reason to go to sea. Spain was Catholic, enormously powerful, and already dominant in the Americas. For Protestant England, that combination made Spain simultaneously a religious opponent and a strategic threat — and those two things reinforced each other.

Breaking It Down

Catholic: Spain was the leading Catholic power in Europe and understood itself as a defender of the faith.

Extremely powerful: Spanish armies and Spanish money were decisive in European conflicts. This was not an even match.

Dominant in the Americas: Spain had been extracting American wealth for decades before England attempted anything permanent.

The Armada Portrait: Look closely at the image. English ships sail in calm water on one side; Spanish ships wreck in a storm on the other; the queen’s hand rests on a globe between them. The painting is making an argument — that England’s survival was divinely favored and its reach was global. Paintings like this are evidence of what people wanted believed, which is its own kind of historical fact.

Why This Matters

Once opposition to Spain becomes part of English identity, actions that look purely economic start carrying religious weight too. Raiding a Spanish treasure ship could be described as theft, as patriotism, or as service to God — and Elizabethan English people described it as all three. That overlap is what you will see in the next section.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Patrick Collinson – The Elizabethan Puritan Movement

Collinson’s work is the reason the caution box on this slide exists. He showed that the Elizabethan settlement did not end English religious argument — it relocated it. Puritans were Protestants who believed the Church of England had kept too much of its Catholic inheritance and needed further reform. Keep them in the back of your mind: they will matter enormously in Lecture 3, when we look at why some English people crossed an ocean to build a society rather than merely to make money in one.

⏸ Pause & Reflect

Which connection between religion and colonization is strongest?

A. Escape religious conflict

B. Spread Protestant influence

C. Challenge Catholic Spain

Choose one. Defend it in a sentence.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A genuine question with no answer key. All three options are defensible, and the point of the exercise is the defending, not the choosing.

Breaking It Down

A. Escape religious conflict — strongest for groups who felt squeezed by the English settlement. Weakest as an explanation of why the Crown or investors cared.

B. Spread Protestant influence — this was an argument colonial promoters genuinely made. It sounds like decoration to modern ears; it was not treated as decoration at the time.

C. Challenge Catholic Spain — the strongest fit for state policy and for the raiding we are about to study, since it links religion to something with ships and money behind it.

Why This Matters

If you found yourself wanting to pick more than one, that is the lesson. Historical causes are rarely exclusive. A good answer here names a connection, states why it is strongest, and acknowledges what it leaves unexplained. That structure — claim, reason, limitation — is what strong historical writing looks like.

Section II

Rivalry and Profit Pull England Outward

Why chase an empire that someone else has already built?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section II turns to rivalry and profit together, because in practice they were inseparable. The divider question is the genuinely puzzling one: if Spain had already built a wealthy American empire, why would England chase it rather than avoid it? The answer turns out to be that Spain’s empire was both the threat and the proof of concept.

Spain's Head Start

Territories, mines, shipping networks, ports, administration — and enormous wealth.

A threat

  • Wealth strengthened England's rival
  • Funded Spanish armies

An opportunity

  • Proof that expansion paid
  • A model worth copying
Rivalry and profit stop being separate motives. They begin reinforcing each other.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Spain’s American empire looked different depending on where an English observer stood. Seen one way it was frightening: it made an already dangerous rival richer. Seen another way it was inviting: it demonstrated that overseas expansion could generate spectacular wealth. Both readings were true at once, and both pushed in the same direction — toward the Atlantic.

Breaking It Down

Territories, mines, shipping networks, ports, administration: Notice that this is a list of infrastructure, not just treasure. Spain had built the machinery to move American wealth across an ocean reliably. That is much harder than finding silver.

A threat: Every peso that reached Spain could fund armies pointed at Protestant Europe. English hostility to Spanish wealth was not envy alone; it was strategic.

An opportunity: Before Spain, no European state knew that transatlantic empire was possible. Afterward, everyone did. Spain absorbed the risk of proving the concept.

Why This Matters

The callout is the slide’s real content: rivalry and profit stop being separate motives here. Attacking Spanish wealth weakened an enemy and enriched the attacker in a single act. When two motives point the same way, they are much harder to disentangle — which is exactly the problem you will face when you try to say why any individual English person supported colonization.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

J. H. Elliott – Empires of the Atlantic World

Elliott compares Spanish and British America side by side and argues that the differences between them came substantially from timing and from what each encountered on arrival. His point is useful here: England did not simply choose a different path from Spain’s. It arrived a century later, at a different stage of its own development, and had to work around an Atlantic that Spain had already organized. Being second shaped English colonization as much as any English intention did.

Easier to Raid Than to Build

Privateering — privately financed ships, authorized by the government, attacking enemy shipping.

One man, three verdicts

  • To Spain: a pirate
  • To England: a patriot
  • To investors: a return
Francis Drake was all three at once. Which label fits?

A raider painted as a gentleman — the globe and the jewel do the work

📖 Understanding This Slide

England’s first serious Atlantic strategy was not to build an empire. It was to rob one. Privateering gave English mariners a way to profit from Spanish wealth decades before England could finance, supply, and sustain colonies of its own — and it made Francis Drake a figure who cannot be described with one word.

Breaking It Down

Privateering: Privately financed ships, authorized by a government, attacking enemy shipping and keeping a share of what they took. The authorization is what separates a privateer from a pirate — and it is granted by the state that profits from the raid.

To Spain: a pirate. From the Spanish side these were criminal attacks on lawful commerce, and Spain said so repeatedly.

To England: a patriot. The same voyages weakened a dangerous rival at no cost to the Crown.

To investors: a return. Voyages were funded like ventures, and successful ones paid.

Why This Matters

Drake is on this slide because he makes a hard idea concrete. The same career carried private profit, national rivalry, and religious hostility simultaneously, and no single label captures it. When you are later asked why England colonized, remember Drake: asking which motive was “really” behind an Elizabethan Atlantic voyage often misunderstands how those people thought. Look at the portrait too — the globe and the jewel are arguing that a raider is a gentleman.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Harry Kelsey – Sir Francis Drake: The Queen’s Pirate

Kelsey wrote deliberately against the heroic Drake of English tradition, emphasizing self-interest, ruthlessness, and the plain fact that plunder was the business. You do not have to accept his portrait wholesale, but it is a useful corrective. The version of Drake you meet in popular history was constructed later, partly by paintings like the one on this slide.

The Armada, 1588

What was the Spanish Armada? Philip II's invasion fleet, sent to control the English Channel so a Spanish army could cross from the Netherlands, overthrow Elizabeth I, and restore Catholic rule.

What it did

  • Showed how serious the rivalry was
  • Reinforced English confidence
  • Extended the war into the Atlantic
And it helped delay John White's return to Roanoke.

Why the Spanish Armada Really Failed in 1588 — Seek Learning

Resist the shortcut — the Armada did not create English Atlantic ambitions. Raleigh's Roanoke ventures were already underway before 1588.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Spanish Armada of 1588 is the most famous event in this lecture and the one most likely to be misunderstood. This slide is arranged to give you the drama and then immediately take away the wrong conclusion.

In 1588, Philip II of Spain dispatched a fleet of roughly 130 ships up the English Channel. The Armada's task was to gain enough control of the Channel to rendezvous with the Duke of Parma's veteran army in the Spanish Netherlands and escort it across for an invasion of England. Philip intended to depose the Protestant Elizabeth I, stop English support for Dutch rebels and attacks on Spanish shipping, and return England to Catholic alignment. English attacks disrupted the fleet near Calais and Gravelines; unable to link with Parma, the surviving ships retreated north around Scotland and Ireland, where storms, shortages, and shipwrecks caused further losses. Spain remained a formidable European power: the Armada's defeat neither destroyed Spanish naval power nor ended the Anglo-Spanish War, which continued until 1604.

Breaking It Down

Showed how serious the rivalry was: Philip II committed an invasion fleet. This was not skirmishing; it was an attempt to settle the Anglo-Spanish conflict permanently.

Reinforced English confidence: Survival was interpreted at the time as evidence of divine favor — which is precisely what the Armada Portrait two slides back was built to say.

Extended the war into the Atlantic: This is the consequence that matters most for us. An ongoing naval war consumed English ships, crews, and attention for years afterward.

The embedded video: Why the Spanish Armada Really Failed in 1588 replaces the earlier fireship painting. Use it to reinforce that the Armada's defeat had several causes rather than one dramatic turning point, then reconnect the naval campaign to the longer war that delayed John White's return to Roanoke.

Why This Matters

Read the caution box carefully, because it is doing real historical work. It is tempting to build a clean story in which England defeats the Armada, gains confidence, and sails off to found colonies. That story is backwards. Raleigh’s Roanoke ventures were already underway before 1588. The Armada’s actual effect on colonization was almost the opposite of inspiring: the war it extended is part of why John White could not get back to Roanoke with supplies. Watch for this pattern all semester — famous events often matter for reasons other than the ones we remember them for.

⏸ Pause & Predict

England can raid Spanish shipping successfully.
England wants lasting wealth from the Atlantic.

What would England have to do differently to get it?

📖 Understanding This Slide

A prediction exercise. You have everything you need: England can raid successfully, and England wants lasting Atlantic wealth. The question asks what would have to change for the second goal to be met.

Breaking It Down

Useful things to notice. Raiding is episodic — a voyage sails, seizes, and returns. Lasting wealth requires something that keeps producing after the ships go home. Raiding takes wealth that someone else has already created; producing wealth means creating it yourself, which requires land, workers, and time. And raiding depends on there being an enemy to rob, which means it stops paying the moment peace arrives.

Why This Matters

If your prediction involved settling people permanently, financing them over years, and finding something worth growing or trading, you have anticipated the next section. That is not a trick — it is the point. The requirements of permanent colonization are discoverable by reasoning, and the Elizabethans discovered them the expensive way.

Section III

Wanting a Colony Is Not Having One

What does a permanent colony actually require?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section III is the hinge of the lecture. The first two sections explained why England wanted colonies. This one asks why wanting them was not enough — and the answer explains why permanent English settlement arrives so late, roughly a century after Spain’s.

Two Very Different Projects

A raid needs

  • Ships
  • Sailors
  • Weapons
  • A target

A colony needs

  • Settlers and financing
  • Food and leadership
  • Years of resupply
  • Workable relations with Native peoples
Attacking another empire is easier than building your own.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Two columns, two completely different undertakings. The comparison looks simple, and that simplicity is the tool. Once you see how short the left column is and how long the right one is, the puzzle of England’s slow start answers itself.

Breaking It Down

A raid needs ships, sailors, weapons, a target. All four are available for hire or purchase, the venture lasts months, and success is measured once when the ship comes home.

A colony needs settlers and financing: not one payment but a sustained flow of money over years, with no return for a long time.

Food and leadership: people have to eat every day, and someone must hold authority when things go badly.

Years of resupply: the colony must be reachable and reachable repeatedly, across an ocean, in the face of weather and war.

Workable relations with Native peoples: English settlements were planted in places that already had populations, politics, and territorial claims. Nothing else on the list functions if this one fails.

Why This Matters

The callout — attacking another empire is easier than building your own — is one of the lines to take out of this lecture. It reframes England’s late start. England was not slow because it lacked ambition or ships. It was slow because permanent colonization is a fundamentally harder problem than raiding, and no amount of enthusiasm substitutes for solving it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Kenneth R. Andrews – Trade, Plunder and Settlement

Andrews traced English overseas expansion from maritime raiding and trade toward permanent settlement, and argued it was not the execution of a coherent imperial plan. The title alone captures this slide’s argument: plunder came before settlement, and moving between them required capacities England had to build rather than intentions it had to form.

Roanoke, 1585–1590

  • 1585 — Raleigh sponsors a first attempt
  • 1587 — a second settlement, including women
  • White sails home for supplies
  • War with Spain delays him
  • 1590 — abandoned, colonists gone
CROATOAN carved at the site. Bringing women in 1587 shows the intent was permanence.

White mapped a coast already full of towns

📖 Understanding This Slide

The most famous failed colony in English America, told as a sequence rather than a mystery. Watch the dates: two attempts, a supply voyage home, a delay, and a return to an empty settlement.

Breaking It Down

1585 — Raleigh sponsors a first attempt: Sir Walter Raleigh financed and organized the venture. Notice that it is a private undertaking with royal authorization, not a state project.

1587 — a second settlement, including women: This detail carries more weight than it appears to. A garrison of men is an outpost. Bringing families signals an intention to stay.

White sails home for supplies: Governor John White returned to England — itself evidence that the settlement could not yet feed and supply itself.

War with Spain delays him: The Anglo-Spanish conflict from the previous section reaches directly into this story and consumes the shipping White needed.

1590 — abandoned, colonists gone: White returned to find the settlement empty and the word CROATOAN carved at the site.

Why This Matters

Look hard at John White’s map. He was the colony’s governor and also its artist, and the coastline he drew is dotted with named Native towns. That is the visual answer to a question students often carry into this course: what was here before? The English were not mapping empty land. They were mapping an inhabited coast whose communities they needed and, increasingly, threatened.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Karen Ordahl Kupperman – Roanoke: The Abandoned Colony

Kupperman moved the conversation away from the disappearance and toward the logistics. Her Roanoke is a story about supply, timing, financing, and relationships with neighbors — ordinary problems that proved fatal because they had to be solved simultaneously and across three thousand miles of ocean. Her framing is the reason the next slide exists.

Why Was a Permanent Colony So Hard?

  • Financing and resupply
  • Food
  • Leadership
  • Settlers willing to stay
  • Transatlantic communication
  • Knowledge of local conditions
  • Relations with Native communities
One failure could threaten everything. A royal charter and a few ships did not make a colony.
→ Independent Study: Why Roanoke Failed

📖 Understanding This Slide

A deliberate redirection. The disappearance of the Roanoke colonists is genuinely interesting, and it is also a dead end for our purposes — the evidence will not settle it. The productive question is the one we can answer, and it turns out to explain far more.

Breaking It Down

Read the two columns as a single list of simultaneous requirements. Financing and resupply had to continue for years with no profit. Food had to be grown or traded for, in an unfamiliar environment, immediately. Leadership had to hold when conditions deteriorated. Settlers willing to stay had to be recruited and kept. Transatlantic communication took months each way, so decisions were made on old information. Knowledge of local conditions had to be acquired fast, usually from the people already there. Relations with Native communities determined access to food, land, and information.

Why This Matters

The callout is the lecture’s core insight about capability: one failure could threaten everything, because these requirements were not independent. Lose your relationship with neighboring communities and you lose your food supply; lose resupply and you lose your settlers’ willingness to stay. A royal charter and a few ships did not make a colony. That is why Roanoke marks the distance between wanting colonies and being able to keep them — and why the last part of this lecture is about people rather than ambition.

Colonies Need People

So why would anyone leave?

Pressure at home

  • Population growth
  • Enclosure and displacement
  • Underemployment
  • Crowded, fast-growing cities

Possibility abroad

  • Land
  • Work
  • Advancement
  • A new community
America seemed to offer what England could not offer equally to everyone.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A shift in perspective, and an important one. Everything so far has been about what monarchs, investors, and captains wanted. This slide asks a different question: why would an ordinary English person actually get on a ship? Governments cannot colonize without volunteers.

Breaking It Down

Population growth: England’s population was rising, which meant more people competing for the same land and work.

Enclosure and displacement: Enclosure converted land that had been held or used in common into private, fenced holdings. For families who had depended on common rights, this removed a livelihood.

Underemployment: not the absence of work but not enough of it — seasonal, irregular, insufficient to build a life on.

Crowded, fast-growing cities: displaced rural people moved toward towns, which grew faster than they could absorb them.

The right column is the pull: land, work, advancement, and a new community. These are ordinary human ambitions, and that is the point.

Why This Matters

The callout is carefully worded: America seemed to offer what England could not offer equally to everyone. Read that as a claim about distribution rather than absolute scarcity. England was not empty of opportunity; it was distributing opportunity in a way that left many young people without a realistic path to land or independence. That is a very different explanation from simple desperation, and it will matter when you meet indentured servitude in Lecture 2.

One Word, Many Journeys

700,000+
Europeans and Africans crossing to England's North American and Caribbean colonies in the seventeenth century

Migration split by destination

  • Chesapeake and Caribbean — young, single, servants
  • New England — more families

📖 Understanding This Slide

One statistic and one crucial complication. More than seven hundred thousand people crossed to England’s North American and Caribbean colonies during the seventeenth century — and that single number conceals almost entirely different lives.

Breaking It Down

700,000+: Note the phrasing on the slide, “Europeans and Africans.” Not everyone in this figure chose to come. The same movement of people that meant opportunity for some was forced transportation for others, and the number does not distinguish between them.

Chesapeake and Caribbean — young, single, servants: predominantly young men, many bound by labor contracts, arriving alone.

New England — more families: a proportionately greater stream of households migrating together.

Migration: a single word covering a young servant with no property and a family arriving with resources and a plan. Their experiences had almost nothing in common.

Why This Matters

The callout carries the lecture’s answer to the capability problem. Roanoke could vanish; Jamestown could suffer terrible mortality and still receive more people. What made colonization permanent was not any single successful expedition but a continuous stream — a colony could fail badly and still be replenished. And the split between those streams is the seed of Lecture 2: different people, arriving for different reasons, would build different societies.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Alison Games – Migration and the Origins of the English Atlantic World

Games worked closely from port records and found migrants who were strikingly young and already mobile — people who had often moved within England before they moved across an ocean. Her work supports the caution box on this slide. Crossing the Atlantic was frequently one more step for people already accustomed to moving in search of work, not a final act of desperation.

David Cressy – Coming Over

Cressy studied the traffic between England and New England and emphasized how much contact continued after migration — letters, goods, return voyages, and people crossing more than once. That matters here because it complicates the picture of migration as one-way departure. For many, the Atlantic was a route they expected to use again.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The lecture’s payoff exercise, in two moves. First match four people to four motives — which is easy. Then complicate the matches — which is the actual work.

Breaking It Down

A London merchant maps most obviously onto profit. The English Crown onto rivalry. A Puritan family onto religion. A young migrant onto opportunity. Those are reasonable first answers.

Now the second column. Could the merchant want Spain weakened? Certainly — Spanish power threatened English trade directly, so his commercial interest and the national rivalry point the same way. Could the Puritan family want land? Of course; building a religious community requires somewhere to build it, and land meant survival and independence. Could the Crown care about trade? Very much — customs revenue and national wealth were matters of state.

Why This Matters

The closing question — so what was the reason England colonized? — is a trap, and you should be able to explain why. Every clean match dissolves on inspection. Real historical actors held several motives at once, and those motives reinforced rather than competed with each other. If you can articulate that, you can answer this lecture’s essential question properly rather than picking a favorite cause.

Religion + Rivalry + Profit + Opportunity

Religion

Protestantism became national identity, and Catholic Spain became the opposite of England.

Rivalry

Competing with Spain pushed England into the Atlantic, first to raid and then to settle.

Profit

Quick plunder gave way to slower returns from land, trade, and commodities.

Opportunity

Pressure at home and possibility abroad supplied the people colonies required.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The four words from the roadmap, now filled in. If you were building an answer to the essential question, this slide is your outline — one claim under each heading.

Breaking It Down

Religion: Protestantism became national identity, and Catholic Spain became England’s opposite. This is the work of Section I: a change of church became a change in what England understood itself to be.

Rivalry: Competing with Spain pushed England into the Atlantic, first to raid and then to settle. Note the sequence — raiding came first because it was achievable first.

Profit: Quick plunder gave way to slower returns from land, trade, and commodities. The motive did not disappear; it changed shape, and the new shape required permanence.

Opportunity: Pressure at home and possibility abroad supplied the people that colonies required. Without this, the other three produce ambition without settlement.

Why This Matters

Notice that these are not four parallel causes sitting side by side. They interlock. Religion supplied the hostility, rivalry supplied the target, profit supplied the funding, and opportunity supplied the people — and colonization required all four to arrive together. If you are asked to explain England’s late start, the honest answer is that any one of these alone was insufficient.

The Point Worth Keeping

England did not colonize because everyone agreed on one national goal.

Monarchs wanted power. Merchants wanted profit. Religious migrants wanted communities. Servants and sailors wanted a future. Colonization happened where those forces converged.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One idea, stated once. If you remember nothing else from this lecture, remember this: England did not colonize because everyone agreed on a national goal. There was no plan that different groups signed on to.

Breaking It Down

Monarchs wanted power — standing against Spain, revenue, prestige. Merchants wanted profit and were investing money they expected to see returned. Religious migrants wanted communities they could organize according to their beliefs. Servants and sailors wanted a future — land, wages, independence, something England was not offering them.

These groups were not pursuing the same objective. They were pursuing different objectives that happened, for a few decades, to be achievable through the same activity.

Why This Matters

The word doing the work is converged. Convergence is not agreement. It means separate interests pointing the same direction for a time — which also implies they could later point apart. Hold onto this, because it explains something Lecture 2 takes up directly: if colonization was driven by different people wanting different things, we should expect the colonies they built to differ from one another. And they did.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The handoff. Lecture 1 answered why colonization happened. Lecture 2 asks why it produced such different results.

Breaking It Down

The question on screen is genuinely puzzling, and it is worth feeling that before the next lecture. Virginia and New England were settled by people from the same country, in the same century, along the same coastline, speaking the same language and carrying the same legal traditions. Within a few decades they had developed strikingly different economies, labor systems, and social structures.

If national origin explained colonial society, that should not have happened.

Why This Matters

You already have part of the answer from today. Different people came for different reasons — young servants to the Chesapeake, families to New England. Carry that forward. Lecture 2 adds what those settlers found when they arrived and what they decided to do about it.

Everything past this point is for your independent study hour rather than class time. These four slides go deeper on questions the lecture raised but could not develop: why Roanoke actually failed, what colonial promoters argued, where English colonial habits were formed before America, and what was pushing people out of England. Work through them on your own; they are written to be read rather than presented.

Why Roanoke Failed

What had to work

  • Continuous financing
  • Reliable resupply across an ocean
  • Food the settlers could actually grow
  • Leadership that held together

What went wrong

  • White's resupply was delayed for years
  • War with Spain consumed English shipping
  • The colony had to survive unaided meanwhile
The lesson is not the mystery of the disappearance. It is that permanence required every one of these to hold at once — and a single failure could undo the rest.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A closer look at the requirements list from the lecture, applied to the case that failed. Read the two columns against each other: the left is what had to work, the right is what we know went wrong.

Breaking It Down

Continuous financing: Roanoke was privately sponsored, which meant its survival depended on one man’s continued willingness and ability to pay.

Reliable resupply: The settlement was not self-sufficient. It needed ships to arrive on a schedule, and ocean crossings did not run on schedules.

Food the settlers could grow: Familiar English crops and farming knowledge did not transfer neatly to an unfamiliar environment.

Leadership that held: Authority had to survive hunger, fear, and disagreement about what to do next.

White’s resupply delayed for years: He left in 1587 and did not return until 1590.

War with Spain consumed English shipping: The Anglo-Spanish conflict absorbed exactly the vessels a supply voyage required.

Why This Matters

The callout states the lesson plainly: permanence required every one of these to hold at once, and a single failure could undo the rest. This is why Roanoke belongs in a lecture about causes rather than mysteries. It is the clearest available demonstration that ambition, authorization, and ships were not enough.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David Beers Quinn – Set Fair for Roanoke

Quinn spent much of his career reconstructing the Roanoke voyages in detail from surviving records. His work is the foundation most later accounts build on, and its value here is its refusal to treat the colony as a puzzle. Read closely, Roanoke is a well-documented sequence of decisions, delays, and constraints — and the documented parts explain the failure without requiring the undocumented ones.

The Case Made for Colonies

English promoters argued colonies would do several things at once.

  • Weaken Spain
  • Spread Protestant influence
  • Expand English trade
  • Provide new markets
  • Create opportunities for settlers
Notice that this single list contains all four forces. Religion, rivalry, profit, and opportunity were never separate arguments — contemporaries bundled them deliberately.

📖 Understanding This Slide

What did colonial promoters actually argue? This slide lists the case as it was made at the time — and the striking thing is how many different appeals appear in a single list.

Breaking It Down

Weaken Spain: a strategic argument aimed at the state.

Spread Protestant influence: a religious argument. Modern readers often assume this was decoration added to make profit-seeking respectable. Treat that assumption carefully — it was not received as decoration.

Expand English trade and provide new markets: commercial arguments aimed at merchants and investors, and at a Crown that collected customs revenue.

Create opportunities for settlers: a social argument, addressing the pressures on ordinary English people that the lecture covered.

Why This Matters

The callout is the payoff, and it is worth pausing over. This single list contains all four forces from the roadmap. Religion, rivalry, profit, and opportunity were not four separate arguments made by four separate groups — contemporaries bundled them deliberately, in one document, aimed at one audience. That is strong evidence that the convergence described in this lecture is not something historians imposed afterward. The Elizabethans saw it themselves and made use of it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Peter C. Mancall – Hakluyt’s Promise

Mancall studied Richard Hakluyt, the clergyman and compiler who assembled and promoted the argument for English colonization more energetically than anyone. Hakluyt’s significance is that he was a publicist: he gathered voyage accounts, wrote for ministers and investors, and worked to make American colonization seem both possible and necessary. You do not need to memorize his name for an exam. You should notice that arguments like the ones on this slide had authors with purposes.

The Irish Rehearsal

What England had already tried

  • Conquering territory
  • Planting English settlers on it
  • Imposing English institutions

What carried across

  • Assumptions about land and its use
  • Assumptions about the people already there
  • Habits of settlement by plantation
English settlers did not arrive in America without preconceptions. Ireland shaped how they understood Native peoples and land before they ever crossed the Atlantic.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before England colonized America, it had been attempting something similar much closer to home. This slide asks what carried across — not ships or soldiers, but assumptions.

Breaking It Down

Conquering territory, planting settlers, imposing institutions: these were the components of English activity in Ireland, and the vocabulary matters. English writers used the word plantation for settling loyal populations on land taken from others. That word crossed the Atlantic with the practice.

Assumptions about land and its use: a settled conviction that land used in unfamiliar ways was being underused, and that this justified taking it.

Assumptions about the people already there: frameworks for understanding an existing population as an obstacle to be subdued, converted, or displaced.

Habits of settlement by plantation: a repeatable method rather than an improvisation.

Why This Matters

This is a slide about intellectual baggage. English settlers did not arrive in America as blank observers who then formed opinions about Native peoples and land. They arrived carrying a framework built somewhere else. That does not make what followed inevitable, but it does mean the encounter was shaped before it began — which is a more precise and more useful claim than saying the English were simply prejudiced.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Nicholas Canny – The Elizabethan Conquest of Ireland

Canny made this connection central to the field. He argued that the attitudes, methods, and justifications English colonizers applied in America were substantially worked out first in Ireland, and that many of the same people and families were involved in both. His argument has been debated and qualified since, but it permanently changed the question. American colonization can no longer be studied as though it began at the Atlantic coast.

Leaving England

What was changing

  • Population rising
  • Common land enclosed for private use
  • Agricultural practice shifting
  • Rural people displaced toward towns

What it produced

  • More people competing for less land
  • Underemployment
  • Rapid, crowded urban growth
Migration was not desperation alone. It was a calculated response to an England that was distributing opportunity very unevenly.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A closer look at the push side of migration. The lecture said England was distributing opportunity unevenly; this slide shows the machinery that produced the unevenness.

Breaking It Down

Population rising: more people needing land and work, without a matching increase in either.

Common land enclosed: Enclosure converted land held or used in common into private, fenced holdings. For households that had relied on common rights — grazing an animal, gathering fuel — this removed part of a livelihood that had never been written down as property.

Agricultural practice shifting: changes in farming altered how much labor was needed and what kind.

Rural people displaced toward towns: and towns grew faster than they could absorb arrivals, producing crowding and irregular work.

Why This Matters

The callout makes the argument this lecture wants you to carry: migration was not desperation alone but a calculated response. That distinction matters for how you read the seventeenth century. If migrants were simply fleeing starvation, their choices need no further explanation. If they were making a bet — weighing a bad situation at home against an uncertain but possible future abroad — then they were historical actors with reasoning you can reconstruct. That is the more accurate picture, and it is the one that makes indentured servitude comprehensible when you meet it in Lecture 2.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Carl Bridenbaugh – Vexed and Troubled Englishmen

Bridenbaugh set out to describe the England that migrants were leaving rather than the America they reached — its economy, its households, its social strains. The framing is the useful part. If you want to explain why people crossed an ocean, the explanation begins on the near side of it, in conditions that made the crossing look reasonable.

The English Reformation

The break of the English church from Rome, beginning under Henry VIII and settled durably under Elizabeth I after 1558.

It matters here because of how fast it worked. Within roughly one generation, being English and being Protestant became difficult to separate — which meant that a conflict with Catholic Spain was not only a war over territory and trade, but a conflict over what England understood itself to be.

Roanoke

Sir Walter Raleigh's settlement attempts on Roanoke Island, begun in 1585 and renewed in 1587. When Governor John White returned in 1590 the settlement stood abandoned, the colonists gone, and the word CROATOAN carved at the site.

Migration

The sustained movement of people from England to the colonies across the seventeenth century.

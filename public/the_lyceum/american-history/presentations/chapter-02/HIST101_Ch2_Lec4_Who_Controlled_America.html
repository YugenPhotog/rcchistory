📖 Start Here

Welcome to Lecture 4 of Chapter 2. The first three lectures asked why England colonized, why the colonies turned out so different from one another, and what the Puritans thought they were building. This one asks a political question: by the middle of the 1600s, who was actually in charge of English America?

The honest answer is that nobody was fully in charge, and that is the whole point. Four different kinds of authority — Native nations, colonial assemblies, proprietors, and the Crown — all claimed the right to govern the same places and people. Your job today is to understand how those claims overlapped and where they collided.

The map behind this title is a real Dutch chart printed in Amsterdam in 1685. Look closely at it in the deck: it shows New Netherland, New England, and part of Virginia all drawn onto one sheet, with Native and European names lettered across the same ground. That crowded page is a good picture of the problem this lecture is about.

Three Voices, One Continent

The Crown

“We granted these colonies.”

Colonists

“We govern ourselves.”

Native Nations

“You entered lands we already governed.”

As English settlements expanded, who had the right to govern people and control land?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This lecture opens with an argument rather than a date. Three very different groups looked at the same stretch of North America in the mid-1600s, and each said, in effect, “we have the right to govern here.” Imagine three people holding three deeds to the same house, each issued by a different office, each one sincerely convinced that theirs is the valid one. That is roughly the situation.

Breaking It Down

The Crown: English monarchs issued royal charters that created colonies, appointed governors, and claimed ultimate authority over English settlements. From London's point of view, colonial government existed because the king said it could.

Colonists: On the ground, settlers ran assemblies, town meetings, courts, churches, and militias that made real decisions about taxes, land, roads, and law. From their point of view, they were exercising the ordinary rights of English subjects.

Native Nations: Indigenous peoples continued to govern their own communities, make war and peace, control access to land and trade, and negotiate alliances. From their point of view, the English had arrived inside political worlds that already existed and already had rulers.

Why This Matters

Notice what the essential question does not ask. It does not ask who legally owned America, and it does not ask who eventually won. It asks who had the right to govern — which makes this a question about legitimacy, about how power gets justified. That is a harder and more interesting question, and it is the one historians actually argue about. Hold onto it; every slide today is evidence for answering it.

Every claim we meet today belongs under one of these.

Representation

Sovereignty

📖 Understanding This Slide

These three words are your filing system for the next thirty-five minutes. Every claim, conflict, and person you meet today belongs under one of them. If you write nothing else down, write these three and the question attached to each.

Breaking It Down

Representation — Who makes the laws? This covers assemblies, town meetings, elections, and the slow growth of the expectation that local people should have a voice in local lawmaking.

Sovereignty — Whose land is it? This covers the competing claims of Native nations and English settlers, and the deeper problem that both sides had political systems that did not translate cleanly into each other.

Empire — Who answers to London? This covers the Crown's effort, especially after 1660, to turn a scattered set of settlements into an organized imperial system.

Why This Matters

These are not three separate stories running side by side. They interlock. Colonial representation grew stronger at exactly the moment Native sovereignty was being squeezed, and both of those developments shaped what the Crown was able to do about empire. You will see these three words again on every section divider and one last time at the end, when we use them to answer the opening question.

Section I

Representation — Colonists Learn to Govern Themselves

Does electing representatives make a society democratic?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section I takes up the first of our three words: representation. The question on the screen is a trap, and it is meant to be. “Does electing representatives make a society democratic?” sounds like it should have an obvious yes attached to it. It does not.

What to Watch For

Over the next three slides you will see colonists build genuine representative institutions — assemblies, town meetings, elected deputies — and you will also see how narrow those institutions were. Both things are true at once, and the skill this section is teaching you is the ability to hold them together without collapsing into either “early American democracy” or “it was all a sham.”

Keep a running list in your head of two categories: what colonists could actually decide for themselves, and who was allowed to be in the room when they decided it.

They Did Not Arrive as Democrats

What they brought

  • Monarchy, hierarchy, property
  • Local offices and juries
  • Consent in taxation
  • Rights of English subjects

What America added

  • Distance from London
  • Weak administrative oversight
  • Local problems needing local decisions
Colonial conditions gave ordinary English habits unusual room to grow.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here is a tempting mistake: assume that English settlers crossed the Atlantic because they wanted freedom, and that self-government was the natural expression of that desire. That story is backwards. These colonists were ordinary seventeenth-century English people who accepted kings, ranks, and inequality as normal. What changed was not their beliefs but their circumstances.

Breaking It Down

Monarchy, hierarchy, property: English political culture took for granted that society had ranks, that heads of household ruled their dependents, and that political participation belonged to men who owned property. Nobody arrived planning to abolish that.

Local offices and juries: But English tradition also included a dense layer of local self-administration — parish officers, constables, jurymen, magistrates. Ordinary men were already used to holding small offices and making decisions in their own communities.

Consent in taxation: English political thinking held that taxes and laws ought to pass through recognized constitutional forms rather than simply being imposed. This is an old idea, not an American invention.

Rights of English subjects: Colonists believed they carried their legal identity with them. Crossing an ocean did not, in their view, make them something less than English.

Distance, weak oversight, local problems: Now add America. London was months away by ship. Imperial administration was thin and often distracted. And the actual work of running a colony — fences, roads, land grants, militia, taxes — had to be done by people who were physically present.

Why This Matters

Put those two columns together and you get the argument of this section: colonial self-government grew out of old English habits placed in unusual conditions, not out of a new democratic philosophy. That distinction matters, because it explains why colonists later defended their assemblies as ancient rights rather than as revolutionary innovations.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jack P. Greene – Peripheries and Center and Negotiated Authorities

Greene is the historian who reshaped how we think about this. The older picture had London handing down political forms to passive colonies. Greene argues the opposite: because distance was great and administrative capacity was weak, provincial governments acquired real power simply by exercising it and getting away with it. Authority became legitimate not because it was granted from above but because it had been used, defended, and locally accepted over many years.

Why does this matter for you? Because it explains a puzzle that will return in Chapters 4 and 5. A privilege that started as a corporate convenience or a practical improvisation could, two generations later, be defended as an inherited liberty that no king had the right to touch. Greene calls this process negotiated authority, and it is the single most useful phrase for understanding colonial politics.

  • Governor still holds power

New England towns

  • Freemen elect deputies

Different origins. One habit: local decisions require local representatives.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Three colonies, three different origin stories, one shared result. This slide is asking you to look past the differences and notice the pattern underneath. The institutions do not resemble each other much; the habit they produce is nearly identical.

Breaking It Down

Virginia, 1619: The House of Burgesses first met in 1619, when the Virginia Company was trying to make a struggling colony more attractive to settlers and investors. Eligible settlers elected representatives — burgesses — who met with the governor and council to make law. Note the third bullet: the governor kept substantial power. This was a reform, not a revolution.

Plymouth, 1620: You met the Mayflower Compact in Lecture 3. The key detail for today is the second bullet: the passengers landed outside the jurisdiction they had expected to settle, which meant they had no lawful government at all. So they made one, by agreement, on the spot. It was an emergency solution to a legal problem.

New England towns: In Massachusetts Bay, freemen elected magistrates and deputies, the General Court acquired real legislative authority, and towns handled the daily business of life — roads, fences, common land, schools, meetinghouses, poor relief, livestock. Authority here was experienced close to home, which is exactly why it stuck.

Why This Matters

A company reform, a shipboard emergency, and a religious settlement produced the same expectation: local decisions require local representatives. That is the takeaway. Once a few generations of colonists have lived inside that assumption, it stops feeling like a privilege somebody granted them and starts feeling like a right they have always had. Remember this when the Crown tries to tighten control in Section III — it will be arguing against a habit, not just against a policy.

Who Actually Took Part?

  • Church members in Massachusetts

  • Servants and enslaved people

  • Many religious dissenters

How later Americans pictured the Compact — notice who is seated at the table

Representative government is not the same thing as modern democracy. Keep that distinction all semester.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the slide that keeps you honest. Yes, colonists built representative institutions. Now ask the follow-up question that historians always ask: representative of whom? The answer is a fairly small slice of the people actually living in these colonies.

Breaking It Down

Included: adult men who held property, church members in Massachusetts (where religious membership shaped political membership), and men admitted as freemen of a town. Property qualifications and franchise rules changed over time and varied by colony, but the general shape held.

Excluded: women, servants and enslaved people, Native neighbors, and many religious dissenters. That is not a footnote to the story of colonial government. In most of these communities it is the majority of the population.

About the painting: Look carefully at the image and then at the caption. This is not a picture made in 1620. It is a patriotic painting from the early twentieth century, imagining the scene almost three hundred years later. Notice who the artist chose to put around the table, and notice that the painting itself reproduces the same exclusion the slide is describing. A later image can be excellent evidence — just not of the event it depicts. It is evidence of how a much later generation wanted to remember that event.

Why This Matters

Here is the distinction to carry all semester: representative government means decisions pass through elected representatives. Democracy, as we now use the word, implies broad political equality. Early America had a meaningful amount of the first and very little of the second. Historians care about early assemblies less because they resembled modern democracy and more because of the expectations they created. Confuse the two and you will misread the next two hundred years of American history.

⏸ Pause & Reflect

Were the English colonies becoming democratic?

Yes  •  No  •  Sort of
Choose one and defend it.

🤔 What This Activity Is For

This is not a quiz and there is no hidden right answer. The question asks you to make a historical judgment and then justify it with evidence from the last three slides. That skill — taking a position and supporting it — is most of what history courses are actually training you to do.

How to Answer Well

A weak answer is “sort of, I guess.” A strong answer names specific evidence. Here is what each position can defend:

Yes: Colonists elected representatives, taxed themselves, ran town meetings, and increasingly assumed that lawmaking required local consent. Compared with almost any other society in the seventeenth-century Atlantic world, that is a striking amount of participation.

No: Women, servants, enslaved people, Native neighbors, and many dissenters were excluded entirely. Governors kept real power, property qualifications restricted the franchise, and elites dominated officeholding. A system that excludes most adults is not a democracy in any modern sense.

Sort of: The strongest version of this answer is not a shrug — it is an argument that the categories do not fit. These colonies were becoming more participatory without becoming more equal, and those are different things that can move in different directions.

Why This Matters

Whatever you choose, you have just done the thing this section was built to teach: you separated the fact that representation existed from the question of how democratic it was. Keep that separation. It will make the American Revolution far less confusing later in the course.

Section II

Sovereignty — Native Nations Govern Their Own Homelands

What kind of political world did English settlers enter?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section II turns to the second of our three words: sovereignty. And it makes a move that older textbooks often skipped. Instead of narrating English settlement first and adding Native peoples afterward as a separate topic, we are going to ask what political world the English actually walked into.

What to Watch For

The answer is that they walked into a crowded one. Native nations had territories, leaders, laws, diplomatic protocols, alliances, rivalries, and tribute relationships long before any English ship arrived. Watch for the moment when that fact stops being background and becomes the central problem: as English settlements expand, two political systems start making claims about the same ground.

One more thing to notice. Nothing in this section treats Native peoples as a single group with a single interest. That flattening is one of the most common errors in early American history, and this section is designed to make it impossible for you to make it.

What Was Already Here

Sovereignty — the claimed right of a political community to govern itself and control its territory.
  • Territories and boundaries
  • Sachems, councils, and law
  • Alliances and rivalries
  • Diplomatic protocols
  • Tribute relationships

The English did not enter an empty political landscape.

A Dutch map of the same coast — count how much of it carries Native names

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before we can ask who had the right to govern, we need a working definition of what governing even means. That is what sovereignty gives us. And the moment you have the definition in hand, something becomes obvious: Native nations plainly had it.

Breaking It Down

Sovereignty: the claimed right of a political community to govern itself and control its territory. Use the word carefully here, though. Neither English nor Native political systems matched the later nation-state model, and authority in the seventeenth century could be personal, territorial, tributary, corporate, religious, proprietary, or imperial — sometimes several of these at once, in the same place.

Territories and boundaries: Native nations held understood territories with recognized limits, even where those limits were not drawn as lines on a European map.

Sachems, councils, and law: A sachem was a leader whose authority rested on consultation, kinship, reputation, and consent rather than on inherited absolute command. Communities had ways of making binding decisions and settling disputes.

Alliances and rivalries: Native nations were not neutral toward one another. They had long-standing friendships, enmities, and competitions, and those shaped every decision they made about Europeans.

Diplomatic protocols: There were established forms for negotiating — speeches, gifts, wampum, ceremony — that carried real legal and political weight.

Tribute relationships: Some communities paid tribute to more powerful neighbors, creating layered hierarchies of obligation. This is important later, because it explains how some Native leaders understood their agreements with the English.

Why This Matters

Look at the map beside the bullet list and count how much of it carries Native names. Seventeenth-century European mapmakers recorded Indigenous nations across the interior because those nations were the political reality of the region — the people you had to deal with if you wanted to trade, travel, or settle. The English did not enter an empty political landscape. They entered a full one and began making claims inside it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Andrew Lipman – The Saltwater Frontier (2015)

Lipman does something clever with geography. Most accounts of early New England treat the coastline as an edge — land on one side, empty ocean on the other. Lipman argues that Long Island Sound and the southern New England coast were actually a connected maritime political world, crossed constantly by Native boatmen, traders, raiders, fishermen, and diplomats, as well as by English and Dutch ships.

The payoff for this lecture is that it dissolves the idea of a simple frontier line with settlers on one side and Native peoples on the other. Instead you get a contested network in which several groups were competing for routes, trade, and influence. Keep that image when we reach the Pequot War, because that conflict makes almost no sense on a frontier-line model.

One Agreement, Two Meanings

Plymouth allied with the Wampanoag sachem Ousamequin, whom the English called Massasoit.

What Ousamequin understood

  • An alliance with useful newcomers
  • The English as tributary dependents
  • Leverage against Native rivals

What the English claimed

  • He had accepted English sovereignty
  • His people were now subjects
  • The agreement transferred authority
Same agreement. Two political systems reading it differently.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is one of the most important slides in the lecture, and it is built around a single alliance. Plymouth and the Wampanoag sachem Ousamequin — whom the English called Massasoit, a title meaning roughly “great sachem” that the English mistook for his name — reached an agreement. Both sides thought they knew what it meant. They did not mean the same thing.

Breaking It Down

What Ousamequin understood: He was a leader operating in a region reshaped by devastating epidemics and by rivalry with neighboring nations. A small, weak group of newcomers could be useful — an alliance that brought trade goods, a military partner, and leverage against Native rivals. In the tributary logic of the region, the weaker party in such an arrangement was the one taking on obligations. That was not, in his view, him.

What the English claimed: The English read the same agreement through European legal categories. To them, it looked like a Native leader placing himself and his people under English sovereignty — becoming subjects, transferring authority, accepting a superior. Later English officials would cite it exactly that way.

Why This Matters

Be careful with how you explain this. The problem was not that one side understood politics and property while the other did not. Both had sophisticated political systems. The problem is that the two systems assigned different meanings to the same act. An alliance in one framework is a submission in the other, and there was no neutral court to decide which reading was correct.

Now notice the consequence. Over time, English colonists could point to a stack of such agreements as evidence that Native nations had already accepted English authority — and could describe later coercion as merely enforcing what had been agreed. Documents that looked like consent could do the work of conquest. This pattern will return every time you study a treaty in this course, right through Indian Removal in Chapter 10.

Success Makes Authority a Practical Problem

Virginia

Tobacco  →  More land

New England

New towns  →  More land

Expansion forces someone to decide:

  • Who owns the land?
  • Where may settlement spread?
  • Who may trade, and on what routes?
  • Whose law settles the dispute?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here the economics of Lecture 2 turns into the politics of Lecture 4. Notice that the chains on this slide do not start with conflict. They start with success. Colonies that were failing did not generate much of a sovereignty crisis. Colonies that were thriving did.

Breaking It Down

Virginia: Tobacco → More land. You saw this chain in Lecture 2. Tobacco exhausts soil, profits reward expansion, and planters push outward for fresh acreage.

New England: New towns → More land. A different mechanism, the same outcome. New England grew through family migration and healthy population growth, and growing communities kept founding new towns, each needing fields, pasture, and woodlot.

The four questions: Once expansion is under way, abstract questions become urgent practical ones. Who owns the land? Where may settlement spread? Who may trade, and on what routes? Whose law settles the dispute? Somebody has to answer these, and every possible answer favors somebody.

Why This Matters

This is the hinge of the whole lecture. Sovereignty is not an argument seventeenth-century people had in the abstract. It became unavoidable because two growing societies needed the same ground. And note the uncomfortable implication: the more successful English colonies became, the more pressure they put on Native political independence. Prosperity and conflict were not opposites here. They were connected.

Watch that fourth question especially — whose law settles the dispute? As English courts increasingly judged land disputes by English rules, the answer to that question quietly decided many of the others.

The Pequot War: A Contest of Authority

  • Southern New England was politically complex
  • Pequots held regional power and trade
  • English settlement shifted the balance
  • Mohegans and Narragansetts allied with the English
Expansion was also a struggle over who held legitimate authority here.

Drawn for one of the English commanders — a justification, not a neutral record

Resist “English versus Indians.” Multiple Native and European groups were pursuing their own interests.
→ Independent Study: Uncas and the Politics of Alliance

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide sets up the Pequot War of 1636–1638 without narrating it, because your Stream & Discuss assignment will do that work. What the lecture gives you is the political framework you need in order to watch the documentary well. Think of this as the lens, not the picture.

Breaking It Down

Southern New England was politically complex: The region contained Pequots, Mohegans, Narragansetts, Niantics, and others, plus two competing European empires — English and Dutch. Every one of these had its own interests.

Pequots held regional power and trade: The Pequots were a major power with influence over tributary communities and valuable access to wampum and the fur trade. Their strength is part of why other Native nations had reasons of their own to want it reduced.

English settlement shifted the balance: English expansion into the Connecticut River valley changed the regional equation. Disputes over the deaths of English traders, punitive expeditions, retaliation, trade rivalry, and collapsing diplomacy escalated into open war.

Mohegans and Narragansetts allied with the English: This is the detail that breaks the simple story. Native forces fought alongside English forces, for their own reasons.

About the engraving: This image was designed for a book published by one of the English commanders of the attack. Look at its structure: the Pequot village in the center, a ring of English soldiers around it, a further ring of Native allies beyond them. It was made to explain and justify what the English had done. It is superb evidence of how the English wanted the event understood — and poor evidence of what it was like inside that palisade.

Why This Matters

Read the caution box carefully. Native alliances do not make this war less colonial. They reveal how colonial expansion actually worked: not only through direct English force, but through existing Indigenous rivalries. The English entered a political contest already in progress, strengthened some parties, destroyed or subordinated others, and then used the rearranged balance of power to expand their own claims.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Alfred A. Cave – The Pequot War (1996)

Cave wrote the first full-scale modern study of the conflict, and he directly challenged the old English explanation that settlers were defending themselves against a Pequot plot to exterminate Europeans. Cave points instead to English preconceptions about Native peoples, competition over wampum and trade, ordinary political interest, and ideology. His most important argument for this course concerns what happened afterward: postwar accounts converted a war of conquest into a story of defensive necessity. He calls this “the mythology of the frontier,” and you will meet it again in every chapter about westward expansion.

Alden T. Vaughan – New England Frontier (1965; later revised)

Vaughan is here for contrast, and for a lesson about how history works. He argued against the picture of uniformly hostile Puritan–Indian relations, emphasizing long stretches of negotiation, legal exchange, and missionary contact. That was a genuine corrective to crude conquest narratives, but his relatively favorable read of Puritan policy drew heavy criticism, and Cave's work is part of the response. Read together, the two books show you something valuable: historians disagree, revise each other, and the argument itself is part of the knowledge.

⏸ Pause & Predict

English settlements keep growing.
Native nations keep governing their homelands.
Both need the same land.

Which conflict would be hardest to settle — and why?

🤔 What This Activity Is For

You are being asked to reason forward from a situation rather than recall a fact. Historians do this constantly: given these conditions and these interests, what kinds of conflict become likely? Then they check the prediction against the evidence.

How to Think It Through

Start by listing the possible flashpoints: land and settlement boundaries, hunting and planting grounds, trade routes and prices, alliances and who gets protection, punishment when someone is killed, and whose courts decide any of it.

Then ask what makes a conflict settleable. Disputes over a single transaction can be negotiated. Disputes over prices can be split. But a conflict is very hard to resolve when the two sides do not share a framework for what the disagreement even is — or when one side controls the court that decides.

Strong answers usually land on jurisdiction: whose law applies? Land disputes can in principle be negotiated, but only if both parties agree on what a land agreement means and who enforces it. That is exactly the agreement that did not exist.

Why This Matters

Whatever you predicted, notice that you just built the analytical setup for the entire rest of the course. The pattern — expansion, competing claims to authority, violence framed as security — recurs from New England in the 1630s all the way to the Great Plains in the 1870s. Getting the logic right once here saves you enormous effort later.

Section III

Empire — The Crown Tries to Build One

What happens when the government that supposedly rules you collapses?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section III takes up the third word: empire. And it starts somewhere that may surprise you — in England, not America. The question on the screen is genuinely strange when you first meet it: what happens to a colony when the government that supposedly rules it falls apart?

What to Watch For

Between 1640 and 1660 England had a civil war, executed its king, abolished the monarchy, tried a republic, and ended up under military rule before restoring the Stuarts. Older American textbooks treated all of that as distant background noise. It was not. Watch for two effects running at the same time: colonial self-government gained room to grow, and English governments began experimenting with tighter Atlantic control.

Then watch what the restored monarchy inherits in 1660 — and why that inheritance made the imperial problem harder rather than easier.

England Falls Apart

  • Charles I rules without Parliament
  • 1642 — civil war begins
  • 1649 — Parliament wins; the king is executed
  • England becomes a commonwealth
  • Cromwell rules as Lord Protector
  • 1660 — Charles II restored

Printed for readers across Europe: a king tried and executed by his own subjects

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the one-minute version of two decades of English upheaval. You are not being asked to master English constitutional history. You need enough of it to understand why the colonies had room to maneuver and why the Crown came back so determined to organize them.

Breaking It Down

Charles I rules without Parliament: In the 1630s Charles governed for years without calling Parliament, quarreled with Scotland, and fought with his subjects over royal authority, taxation, and religion. Eventually he needed parliamentary money again, and the reckoning arrived.

1642 — civil war begins: The breakdown became armed conflict between the king's supporters and Parliament's.

1649 — Parliament wins; the king is executed: Look at the print beside the list. A reigning monarch was tried by his own subjects and beheaded in public. Prints like this one circulated across Europe. It is hard to overstate how shocking this was to people who believed kings ruled by divine right.

England becomes a commonwealth: Monarchy and the House of Lords were abolished outright.

Cromwell rules as Lord Protector: Oliver Cromwell rose from the parliamentary army to dominate the 1650s. The political forms kept shifting — parliamentary government, military influence, republican ideals, religious experiments, and authoritarian measures all coexisting uneasily.

1660 — Charles II restored: After Cromwell's death the regime unraveled and the monarchy returned.

Why This Matters

Two things to carry forward. First, for twenty years the source of colonial legitimacy was itself in question — if you held a charter from a king who had been executed, what exactly did you hold? Second, the sheer instability of these years is the reason colonists gained so much practical experience governing themselves. Nobody in London had the attention to spare.

What That Meant Three Thousand Miles Away

In England

  • Authority collapses and reforms repeatedly
  • London cannot supervise closely
  • New trade rules arrive anyway

In the colonies

  • Colonies take sides in the quarrel
  • West Indian settlers gain elective assemblies
  • Local government handles more

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now translate the English crisis into colonial consequences. The Atlantic was not a wall that kept English politics out. It was a slow, leaky connection — and what leaked through changed colonial life in ways that lasted.

Breaking It Down

Authority collapses and reforms repeatedly: Colonists watched the legitimate government of England change hands several times in twenty years. That is a powerful education in the idea that political authority is made rather than simply given.

London cannot supervise closely: Officials consumed by a civil war had little capacity to police what colonial assemblies did.

New trade rules arrive anyway: This bullet keeps you from oversimplifying. Parliamentary and Cromwellian governments were not simply absent — they also pursued a stronger Atlantic policy, including a navigation act in 1651 and military action against colonies that resisted. The English revolution widened local experimentation and stimulated new efforts to build an integrated Atlantic state at the same time.

Colonies take sides in the quarrel: Colonial governments were not neutral. Maryland saw conflict between its Catholic proprietors and Protestant settlers; Puritan New England had its own relationship with parliamentary governments; some colonies stayed loyal to the king. The Atlantic was politically connected.

West Indian settlers gain elective assemblies: Assemblies in the Caribbean expanded their roles substantially during these years.

Local government handles more: Colonies developed trade relationships London could not easily supervise, and New England colonies coordinated defense through the New England Confederation.

Why This Matters

Read the gold box slowly, because it contains a paradox worth keeping: imperial supervision weakened while colonial habits of self-government grew. Those two things happening simultaneously is what makes 1660 such a difficult moment. The Crown returns wanting more control over colonies that have just spent twenty years getting comfortable with less.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Carla Gardina Pestana – The English Atlantic in an Age of Revolution, 1640–1661 (2004)

Pestana's argument is a direct attack on the older habit of treating the English Civil War as background scenery in an American story. She shows that the upheavals of 1640–1660 transformed colonial religion, trade, labor, political alignments, and arguments about liberty. By 1660, she argues, the English Atlantic was more economically integrated, more religiously polarized, more socially exploitative, and more anxious about liberty than it had been in 1640.

Why that matters here: it prevents the Restoration from looking like the moment England suddenly discovered imperial organization. The work had already begun during the revolution.

Robert M. Bliss – Revolution and Empire (1990)

Bliss makes a complementary point. Changes in English political thought and institutions repeatedly generated colonial reactions, which means colonial constitutional development cannot be separated from the crises of the Stuart state. Put simply: to understand American politics in this century, you have to watch England too.

📖 Understanding This Slide

In 1660 the monarchy came back. But the Restoration did not return England to 1640, and it certainly did not return the colonies there. This slide lays out the constitutional disagreement that resulted — a disagreement that never really got settled and that later generations inherited.

Breaking It Down

The Crown's claim: Royal charters created colonial governments; therefore colonial privileges exist because the Crown granted them, and they remain dependent on imperial authority. On this logic, what a king gives, a king may adjust.

The colonists' claim: Assemblies and English liberties belong to them as English subjects, and generations of local practice have made those institutions genuinely theirs. On this logic, the Crown is not granting anything — it is recognizing what already exists.

The portrait: Look at how Charles II is presented — enthroned, crowned, holding orb and sceptre beneath a canopy. Every element is chosen to display legitimate, unbroken royal authority, which is precisely what had just been interrupted for eleven years. Portraits like this are political arguments painted in oil.

Why This Matters

The gold box compresses the whole dispute: the Crown says it granted constitutional government; colonists say they possess it. Notice that both sides accepted monarchy and empire. This is not an independence movement. It is a disagreement about where legitimate authority sits inside the imperial constitution — and because that question stayed unanswered, both sides could live with the ambiguity for another century.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jack P. Greene – the center–periphery model

Greene returns here, and his framework explains something that might otherwise look strange: why this disagreement could deepen for decades without producing a revolution. Because both sides shared basic commitments — monarchy, empire, English liberty — compromise remained available. The metropolitan center and the provincial periphery could each keep asserting its view, negotiate around the edges, and avoid a final test.

But an unresolved constitutional argument does not disappear; it accumulates. When eighteenth-century governments finally tried to settle the question in London's favor, colonists did not encounter those measures on a blank slate. They compared them against a hundred years of negotiated authority.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Restoration colonies were different in kind from what came before. Earlier colonies had grown from companies, congregations, and improvisation. These were designed — granted from the top down as pieces of an imperial strategy. The slide's job is to show you both the design and its limits.

Breaking It Down

Granted to loyal proprietors: The Crown handed enormous territories to favored supporters, who received broad governmental powers while still holding their grant from the king. It was patronage and imperial strategy at once, and it shifted most of the cost and risk of settlement onto somebody else.

Six founded, 1660–1688: A burst of colonial creation in under thirty years. This is the period that fills in the map between New England and Virginia.

Designed as parts of an empire: Charles II's government increasingly treated colonies as components of a commercial and strategic system — regulating trade, securing customs revenue, restraining foreign competitors, and supervising governors.

New York's Charter of Liberties, 1683: New York is the sharpest test case. Its proprietor initially governed with no elected assembly at all. Settlers — many of them used to assemblies in neighboring colonies — pressed for one and resisted taxation without it. In 1683 they won, and the resulting charter declared that legislative power involved the governor, council, and representatives of the people.

Why This Matters

Here is the pattern that defeats top-down design: settlers had choices. Colonies needed migrants, and migrants could compare land terms, taxes, religious policy, and political rights across jurisdictions before deciding where to go. A proprietor with sweeping legal powers still had to attract people who could simply move somewhere better.

Now read the caution box, because it protects you from the most common error in this material. It is tempting to draw a straight line from 1683 to 1776. Do not. Seventeenth-century colonists overwhelmingly remained committed to English identity and monarchy, and their arguments concerned the rights of English subjects within the empire, not the legitimacy of empire itself. The honest connection forward is institutional, not emotional: a tension was established that would later be transformed into revolution by very different circumstances.

🤔 What This Activity Is For

This is the application exercise. You have spent the lecture learning three claims to authority; now you have to put them in the same room and find where they grind against each other. This is exactly what a historian does with the sources.

How to Work It

Pick one arena — land, law, taxes, trade, alliance, or settlement — and ask what each of the three parties would say about it. Then ask what happens when their answers conflict.

A quick worked example with land. The Crown says it granted the territory by charter. Colonists say they purchased, improved, and settled it, and their assembly regulates it. A Native nation says it is their homeland and any agreement meant something different from what the English claim. Three answers, one piece of ground, no shared court.

Try another with taxes, and notice something interesting: on that question the conflict is really only two-sided, between Crown and colonists. Different arenas activate different pairs of claimants. That observation is itself worth having.

Why This Matters

Most students land on land or law as the hardest to resolve, and for good reason. Trade disputes can be split and taxes can be negotiated, because both parties agree on what money is. Land and jurisdiction are harder, because the disagreement is not about the amount — it is about the framework. When there is no shared framework and no neutral authority, the party with more power tends to supply the answer. That is the uncomfortable conclusion this lecture is walking you toward.

Colonists built assemblies and town governments, and came to treat local participation as a right rather than a favor.

Sovereignty

Empire

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here are your three words again, now filled in. This slide is the answer to the question we opened with, and it is deliberately built so you can reconstruct the whole lecture from three headings. If you can explain each column in your own sentences, you have the lecture.

Breaking It Down

Representation: Colonists built assemblies, town governments, and courts, and over generations they stopped experiencing local participation as a privilege somebody granted and started treating it as a right they held. That shift — from favor to right — is the most consequential thing in Section I.

Sovereignty: Native nations continued governing their homelands, negotiating, choosing allies, and defending their political independence. They were not passive. But expanding settlement, backed at moments by force, steadily narrowed the ground on which they could exercise that independence.

Empire: The Crown claimed ultimate authority throughout, and after 1660 it tried to convert that claim into a working system — charters, appointed governors, trade regulation, customs revenue, and proprietary grants. It succeeded partially and unevenly.

Why This Matters

Now put the three columns beside each other and notice the timing, because this is the analytical payoff of the lecture. Colonial self-government expanded at the same time Indigenous sovereignty contracted, and both happened while the Crown was becoming more capable of governing an empire. Those are not three unrelated trends that happened to overlap. Settler political liberty was strengthened by control over land and labor that depended on subordinating other people. Hold that connection; the course returns to it constantly.

The Point Worth Keeping

Seventeenth-century English America was not governed by one uncontested authority. Colonists, Native nations, proprietors, and the Crown all claimed legitimate power. As settlements expanded, those claims collided.

So who controlled America? No one completely.

📖 Understanding This Slide

If you remember one sentence from this lecture a week from now, make it this one. Seventeenth-century English America had no single uncontested authority. Four kinds of claim — Native, colonial, proprietary, royal — operated at once, and expansion forced them into collision.

Breaking It Down

Why did this layered arrangement persist rather than resolve? Because no level of government could easily impose its will everywhere. Distance slowed communication. Colonial economies depended on local cooperation. Assemblies could withhold money. Proprietors needed settlers. Governors needed local elites. Native diplomacy remained essential across whole regions. Imperial power was real, but it was incomplete — and incomplete power has to negotiate.

So the answer to “who controlled America?” is no one completely. That is not a dodge. It is the most accurate available answer, and learning to give an accurate complicated answer instead of a satisfying simple one is a large part of what this course is for.

Why This Matters

Notice that the question we can actually answer has shifted. Not “which institution was legally supreme?” — that question has no clean answer in the 1600s. The better question is what happens to political legitimacy when several communities claim the right to govern the same people, territory, or resources, and then one of those communities acquires the power to make its claim stick. That is a question you can carry into the Revolution, the early republic, westward expansion, and the Civil War.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Edmund S. Morgan – American Slavery, American Freedom (1975)

Morgan's classic study of colonial Virginia asks a question that sits right underneath this slide: how did a society that prized liberty so intensely also build one of the harshest systems of unfreedom in the Atlantic world? His answer is that these were not opposites in tension — they developed together. Widespread liberty among white property-holding men became more secure precisely because a permanently subordinated labor force absorbed the conflict that had previously set poor colonists against rich ones.

Apply that logic to today's material and you get the lecture's hardest point. Colonial political liberty was not simply coexisting with conquest and slavery by accident. It was, in important ways, built on them. That is not an accusation to make settlers into villains — it is a structural observation about how settler societies developed, and it is why the course's themes of liberty, equality, and power become inseparable at exactly this moment.

Watch For These Three Things

There was no single Native side: Mohegan and Narragansett forces fought alongside the English, for reasons of their own that had nothing to do with loyalty to English rule. If the documentary ever seems to be telling a story of “settlers versus Indians,” that is your cue to push back and ask which Native nations, and what did each of them want.

Competing explanations of the cause: You will encounter several accounts of why the war started — the killing of English traders, punitive expeditions, trade and wampum rivalry, land pressure from the Connecticut valley, fear, and English assumptions about Native intentions. Notice which explanation any given source reaches for first, and ask what that choice does for the person making it.

How later Americans remembered it: Pay attention to the difference between what happened and how it was later told. Recall Cave's point about the mythology of the frontier: postwar narratives converted conquest into defensive necessity. Memory is not a neutral record of events — it is something people make, for reasons.

Land Was Never Just Real Estate

What English towns wanted

  • Clear documentary title
  • Permanent exclusive possession
  • Boundaries enforceable in court

What Native communities held

  • Overlapping use rights
  • Kin claims and sachemship
  • Communal expectations about land
Avoid both myths — that every acre was simply stolen, and that a signed deed proves fair consent. The bargaining context was reshaped by epidemic disease, debt, trade dependency, and English courts.

📖 Understanding This Slide

In seventeenth-century New England, land was never merely real estate. Every sale, gift, permission, or settlement agreement rearranged political relationships. Think of a land transaction here less like buying a house and more like signing a treaty — except that the two parties had different ideas about what treaties do.

Breaking It Down

What English towns wanted: clear documentary title, permanent and exclusive possession, and boundaries that an English court would enforce. English legal culture assumed that a properly executed deed transferred everything, forever, to one owner.

What Native communities held: overlapping use rights, claims grounded in kinship and sachemship, and communal expectations about how land could be used. In that framework, a single agreement might permit shared use, create an alliance, or establish reciprocal obligation — without ending anyone's relationship to the ground.

The result is that the same signed document could be, in perfectly good faith, a permission in one legal world and a permanent sale in the other.

Why This Matters

The gold box asks you to avoid two opposite myths, and it is worth taking both seriously. The first myth is that English settlers simply seized everything without negotiation — transactions genuinely occurred, were recorded, and were often taken seriously by both parties. The second myth is that a fair-looking deed proves equitable consent.

Here is why the second myth fails. Consent depends on the conditions under which it is given, and those conditions were transformed by epidemic disease that devastated Native populations, by growing settler numbers, by debt, by dependence on European trade goods, and by English courts that increasingly judged disputes according to English norms. A signature can be authentic and the bargain still be structurally unequal. Learning to hold both halves of that at once is the historical skill this slide is teaching.

Uncas and the Politics of Alliance

His position

  • Mohegan sachem in a shifting region
  • Rivals among Pequots and Narragansetts

What the alliance did

  • Strengthened the Mohegans
  • Required continuous negotiation

📖 Understanding This Slide

Uncas is the single best cure for lazy thinking about Native–colonial relations. If you can explain his career accurately, you cannot fall back on “English versus Indians” ever again.

Breaking It Down

Mohegan sachem in a shifting region: Uncas led the Mohegans through a period when epidemic disease, European trade, and English settlement were rapidly rearranging the balance of power in southern New England.

Rivals among Pequots and Narragansetts: His most pressing problems were often other Native nations, not Europeans. Any account that treats Native peoples as a single bloc with a single interest simply cannot describe his situation.

Allied with Connecticut and Massachusetts: He chose to work with English colonial governments — a strategic decision, not a submission.

Strengthened the Mohegans: The alliance did what he wanted it to do. It improved Mohegan standing against their rivals.

Required continuous negotiation: But it also tied him to colonial governments that kept growing more powerful, which meant the negotiating never stopped and the terms kept shifting under him.

Never made him English: The colonists called him a friend to the English. He was, first and last, a guardian of Mohegan interests.

Why This Matters

Uncas shows you that Native leaders were making calculated political choices inside a world they understood well — and that those choices had real consequences for how colonization actually unfolded. It also shows the trap in his position: an alliance that genuinely served Mohegan interests still bound him to partners whose power was increasing faster than his own.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael Leroy Oberg – Uncas: First of the Mohegans (2003)

Oberg wrote this biography against a long tradition that offered only two versions of Uncas: loyal friend of the English, or self-serving traitor to other Native peoples. Both versions, Oberg argues, judge him by whether he helped or hurt somebody else's project. Neither asks what he was actually trying to do.

Oberg's answer is that Uncas was a skilled political actor who adapted to a new colonial order while consistently pursuing Mohegan interests — and that he was remarkably successful at it, holding influence over many communities, living a long life, and dying peacefully in his people's homeland. The methodological lesson matters as much as the biography: when you evaluate a historical figure only by their usefulness to another group's story, you guarantee you will misunderstand them.

Carolina: A Plan Meets Its Settlers

The design, 1663–1665

  • Granted to eight Lords Proprietors
  • Fundamental Constitutions drafted
  • Hereditary ranks and great estates
  • Anthony Ashley Cooper and John Locke

What actually happened

  • Settlers wanted workable land terms
  • They demanded local political influence
  • Barbados migrants brought plantation slavery
A charter could grant sweeping powers without guaranteeing they would work as intended. Read the Fundamental Constitutions as a proprietary document, not as Locke's political philosophy.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Carolina is the clearest test of whether imperial planning actually worked. The proprietors produced one of the most elaborate blueprints for a society anyone attempted in English America. Almost none of it governed the colony as written.

Breaking It Down

Granted to eight Lords Proprietors, 1663–1665: Many were closely tied to the restored Stuart regime. They wanted a colony that would reward large landholders, attract settlers, and secure English claims south of Virginia.

The Fundamental Constitutions: An unusually detailed attempt to prescribe social and political order from above — hereditary ranks, large estates, and a carefully structured political hierarchy, all specified in advance.

Anthony Ashley Cooper and John Locke: The document is traditionally associated with Ashley Cooper and his secretary, John Locke. Students often seize on this as a scandal, since Locke is later identified with natural rights and government by consent. Handle it carefully, as the gold box says. Locke participated in drafting within Shaftesbury's political circle, but the Constitutions were a collaborative proprietary document produced for a client, not a statement of the philosophy he would publish decades later.

What actually happened: Settlers wanted land on workable terms and real local political influence. And migration from Barbados and other colonies brought plantation experience and slavery into Carolina's development in ways the abstract design could not control at all.

Why This Matters

The lesson is the one the lecture stated more briefly: a charter could grant sweeping powers without guaranteeing those powers would work. Proprietors kept rediscovering the constraint that had defeated earlier companies — colonies need migrants, and migrants compare their options. Paper authority is not the same thing as functioning authority.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Robert M. Weir – Colonial South Carolina

Weir's synthesis is built around the colony's persistent paradoxes: aristocratic ambition alongside genuinely contentious political participation, enormous later prosperity alongside racial slavery, and proprietary authority alongside repeated struggles over local control. For our purposes, Carolina is most useful as evidence that grand design and actual colonial society were two very different things — and that the gap between them was filled by what settlers would accept.

New York: A Charter Won and Revoked

An inherited society, 1664

  • Taken from the Dutch by conquest
  • Dutch law, property, and trade networks
  • A diverse population with expectations

The fight over an assembly

  • James governed without one
  • Settlers resisted taxation without it
  • 1683 — Charter of Liberties granted
In 1686 the charter was rejected and the assembly abolished. The lesson is that colonial expectations were strong enough to win — and the Crown was still strong enough to take it back.

📖 Understanding This Slide

New York is the sharpest single illustration of this lecture's argument, which is why it earns its own slide. In one colony you can watch settlers win representative government by pressure — and then watch the Crown take it away.

Breaking It Down

Taken from the Dutch by conquest, 1664: Charles II granted the territory to his brother James, Duke of York, before English forces took control of New Netherland. The English did not create a society from blank space; they took over a functioning one.

Dutch law, property, and trade networks: The colony came with established legal and property traditions, commercial connections, religious diversity, and residents whose political expectations had formed under Dutch rule.

James governed without an assembly: The Duke of York initially ruled through a governor and council with no elected body at all. That made New York an unusually clean test of proprietary authority.

Settlers resisted taxation without one: English settlers, especially those familiar with assemblies in neighboring colonies, pressed repeatedly for representative government and objected to being taxed without it.

1683 — Charter of Liberties granted: Governor Thomas Dongan convened an elected assembly, and the resulting charter asserted that legislative power in the province involved the governor, council, and representatives of the people.

Why This Matters

Then read the gold box, which contains the twist. When the Duke of York became James II, the charter was rejected and the assembly abolished in 1686. Settler demands had been strong enough to convert a political expectation into constitutional language — and the Crown was still strong enough to revoke it.

That is the whole lecture in a single episode. Authority in English America was layered, contested, and genuinely negotiated, but negotiation is not the same as security. It also gives you a useful corrective to any triumphal story of steadily expanding liberty: colonial self-government did not simply grow. It advanced, retreated, and had to be fought for again.

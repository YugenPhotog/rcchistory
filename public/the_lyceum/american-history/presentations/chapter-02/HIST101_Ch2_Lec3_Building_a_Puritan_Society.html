“They Came for Religious Freedom.”

The Pilgrims and Puritans crossed the Atlantic to escape persecution and practice their faith freely.

True enough. But freedom for whom?

What happens when people cross an ocean not simply to practice a religion, but to build an entire society around it?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide opens by handing you the sentence almost everyone has heard: the Pilgrims and Puritans came to America for religious freedom. It's not wrong, exactly — it's incomplete. Think of it like being told a marathon runner "ran because she loves running." True, but it skips the actual training plan, the coach, the race strategy. That's what this lecture fills in.

Breaking It Down

Religious freedom in the popular version usually means something like modern religious liberty — the right of any individual to believe whatever they want, free from government interference. That's a useful modern value, but it's not quite what most Puritan migrants were after in the 1620s and 1630s.

What they actually wanted was freedom to establish properly reformed churches and communities — not freedom for every resident of that community to reject the religious premises it was built on. That's the crucial distinction the essential question is pointing you toward: not just "did they escape persecution?" but "what did they build once they got here, and who did that building include or exclude?"

Why This Matters

This reframing is the engine of the entire lecture. If you walk in assuming "religious freedom" means what it means today, almost everything that follows — the strict church membership rules, the banishments of Williams and Hutchinson, the intertwining of church and town government — will look like a betrayal of the founders' stated ideals. Once you see that Puritans were building a covenanted society, not just seeking personal liberty, those same events start to make internal sense, even if we still judge them critically today.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael P. Winship – Hot Protestants

Winship's transatlantic history describes Puritans as "hot Protestants" — intense religious reformers whose energy went into building institutions, not just fleeing persecution. His framing helps you see Massachusetts as one episode in a much larger, conflict-ridden Protestant reform movement stretching back to England, rather than a simple story of refugees seeking a safe harbor.

Section I

A Religious Society, Not Just a Refuge

What did Pilgrims and Puritans actually want?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is a section marker — think of it as a chapter heading. Section I asks a deceptively simple question: what did Pilgrims and Puritans actually want when they crossed the Atlantic?

Why This Matters

Before you can understand the society Puritans built (Section II) or the limits of that society (Section III), you need to understand what they were building toward. This section clears away the "simple refuge" myth and replaces it with something more specific: a plan to construct a whole religious community, not just a place to worship in peace.

Two Movements, Not One

Pilgrims (Separatists)

  • Church of England beyond reform
  • Left England, then Holland
  • Founded Plymouth, 1620
  • Always a small colony

Puritans (Non-Separatists)

  • Most stayed inside the Church
  • Wanted to reform it from within
  • Massachusetts Bay: the big experiment
Pilgrims were one small branch of the larger Puritan movement.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here's a distinction that trips up almost everyone: Pilgrims and Puritans were not the same group. Think of Pilgrims as one small, particularly stubborn splinter group within the much larger Puritan reform movement — like a small breakaway union local within a huge national labor movement.

Breaking It Down

Pilgrims were Separatists — they had concluded that the Church of England was corrupt beyond repair and needed to be abandoned entirely in favor of independent congregations. Some had already moved to the Dutch Republic before making the longer voyage to Cape Cod in 1620. They founded Plymouth, which stayed small — only a few hundred settlers even a decade later.

Puritans, by contrast, were the much larger group who believed the Church of England could still be reformed from the inside. Most Puritans were not Separatists at all — they saw themselves as loyal (if frustrated) members of the English church, hoping their example in New England might help reform it. Massachusetts Bay, founded a decade after Plymouth, was their much bigger project.

Why This Matters

If you treat Plymouth as the template for all Puritan migration, you'll misunderstand almost everything that follows. Plymouth's small scale and separatist theology make it an interesting prologue, but Massachusetts Bay — nonseparating, vastly larger, and intellectually connected to England — is where the real "godly commonwealth" experiment unfolds. Keep that scale difference in mind as we move forward.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Winship's transatlantic framing is especially useful here: he treats Massachusetts not as an isolated American origin story, but as one episode within a larger, conflict-ridden struggle over the future of English Protestantism — a struggle Plymouth's Separatists had, in a sense, already given up on.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the slide most students already half-know from grade school — the Mayflower, the Compact, the hard first winter. The goal here isn't to re-tell that story; it's to correctly scale its historical importance.

Breaking It Down

Of the roughly 102 passengers on the Mayflower, only about a third were actually Separatist Pilgrims — the rest were "strangers": merchants, craftsmen, servants, and others along for practical reasons. When the ship landed north of the territory their patent covered, they had a real legal problem: no clear authority to govern the colony.

The Mayflower Compact solved that immediate problem. In the quoted language, the signers agreed to "covenant and combine ourselves together into a civil Body Politick" — essentially, a practical agreement among adult male passengers to recognize common laws and officers, while remaining loyal subjects of King James. It didn't declare independence, establish universal voting rights, or create a detailed constitution.

Why This Matters

The Compact's later reputation as "the first democracy in the Western world" is a piece of national mythology layered on top of a much narrower, more practical document. Understanding the gap between what the Compact actually did and what later Americans wanted it to mean is itself a lesson in how historical memory works — a theme that will come back later in the lecture with Winthrop's "city upon a hill."

🎓 Historians Weigh In

The Library of Congress – on the Compact's original purpose

The Library of Congress's own account of the Compact emphasizes its narrow, immediate goal: preventing faction and lawlessness in a community that was, for the moment, operating without secure legal authorization. That's a useful check against the temptation to read 1620 through a 1776 lens.

By 1630, Plymouth was still small. Massachusetts Bay was about to become the real experiment.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide pushes back against the "grateful Pilgrims, generous Indians" version of the Thanksgiving story. Both Plymouth's settlers and the Wampanoag were making calculated political decisions — not acting out of simple kindness.

Breaking It Down

By 1620, epidemics tied to earlier European contact had already devastated coastal Native communities. The Wampanoag polity, led by Ousamequin (commonly called Massasoit), faced its own strategic problems — including rivalry with the Narragansetts — and sought an alliance that could help offset those rival powers. Figures like Samoset and Tisquantum (Squanto) served as intermediaries in a region already reshaped by years of trade, kidnapping, and shifting political relationships.

Why This Matters

Treating this alliance as simple charity toward starving newcomers erases Native political agency. Both sides sought advantage, and each interpreted the relationship through different assumptions about obligation and sovereignty. This sets up a theme you'll see again in Section II and at the very end of the lecture: Puritan expansion always took place inside an already-organized Indigenous political world, not an empty one.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Neal Salisbury – Manitou and Providence

Salisbury's work is a major corrective to narratives where Native people appear only as helpers or enemies of English colonists. He treats Wampanoag diplomacy as strategic action in its own right — not a reaction to English arrival, but a continuation of long-standing regional politics that predated the Mayflower by generations.

Section II

Belief Built the Society

What does a society look like when it is organized around religious purpose?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section II is where the lecture really gets going. Having established what Puritans wanted (Section I), we now ask: what did that want actually build?

Why This Matters

This section covers a lot of ground — Winthrop's charter, covenant theology, family and town structure, education, and government — but it's all one argument: religious belief didn't stay private. It got built into the institutions of everyday colonial life.

Massachusetts Bay, 1630

John Winthrop and other leaders carried the Massachusetts Bay Company's charter with them across the ocean.

  • Unusual control over their own government
  • Purpose mattered more than profit
Virginia: How can this colony make money?
Massachusetts Bay: How should a Christian community live?

John Winthrop, first governor of Massachusetts Bay

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide sets up the biggest contrast of the lecture — not Virginia versus New England in general (you covered that last time), but the specific difference in founding purpose that will shape everything else in Section II.

Breaking It Down

Massachusetts Bay was a chartered joint-stock company, much like the Virginia Company you learned about last lecture. But there was a crucial legal quirk: the 1629 charter didn't require the company's governing headquarters to stay in England. So John Winthrop and other Puritan leaders simply brought the charter — and the company government — with them across the Atlantic.

That gave Massachusetts Bay's colonists unusually direct control over their own affairs, compared to a colony like Virginia that was still being directed from London. And unlike the Virginia Company, profit wasn't the primary goal here — demonstrating what a godly, reformed English society could look like was.

Why This Matters

This is the hinge the whole lecture turns on. Because the colony's founding question was "how should a Christian community live?" rather than "how can this colony turn a profit?", almost every institution that follows — family structure, town government, schools — gets shaped by that religious purpose instead of a commercial one.

A Covenant With God

Covenant theology

  • God is sovereign
  • Salvation cannot simply be earned
  • Covenant: a binding, mutual obligation
“We shall be as a city upon a hill. The eyes of all people are upon us.”
— John Winthrop, A Model of Christian Charity, 1630
Winthrop was warning the colonists: because their covenant made them highly visible, failure would disgrace their religious mission before the world.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide does some heavy theological lifting quickly, so slow down here. The goal is to understand covenant well enough to see why it turned private faith into a public, shared project.

Breaking It Down

Puritan theology held that God is sovereign and that salvation depends on God's grace, not human effort (a doctrine called predestination). That might sound like it would make behavior irrelevant — but because outward conduct couldn't earn salvation, believers scrutinized their own lives constantly for signs of genuine conversion. That anxious self-examination is part of why Puritan preaching and church discipline could feel so intense.

Covenant language gave this individual anxiety a social dimension. A covenant was a binding relationship of mutual promise and obligation — between God and the redeemed, between members of a congregation, and, in New England, sometimes describing the obligations of an entire community acting under what they believed was God's providence. Crucially, covenant thinking didn't erase hierarchy — but it did insist that relationships came with real obligations on both sides: rulers had to govern justly, parents had to raise children religiously, and the whole community could interpret a bad harvest or an epidemic as evidence of collective failure.

Winthrop's famous "city upon a hill" image, from his 1630 text A Model of Christian Charity, needs to be read in this covenantal context. It's often quoted today as a boast about American greatness — but in its original setting, the very next lines warn that failure to keep covenant with God would make the whole community "a story and a by-word" of disgrace before the world. It's a warning about visibility and accountability, not a celebration of exceptionalism.

Why This Matters

Covenant theology is the theological engine behind everything in the rest of Section II. Because the whole community was understood to be bound together under God's watchful eye, family order, church membership, education, and civil government all became matters of collective religious stakes — not just private choices.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Perry Miller – The New England Mind

Miller's landmark work reconstructed the dense theological vocabulary — reason, grace, covenant, providence — through which educated Puritans made sense of their world. His central point still holds even though later scholars have challenged his focus on elite male ministers: Puritan doctrine wasn't decorative theology sitting on top of colonial life. It structured how people understood authority, community, and their own historical purpose.

Abram C. Van Engen – City on a Hill

Van Engen traces how Winthrop's phrase moved from relative historical obscurity into the center of twentieth-century American exceptionalism narratives. His work is a great reminder that the phrase's modern political meaning is not the same as its seventeenth-century one — a distinction worth keeping in mind whenever a politician quotes it today.

Family and Town

Family

  • Migrants arrived as families
  • Higher birth rates, longer lives
  • Communities of related households

Town

  • Meetinghouse and congregation
  • Local government, nearby farms
  • Settlement clustered, not scattered

📖 Understanding This Slide

If covenant theology is the "why," this slide is the first "what" — the household and the town as the basic building blocks of Puritan society.

Breaking It Down

Family: Massachusetts Bay attracted a very different migration stream than early Virginia. Thousands arrived during the 1630s in family groups, often bringing property, skills, and social connections with them. Combined with healthier demographic conditions than the Chesapeake, this let the settler population grow rapidly through natural increase — stable households could reproduce labor, transfer property, and sustain local institutions across generations in a way Virginia's young, mostly male migrant stream could not.

Town: The town developed alongside, but wasn't identical to, the congregation. Town meetings distributed land, levied taxes, maintained roads, and selected local officers — accustoming many male settlers to regular participation in public decisions, even though land distribution was never perfectly equal from town to town.

Why This Matters

Don't read this contrast as a morality tale — "community-minded New England" versus "greedy Virginia." New Englanders pursued markets and wealth too. The real difference is that migration patterns and environment made a self-reproducing settler society possible in New England much earlier than in the Chesapeake. But don't over-romanticize the family, either: these households were strongly patriarchal, and family stability did not mean equality within the family.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

John Demos – A Little Commonwealth

Demos reconstructs household structure, marriage, childhood, and kinship in Plymouth Colony using demographic and social history. His phrase "little commonwealth" captures the key idea: the household was understood as a miniature polity whose internal order contributed to the order of the whole church and society.

Laurel Thatcher Ulrich – Good Wives

Ulrich recovers women's work and agency from court records, diaries, and probate inventories, complicating the image of the passive Puritan wife. Women managed production, healing, neighborhood relationships, and often substantial responsibilities during husbands' absences — important context for understanding Anne Hutchinson later in this lecture, since women commonly discussed sermons and hosted devotional meetings; her conflict wasn't about women speaking at all, but about the specific authority she claimed.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide tackles a word you'll want to use carefully for the rest of the semester: theocracy. Massachusetts Bay is often called one, but the real picture is more interesting — and more precise — than that label suggests.

Breaking It Down

Education: Because Puritan religious life centered on preaching and Scripture, the colony invested heavily in literacy. Harvard College was founded in 1636 partly to train an educated ministry, and Massachusetts later required towns to support schooling — both to spread religious literacy and to make sure ignorance didn't undermine social and religious order.

Government: Ministers held enormous cultural and moral influence — but crucially, they did not typically hold civil office. Massachusetts had elected representatives and town meetings that gave ordinary male settlers a real, if bounded, voice in public decisions.

Why This Matters

The more precise description isn't "theocracy" but a godly commonwealth — a system where civil and ecclesiastical institutions were distinct but closely intertwined, and where consent and participation operated inside a framework of strongly shared religious assumptions. That's neither modern liberal democracy nor simple clerical dictatorship — and getting that balance right will matter again next lecture, when we ask who actually controlled political authority in colonial America.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

David D. Hall – A Reforming People

Hall's work is one of the most important correctives to the "simple theocracy" stereotype. He emphasizes settlers' deep fear of arbitrary authority and their efforts to build both churches and civil government around consent, accountability, written rules, and broad local participation — without turning Massachusetts into a modern democracy in the process.

Section III

Truth Had Limits

What happens when someone inside the community says the community itself is wrong?

📖 Understanding This Slide

Section III turns the lecture's attention from what the Puritan project built to what it couldn't tolerate. If a community believes it holds religious truth collectively, what happens to someone inside that community who disagrees?

Why This Matters

This section is where the "Limits" category from your roadmap gets its full answer, through two real historical conflicts — Roger Williams and Anne Hutchinson — each testing the boundary from a different angle.

How Far Should Government Reach?

Roger Williams pushed the logic of conscience further than Massachusetts would accept.

  • Civil government should not control belief
  • Coerced worship corrupted true religion
  • Challenged England's claim to Native land
  • Banished, 1636 — founded Providence
“God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any civill state.”
— Roger Williams, The Bloudy Tenent of Persecution, 1644

📖 Understanding This Slide

Roger Williams took the logic of individual religious conscience — a logic Puritans themselves relied on — and pushed it further than Massachusetts leaders were willing to go.

Breaking It Down

Roger Williams argued that civil magistrates had no legitimate authority over spiritual conscience, and that coerced worship actually corrupted true religion rather than protecting it. In his words: "God requireth not an uniformity of Religion to be inacted and inforced in any civill state." He separated civil peace from religious uniformity — a state could punish theft or violence without compelling correct worship.

Williams also challenged the legitimacy of English land claims that rested only on royal grant rather than actual purchase from Indigenous owners — connecting his religious argument to a critique of colonial land policy. Massachusetts banished him in 1635–1636, and he went on to establish Providence, which became part of Rhode Island.

Why This Matters

Don't turn Williams into a modern secular liberal ahead of his time. His defense of liberty of conscience came from intense Protestant conviction, not religious indifference — he believed state coercion contaminated the church, not that religion didn't matter. His banishment shows exactly where Massachusetts drew its line: freedom to build a godly community, not toleration of people who rejected the community's religious authority.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Edmund S. Morgan – Roger Williams: The Church and the State

Morgan's concise biography centers on Williams's radical separation of civil authority from spiritual conscience, showing why his defense of religious liberty grew out of intense Protestant commitment rather than modern religious indifference.

📖 Understanding This Slide

If Williams tested the boundary between church and state, Anne Hutchinson tested the boundary of who got to define religious truth inside the church itself.

Breaking It Down

Anne Hutchinson hosted meetings in Boston where participants discussed sermons and theology. She developed a large following and became associated with political figures who challenged opponents within the colony. The dispute — often called the Antinomian Controversy or Free Grace Controversy — involved genuinely difficult theological questions about grace, assurance, and how believers could recognize authentic salvation. During her civil trial, her claim to receive direct revelation from God intensified the crisis, since it seemed to place her private spiritual authority beyond the judgment of ministers and magistrates. She was banished in 1638 and later helped found parts of what became Rhode Island.

Why This Matters

Gender mattered here — a woman exercising this much religious influence over men, and judging ministers publicly, crossed accepted boundaries. But reducing the whole controversy to misogyny alone misses why male allies like John Wheelwright and Governor Henry Vane also became targets. The stronger reading treats gender as one element of a larger fight over who could define orthodoxy and whose religious experience counted as legitimate evidence of grace.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Michael P. Winship – Making Heretics

Winship argues the older "Antinomian Controversy" label oversimplifies a shifting conflict among ministers, lay factions, and political leaders. His key insight: "heresy" here was produced through escalating controversy, not discovered as a stable, pre-existing package of beliefs.

Emery Battis – Saints and Sectaries

Battis's classic social reconstruction of Hutchinson's supporters and their political networks remains a major reference point. Reading Battis alongside Winship shows how interpretations have shifted from a relatively stable "Antinomian" party toward a more dynamic account of controversy and heresy-formation.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Williams and Hutchinson didn't just get banished into nothing — their exile helped produce an entirely different regional model of religious life. This slide builds a simple comparison, then immediately complicates it.

Breaking It Down

Massachusetts Bay protected freedom for its own covenanted community, defined and enforced truth collectively, and banished dissenters who threatened that definition. Rhode Island, founded in large part by people banished from Massachusetts, became known for far broader religious liberty and the absence of an established church — a genuinely different institutional experiment, not just a punishment colony.

Why This Matters

This comparison table is useful, but treat it the same way you treated the Virginia/New England table last lecture: a starting model, not a fixed rule. Rhode Island had its own internal conflicts, and Massachusetts's civic participation (town meetings, elected officers) was real, not fake. Rhode Island's existence mainly demonstrates that seventeenth-century New England was not one uniform, unchanging religious system — dissent could generate entirely new colonies, not just disappear.

⏸ Freedom or Toleration?

Religious Freedom

Our community should be free to practice our religion.

Religious Toleration

People who disagree should also be free to practice theirs.

Which better describes Massachusetts Bay? Were the Puritans hypocrites?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This activity asks you to hold two ideas apart that get blurred together constantly, both in casual conversation and in textbooks: freedom and toleration.

Breaking It Down

Religious freedom, in the Puritan sense, meant the freedom of a covenanted community to establish and practice its own true religion without outside interference (from bishops, the king, and so on). Religious toleration means something different: a willingness to let people who hold different religious views practice those views too, inside the same political community.

Why This Matters

Massachusetts Bay clearly wanted the first. It just as clearly did not want the second — and that's not really a contradiction once you see freedom and toleration as two separate things, rather than two words for the same idea. Whether you call that "hypocrisy" is a judgment call worth arguing about — but the historical claim is more precise than a simple accusation of hypocrisy.

Religious Freedom, Without Toleration

Puritan New England expanded religious freedom — for Puritans.

It restricted religious toleration — for everyone else.

Building a godly society and tolerating dissent turned out to be two different things.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the one sentence — well, two — worth remembering a week from now, even if you forget every date and name.

Breaking It Down

Puritan New England expanded religious freedom for the people already inside its covenanted community. At the very same time, it restricted religious toleration for anyone outside that community's definition of truth. These aren't contradictory claims about the same thing — they're two different, simultaneously true facts about one society.

Why This Matters

Watch for this tension — expanding participation for some while restricting it for others — to reappear throughout the semester in very different contexts: indentured servitude and slavery, the American Revolution's promises versus its exclusions, and beyond. Puritan New England is your first full case study of it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide takes the caution box from the classroom deck and gives it full room to breathe: was the Mayflower Compact really "the birth of American democracy"?

Breaking It Down

What the Compact actually did: it bound the signers to recognize a common set of laws and officers, specifically to prevent faction and lawlessness while the colony was in an uncertain legal position — and it kept the signers loyal subjects of King James the whole time.

What it didn't do: it did not establish universal suffrage (only adult male signers were involved, and several passengers didn't sign at all), it did not declare independence from England, and it did not create a detailed system of government or a constitution.

Why This Matters

The Compact's later transformation into a founding constitutional myth is itself part of the history of American memory — not unlike what happens to Winthrop's "city upon a hill" phrase later in American history. A religious migrant community needed practical civil rules to survive its first winter, before it even had functioning churches. That's a smaller, but arguably more interesting, historical claim than "birth of democracy."

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide digs into how historians' understanding of the Anne Hutchinson crisis has actually changed over time — a good example of historiography (the study of how historical interpretation itself evolves) in action.

Breaking It Down

The older label, "Antinomian Controversy," implies a fixed heresy — as if Hutchinson and her allies simply rejected moral law (antinomian literally means "against the law") — and tends to treat Hutchinson as the whole story.

A newer reading, following historian Michael Winship, reconstructs this as a shifting sequence of disputes among ministers, lay factions, Governor Henry Vane, John Wheelwright, John Cotton, Thomas Shepard, Hutchinson, and others — where "heresy" itself was produced by escalating conflict, rather than discovered as a stable, pre-existing set of beliefs.

Why This Matters

This matters for how you write about controversies in general, not just this one: naming a "heresy" or a "controversy" often flattens a much messier, more contingent process into something that sounds inevitable in hindsight. Winship's reframing invites you to ask, for any historical conflict, "who is doing the labeling, and when?"

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide tackles a genuinely tricky historical question: did the intense religious energy of the founding generation actually fade over time, or does it just look that way?

Breaking It Down

The problem: New England's church membership system required credible evidence of personal conversion. Children of church members were baptized, but weren't automatically full communicant members as adults — they had to experience and describe their own conversion. As the first generation aged, many baptized adults hadn't achieved full membership themselves, raising awkward questions about baptizing their children.

The 1662 compromise, known as the Half-Way Covenant, let these baptized-but-unconverted adults present their own children for baptism, even though the adults themselves still couldn't take communion as full members. It preserved a broad connection between church and community while keeping the distinction between full "saints" and everyone else.

Why This Matters

Whether this counts as "decline" depends a lot on what you think institutions are supposed to do. It's true that later ministers' sermons (called jeremiads) worried openly about fading piety — but jeremiads were also a conventional literary form of religious self-criticism, and ministers had real incentives to describe their communities as falling short. A more balanced reading treats the Half-Way Covenant as evidence of a demographically successful religious experiment adapting to reproduce itself across generations, not necessarily a spiritual collapse.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Edmund S. Morgan – Visible Saints

Morgan shows that the "problem" behind the Half-Way Covenant wasn't incidental bureaucracy — it arose directly from the New England ideal of a pure church made up of tested, professing saints. A system built to preserve the church's purity risked narrowing that same church across generations.

Perry Miller – on the "declension" thesis

Miller made "declension" a central theme of New England intellectual history, reading the jeremiads as genuine evidence that the founders' covenantal mission was eroding under prosperity and generational change. Later historians — including David Hall and Michael Winship — have pushed back, emphasizing institutional adaptation and continuing reform rather than a simple, straight-line decline.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide picks up a thread the classroom lecture could only gesture at: Puritan missionary work among Native peoples, and why it's so hard to categorize as either purely spiritual or purely coercive.

Breaking It Down

Puritan interest in Indigenous conversion intensified in the 1640s. John Eliot learned the Massachusett language, preached in it, and eventually helped produce a Bible translation. The Mayhew family pursued similar missionary work on Martha's Vineyard.

The so-called praying towns that resulted could offer Native converts real advantages: access to English allies, literacy, trade connections, and a measure of protection in a world increasingly dominated by settlers. But they could also demand deep cultural change — new religious practice, new political authority structures, new gendered patterns of labor — and weaken older Native political and religious institutions in the process.

Why This Matters

Resist collapsing this into either "sincere religious outreach" or "cynical colonial control" — both readings are too simple on their own. Conversion in praying towns often contained genuine spiritual agency and coercive structural pressure at the same time. This ambiguity is itself the historical lesson, and it connects directly to the providential worldview from Section II: Puritans interpreted their own expanding success as a sign of divine favor, which made it easier to see missionary and territorial expansion as righteous rather than as displacement.

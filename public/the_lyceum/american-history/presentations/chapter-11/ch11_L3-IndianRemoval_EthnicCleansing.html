The homeland · 1820s

The Cherokee Nation held its homeland where four states met, and Georgia claimed it.

The land: farms, towns, and a capital at New Echota, in today's northwest Georgia.
The people: about 16,000 Cherokee still lived in the East in 1835.
The neighbors: Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole; some 60,000 people in five nations.
The claim: Georgia said every acre inside its borders belonged under Georgia law.
The Cherokee were not a remnant. They were a nation with borders, and Georgia drew its own borders around them.

Five Tribes: the five southeastern nations removed in the 1830s. White Americans later called them the "Five Civilized Tribes," a label that ranked peoples by how much they resembled white Americans.

Sources: Perdue & Green, The Cherokee Nation and the Trail of Tears (2007); 1835 Cherokee census (Henderson Roll); course case study, "Jacksonian Indian Removal."

Before the removal, there was a homeland. In the 1820s the Cherokee Nation covered the mountains and valleys where Georgia, Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina meet. Its capital, New Echota, sat in what is now northwest Georgia.

Most Cherokee were farmers. They raised corn, hogs, and cattle, and some ran mills, ferries, and stores. A census in 1835 counted about 16,000 Cherokee in the East.

They had neighbors in the same situation: the Creek, also called the Muscogee; the Choctaw; the Chickasaw; and the Seminole in Florida. Together these five nations numbered roughly 60,000 people. Read the gloss at the bottom: white Americans later called them the "Five Civilized Tribes," a label that judged them by how much they looked like white society. We will just say the Five Tribes.

Look at the map. It is a real 1830 map of Georgia, and in the northwest corner it labels "Cherokee Indians." Even the mapmaker knew it was their land. But Georgia claimed it too.

Why this matters: this is a story about two governments claiming the same ground.

Building a nation · 1821–1828

The Cherokee built a written language, a constitution, and a newspaper.

1821: Sequoyah finished a syllabary; within a few years thousands of Cherokee could read and write.
1827: a written constitution created a legislature, courts, and a principal chief.
1828: the Cherokee Phoenix, edited by Elias Boudinot, printed in English and Cherokee.
A hard truth: by 1835 wealthy Cherokee planters held more than 1,500 enslaved Black people.
The Cherokee did what U.S. "civilization" policy asked. It made them a stronger nation, not a safer one.

Syllabary: a writing system in which each symbol stands for a syllable. Sequoyah's had 86 symbols.

For thirty years, U.S. officials had told Native nations to farm, go to school, and govern themselves like Americans. The Cherokee took that advice further than anyone expected.

First, writing. Around 1821 a Cherokee silversmith named Sequoyah finished a syllabary, a set of symbols for every syllable in the Cherokee language. It was so easy to learn that within a few years thousands of Cherokee were reading and writing letters in their own language.

Second, government. In 1827 the Cherokee wrote a constitution with a legislature, courts, and an elected principal chief. In 1828 they launched a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, printed at New Echota in English and Cherokee. Its editor was Elias Boudinot; remember his name.

Look at the two images. Both are real: Sequoyah holding his syllabary, from an 1836 print, and the first page of the Phoenix.

There is a hard truth in the red box. Copying the plantation South also meant copying slavery. By 1835 Cherokee planters held more than 1,500 enslaved Black people.

Why this matters: a nation with a constitution looked more like a rival state, and Georgia treated it that way.

Georgia's claim · 1802–1832

Georgia wanted Cherokee land, and gold made it want it faster.

1802: the U.S. promised Georgia it would end Indian land titles in the state "as early as the same can be peaceably obtained on reasonable terms."
1828–1829: gold near today's Dahlonega; thousands of prospectors poured into Cherokee country.
1828–1830: Georgia declared Cherokee laws void and barred Cherokee from testifying against whites.
1832: a state lottery handed Cherokee land to white Georgians.
Georgia did not wait for removal. It made staying unbearable.

Sources: Articles of Agreement and Cession (Compact of 1802); Georgia acts of 1828–1830; Perdue & Green (2007); Saunt, Unworthy Republic (2020).

Now the pressure. In 1802 Georgia gave up its claim to western lands, which became Alabama and Mississippi. In return, the federal government promised to end Indian land titles inside Georgia "as early as the same can be peaceably obtained on reasonable terms." For twenty-five years Georgians reminded Washington of that promise.

Cotton raised the stakes. The upland South was turning into cotton country, and planters and small farmers wanted more ground. Then, in 1828 and 1829, gold was found in the Cherokee hills near today's Dahlonega, and thousands of prospectors rushed in.

Georgia's legislature acted between 1828 and 1830. It extended state law over the Cherokee Nation, declared Cherokee laws void, and barred Cherokee from testifying against white people in Georgia courts. A Cherokee whose farm was robbed had no way to win in court. In 1832 Georgia held a lottery that gave Cherokee land to white citizens.

The president · 1814–1830

Andrew Jackson came to the White House determined to move the southern nations west.

1814: after defeating the Red Stick Creeks, General Jackson imposed the Treaty of Fort Jackson, taking about 23 million acres, including land of Creeks who had fought beside him.
1829: President Jackson told the Cherokee and Creek he would not stop states from ruling over them.
His argument: move west, beyond the states, and survive; stay, and live under state law.
Jackson called removal a kindness. The people being moved called it exile.

Sources: Treaty of Fort Jackson (1814); Remini, Andrew Jackson and His Indian Wars (2001); Howe, What Hath God Wrought (2007).

Georgia had an ally in the White House. Andrew Jackson, elected in 1828, had built his fame fighting Native nations as well as the British.

In 1814, during the Creek War, Jackson's army crushed the Red Sticks, a militant Creek faction, at Horseshoe Bend. The peace he dictated, the Treaty of Fort Jackson, took about 23 million acres from the Creek Nation, including land belonging to Creeks who had fought on Jackson's side.

As president, Jackson took Georgia's side. In 1829 he told the Cherokee and Creek that the federal government would not protect them from state laws. Their choice, as he framed it, was to move west, beyond any state, or stay and live as subjects of Georgia or Alabama.

The portrait is real, painted by Ralph Earl in 1817, when Jackson was still a general.

Why this matters: Jackson sincerely believed removal was best for everyone, including Native people. He also believed white settlers had a better claim to the land. Both beliefs pushed the same way.

Primary source · Jackson's Second Annual Message, December 6, 1830

How did Jackson explain removal to Congress?

"It gives me pleasure to announce to Congress that the benevolent policy of the Government, steadily pursued for nearly thirty years, in relation to the removal of the Indians beyond the white settlements is approaching to a happy consummation. . . .

What good man would prefer a country covered with forests and ranged by a few thousand savages to our extensive Republic, studded with cities, towns, and prosperous farms . . . ?"
Andrew Jackson, Second Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1830

Consummation: completion. Benevolent: kind, well-meaning.

Here is Jackson in his own words. Every December the president sent Congress an annual message, what we now call the State of the Union. In 1830 Jackson used his to celebrate removal.

Read the first sentence. He calls the policy "benevolent," meaning kind, and says it is nearing a "happy consummation," a happy completion. Elsewhere in the same message he promised that removal would free the tribes from the power of the states and let them live "in their own way."

Now read the second sentence, his question. He pictures Native land as empty forest "ranged by a few thousand savages" and asks what good man would not prefer farms and cities. Notice what that leaves out: Cherokee farms, mills, and towns already stood on that land.

The law · May 28, 1830

Congress passed the Indian Removal Act by five votes.

The deal: the president could trade western land to tribes "as may choose to exchange" their eastern homes.
The promise: the U.S. would "forever secure and guaranty" the new land, with $500,000 to pay the costs.
The vote: Senate 28–19; House 102–97. Senator Theodore Frelinghuysen and Rep. Davy Crockett opposed it.
On paper, removal was a choice. Georgia's laws and Jackson's pressure took the choice away.

Sources: An Act to provide for an exchange of lands with the Indians residing in any of the states or territories, May 28, 1830 (4 Stat. 411); Register of Debates, 21st Congress, 1st Session.

In the spring of 1830 Congress debated removal for weeks, and it was close. The Senate passed the bill 28 to 19. The House passed it 102 to 97, a margin of five votes. Jackson signed it on May 28, 1830.

Read what the law actually said. It let the president trade land west of the Mississippi to tribes "as may choose to exchange" their eastern lands. It promised that the United States would "forever secure and guaranty" the new western lands to them. It set aside $500,000 to pay for it all.

Notice the word "choose." The act did not order anyone removed by force. But Georgia's laws were already making life impossible, and Jackson would not protect the tribes from those laws.

Opponents saw this. Senator Theodore Frelinghuysen of New Jersey spoke against the bill for about six hours over three days, and Congressman Davy Crockett of Tennessee voted no.

Memorial: a formal written petition to a government. Molestation: harassment, interference.

The Cherokee answered with the tools of a free press and a free people. In 1830 they sent memorials, formal petitions, to Congress, and that July the Cherokee government addressed the American people directly. The Cherokee Phoenix printed the address, and newspapers across the country reprinted it.

Read the slide. "We are not willing to remove." "We wish to remain on the land of our fathers." Then comes the legal argument: treaties with the United States, and federal laws made under those treaties, guarantee their right to stay. Treaties, under the Constitution, are part of the supreme law of the land.

Elsewhere the address says the western country "is not the land of our birth, nor of our affections." This is not a plea for pity. It is a nation arguing its rights.

Appeal to the courts · 1831–1832

The Cherokee took Georgia to the Supreme Court, and in 1832 they won.

1831, Cherokee Nation v. Georgia: the Court called Indian tribes "domestic dependent nations" and would not hear the suit.
1831: Georgia sentenced missionary Samuel Worcester to four years at hard labor for living in the Nation without a state license.
March 1832, Worcester v. Georgia: the Cherokee Nation is "a distinct community . . . in which the laws of Georgia can have no force."
The highest court in the land agreed with the Cherokee.

Sources: Cherokee Nation v. Georgia, 30 U.S. 1 (1831); Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. 515 (1832).

Next the Cherokee went to court. In 1831 they sued Georgia in the Supreme Court. Chief Justice John Marshall, whom you met in Lecture 1, ruled that the Cherokee were not a foreign nation but one of the "domestic dependent nations," something like a ward under a guardian, so the Court would not hear the suit. It was a loss, but a narrow one.

Then Georgia gave them a better case. A Georgia law required every white person living in the Cherokee Nation to get a state license and swear loyalty to Georgia. A missionary from Vermont named Samuel Worcester refused. Georgia arrested him and sentenced him to four years at hard labor.

His appeal, Worcester v. Georgia, reached the Court in 1832, and Marshall ruled for him and for the Cherokee. Read the quotation: the Cherokee Nation was "a distinct community" in which "the laws of Georgia can have no force." Only the federal government could deal with the Cherokee, through treaties.

Why this matters: the Cherokee had now won on the highest legal ground the United States offered.

The answer · 1832–1833

Georgia ignored the ruling, and Jackson would not enforce it.

Georgia: refused to free Worcester; he stayed in prison until January 1833.
Jackson, April 7, 1832: the decision "has fell still born, and they find that it cannot coerce Georgia to yield to its mandate."
The famous line "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" first appeared in print in 1864. He probably never said it.
1832: Georgia's lottery gave away Cherokee land while Cherokee families still lived on it.
A court can say what the law is. It cannot make a president act.

Sources: Jackson to John Coffee, April 7, 1832, in Correspondence of Andrew Jackson, ed. Bassett, vol. 4; Horace Greeley, The American Conflict (1864); Perdue & Green (2007).

The Cherokee celebrated, but the victory went nowhere. Georgia simply ignored the Supreme Court and kept Samuel Worcester in prison.

Enforcing a court ruling is the president's job, and Jackson did nothing. Read his private letter from April 7, 1832: the decision had "fell still born," born dead, because the Court could not force Georgia to obey.

You may have heard the line "John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it." It first appeared in print in 1864, decades after Jackson's death, and historians doubt he said it. His real letter is just as revealing.

Worcester finally left prison in January 1833, after he stopped his legal fight and the governor pardoned him. Meanwhile Georgia ran its lottery. The image shows a real land grant from it: a piece of the Cherokee Nation given to a white Georgian, while Cherokee families still farmed it.

Why this matters: the Cherokee's best argument was legal, and the law turned out to be only as strong as the people willing to enforce it.

The other four nations · 1830–1842

The Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole lost their homelands too, or fought.

Choctaw, 1831–1833: the first nation removed under the act; blizzards and cholera killed thousands.
Creek, 1836–1837: after fraud and violence, the army removed about 15,000 people, some in chains.
Chickasaw, 1837–1838: paid for their own removal from the sale of their land.
Seminole, 1835–1842: fought the Second Seminole War; several hundred stayed in Florida.

Each nation's story continues in the Pierian Spring: Choctaw · Creek · Chickasaw · Seminole

Sources: Foreman, Indian Removal (1932); Akers, Living in the Land of Death (2004); Saunt, Unworthy Republic (2020). Death tolls are estimates.

Before we follow the Cherokee to the end, look at the map. The Cherokee were not the first to go. While they fought in court, the other four nations were already losing their homelands.

The Choctaw of Mississippi signed the first removal treaty under the new act in 1830 and moved west between 1831 and 1833, through blizzards and then a cholera epidemic. Estimates of the dead run into the thousands.

The Creek of Alabama were cheated out of their land by speculators. When some fought back in 1836, the army removed about 15,000 Creek, some of the men in chains.

The Chickasaw negotiated harder and paid for their own removal out of the money from selling their land, but they still suffered disease and hunger on the way.

The Seminole of Florida fought. The Second Seminole War lasted from 1835 to 1842, and several hundred Seminole were never removed.

A nation divided · 1832–1835

A small Cherokee faction decided that a bad treaty was better than none.

National Party: Principal Chief John Ross and most Cherokee refused to sell.
Treaty Party: Major Ridge, his son John Ridge, and his nephew Elias Boudinot believed removal could not be stopped.
1832: Boudinot resigned from the Phoenix when the council would not let him print the case for a treaty.
Cherokee law, 1829: selling national land without the nation's consent was punishable by death.
Both sides wanted the Cherokee to survive. They disagreed about whether staying was possible.
Names: Elias Boudinot was born Gallegina, or Buck, Watie; he took the name of a New Jersey patron. Major Ridge's Cherokee name was Kahnungdatlageh.

After 1832, the Cherokee faced a terrible question. Was there any way left to stay?

Most said yes, or at least that they would never sell. Their leader was the principal chief, John Ross, and his supporters were called the National Party.

A smaller group said no. Major Ridge, an elder and war hero; his son John Ridge; and his nephew Elias Boudinot, the newspaper editor, came to believe removal could not be stopped. Better, they argued, to sign a treaty now and get money and western land than to lose everything for nothing. They became known as the Treaty Party.

In 1832 Boudinot resigned as editor of the Phoenix after the council would not let him print that argument.

Read the red box. In 1829 the Cherokee had written into law that selling national land without the nation's consent was punishable by death. Major Ridge himself had supported that principle. Keep it in mind.

The treaty · New Echota, December 29, 1835

At New Echota, a few hundred Cherokee agreed to sell the whole homeland.

Who: a few hundred Cherokee at most, out of about 16,000; roughly twenty men signed.
The terms: all land east of the Mississippi for $5 million and land in Indian Territory; two years to move.
Not authorized: the Cherokee National Council never approved it; Ross was in Washington.
May 1836: the Senate ratified it 31–15, exactly the two-thirds required; one vote fewer and it would have failed.
The treaty was law to the United States and a fraud to the Cherokee Nation.

Sources: Treaty with the Cherokee, December 29, 1835 (7 Stat. 478); Perdue & Green (2007); Smithsonian NMAI, Nation to Nation.

In December 1835 a U.S. commissioner, the Reverend John Schermerhorn, called a meeting at the Cherokee capital, New Echota. He warned that anyone who stayed away would be counted as agreeing. Most Cherokee stayed away anyway.

A few hundred people came, out of about 16,000. On December 29, roughly twenty Treaty Party men signed the Treaty of New Echota. The Cherokee would give up every acre east of the Mississippi in exchange for five million dollars and land in Indian Territory, today's Oklahoma, and they would have two years to leave.

Principal Chief John Ross was in Washington at the time with the nation's official delegation. The Cherokee National Council never approved the treaty.

The U.S. Senate ratified it anyway in May 1836, by 31 to 15. Treaties need two-thirds of the senators voting. Thirty-one was exactly enough; one vote fewer and the treaty would have failed.

This is John Ross, principal chief of the Cherokee Nation. His father was Scottish and his mother part Cherokee, and he grew up speaking English more easily than Cherokee. But the Cherokee people trusted him, and they re-elected him for decades.

In September 1836, Ross wrote to Congress about the Treaty of New Echota. Read the slide. He refuses even to call it a treaty; he calls it "this instrument." Ross did not only write. In 1838 he brought Congress a protest signed by 15,665 Cherokee, nearly the whole nation. Congress took no action.

The deadline · May 1838

When the deadline passed, General Winfield Scott told the Cherokee that the army had come.

"Cherokees! The President of the United States has sent me, with a powerful army, to cause you, in obedience to the Treaty of 1835, to join that part of your people who are already established in prosperity, on the other side of the Mississippi. . . . The full moon of May is already on the wane, and before another shall have passed away, every Cherokee man, woman and child . . . must be in motion to join their brethren in the far West."
General Winfield Scott, address to the Cherokee, May 10, 1838
The president: Martin Van Buren, Jackson's successor, kept the policy.
By May 23, 1838: only about 2,000 Cherokee had gone west. Scott had about 7,000 regulars and militia.
Scott called his soldiers "your friends." He also called resistance and escape "hopeless."

Sources: Winfield Scott, Address to the Cherokee Nation, May 10, 1838, quoted in NPS, "The Trail of Tears and the Forced Relocation of the Cherokee Nation"; reader Doc 5.

The treaty gave the Cherokee two years, until May 23, 1838. Jackson left office in 1837, and his successor, Martin Van Buren, kept the policy.

When the deadline came, only about 2,000 Cherokee had moved west, most of them Treaty Party families. Roughly 16,000 were still at home, many still hoping Ross could stop it.

Van Buren sent General Winfield Scott with about 7,000 soldiers, a mix of regular army and state militia. On May 10, 1838, Scott issued the address on the slide. Read it: "every Cherokee man, woman and child" must be moving west before the next full moon.

The rest of the address mixes a threat with a plea. Scott calls the troops "your friends," but says there are enough of them to make resistance and escape "hopeless." He calls himself "an old warrior" and begs the Cherokee to spare him "the horror of witnessing the destruction of the Cherokees."

The roundup · May–June 1838

Soldiers went from farm to farm, and families had minutes to leave.

May 26, 1838: the roundup began in Georgia, then spread to Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina.
Seized: people were taken in fields, on roads, and at home, often with only the clothes they wore.
Behind the troops: settlers took livestock, furniture, and houses.
"The Cherokees are nearly all prisoners. They have been dragged from their houses, and encamped at the forts and military posts, all over the nation."
Evan Jones, Baptist missionary, Camp Hetzel, Tennessee, June 16, 1838

Sources: Evan Jones, letter of June 16, 1838 (Baptist Missionary Magazine, 1838); Perdue & Green (2007); Neugin in Foreman (1932).

On May 26, 1838, the roundup began in Georgia, where state militia did much of the work, and over the next weeks it spread to Tennessee, Alabama, and North Carolina.

Soldiers went from farm to farm. People were taken while plowing, walking on the road, or eating dinner. Many left with only the clothes they wore. Remember Rebecca Neugin's mother begging to go back for bedding: that was this moment.

Behind the soldiers came settlers. They drove off cattle and hogs, carried off furniture, and moved into houses still warm from the families who had lived there.

Read the quotation. Evan Jones was a white Baptist missionary who had lived among the Cherokee for years. On June 16, 1838, he wrote from a camp in Tennessee: "The Cherokees are nearly all prisoners." Notice his word: prisoners, not emigrants.

The stockades · summer 1838

Thousands waited in stockades and camps through a summer of heat and disease.

The camps: forts and holding camps, the largest near the Cherokee Agency on the Hiwassee River in Tennessee.
June 1838: the army sent about 2,800 people west by river; heat and sickness were so deadly that Scott halted removal until fall.
Disease: dysentery, measles, and whooping cough spread through crowded camps.
Ross's request: the Cherokee won the right to run their own removal, in 13 detachments, once the heat broke.
The dying started before the walking.

Sources: NPS, Trail of Tears National Historic Trail; Perdue & Green (2007); Thornton, "Cherokee Population Losses" (1984).

The prisoners were marched to stockades, log-walled forts, and then to larger holding camps, many near the Cherokee Agency on the Hiwassee River in southeastern Tennessee.

In June the army tried to send the first groups west by river, about 2,800 people in three detachments. It was a drought summer, rivers ran low, and the heat and sickness were so deadly that Scott stopped further removal until the weather cooled.

That meant months of waiting in crowded camps with poor water and poor food. Dysentery, measles, and whooping cough spread fast, and children and elders died first. Hundreds died before the main journey began; some estimates run higher.

John Ross then won a concession: the Cherokee themselves would organize the rest of the removal, in thirteen detachments with Cherokee leaders, paid for out of treaty funds.

In late summer and fall of 1838 the Cherokee-led detachments set out, each of about a thousand people with wagons, horses, and oxen.

Most took a northern overland route: across Tennessee and Kentucky, over the Ohio River into southern Illinois, across the Mississippi into Missouri, and down through Arkansas into Indian Territory. The winter of 1838 to 1839 was hard. Ice on the Ohio and Mississippi stopped several detachments for weeks in southern Illinois, camped in the snow, with too little food and too few blankets. People died of pneumonia, exposure, and disease, and were buried beside the road.

How many people died in Indian Removal? Not just the Cherokee: every nation forced west paid in lives. Historians give ranges, and you should learn both the numbers and why they are uncertain.

The Choctaw went first, from 1831 to 1833, through blizzards and then cholera. A National Park Service study counts about 12,500 who reached Indian Territory and about 2,500 deaths on the way; older contemporary estimates ran as high as 6,000.

The Muscogee, or Creek, were removed in 1836 and 1837 after the Creek War, about 14,500 to 15,000 people, some in chains. The Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture says several hundred died on the journey and about 3,200 more from disease, hunger, and exposure after arriving. Historian Christopher Haveman puts the total near 3,500. That includes about 300 who drowned when the steamboat Monmouth sank in 1837.

The Chickasaw paid for their own removal in 1837 and 1838. More than 500 died of smallpox, but no one has produced a full count of their losses.

The Cherokee were removed in 1838 and 1839, about 16,000 people. About 4,000 deaths is the most-cited figure, from the missionary doctor Elizur Butler. Some studies come in near 2,000, and the demographer Russell Thornton argued the loss may have been about twice the usual figure, around 8,000, counting the whole period. That is roughly one in four or five.

The Seminole fought the Second Seminole War from 1835 to 1842 instead of leaving quietly. About 3,000 were eventually sent west. Deaths in battle, in prison camps, and on the way west overlap, and no reliable separate removal toll exists.

Removal reached northern nations too. In 1838 militia marched 859 Potawatomi from Indiana to Kansas at gunpoint. The Kansas Historical Society records 42 deaths, 28 of them children; the Potawatomi call it the Trail of Death. The Sauk and Meskwaki, Shawnee, Miami, and others were also pushed west in these years, but there are no reliable death totals for their removals, so they are not in the table. The hundreds of Sauk and Meskwaki killed in the Black Hawk War of 1832 were war deaths, not a removal count.

Add up the low ends and the high ends of the estimates we have, and you get roughly 8,500 to 18,500 deaths. That is a sum of partial estimates, not a census. The National Park Service says simply that over 10,000 Native Americans died during removal or soon after arrival. Treat any single total as a floor: the nations and months nobody counted would only push it higher.

The survivors arrived in Indian Territory, in what is now eastern Oklahoma, sick, grieving, and short of everything. They began again: clearing fields, building cabins, opening schools, and setting up a new capital at Tahlequah.

Naming it · from the 1830s to the 1990s

Historians now ask whether a newer word fits this older story: ethnic cleansing.

Where the term came from

It spread in 1991–1992 from Serbian and Croatian etničko čišćenje, used by perpetrators and nationalist media in the wars of Yugoslavia's breakup. Western journalists and diplomats adopted it.

Why it is uncomfortable

"Cleansing" was a euphemism. It made driving people out sound like cleaning something dirty. When we use the term, we should remember whose word it was.

What it is not

It is not a crime defined on its own in international law, as genocide is. It is a descriptive term; the acts it describes can be prosecuted as crimes against humanity.

The word is new. The question is old: when is moving a people really driving them out?

Euphemism: a mild word used in place of a harsh one. Anachronism: judging the past with ideas from a later time; this is why you will test the term against evidence instead of assuming it. The Pierian Spring explains how historians handle it.

UN Commission of Experts (1993): "rendering an area ethnically homogenous by using force or intimidation to remove persons of given groups from the area." Interim report S/25274, para. 55, quoted in the Final Report, S/1994/674, para. 129. Also: course framework, "Understanding Ethnic Cleansing".

The term "ethnic cleansing" entered English in 1991 and 1992, during the wars that broke Yugoslavia apart. It came from the Serbian and Croatian phrase etničko čišćenje, used by the perpetrators and nationalist media themselves. Western reporters and diplomats picked it up, and it became the standard term.

Notice the problem in the red card. "Cleansing" is a euphemism, a soft word for a harsh act. It makes driving people from their homes sound like cleaning something dirty. Your course framework asks you to remember that we are borrowing the perpetrators' language.

Notice also the gold card. Unlike genocide, ethnic cleansing is not defined as a separate crime in international law. It is a descriptive term, though the acts involved, such as forced deportation and persecution, can be prosecuted as crimes against humanity.

No one in the 1830s used this word. Applying it is a historian's judgment, made with evidence, not a label we stamp on automatically. That caution is part of good historical thinking.

The first official definition came from the UN Commission of Experts, which the Security Council created in 1992 to investigate crimes in the former Yugoslavia. Its first interim report (S/25274, 1993) said that ethnic cleansing "means rendering an area ethnically homogenous by using force or intimidation to remove persons of given groups from the area." The 1994 Final Report repeats that sentence. ("Homogenous" is the report's own spelling.) Your capstone's working definition, on the next slide, builds on this idea: force, a targeted group, and a territory made homogeneous.

The capstone's definition

Your capstone defines ethnic cleansing as forced removal meant to make a territory homogeneous.

Working Definition: Ethnic Cleansing"Ethnic cleansing is the systematic forced removal of an ethnic, racial, or religious group from a geographic area through violence, intimidation, forced deportation, or other coercive means, with the intent to create an ethnically homogeneous region. It is distinct from genocide in that the primary goal is removal rather than complete physical destruction (though the two can overlap)."

This is the definition your capstone worksheet uses, word for word. Read it slowly, because every phrase matters.

"Systematic": organized, not random. "Forced removal": people are made to leave, not choosing to. "An ethnic, racial, or religious group": people are targeted for who they are, not for anything they did. "Violence, intimidation, forced deportation, or other coercive means": force can be direct or can be pressure that leaves no real choice. "With the intent to create an ethnically homogeneous region": the goal is a territory that belongs to one group.

The last sentence draws a line. Ethnic cleansing aims to remove a people; genocide aims to destroy a people. The definition admits the two can overlap, and the Pierian Spring section shows historians arguing about exactly that for the 1830s.

The capstone's definition · what it is not

Not every movement of people is ethnic cleansing. Look for force, a targeted group, and a goal of emptying a territory.

TermHow it differs from ethnic cleansing
Voluntary migrationPeople choose to move, for land, work, or family. No one forces them out.
War refugeesPeople flee the fighting itself. No one plans to empty the area of their group, and they can usually return.
Population transfer or exchangeGovernments agree to move groups. It can still be ethnic cleansing when the people moved have no real choice: the 1923 Greece–Turkey treaty forced about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians and about 400,000 Muslims out of their homelands by religion.

A definition is only useful if you know where its edges are. This table shows four things that can look like ethnic cleansing and how they differ.

Voluntary migration is a choice. War refugees run from fighting; nobody has a plan to make the area belong to one group. Genocide aims at destroying a people, not just moving them, though the two can overlap.

The hardest row is population transfer. When governments sign an agreement to move people, it can sound orderly. But ask whether the people being moved had any say. In 1923 Greece and Turkey signed a treaty at Lausanne for a compulsory exchange: about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians from Turkey and about 400,000 Muslims from Greece, sorted by religion. Many Greeks had already been driven out by force in 1922 before the treaty made it official. A signed agreement did not make the removal voluntary.

Keep that in mind for the Cherokee. The Removal Act used the language of exchange, and the Treaty of New Echota was a signed treaty. Your job is to decide whether the evidence shows a real choice or a forced removal with paperwork.

Pierian Spring · The Creek (Muscogee), 1825–1837

The Creek lost their land to a forbidden treaty, then to fraud, and then to the army.

Review question: How did both Creek law and U.S. treaties shape what happened to William McIntosh and to Creek land?

Sources: Green, The Politics of Indian Removal: Creek Government and Society in Crisis (1982); Saunt, Unworthy Republic (2020); Foreman (1932).

The Creek, who call themselves Muscogee, lived in Georgia and Alabama. Like the Cherokee, they had a national law forbidding any leader to sell land without the council's consent.

In February 1825 William McIntosh, a Creek leader allied with Georgia, signed the Treaty of Indian Springs anyway and sold Creek land in Georgia. On April 30, 1825, Creek warriors carried out the law: they surrounded his house and killed him. The federal government admitted the treaty was tainted and replaced it in 1826, but Georgia took the land anyway.

The Creek of Alabama then signed the Treaty of Cusseta in 1832. Instead of a nation's land, families got individual plots they could sell. Speculators cheated thousands of Creek out of them, sometimes by having the wrong person "sign" for a plot.

Starving and dispossessed, some Creek fought back in 1836. The army called it the Creek War and removed about 15,000 people, some of the men in chains. In October 1837 an overcrowded steamboat carrying Creek emigrants, the Monmouth, collided with another vessel on the Mississippi; about 300 died.

The Choctaw lived in central and southern Mississippi. In September 1830, months after the Removal Act passed, federal commissioners pressed Choctaw leaders into the Treaty of Dancing Rabbit Creek, the first removal treaty under the new law.

The Choctaw moved in three waves between 1831 and 1833. The first ran into one of the worst winters anyone remembered, the second into a cholera epidemic. Estimates of the dead are usually put around 2,500, and some historians give higher figures. The French writer Alexis de Tocqueville watched a group of Choctaw cross the Mississippi at Memphis in the winter of 1831 and wrote about it in Democracy in America.

Article 14 of the treaty let Choctaw families stay in Mississippi as U.S. citizens on their own land. In practice the federal agent failed to register many of the claims and speculators stripped others. Some Choctaw stayed anyway; their descendants form today's Mississippi Band of Choctaw Indians.

The Chickasaw lived in northern Mississippi and had watched what happened to their neighbors. They bargained hard.

In the Treaty of Pontotoc Creek, signed in October 1832, the Chickasaw gave up their land but insisted that it be sold and the money used to pay for their removal and support. They also refused to move until they found suitable land in the West.

In January 1837, at Doaksville, they agreed to settle inside the Choctaw Nation in Indian Territory. Between 1837 and 1838 roughly four to five thousand Chickasaw moved west, many with livestock and with the Black people they held in slavery.

Better terms did not mean safety. Smallpox broke out, and contractors supplied spoiled rations. Many died in their first year in the West.

The Seminole fought removal for seven years, and some were never removed.

The Seminole of Florida were a mix of Creek migrants and older Florida peoples. Living among them were Black Seminoles, people of African descent, many of them escaped from slavery in Georgia and the Carolinas.

In 1832 some Seminole leaders signed the Treaty of Payne's Landing. Many Seminole rejected it, and in December 1835 they went to war. The Second Seminole War lasted until 1842, cost the United States tens of millions of dollars and the lives of well over a thousand soldiers, and was the costliest of the removal-era wars.

Osceola became its best-known leader. In October 1837 General Thomas Jesup's troops seized him while he was meeting under a white flag of truce, an act many Americans at the time called dishonorable. He died in prison at Fort Moultrie, South Carolina, in January 1838.

Abraham, shown on the right, was a Black Seminole leader and interpreter who negotiated with U.S. officers.

In the end about 3,000 or more Seminole were removed west. Several hundred stayed hidden in the Everglades, and their descendants remain in Florida today.

The men of the Treaty Party were not simply sellouts, and they were not simply heroes either. Historians still argue about them.

Their argument was that Georgia's laws, the gold rush, and Jackson's refusal to help meant staying would end in ruin. A treaty, they said, would at least give the Cherokee money and land in the West. In 1837 Elias Boudinot published a long reply to John Ross, accusing him of keeping the people in false hope while their situation worsened.

But the treaty broke the nation's own law against selling land without consent, and most Cherokee never forgave them. On June 22, 1839, after removal, groups of Cherokee killed Major Ridge, John Ridge, and Elias Boudinot in separate attacks on the same day.

Boudinot's brother, Stand Watie, escaped. Violence between the factions went on for years, until a new treaty in 1846 brought an uneasy peace. Watie later became a Confederate general.

Not every Cherokee went west. In the mountains of western North Carolina, several hundred stayed, and their descendants form today's Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians.

Many of them were the Oconaluftee Cherokee. Under an 1819 treaty they had taken land just outside the Cherokee Nation's borders and accepted North Carolina citizenship, so the Treaty of New Echota did not clearly cover them. A white trader named William Holland Thomas, who had been adopted by the Cherokee leader Yonaguska, bought land for them in his own name, because state law would not let them hold title.

You may hear the story of Tsali. In late 1838 Tsali and members of his family killed two soldiers during the roundup. He was captured and executed. A later legend, popularized in the 1900s, says his sacrifice won the right of the others to stay. The records show the Oconaluftee had their own legal claim already, and historians treat the legend with caution.

The Eastern Band was recognized by the federal government after the Civil War and lives today on the Qualla Boundary.

Numbers feel solid, but each one comes from a method. Here are the main estimates of Cherokee deaths.

About 4,000 comes from Dr. Elizur Butler, a missionary physician who traveled west with a detachment and made his estimate in 1839, and the ethnologist James Mooney repeated it in 1900. It is the figure you will see most often.

A 1973 study that worked from the detachment records found about 2,000 deaths during the journey itself. That is lower partly because it counts a narrower period and only what officials recorded.

In 1984 the demographer Russell Thornton approached it differently. He estimated what the Cherokee population should have been without removal and compared it with what it was. He concluded deaths may have been twice the usual figure, about 8,000.

A term like "ethnic cleansing" is a tool for naming a pattern: a targeted group, forced from a territory, by a state, with the aim of making that land belong to someone else. The Trail of Tears is surrounded by powerful stories, and some of them are shaky. Your capstone grade depends on handling sources carefully, so here are some to watch.

"John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it" first appeared in print in 1864, more than thirty years after the ruling. Jackson's real letter is better evidence.

John G. Burnett's "birthday story" of 1890 claims to be a soldier's memory of the removal. It was written 52 years later, and historians doubt parts of it. That is why your primary source reader no longer includes it. It uses letters and journals written in 1838 instead.

Quatie Ross, John Ross's wife, really did die near Little Rock in 1839. The story that she gave her blanket to a sick child appears in later retellings, not in records from the time.

Rebecca Neugin's memory is real but was recorded 94 years later. It matches what Evan Jones wrote in 1838, which strengthens it.

Finally, watch dates. General Scott addressed the Cherokee on May 10, 1838; his orders to the troops came later that month. Websites often mix the two up, so check which document a quote really comes from. And remember that the Cherokee Phoenix stopped publishing in 1834, so no quotation from it can be dated 1838.

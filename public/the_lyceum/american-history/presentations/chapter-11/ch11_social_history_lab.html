What is social history?

Social history studies the lives of ordinary people (workers, women, families) rather than only presidents and generals. Historians read the few traces these people left behind and ask careful questions of each one.

Instructions

  1. Read each source in Part A and Part B. Use the view source link if you want more context.
  2. For each source, fill in the four columns:
    • Who wrote it? For whom? Who is speaking, and who were they trying to reach?
    • What does it reveal? Something about daily life or about who held power.
    • Feeling shown: an emotion you hear in the voice of the source.
    • Limits: one thing this source cannot tell us, or whose voice is missing.

Part A — Urban Workers (Philadelphia & Boston)

Source Who wrote it? For whom? (author & audience) What does it reveal? (daily life or power) Feeling shown (1–2 words) Limits (what can’t it tell us?)
1. Mechanics’ Union of Trade Associations, Philadelphia, 1827
“Do not those who labour, while acquiring to themselves thereby only a scanty and penurious support, likewise maintain in affluence and luxury the rich who never labour?”
Source: Preamble of the Mechanics’ Union of Trade Associations (1827) — view source
2. Boston Ten-Hour Circular, 1835
“…the Natural Right to dispose of our own time in such quantities as we deem and believe to be most conducive to our own happiness.”
“…a contest between Money and Labor: Capital, which can only be made productive by labor, is endeavoring to crush labor the only source of all wealth.”
Source: Ten-Hour Circular, Boston (1835) — view source

Part B — Lowell Mill Women

Source Who wrote it? For whom? (author & audience) What does it reveal? (daily life or power) Feeling shown (1–2 words) Limits (what can’t it tell us?)
3. “The Spirit of Discontent,” Lowell Offering, 1841
“Up before day, at the clang of the bell—and out of the mill by the clang of the bell—into the mill, and at work, in obedience to that ding-dong of a bell—just as though we were so many living machines.”
(A story written by a mill worker; spoken by one of its characters.)
Source: a story by a mill worker in the Lowell Offering (1841), quoted in Smithsonian — view source
4. Hamilton Manufacturing Company, Regulations (Lowell)
“They are not to be absent from their work without the consent of the overseer, except in cases of sickness…”
(The same rules also required Sabbath church attendance and a 10 p.m. boardinghouse curfew.)
Source: Hamilton Manufacturing Co. regulations, via UMass Lowell Libraries — view source
5. Sarah Bagley, Voice of Industry, June 5, 1845
“When our rights are trampled upon and we appeal in vain to legislators, what shall we do but appeal to the people? Shall not our voice be heard, and our rights acknowledged here?”
Source: Sarah Bagley, Voice of Industry (June 5, 1845), via Speaking While Female — view source
Part B: Source 4 was written by the company; Sources 3 and 5 were written by workers. How does that change what each can tell us?

Putting It Together

In 1–2 sentences: what can these sources teach us about the 1830s–40s that a history of presidents and elections would miss?

Tip: There is no single right answer. Good social history answers point back to specific words in the source.

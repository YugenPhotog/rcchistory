HIST 101 · U.S. History to 1877

A House Divided

Dred Scott, Lincoln, and John Brown, 1857–1859

Chapter 14 · Lecture 2

Start at the end. On the morning of December 2, 1859, in Charles Town, Virginia, John Brown rode to the gallows sitting on his own coffin in a wagon. Hundreds of soldiers stood guard. Among them was a young actor in a borrowed militia uniform, John Wilkes Booth, and a professor from the Virginia Military Institute named Thomas J. Jackson, who would soon be called Stonewall.

Before he left the jail, Brown handed a guard a short note. It said: "I, John Brown, am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty land will never be purged away but with blood." A few minutes later, he was hanged.

The engraving on this slide, from a New York illustrated paper, shows Brown climbing the scaffold. How had the country come to this, with a white Northerner hanged for trying to start a war against slavery, and much of the North mourning him as a saint? This lecture follows three blows, between 1857 and 1859, that closed every road to compromise: a Supreme Court decision, a Senate race in Illinois, and a raid in Virginia.

Takeaway: by the end of 1859, many Americans on both sides believed the slavery question would be settled by force.

Here is where Lecture 1 ended. The Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854 had repealed the Missouri Compromise and let settlers vote on slavery. That law destroyed the Whig Party and helped create the Republicans, a party with no Southern members. In Kansas, popular sovereignty had produced fraud, two rival governments, and a guerrilla war. In 1856, James Buchanan won the presidency, but the Republican John C. Frémont carried most of the free states.

Buchanan was inaugurated on March 4, 1857. In his inaugural address he said the question of when settlers could decide about slavery belonged to the Supreme Court, and that it would soon be settled. He already knew what the Court was about to say. Two days later, it said it.

Takeaway: Buchanan hoped the Supreme Court would end the slavery fight; instead, its ruling set off a new one.

Lecture 2 · Part I

The Court and Kansas

1857

Part I covers one year, 1857. The Supreme Court tried to settle the slavery question in the Dred Scott case. In Kansas, proslavery men tried to force a slave-state constitution through. And a financial panic made many white Southerners more confident than ever that their slave economy was stronger than the North's.

Takeaway: each event of 1857 was meant to settle something, and each one made the conflict worse.

Dred Scott Sued for His Freedom

  • Dred Scott was enslaved by an army surgeon, John Emerson, who took him to the free state of Illinois
  • Then to Fort Snelling, in land where the Missouri Compromise banned slavery; there he married Harriet Robinson
  • 1846: back in St. Louis, Dred and Harriet Scott sued for freedom; they won in 1850 but lost on appeal in 1852
  • The case reached the U.S. Supreme Court as Dred Scott v. Sandford

Dred Scott was born into slavery in Virginia around 1799. His owners moved to St. Louis, Missouri, and in the 1830s he was sold to John Emerson, an army doctor. Emerson took Scott with him to army posts in Illinois, a free state, and then to Fort Snelling, in what is now Minnesota. Slavery was banned there by the Missouri Compromise. At Fort Snelling, Scott married Harriet Robinson, an enslaved woman. Their daughter Eliza was born on a steamboat in free waters.

Back in St. Louis, in 1846, Dred and Harriet Scott sued for their freedom. Missouri courts had often freed enslaved people who had lived on free soil, and in 1850 a St. Louis jury agreed that the Scotts were free. But in 1852 the Missouri Supreme Court reversed the ruling. The case then moved into the federal courts against John Sanford, the brother of Emerson's widow. A clerk misspelled his name, so the case is known as Dred Scott v. Sandford.

The newspaper page on this slide ran in June 1857. It shows the Scotts' daughters at the top and Dred and Harriet Scott at the bottom.

Takeaway: Dred Scott's case asked a simple question: did living on free soil make an enslaved person free?

Taney Ruled That Black Americans Could Not Be Citizens

  • March 6, 1857: Chief Justice Roger B. Taney wrote for a 7 to 2 majority
  • Black Americans, free or enslaved, could not be U.S. citizens, so Scott had no right to sue
  • Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories; the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional
  • Living on free soil had not freed Scott

"...so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect."Chief Justice Roger B. Taney, Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)

Roger B. Taney of Maryland had been chief justice since 1836. He was 79 years old, a former slaveholder, and a firm defender of the South. Five of the nine justices were Southerners. On March 6, 1857, Taney read the Court's decision. Seven justices agreed with the result, and two dissented.

Taney went much further than he needed to. First, he ruled that Black Americans, whether enslaved or free, were not and could never be citizens of the United States, so Scott had no right to sue in federal court. Taney claimed that the men who wrote the Constitution had seen Black people as "so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect." That claim was false: in 1787, free Black men could vote in several states.

Second, Taney ruled that Congress had no power to ban slavery in a territory, because enslaved people were property protected by the Constitution. That meant the Missouri Compromise had been unconstitutional all along. Third, Scott's time on free soil had not made him free.

Dred Scott did not stay enslaved for long. In May 1857, Taylor Blow, a son of Scott's first owner, gained legal ownership of the Scott family and freed them. Dred Scott died of tuberculosis in September 1858.

Takeaway: the Dred Scott decision denied Black citizenship and declared that Congress could not stop slavery from spreading into the territories.

The North Saw a Slave Power Plot

  • The ruling struck down the heart of the Republican platform: no slavery in the territories
  • It also undercut popular sovereignty: if Congress could not ban slavery, could a territory?
  • Many Northerners believed a controlled the government, from the Court to the White House
  • Instead of killing the Republican Party, the ruling helped it grow

Buchanan had quietly urged a Northern justice to join the majority. The public did not know it, but many suspected a deal.

Southerners cheered the decision. Republicans were outraged. The core of their party was the promise to keep slavery out of the territories, and the Supreme Court had just declared that promise unconstitutional.

Stephen Douglas had a problem too. If Congress could not ban slavery in a territory, how could a territorial legislature, which Congress created, ban it? Popular sovereignty seemed to be dead as well.

To many Northerners, the timing looked suspicious. Buchanan's inaugural address had told Americans to accept whatever the Court decided, and two days later the Court decided exactly what the South wanted. In fact, Buchanan had written privately to Justice Robert Grier of Pennsylvania, urging him to join the majority so the ruling would not look purely Southern. Republicans did not have proof of that, but they talked about a Slave Power conspiracy reaching from the Court to the White House.

Frederick Douglass predicted that the decision would backfire, and it did. Northern voters who had never cared much about slavery began to fear it could reach into their own states. The Republican Party grew stronger.

Takeaway: the Dred Scott decision convinced many Northerners that a Slave Power controlled the federal government.

Kansas Voters Rejected the Lecompton Constitution

  • Fall 1857: a proslavery convention at Lecompton wrote a constitution that protected slavery
  • December 1857: free-state voters boycotted a vote that offered no way to reject slavery entirely
  • Buchanan pushed to admit Kansas as a slave state; Douglas broke with him over the fraud
  • The Senate approved; the House balked. In August 1858, Kansas voters rejected Lecompton, about 11,300 to 1,800

Kansas finally entered the Union as a free state in January 1861.

Meanwhile, Kansas still had two governments. In the fall of 1857, proslavery delegates met in the territorial capital, Lecompton, and wrote a state constitution that protected slavery. Free-state settlers, who by now were a large majority, had boycotted the election for delegates.

The convention did not let voters accept or reject the whole constitution. In December 1857, voters could only choose between the constitution "with slavery" and "without slavery," and even "without slavery" protected the enslaved people already in Kansas. Free-state voters boycotted that vote too, so the constitution "with slavery" won easily. A separate vote in January 1858, organized by the new free-state legislature, rejected the whole constitution by more than 10,000 votes.

President Buchanan asked Congress to admit Kansas as a slave state anyway. Stephen Douglas refused. He said Lecompton made a mockery of popular sovereignty, and he broke with his own party's president. The Senate approved Lecompton, but the House would not. Congress then passed a compromise, the English Bill, which sent the constitution back to Kansas voters. On August 2, 1858, they rejected it, about 11,300 to 1,800. Kansas stayed a territory until January 1861, when it entered the Union as a free state.

Takeaway: the Lecompton fraud failed, but it split the Democratic Party between Buchanan and Douglas.

The Panic of 1857 Made the South Feel Like King

  • August 1857: the collapse of an Ohio bank's New York office set off a financial panic
  • Banks and factories closed; Northern cities filled with unemployed workers
  • Cotton prices held up, so the South suffered less
  • Southern leaders took the panic as proof that slavery made their economy stronger

"No, you dare not make war on cotton. No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is king."Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina, U.S. Senate, March 4, 1858

In August 1857 the New York branch of the Ohio Life Insurance and Trust Company collapsed after bad investments in railroads and land. Panic spread. People rushed to their banks to pull out their savings, as in the engraving here from Harper's Weekly. Banks failed, factories closed, and by winter many thousands of workers in Northern cities had lost their jobs.

The South was hurt much less. World demand for cotton stayed strong, and cotton prices recovered quickly. Southern leaders drew a lesson: their economy, built on slave labor and cotton, was stronger than the North's.

In March 1858, Senator James Henry Hammond of South Carolina said so on the Senate floor. The North, he said, could never make war on the South, because the world depended on Southern cotton: "Cotton is king." In the same speech he called Northern wage workers the "mudsills" of society, the low timbers a house rests on, and said every society needed such a class. Republicans quoted that insult for years.

The confidence was misplaced. When war came, Britain had large stocks of cotton and found new supplies in India and Egypt. The Independent Study section tells more about the two economies.

Takeaway: the Panic of 1857 convinced many white Southerners that King Cotton made them strong enough to stand alone.

Lecture 2 · Part II

Lincoln and Douglas

1858

Part II moves to Illinois in 1858, where Stephen Douglas ran for reelection to the Senate against a little-known Republican lawyer named Abraham Lincoln. Their seven debates were about the biggest question in the country: could the nation keep living half slave and half free?

Takeaway: watch how a state election became a national argument about slavery's future.

Lincoln Warned of a House Divided

  • June 16, 1858: Illinois Republicans chose Abraham Lincoln to run for Douglas's Senate seat
  • Lincoln, a former one-term Whig congressman, was little known outside Illinois
  • He warned that the nation would become "all one thing, or all the other"
  • He charged that Douglas, Pierce, Taney, and Buchanan had worked together to spread slavery

"A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free."Abraham Lincoln, Springfield, Illinois, June 16, 1858

Abraham Lincoln was born in a Kentucky log cabin in 1809 and grew up poor in Indiana and Illinois. He taught himself law, became a successful lawyer in Springfield, and served one term in Congress as a Whig in the 1840s. The Kansas-Nebraska Act pulled him back into politics, and he became a Republican.

In 1858, Illinois Republicans named Lincoln as their choice for the Senate. In those days voters did not elect senators directly. They elected the state legislature, and the legislature chose the senator. So Lincoln and Douglas were really campaigning for the legislators who would vote for them.

Lincoln accepted with the most famous speech of his career. Quoting Jesus in the Gospels, he said a house divided against itself cannot stand. The nation, he said, would not stay half slave and half free forever. Either slavery would be put on the road to extinction, or it would spread to every state, North as well as South. He described Douglas, Pierce, Taney, and Buchanan as builders working from one plan, and Dred Scott as their latest step. The photograph shows Lincoln in Macomb, Illinois, in August 1858.

Takeaway: Lincoln argued that the nation had to choose: slavery would either be contained until it died out or spread everywhere.

Seven Debates Across Illinois

  • August 21 to October 15, 1858: one debate in each of seven Illinois districts
  • Each debate ran three hours; crowds ranged from about 1,500 to 15,000 or more
  • Douglas: let the people of each territory decide; he called Lincoln a dangerous radical on race
  • Lincoln: slavery was a moral wrong and must not spread; neither man supported full racial equality

Lincoln challenged Douglas to a series of debates, and Douglas agreed to seven, one in each congressional district where they had not already spoken. They began at Ottawa on August 21, 1858, and ended at Alton on October 15. Each debate followed the same format: one man spoke for an hour, the other for an hour and a half, and the first man had a half hour to reply.

Crowds came by wagon, train, and riverboat, with brass bands and banners. About 1,500 people came to Jonesboro, in southern Illinois; at Ottawa and Galesburg, crowds of 10,000 to 15,000 or more stood in the open air. Reporters took the speeches down in shorthand, and newspapers across the country printed them.

Douglas defended popular sovereignty: let the people of each territory decide, and keep the national government out of it. He also appealed to white racial fears, accusing Lincoln of wanting equality between Black and white people. Lincoln insisted that slavery was a moral wrong and that the nation must stop its spread. But he also told one crowd he did not favor Black citizens voting or serving on juries. Both men held views on race that most Americans today would reject.

Takeaway: the debates set Douglas's "let the people decide" against Lincoln's claim that slavery was simply wrong.

At Freeport, Douglas Kept Illinois and Lost the South

  • August 27, 1858: Lincoln asked whether settlers could still keep slavery out of a territory after Dred Scott
  • Douglas said yes: slavery could not survive without local laws to protect it
  • That answer, the , pleased Illinois voters
  • Southern Democrats called it betrayal; it helped split their party in 1860

Douglas won the 1858 race. His answer at Freeport helped cost him the presidency in 1860.

The second debate took place at Freeport, in northern Illinois, on August 27, 1858. Lincoln set a trap with a question: after the Dred Scott decision, could the people of a territory, in any lawful way, keep slavery out before they wrote a state constitution?

If Douglas said no, he would admit that popular sovereignty was dead, and Illinois voters would turn on him. If he said yes, he would seem to defy the Supreme Court, and Southern Democrats would turn on him.

Douglas said yes. Slavery, he argued, could not exist anywhere unless local laws and police protected it. If settlers refused to pass a slave code, slaveholders would not bring enslaved people there, whatever the Court said. Newspapers called this the Freeport Doctrine.

The answer helped Douglas in Illinois. But Southern Democrats were furious. They had just won Dred Scott, and now the leading Northern Democrat was explaining how to get around it. Together with his fight against Lecompton, Freeport made Douglas unacceptable to much of the South. In 1860, when the Democrats met to choose a candidate, the party split in two.

Takeaway: the Freeport Doctrine saved Douglas in Illinois but helped split the Democratic Party, the last national party left.

An Illinois voter who disliked slavery but distrusted abolitionists could hear Douglas's answer as good news: the settlers could still keep slavery out, and the national government could stay out of it. A Georgia planter heard the opposite: the Supreme Court had just protected his right to take enslaved people into any territory, and now the leading Northern Democrat was telling settlers how to block him.

Takeaway: on slavery, any answer that pleased the North now angered the South, and the reverse.

Lecture 2 · Part III

John Brown's War

1859

Part III returns to John Brown, whom you met at Pottawatomie Creek in Kansas. In 1859 he tried something far bolder: to seize federal weapons in Virginia and start an uprising of enslaved people. He failed within two days. But his trial and death shook the nation more than his raid did.

Takeaway: watch how a failed raid became a turning point.

Brown Planned a War on Slavery

  • After Kansas, Brown planned to strike slavery in Virginia and lead freed people to a mountain stronghold
  • May 1858: at Chatham, Canada West, he wrote a "Provisional Constitution" for his free state
  • paid for guns and supplies
  • Summer 1859: his men gathered at a rented farm in Maryland, about five miles from Harpers Ferry

John Brown left Kansas convinced that God had chosen him to destroy slavery by force. His plan was to attack slavery in the South itself. He would seize weapons, free enslaved people, and lead them into the Appalachian Mountains, where they could defend themselves and raid more plantations.

In May 1858 Brown held a meeting in Chatham, in Canada West, where many people who had escaped slavery lived. There he presented a "Provisional Constitution" for the free government he hoped to create. Six well-known Northern abolitionists, later called the Secret Six, raised money for his guns.

Brown asked Frederick Douglass to join him. In August 1859 the two men met secretly in a stone quarry near Chambersburg, Pennsylvania. Douglass refused. He told Brown that attacking a federal arsenal was a trap he would never escape. That summer, Brown's small band gathered at the Kennedy farm, which he rented in Maryland, about five miles from Harpers Ferry, Virginia. The photograph shows Brown in May 1859.

Takeaway: Brown planned an armed uprising against slavery, funded by respected Northern abolitionists.

Brown's Raiders Seized Harpers Ferry

  • Night of October 16, 1859: Brown's band of about 20 men, five of them Black, took the federal armory and arsenal
  • They seized dozens of hostages, but few enslaved people joined them
  • Local militia trapped Brown's men in the armory's engine house
  • October 18: U.S. Marines broke in; ten raiders died, including two of Brown's sons

On the night of Sunday, October 16, 1859, Brown led his band into Harpers Ferry, Virginia, where the Potomac and Shenandoah Rivers meet. A few men stayed behind as a rear guard. About twenty took part in the raid, five of them Black, including Dangerfield Newby, who hoped to free his enslaved wife and children. They quickly took the federal armory, the arsenal, and a rifle works, and they seized dozens of hostages, including a great-grandnephew of George Washington.

Then the plan fell apart. The first man killed was Heyward Shepherd, a free Black railroad baggage master. Brown let a train go through, and news of the raid spread. Few enslaved people in the area joined him. By the next day, local militia had trapped Brown and his remaining men in the armory's small fire-engine house.

President Buchanan sent a company of U.S. Marines from Washington, under the command of Army Lieutenant Colonel Robert E. Lee, who held the honorary rank of colonel. Lee was on leave at his home nearby. On the morning of October 18, Lieutenant Israel Greene led the Marines in, battering the door with a ladder, as the engraving shows. Brown was wounded and captured. In all, ten of his men died, including his sons Watson and Oliver. The raid had lasted about 36 hours.

Takeaway: Brown's raid failed quickly, but it was the armed slave uprising white Southerners had always feared.

Virginia Hanged Brown, and the North Made Him a Martyr

  • Tried in Charles Town for treason against Virginia, murder, and inciting slaves to rebel
  • November 2, 1859: he told the court he had acted for God's "despised poor"; he was sentenced to hang
  • December 2, 1859: Brown was hanged; Northern church bells tolled
  • Ralph Waldo Emerson said Brown would "make the gallows glorious like the cross"

Virginia put Brown on trial quickly in Charles Town. He was charged with treason against the state of Virginia, murder, and conspiring with enslaved people to rebel. He lay on a cot in the courtroom, still weak from his wounds. On October 31 the jury found him guilty.

On November 2, before he was sentenced, Brown spoke to the court. Had he acted for the rich and powerful, he said, everyone would call it right. But he had acted "in behalf of His despised poor," and he believed it was no wrong. He was sentenced to hang. For a month he wrote letters from his cell, calm and religious, and Northern newspapers printed them.

On December 2, 1859, Brown was hanged. In many Northern towns church bells tolled and people gathered to pray. Weeks earlier, the writer Ralph Waldo Emerson had predicted that Brown would "make the gallows glorious like the cross." The painting on this slide, made more than twenty years later, shows a famous legend: Brown kissing a Black baby on his way to the gallows. No reliable witness saw it happen; soldiers kept the crowd away. The legend shows how quickly Brown became a saint in Northern memory.

Takeaway: by dying calmly, Brown turned a failed raid into a powerful antislavery symbol in the North.

The South Saw John Brown in Every Republican

  • The raid confirmed white Southern fears of a slave revolt led by Northerners
  • Northern praise for Brown seemed to prove the North wanted the South destroyed
  • Lincoln and other Republicans condemned the raid, but many Southerners did not believe them
  • Southern militias drilled, and talk of secession grew louder

"The punishment of John Brown may consolidate slavery in Virginia, but it will certainly shake the whole American democracy."Victor Hugo, letter of December 2, 1859

For white Southerners, Harpers Ferry was a nightmare come true. They had always feared a slave uprising like Nat Turner's rebellion in 1831. Now a white Northerner, paid by Northern money, had tried to start one.

What frightened them most was the Northern reaction. When Northern ministers, writers, and crowds praised Brown as a hero, many white Southerners concluded that the North wanted them dead. Republican leaders, including Lincoln, called the raid a crime, and most Republicans had never supported Brown. But Southern newspapers called it the logical result of Republican teaching. A Senate committee investigated the raid, and Southern states bought weapons and drilled their militias.

People abroad watched too. The French writer Victor Hugo, living in exile on the island of Guernsey, wrote a public letter begging the United States to spare Brown. He warned that hanging him would shake American democracy and open a crack that could split the Union.

Takeaway: after Harpers Ferry, many white Southerners believed that a Republican government would mean war against slavery, and against them.

Independent Study · The American System

Machines Made Interchangeable Parts

  • Interchangeable parts: machine-made pieces so alike that any one fit any product
  • Developed in the federal armories at Springfield and Harpers Ferry, where John H. Hall made rifles from interchangeable parts in the 1820s
  • The method spread to Colt revolvers, clocks, sewing machines, and reapers
  • British visitors after the 1851 London fair called it "the American system of manufactures"

Before the 1800s, a skilled craftsman made each gun, clock, or lock by hand, and no two parts were quite the same. If a part broke, a new one had to be made to fit. The idea of interchangeable parts was to make pieces by machine so exactly that any part would fit any copy of the product.

Eli Whitney, famous for the cotton gin, often gets credit for this. He promoted the idea in the early 1800s, but historians have found that his own muskets did not really have interchangeable parts. The real breakthroughs came in the federal armories at Springfield, Massachusetts, and at Harpers Ferry, Virginia, the same armory John Brown later raided. There, in the 1820s, John H. Hall produced rifles whose parts truly were interchangeable.

By the 1850s the method had spread to private factories, like Samuel Colt's huge armory in Hartford, shown here, and to makers of clocks, sewing machines, and farm machinery. American machines amazed visitors at the 1851 world's fair in London, and a British commission sent to study American factories in 1854 called the method "the American system of manufactures."

Takeaway: the North's factories were learning to mass-produce goods, one reason its economy grew faster than the South's.

Independent Study · The Southern economy

The South Grew Rich on Cotton and Enslaved Labor

  • Most Southern wealth was invested in land and in enslaved people
  • About four million people were enslaved in 1860
  • The South had far fewer factories, cities, and railroad miles than the North
  • It bought most manufactured goods from the North and from Britain

The South of the 1850s was not poor. Cotton prices were high, and Southern planters were among the richest Americans. But their wealth was tied up in two things: land and enslaved human beings. By 1860 about four million people were enslaved, and their total market value was greater than that of all the railroads and factories in the country combined.

Because planters put their money into land and enslaved people, the South built fewer factories, fewer cities, and fewer miles of railroad than the North. It bought cloth, tools, and machines from Northern and British factories. Most immigrants avoided the South, since they did not want to compete with enslaved labor.

The engraving shows enslaved men, women, and children picking cotton on a Georgia plantation in 1858. Their forced labor produced the crop that made planters rich.

Takeaway: the Southern economy was profitable, but it rested on enslaved labor and built little industry.

Independent Study · King Cotton

Southerners Believed Cotton Would Protect Them

  • By 1860, cotton made up more than half of all U.S. exports
  • British textile mills got most of their cotton from the American South
  • Southern leaders believed Britain would have to support the South in a crisis
  • In the Civil War, Britain used stockpiles and new cotton from India and Egypt

Cotton was the most valuable American export. By 1860 it made up more than half of everything the United States sold abroad, and most of the cotton spun in British mills came from the American South.

From those facts, Southern leaders like Senator James Henry Hammond, shown here, drew a bold conclusion. If war ever came, Britain could not do without Southern cotton, so it would have to support the South. Hammond's "Cotton is king" speech in 1858 expressed that faith.

It proved to be wrong. Huge crops in the late 1850s had left British warehouses full of cotton when the Civil War began. Britain then turned to India and Egypt for more. Many British workers also opposed slavery, and the British government never recognized the Confederacy.

Takeaway: faith in King Cotton made many Southerners confident that they could leave the Union safely, and that faith was mistaken.

Independent Study · Northern workers

Northern Workers Were Free, but Life Was Hard

  • Factory and mill hands often worked 12 to 14 hours a day
  • Children worked in textile mills; there were no safety laws
  • Wages were low, especially for immigrants, and jobs vanished in hard times like 1857
  • Workers formed unions and struck; in 1860 thousands of shoemakers in Lynn, Massachusetts, walked out

Southern defenders of slavery pointed to Northern factories and claimed their enslaved workers were better off. Northern workers answered that they, at least, were free.

Free labor in the North was not easy labor. In textile mills and factories, men, women, and children worked 12 to 14 hours a day, six days a week. There were no laws on safety, child labor, or hours. Wages were low, especially for new immigrants, and in a depression like the Panic of 1857, jobs disappeared overnight.

Workers fought back. They formed unions and went on strike. In February 1860 thousands of shoemakers in Lynn, Massachusetts, and nearby towns walked off the job, the largest strike in the country before the Civil War. Women shoe workers marched through the snow in support.

Defenders of slavery used these hard conditions as an argument. Writers like George Fitzhugh of Virginia claimed that Northern "wage slaves" were worse off than enslaved people, who at least were fed in old age. Northern workers rejected the comparison. They could quit, move, organize, and vote. They could not be sold, and their families could not be taken from them.

Takeaway: Northern workers faced hard conditions, but the difference between a poor free worker and an enslaved person remained enormous.

Independent Study · Free labor

Republicans Believed in Free Labor

  • Every man should own his own labor and keep what he earned
  • A worker could rise: work for wages, save, buy land or a shop, then hire others
  • Slavery, they said, degraded work and blocked that rise for ordinary white men
  • Free labor let Republicans oppose slavery's spread without promising racial equality

"The prudent, penniless beginner in the world, labors for wages awhile, saves a surplus with which to buy tools or land, for himself; then labors on his own account another while, and at length hires another new beginner to help him."Abraham Lincoln, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, September 30, 1859

The idea that held the Republican Party together was called free labor. Republicans believed that the North's society of small farmers, shopkeepers, and workers was the best in the world, because a man who worked hard could rise. He might begin by working for wages, save money, buy his own land or tools, and someday hire workers of his own.

Lincoln described exactly that path in a speech to farmers in Milwaukee in 1859. He was describing his own life, too.

In the Republican view, slavery threatened all of this. Where slavery spread, they said, work became something only enslaved people did, and free white men could not compete or rise. That is why Republicans wanted the western territories kept free.

Free-labor ideas had limits. They let Republicans oppose the spread of slavery without promising equal rights to Black Americans, which few Northern voters then supported. Some Republicans truly hated slavery as a sin. Others mostly wanted the West for white men. Free labor gave both groups a common cause.

Takeaway: free labor was the Republican belief that free work let ordinary people rise, and that slavery's spread would destroy that chance.

Independent Study · Helper

A Southerner Said Slavery Hurt the South

  • 1857: Hinton Rowan Helper of North Carolina published The Impending Crisis of the South
  • He argued with census numbers that slavery kept the South poor and hurt non-slaveholding whites
  • Southern states banned the book; some people were jailed for owning it
  • Republicans printed a cheap shortened edition as a campaign tool

Hinton Rowan Helper was the son of a small farmer in North Carolina. He did not attack slavery because he cared about enslaved people. In fact, he held openly racist views and wanted Black Americans sent out of the country. He attacked slavery because he believed it ruined the South's white majority.

In The Impending Crisis of the South (1857), Helper filled page after page with census statistics. He compared Northern and Southern farms, factories, schools, and wealth, and argued that slavery made a few planters rich while it kept ordinary white Southerners poor and uneducated.

That message was dangerous to planters, because it might turn poor whites against them. Southern states banned the book, and a few people were jailed for owning or selling it. In the North, Republicans paid to print a cheaper, shorter edition in 1859 to use in the coming campaign. When Southerners learned that a Republican candidate for Speaker of the House had endorsed it, Congress was deadlocked for two months.

Takeaway: Helper's book frightened the planter class because it tried to split white Southerners by class.

Independent Study · Nonslaveholders

Most White Southerners Owned No Slaves

  • In 1860, about three-quarters of white Southern families owned no enslaved people
  • Most were small farmers who grew their own food and a little cotton or tobacco
  • Many hoped to buy enslaved people someday
  • Slavery gave every white man a status above all Black people, and fear of revolt united whites

Planters feared books like Helper's, but in 1861 most nonslaveholders supported secession anyway.

Only about a quarter of white Southern families owned any enslaved people in 1860, and only a small number owned twenty or more, the line that usually marked a planter. The majority of white Southerners were small farmers, sometimes called yeomen, who worked their own land.

Why did so many of them support slavery? Some hoped to buy enslaved workers and rise into the planter class. Many depended on planters for loans, markets, and jobs. Above all, slavery gave every white man, however poor, a place above all Black people. Fear played a part too: after Nat Turner's rebellion and Harpers Ferry, many white families believed that ending slavery would bring violence.

Helper hoped poor whites would turn against the planters. In 1861 most did not.

Takeaway: most white Southerners owned no slaves, but race, ambition, and fear tied them to the slave system.

Independent Study · Who was John Brown?

John Brown Was Raised to Hate Slavery

  • Born May 9, 1800, in Torrington, Connecticut; raised in Hudson, Ohio
  • His father, Owen Brown, was an abolitionist and a trustee of Oberlin College
  • He failed as a tanner, land speculator, and wool merchant
  • 1849: he moved to North Elba, New York, where Gerrit Smith gave land to Black families; in 1855 he followed his sons to Kansas

John Brown was born in Connecticut in 1800 and grew up in northern Ohio, in a deeply religious family. His father, Owen Brown, hated slavery and later served as a trustee of Oberlin College, one of the first colleges to admit Black students. As a boy, John saw an enslaved boy beaten, and he never forgot it.

As an adult, Brown struggled. He worked as a tanner, a land speculator, a sheep farmer, and a wool merchant, and he went bankrupt. He married twice and had twenty children, many of whom died young.

Through it all, he hated slavery. He helped people escaping on the Underground Railroad. In 1849 he moved to North Elba, in the Adirondack Mountains of New York, where the abolitionist Gerrit Smith was giving farmland to Black families. In 1855 he followed several of his sons to Kansas, and the story of Pottawatomie Creek began.

Takeaway: Brown's religious upbringing and lifelong hatred of slavery shaped his turn to violence in Kansas and Virginia.

Independent Study · Victor Hugo

Victor Hugo Pleaded for Brown's Life

  • Hugo, the French writer who later wrote Les Misérables, lived in exile on the island of Guernsey
  • December 2, 1859: he wrote a public letter urging the United States to spare Brown
  • He warned that the execution would "open a latent fissure that will finally split the Union asunder"
  • In 1861 he published a drawing of Brown on the gallows

John Brown's raid was news around the world. In France, the great writer Victor Hugo, later famous for Les Misérables, was living in exile on the island of Guernsey because he had opposed the emperor Napoleon III.

On December 2, 1859, the very day Brown was hanged, Hugo wrote a public letter begging the United States to spare him. He did not yet know that the execution had already taken place. He warned that killing Brown would "open a latent fissure that will finally split the Union asunder." Hanging Brown, he said, might strengthen slavery in Virginia, but it would shake the whole American democracy.

Hugo also drew a picture of Brown hanging from the gallows, which was engraved and published in 1861, as the Civil War began. It is shown on this slide.

Takeaway: people abroad saw Brown's execution as a sign that the United States was breaking apart over slavery.

Independent Study · "John Brown's Body"

Union Soldiers Marched to "John Brown's Body"

  • 1861: soldiers at Fort Warren in Boston Harbor set new words to an old hymn tune
  • "John Brown's body lies a-mouldering in the grave, his soul is marching on"
  • Union regiments sang it as they marched south
  • Julia Ward Howe wrote new words to the same tune: "The Battle Hymn of the Republic" (1862)

John Brown's memory lived on in a song. In 1861, Massachusetts soldiers training at Fort Warren in Boston Harbor made up new words to a popular camp-meeting hymn. The song may first have poked fun at a soldier in their battalion who happened to be named John Brown, but listeners heard it as being about the famous one.

"John Brown's body lies a-mouldering in the grave," it went, "his soul is marching on." Union regiments sang it as they marched south, and it became one of the most popular songs of the war. The sheet music on this slide is one of the earliest printed versions.

In November 1861, the poet Julia Ward Howe heard soldiers singing it near Washington. She wrote new words to the same tune, and they were published in 1862 as "The Battle Hymn of the Republic."

Takeaway: within two years of his death, John Brown had become a marching song for the Union army.

Independent Study · Preview of Chapter 15

The Road to War, 1859–1861

  • Oct. 1859 John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry
  • Apr.–June 1860 The Democratic Party split into Northern and Southern wings
  • Nov. 6, 1860 Abraham Lincoln elected president
  • Dec. 20, 1860 South Carolina seceded from the Union
  • Feb. 1861 Seven seceded states formed the Confederate States of America
  • Apr. 12, 1861 Confederate guns fired on Fort Sumter; the Civil War began

Here is a preview of what comes next, in Chapter 15.

In the spring of 1860, the Democrats met in Charleston, South Carolina, to choose a presidential candidate. Southern delegates demanded a promise to protect slavery in every territory. When the convention refused, many of them walked out. By June the party had split in two: Northern Democrats nominated Stephen Douglas, and Southern Democrats nominated Vice President John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky.

With the Democrats divided, Lincoln won the election of November 1860 by carrying the free states, without a single electoral vote from the South. On December 20, 1860, South Carolina declared that it had left the Union. By February 1861, six more states of the Deep South had followed, and together they formed the Confederate States of America. On April 12, 1861, Confederate cannon opened fire on Fort Sumter in Charleston Harbor, and the Civil War began.

Takeaway: within eighteen months of John Brown's raid, the Union had broken apart and the war had begun.

The Slave Power

The belief, widespread in the North in the 1850s, that a small group of wealthy slaveholders controlled the federal government and was plotting to spread slavery everywhere.

Republicans pointed to the Kansas-Nebraska Act, the Dred Scott decision, and Buchanan's support for the Lecompton Constitution as proof.

The Freeport Doctrine

Douglas's claim that the settlers of a territory could keep slavery out, even after Dred Scott, simply by refusing to pass the local laws and police rules that slavery needed.

It saved popular sovereignty in the eyes of Illinois voters. To Southern Democrats it was a trick to get around the Supreme Court.

The Secret Six

Six wealthy or well-known abolitionists who secretly raised money for John Brown: Thomas Wentworth Higginson, Samuel Gridley Howe, Theodore Parker, Franklin Sanborn, Gerrit Smith, and George Luther Stearns.

They knew Brown planned armed action against slavery, though most did not know his exact target. After the raid, Sanborn, Howe, and Stearns fled briefly to Canada; Smith was committed to an asylum; Parker was already abroad, dying of tuberculosis. Only Higginson stayed and spoke out.

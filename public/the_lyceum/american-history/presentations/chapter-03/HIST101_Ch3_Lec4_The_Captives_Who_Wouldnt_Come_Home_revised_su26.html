The Last Turn

Three lectures of fear: holy war, total war, and at Salem, fear turned inward — a colony hunting the Devil out of terror of the frontier world beyond its borders.

Now turn that fear inside out. What about the people carried into that feared world — who, offered the chance to come home, chose not to?

Deerfield, February 1704

A pre-dawn French and Native raid on a Massachusetts frontier town during Queen Anne’s War.

  • 57 killed; 112 taken captive and marched north through winter toward Canada
  • Among the captives: Eunice Williams, age 7 — the minister’s daughter
  • Her mother killed on the march

The 1704 raid and the winter march north — a later depiction

“The Redeemed Captive”

Eunice’s father, minister John Williams, was ransomed and returned. He wrote a famous account: The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion.

The Puritan assumption baked into the title: captivity is a trial the faithful are redeemed from. But John Williams was redeemed. His daughter was not.

She Returned Three Times — Then Went Home

Between 1740 and 1761, Eunice visited Deerfield and nearby communities three times. She appeared as a Catholic, Kanien’kehá:ka-speaking woman in Kanien’kehá:ka dress, then returned to Kahnawake after each visit.

Her marriage, family, repeated refusals to relocate permanently, and returns to Kahnawake document the durability of her Kanien’kehá:ka and Catholic life.
Hold both truths at once The capture was violence done to a child — do not romanticize the raid. Her adult decisions repeatedly sustained family, community, and Catholic life at Kahnawake. Both are true, and the documented pattern of adult action is what makes her story matter.

The Silence of Eunice Williams

We’ve described what she repeatedly did — but she left almost no words of her own. Everything reaches us through others: her father’s narrative, relatives’ letters, the records of those trying to get her back.

The Historian’s Problem — John Demos, The Unredeemed Captive

Silence reveals the structure and limits of the archive; it cannot by itself prove her inner motives. The strongest evidence is behavioral: repeated refusals to relocate permanently, her marriage and family at Kahnawake, her returns there after three New England visits, and the durability of her Kanien’kehá:ka and Catholic life.

The archive cannot give direct access to Eunice’s inner motives. It can document a durable pattern of belonging through what she repeatedly did.

Demos must infer her inner life. The argument is strongest when it distinguishes archival silence from documented action.

The Border Was Porous

The standard picture imagines a hard line between “colonial” and “Indian” worlds, everyone sorted onto one side. These lives dissolve that line.

Colonists didn’t only expand outward. Some were absorbed inward — into the very societies the colonies insisted were beneath them.

A Rebuke the Colony Couldn’t Answer

A colony could lose a battle and recover. It was a harder blow to lose your own people — the minister’s own daughter — and be unable, across decades, to get her back.

If the English way was self-evidently superior — why did her adult life remain at Kahnawake despite decades of efforts to relocate her permanently?

A Frightened Continent

The colonists were never as dominant, as secure, or as clearly superior as their own rhetoric claimed. In 1720 the continent was still, overwhelmingly, Native ground.

Eunice Williams — returning to Kahnawake after each New England visit — is the last and quietest proof: the minister’s daughter whose adult family, faith, and community lay beyond Puritan New England.

The Deerfield Raid in Depth

  • February 29, 1704: a combined French and Native force strikes the palisaded town before dawn
  • 57 killed; 112 taken captive
  • A brutal winter march of some 300 miles north toward Canada — many did not survive it
  • One raid produced the captive story that anchors this entire lecture
Why the detail matters The live lecture compresses the raid to spend its time on the meaning of the choice. Here is the violence that made the choice possible — held in full, not skipped.

The Captivity Narrative as a Genre

Published accounts like John Williams’s The Redeemed Captive were a whole New England genre: the faithful captive endures, resists, and returns to Zion.

Eunice did not follow the captivity narrative’s expected arc of resistance, redemption, and permanent return. That made her life difficult for contemporaries to fit into the genre’s acceptable ending.

Queen Anne's War (1702–1713)

The North American theater of a larger European war (the War of the Spanish Succession), fought between English colonies and a French-and-Native alliance along the northern frontier.

It produced the border raids — Deerfield among them — that carried English captives north into New France and Native communities, setting up the very captivities this lecture examines.

Kahnawake

A Catholic Kanien’kehá:ka (Mohawk) community near Montreal with its own political authority, kinship networks, and diplomatic life. Kahnawake incorporated some captives taken in frontier raids through adoption.

Eunice Williams was adopted into a Kanien’kehá:ka family, baptized, given the name A’ongote, and eventually married. After three visits to New England, she returned to Kahnawake. Her documented pattern of family, faith, and community life there is the heart of this lecture.

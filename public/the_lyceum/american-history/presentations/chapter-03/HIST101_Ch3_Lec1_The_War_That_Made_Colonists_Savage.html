📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide gives you almost nothing on purpose. Twenty years. A pike. That's it — no name yet, no explanation. Think of it like walking into a mystery novel on the last page: you know something shocking happened, but not what, or why anyone would want it to keep happening for two decades.

Breaking It Down

Twenty years: This isn't a battlefield trophy displayed for a victory parade and then quietly taken down. Two decades means an entire generation of children in Plymouth grew up walking past this every day of their childhood.

Why the deck opens here instead of at the beginning of the war: We're using a "reverse chronology" structure — starting with the shocking outcome, then working backward to explain how anyone got there. It's a deliberate storytelling choice, not a random starting point.

Why This Matters

A society doesn't display something for twenty years unless that display is doing important emotional or political work for it. Hold onto that thought — by the end of the lecture, you'll know exactly what work it was doing.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Now you get the name — and immediately a second puzzle. The English called him "King Philip," but that wasn't a respectful title. It was a mocking crown for a man they intended to destroy, applied to an English name he'd adopted or been given in an earlier, friendlier era.

Breaking It Down

Wampanoag sachem: A hereditary Native leader with real political authority — not a tribal "chief" in the flattened, generic sense pop culture often uses.

"King Philip" as mockery: Giving a defeated enemy a fake royal title is a way of ridiculing him while pretending to honor him — a small verbal cruelty worth noticing.

About the image: No portrait of Metacom survives from his own lifetime. Whatever image eventually appears here is almost certainly a later artist's imagining, not a contemporary likeness — worth remembering whenever you look at "portraits" of 17th-century Native leaders.

Why This Matters

This slide plants the essential question for the entire lecture: why did a society need to look at this man's head every single day for twenty years? Hold that question. Every slide between here and the reveal is building toward answering it.

A World Being Taken Apart

  • Land base shrinking for a generation
  • English livestock destroy unfenced cornfields
  • Praying towns pull converts from Native political life
  • New England profits from selling captured Native people into Caribbean slavery
Only about 1,000 Wampanoag remained — half already inside praying towns.

Wampanoag territory, shrinking within living memory

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before we get to Metacom's decision, we need to see the pressure he was reacting to. Think of this slide as four separate leaks in the same boat — land, farming, religion, and slavery all draining Wampanoag autonomy at once.

Breaking It Down

Land base shrinking: Colonial settlement had been eating into Wampanoag territory for a full generation by 1675 — not a sudden crisis, but a slow squeeze.

English livestock: Colonists let hogs and cattle roam unfenced, following English custom. Those animals regularly destroyed unfenced Native cornfields — a constant, grinding source of conflict.

Praying towns: Christian Indian settlements that drew converts away from traditional Native political structures. Click the term above to see why this mattered as much politically as religiously.

The slave trade: New England families profited directly from selling captured Native people — men, women, and children — into Caribbean sugar plantations. This gave colonists a financial incentive to find reasons for conflict.

Why This Matters

Only about 1,000 Wampanoag remained by 1675, and half already lived in praying towns. Metacom watched this erosion happen to his own people in real time. Whatever he decides next slide, he's not deciding it in a vacuum.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Neal Salisbury – “Red Puritans” (William and Mary Quarterly, 1974)

Salisbury's classic study of the praying town system shows it wasn't simply religious outreach — it functioned as a mechanism that fractured Native political and religious authority from within, community by community, even when missionaries' personal intentions were sincere.

A Sachem’s Calculation

Son of Massasoit, whose alliance kept Plymouth alive. Missionaries wanted Metacom converted — correctly guessing that if the sachem fell, his people’s independence fell with him.

“If I be a praying sachem, I shall be a poor and weak one, and easily be trod upon by others.”
— Metacom, Wampanoag sachem, c. 1675

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the sentence that reframes the entire war. Metacom isn't reacting emotionally — he's doing political math, and he says so in his own words.

Breaking It Down

Son of Massasoit: Metacom inherited his role as sachem from his father, who had kept Plymouth alive through a real alliance in its earliest, most vulnerable years. Metacom didn't experience that alliance as a subject's obligation — he experienced it as a relationship between two sovereign peoples.

Why missionaries targeted him specifically: They correctly understood that if the sachem converted, Wampanoag political independence would collapse right along with his religious authority. Converting the leader was a shortcut to converting — and subordinating — the whole people.

The quote itself: "Poor and weak" isn't self-pity. It's a precise prediction: conversion means losing the standing to resist anything that comes next.

Why This Matters

War wasn't Metacom's first move or an impulsive one. It was the outcome of watching every peaceful alternative already close, one institution at a time.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Lisa Brooks – Our Beloved Kin (2018)

Brooks reconstructs this war through Native strategy, kinship networks, and deliberate political decision-making rather than treating Native actors as simply reacting to English initiative. Her account of Metacom is exactly this: a leader calculating, not merely a leader provoked.

The Spark, Not the Fuel

Plymouth executes three Wampanoag men for the murder of John Sassamon — a Native preacher who may have been informing on Metacom.
Weeks later, English settlers in Swansea kill a Native man and mock the Wampanoag demand for justice. Fighting begins.

The trigger took weeks. The fuel took a generation.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A useful distinction for any historical event: what actually triggered it in the moment, versus what had been building for years. This slide is about the trigger.

Breaking It Down

John Sassamon: A Harvard-educated Native preacher who may have been passing information about Metacom's plans to colonial authorities. When he was found dead, Plymouth executed three Wampanoag men for his murder — on evidence Native leaders considered highly questionable.

Swansea: Weeks later, English settlers killed a Native man caught looting an abandoned house, then mocked the Wampanoag demand for justice. That humiliation, on top of the executions, is where actual fighting began.

Why This Matters

Notice the gap between the spark and the fuel: the trigger took weeks, but everything from the last two slides — land loss, praying towns, the slave trade, Metacom's calculation — took a full generation to accumulate. If you only study the spark, you miss the entire explanation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

James D. Drake – King Philip's War: Civil War in New England (1999)

Drake frames this conflict as a civil war within a single, deeply interconnected Anglo-Native society — which is exactly why Sassamon's ambiguous position (educated at Harvard, moving between both worlds) sits right at the center of how the war actually began.

A War of Ambush, Not Volleys

  • Wampanoag fighters: hunters, marksmen, own flintlock muskets
  • English settlers: rarely hunted, fired disciplined volleys
  • Metacom wins early engagements by ambush
24+ towns
destroyed — over half of New England’s colonial settlements

Colonists abandoned the interior entirely and retreated to the coast

📖 Understanding This Slide

English colonists expected an easy repeat of an earlier war against the Pequot. Instead, they got the opposite. This slide explains why the war's opening months were a disaster for the side that assumed it would win.

Breaking It Down

Flintlock muskets: Wampanoag fighters had acquired these weapons and, because they hunted regularly, were genuinely skilled marksmen with them.

English disadvantage: Most settlers rarely hunted and fought in the European tradition of firing disciplined volleys without careful aim — a tactic built for open-field battles against other European armies, not ambush warfare in New England's forests.

24+ towns destroyed: More than half of every English settlement then existing in New England. Colonists abandoned the entire interior and retreated to the coast.

Why This Matters

This is not the version of colonization most people picture — steady English expansion, occasional Native resistance, inevitable English victory. For most of this war's first year, the opposite was true. Keep that discomfort in mind; it's the whole point of this lecture series.

December 1675 Colonial forces massacred hundreds of Narragansett noncombatants — a people who hadn’t even joined the war yet.

Whatever restraint the English claimed at the start was gone by winter.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide is deliberately short — one sharp fact, not the full scene. If you want the complete story (the blizzard, the fort, what happened to the survivors afterward), it's waiting for you in the Independent Study section at the end of this deck.

Breaking It Down

December 1675: Massachusetts and Connecticut militia attacked a Narragansett fort during a blizzard, killing hundreds of people — overwhelmingly women and children.

The Narragansett hadn't joined the war: This confederacy had remained largely neutral up to this point. The single most lethal action English forces took in the entire war was aimed at people who weren't yet fighting them.

Why This Matters

This fact resists a simple story. It's tempting to imagine this war as "Native aggression, English defense" — this single detail makes that model impossible to sustain. Complicated history is usually more accurate history.

Becoming the Enemy

  • Terrified settlers demand death of all nearby Native people — including Christian converts
  • Massachusetts evacuates “praying Indians” to a harbor island — to protect them from colonists
  • Increase Mather: the war is God’s judgment on a sinful New England
By slaughtering people they called savages, some colonists feared they had become savages themselves.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the psychological core of the whole lecture: not just what colonists did, but what they were afraid they'd become while doing it.

Breaking It Down

Demanding death of all nearby Native people: Including Christian converts — the very communities the English missionary project had spent decades trying to build.

Evacuating "praying Indians" for their own protection: Massachusetts authorities had to actively stop colonists from massacring Native converts — not defend the colony from them.

God's judgment: Increase Mather argued the war was punishment for New England's own sins — a framework that let colonists interpret extraordinary violence, including their own, as a moral trial rather than evidence their whole colonial project might be unjust.

Why This Matters

This isn't the behavior of a confident, secure society. It's the behavior of people who suspect that fighting savagery with savagery has already changed them. That fear — not military strategy — is what this entire lecture series is really about.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jill Lepore – The Name of War (1998)

Lepore argues that this war forced English colonists to define, sometimes violently, what separated "English" from "Indian" identity — precisely because the war's brutality on both sides made that boundary far less obvious than colonists wanted it to be.

A War’s True Cost

English settlers killed
~5,000

📖 Understanding This Slide

Raw numbers only tell you so much. This slide's real point is the word "proportionally" — measured against the population of the time, not just the death count itself.

Breaking It Down

~2,000 English settlers killed / ~5,000 Native people killed: Devastating numbers on both sides, out of populations far smaller than modern America.

Hundreds enslaved: Sold into West Indian slavery after the war — including people who had specifically sought asylum from the conflict rather than fought in it. (The full story of this detail is in Independent Study.)

"Deadliest war per capita": When historians measure a war's toll against the total population at the time, this conflict exceeds the Civil War, the Revolution, or any twentieth-century American war in per-capita terms.

Why This Matters

Per-capita thinking is a transferable historical skill: a smaller raw death count can still represent a society's most catastrophic conflict, if the population itself was small. Always ask "compared to what?" before judging a number's true weight.

Twenty Years on a Pike

📖 Understanding This Slide

We're back where we started — but now you have everything you need to actually answer the question. Why the pike, for twenty years?

Breaking It Down

"A fixed civic object": Not a trophy taken down after a parade, but something generations of Plymouth children grew up walking past — a constantly renewed argument, not a one-time display.

What that argument insisted on: That this had been a righteous suppression of savage rebellion — not what actually happened, which was the near-destruction of an English colony that survived only barely and with outside help.

Why This Matters

The gap between what really happened (a colony that barely survived, terrified of its own conduct) and what the pike insisted happened (a righteous, confident victory) is the whole answer to this lecture's opening question. Memory-making isn't a side note to this war — it's how the war's meaning was actually manufactured and sustained for a generation.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jill Lepore – The Name of War (1998)

This is Lepore's central argument in full: the war's deepest significance lies not in its battles but in how English colonists used its memory — including Metacom's literal remains — to construct a confident new sense of English identity in America, distinct from both Native identity and English identity back in England. The pike wasn't an afterthought to the war. In Lepore's reading, it was the point.

What the Pike Actually Proves

English victory in 1676 depended on a Mohawk intervention brokered by New York’s governor — Native power turned against Native power, not English strength.

The Throughline Indian power set the terms.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One detail from the war's ending, plus one sentence you should keep for the rest of this course. This slide names the big idea running underneath everything today.

Breaking It Down

The Mohawk intervention: English victory in 1676 depended on New York's governor persuading the powerful Mohawk to attack Metacom's winter camp when his forces were already low on gunpowder. That's Native military power turned against Native military power — not proof of English strength on its own.

"Indian power set the terms": This is the recurring idea — the throughline — you'll see return across this entire lecture series. It means Native decisions, not European initiative, drove most of what happened across this continent in this period.

Why This Matters

Most introductory courses teach colonization as a story of steady European expansion with occasional Native "resistance." This course is deliberately built the other way — treating Native power as the force that shaped events, and European colonists as the ones reacting to it. Today's lecture is your first piece of evidence for that claim. You'll see the same throughline box return in each of the next three lectures, each time with a different piece of evidence behind it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Daniel K. Richter – Facing East from Indian Country (2001)

Richter's whole project is exactly this reframing — retelling early American history from Native vantage points and Native decision-making rather than treating Native peoples as background to a European story. This course's continental framing follows his lead directly.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A deliberate tease, not a full explanation — you're meant to walk into next lecture curious, not already informed.

Breaking It Down

"Five years later": Metacom's War ended in 1676. Five years after that, in 1680, a different Native leader — on the opposite side of the continent, in Spanish New Mexico — will reach a strikingly similar conclusion: that only war could save his people's world.

"Opposite end of the continent": This is the point where the lecture series stops being a New England story and becomes a genuinely continental one.

Why This Matters

If two Native uprisings this similar happened five years apart on opposite sides of the continent, that's not coincidence — it's a pattern. Next lecture is where that pattern gets its name.

Two Names for One War

English colonists called this “King Philip’s War” — Metacom’s adopted English name, turned into a mocking title for a man they meant to destroy.

  • “King Philip’s War” casts the English as defenders against a rebellious subject
  • “Metacom’s War” restores him as a sovereign leader making his own decision
Names carry arguments. Which name did you assume was neutral before today?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This picks up the naming question the lecture only touched on briefly. It's worth slowing down on, because it changes how you read every sentence in this chapter.

Breaking It Down

"King Philip's War": The English name, using the mocking title given to Metacom. This framing casts the war as a rebellious subject rising against a legitimate crown — even though Metacom never actually recognized Plymouth as his sovereign.

"Metacom's War": The name this course uses, following a shift among historians over the past few decades toward naming Native historical actors as authors of their own history rather than characters in someone else's.

Why This Matters

Ask yourself honestly: before this lecture, did you assume "King Philip's War" was just a neutral historical label? Most people do. Noticing that it isn't is itself a historical thinking skill you can apply to other names — wars, treaties, "discoveries" — throughout this course.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Jill Lepore – The Name of War (1998)

Lepore's title is itself the argument: how a war gets named is never neutral, and the act of naming shapes what a society allows itself to remember about who caused the violence and who suffered from it.

The Great Swamp Fight, in Full

  • December 1675 — a blizzard, an unfinished Narragansett fort
  • A combined Puritan army attacks — hundreds killed, mostly women and children
  • Narragansett snipers kill a disproportionate share of English officers first
  • Survivors join Metacom’s confederacy — and burn still more towns in response
Complication The single most lethal English action of the entire war targeted a people who hadn’t fought yet.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the full scene the live lecture only gave you in one compressed sentence. If you want to sit with the details, here they are.

Breaking It Down

The setting: December 1675, an unfinished Narragansett fort, a blizzard bearing down on both sides.

The attack: A combined Massachusetts and Connecticut militia force, guided by Native scouts, stormed the fort and killed hundreds of people inside — overwhelmingly women and children.

The cost to the attackers: Narragansett snipers inside the fort killed a disproportionate share of the English officers before the fort fell.

The aftermath: Narragansett survivors joined Metacom's confederacy afterward and proved they, too, could wage a war of terror — burning still more English towns in direct response.

Why This Matters

The single most lethal action English forces took in this entire war was against a confederacy that hadn't joined the fighting yet. That fact alone should make you suspicious of any simple story where colonists are purely defensive and Native forces are purely aggressive.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

James D. Drake – King Philip's War: Civil War in New England (1999)

Drake reads this attack as evidence for his larger argument: this conflict makes far more sense as a civil war within one deeply interconnected Anglo-Native society than as a war between two cleanly separate peoples.

A Different Providence

Mather read the war as judgment on New England’s sin. Quakers — persecuted by Puritans for decades — read the same war as divine punishment aimed specifically at their persecutors.

Same providential logic. Opposite target. Interpretation is never neutral.

📖 Understanding This Slide

A short, sharp counterpoint to Mather's reading of the war — same religious framework, completely different conclusion.

Breaking It Down

Mather's version: The war is God's judgment on all of sinful New England, demanding repentance from everyone.

The Quaker version: Quakers had faced real persecution at Puritan hands for decades. They read the same war as divine punishment aimed specifically at the Puritans who had persecuted them.

Why This Matters

Notice that both groups use the exact same idea — providence, God punishing sin through war — and reach opposite conclusions about who's actually guilty. That's a useful reminder: a shared framework for interpreting events doesn't guarantee a shared conclusion. Who's doing the interpreting always matters.

Sought Asylum. Enslaved Anyway.

Hundreds of captured Wampanoag, Narragansett, and allied Native people were sold into West Indian slavery after the war — including individuals who had never taken up arms and had specifically sought refuge from the conflict rather than joined it.

Colonial victory did not distinguish combatants from noncombatants. It targeted a whole population.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The Toll slide in the live lecture gave you the aggregate numbers. This slide gives you one specific, harder-to-shake detail hiding inside those numbers.

Breaking It Down

Who was enslaved: Hundreds of captured Wampanoag, Narragansett, and allied Native people, sold into West Indian slavery after the war ended.

The detail that complicates the story: Some of those enslaved had never taken up arms at all — they had specifically sought refuge from the fighting rather than joined it, and were enslaved anyway.

Why This Matters

This detail tells you something the battle narratives can't: colonial victory wasn't aimed only at combatants. It targeted an entire population, regardless of whether individuals had actually fought. That's a different, harsher kind of "winning" than a simple military victory.

Praying Towns

Settlements of Christian-converted Native people, organized by English missionaries, that drew converts away from traditional Native political and religious structures.

It matters here because it functioned as a mechanism of political erosion as much as religious conversion — by 1675, roughly half of the remaining Wampanoag lived in praying towns, and Metacom saw exactly where that trend led.

Sachem

A hereditary Native leader among Algonquian-speaking peoples of southern New England, holding political and diplomatic authority over a community or confederation.

Metacom inherited this role from his father, Massasoit — along with the diplomatic relationship Massasoit had built with Plymouth, which Metacom did not experience as a subject's obligation but as a sovereign relationship between two peoples.

Flintlock Musket

A muzzle-loading firearm using a flint-striking ignition mechanism, more reliable and faster to reload than earlier matchlock designs.

Wampanoag fighters had acquired these weapons and, because they hunted regularly, were far more accurate marksmen with them than most English settlers — a real tactical advantage in the war's early ambush-driven phase.

Providence / Providential Framing

The belief that historical events — wars, disasters, victories — directly express God's judgment or will, rewarding or punishing a people for their moral conduct.

Increase Mather used this framework to explain the war as punishment for New England's sins, which let colonists interpret extraordinary violence — their own included — as a moral trial rather than as evidence the colonial project itself might be unjust.

Mary Beth Norton's argument: Salem can't be understood apart from the Indian war burning on the Maine frontier at the very same time. The witch panic and the frontier war were connected, not separate.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before testing Norton's claim, we need to see the story it's arguing against — the version you probably already carry in your head.

Breaking It Down

The usual explanation (left panel): Repressed Puritans, hysterical girls, isolated village superstition, a panic that burned itself out. This is the standard pop-culture picture of Salem.

"Not wrong, but incomplete": Norton doesn't say the usual story is false — superstition and feuds were genuinely present. She says it leaves out something huge: the war on the frontier.

The test: Remove the frontier war, and three things stop making sense — the timing (why 1692?), the geography (why here?), and the people (why these specific accusers and accused?).

Why This Matters

A good revisionist argument rarely says "everything you knew was wrong." More often it says "what you knew was incomplete." Learning to tell the difference — between a claim that replaces the old story and one that enlarges it — is exactly what this lecture is training you to do.

The War You Already Saw

From the film York in flames — 50 dead, 100 taken captive. The Falmouth massacre. A stream of traumatized refugees fleeing south into towns like Salem.

The Maine frontier — the war the colony was losing as Salem began

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the piece of context the standard Salem story leaves out — and you've already seen it dramatized in the film.

Breaking It Down

The Wabanaki war: By 1692, Massachusetts was losing a war against the Wabanaki peoples on its northern frontier. Click the term for who they were and why the Puritans saw them as the Devil's agents.

York and Falmouth: The film showed you these raids — York in flames, 50 dead and 100 captured; the Falmouth massacre. Real, recent, terrifying events, not distant background.

Refugees flowing south: Survivors of these attacks fled into the older, denser towns of the colony — including Salem — carrying their trauma with them.

Why This Matters

Hold onto the timing: the town of York burned in January 1692, right as the first girls in Salem began having fits. Norton's whole argument hangs on connections like that one. The war wasn't happening in the background of Salem — for the people involved, it was happening right next to them.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the first "witness" for Norton's claim — and she's the strongest piece of biographical evidence. The film built much of its structure around her, so you know her story.

Breaking It Down

Age 3, Falmouth: Mercy Lewis survived an Indian attack that killed most of her close relatives — grandparents, aunts, uncles, cousins. She was orphaned by frontier violence before she was old enough to remember much else.

Displaced to Salem: She ended up as a servant in Salem Village, far from where her family was killed.

A central accuser: She became one of the most active and influential of the afflicted girls — including testifying against George Burroughs, whom you'll meet in two slides.

Why This Matters

This is why the frontier-war claim is hard to dismiss: it isn't built on vague atmosphere, it's built on a real person's documented life. One of Salem's most important accusers was a frontier-massacre orphan. Keep her biography in mind — the next slide shows why it might have shaped what she "saw."

A girl who watched her family slaughtered on the frontier now describes an unseen enemy, a diabolical conspiracy, a demand to sign the Devil’s book.

Norton’s point isn’t that she lied. It’s that her trauma was real — and Salem handed it a vocabulary.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide makes the careful, humane version of Norton's argument — and it's important not to skip past the nuance.

Breaking It Down

The overlap: A girl who watched her family slaughtered by an unseen, terrifying enemy now describes being tormented by an invisible enemy, a diabolical conspiracy, a demand to sign the Devil's book. The language of her "affliction" maps closely onto the actual war she survived.

"Not that she lied": This is the crucial move. Norton is not accusing Mercy Lewis of faking. She's saying the trauma was genuine — and that Salem's witchcraft framework gave that trauma a language to express itself in.

Why This Matters

Notice how much more sophisticated this is than "the girls were lying" OR "the girls were possessed." A third possibility — real trauma finding the only vocabulary its culture offered — explains the evidence better than either simple option. Holding that kind of "third possibility" in mind is what careful historical thinking feels like.

Witness for the Claim: George Burroughs

  • Harvard-educated minister — once held the Salem Village pulpit himself
  • By 1692, preaching in the frontier settlements of Maine — survived the same attacks Mercy Lewis did
  • Accused as the ringleader of the whole satanic conspiracy
  • Charged with bewitching the soldiers fighting the Wabanaki — causing their defeats
From the film His defiant examination; the accusation that he bewitched the frontier soldiers; reciting the Lord’s Prayer flawlessly at the gallows — and nearly stopping his own hanging.

📖 Understanding This Slide

If Mercy Lewis is the strongest accuser for Norton's claim, George Burroughs is the strongest accused. His case is where the frontier connection becomes almost impossible to explain away.

Breaking It Down

A frontier minister: Burroughs had once held the Salem Village pulpit, but by 1692 he was preaching in the Maine frontier settlements — and had survived the same Wabanaki attacks that orphaned Mercy Lewis.

Accused as the ringleader: Not just a witch, but the alleged leader of the entire satanic conspiracy.

The war-specific charge: Most tellingly, he was accused of bewitching the English soldiers fighting the Wabanaki — supernaturally causing their military defeats.

The gallows scene: You saw it in the film — he recited the Lord's Prayer perfectly (something no witch was supposedly able to do) and nearly stopped his own execution before Cotton Mather intervened.

Why This Matters

Mercy Lewis shows the frontier war shaped who accused. Burroughs shows it shaped who got blamed — and even what they were blamed for. When the accusation is literally "he made us lose the war," you can no longer separate the witch hunt from the frontier war. That's the next slide.

“…the descent of the invisible world upon the people of New England… might have some of its origins among the Indians, whose chief Sagamores are well known to have conversed with demons.”
— Cotton Mather, on the 1692 afflictions (quoted in the film)
The colony was losing a spiritual war on the border — then accused a frontier minister of being Satan’s field general, to explain why it kept losing.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the single hardest piece of evidence for skeptics of Norton's claim to explain away — it comes straight from Cotton Mather, the colony's most important minister, in his official account.

Breaking It Down

Mather's own words: He speculated openly that the 1692 afflictions "might have some of its origins among the Indians, whose chief Sagamores are well known to have conversed with demons." The colony's leading intellectual is directly linking the witchcraft crisis to the Indian enemy.

The logic laid bare: A colony losing a war it understood as a spiritual siege named a supernatural traitor — a frontier minister — to explain why English forces kept losing. The witch hunt wasn't just happening at the same time as the war. In Burroughs's case, it was about the war.

Why This Matters

A suggestive coincidence (Salem happened during a war) becomes something much stronger when the accusations themselves are explicitly about that war. This is the difference between "these two things overlapped in time" and "these two things were causally connected." The evidence on this slide pushes toward the second.

Notice the deck is arguing against itself here on purpose. That's not weakness — it's intellectual honesty. The Verdict Is a Verb

“Caused by”

Salem was really just the frontier war. — Overreaches. The evidence won’t carry it.

“Shaped by”

The frontier war was a powerful ingredient — interacting with feuds, gender, religion, and broken evidence. — Holds.

The real skill Not “what was THE cause” — but how much weight one cause can bear.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the payoff of the whole lecture, and it turns on a single word. The difference between a good and a bad version of Norton's argument is the difference between two verbs.

Breaking It Down

"Caused by" (overreaches): Saying Salem was really just the frontier war claims one cause explains everything. The evidence won't carry that — the Putnam feud, the salary fight, and spectral evidence were all genuinely at work too.

"Shaped by" (holds): Saying the frontier war was a powerful ingredient, interacting with other real causes, fits the evidence. This is what Norton actually argues.

The real skill: The interesting historical question is almost never "what was THE cause?" It's "how much weight can this cause bear?"

Why This Matters

This is the most transferable idea in the entire lecture. Real events almost always have many causes at once. Learning to say "this factor genuinely matters and it isn't the only one" — instead of crowning a single winner — is the difference between a slogan and a real explanation. You can apply this to any historical debate, and to plenty of arguments outside history too.

Why Fear Turned Inward

Why witchcraft accusations against neighbors — instead of rage at the Wabanaki? Because the Wabanaki were winning.

You cannot hang an enemy army that keeps burning your towns. You can hang a neighbor accused of serving the same Devil. Witchcraft offered a war the colony could actually win.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Having established that the frontier war shaped Salem, this slide asks the deeper question: why would war fear come out as accusations against neighbors instead of straightforward rage at the enemy?

Breaking It Down

The Wabanaki were winning: A society can pour fury into a fight it thinks it can win. It's much harder to sit with terror of an enemy that keeps burning your towns and taking your people — one you're demonstrably failing to stop.

A war you can win: Witchcraft accusations offered what the frontier war couldn't: a winnable fight. You can't hang the Wabanaki army. You can hang a neighbor accused of serving the same Devil the Wabanaki were thought to serve.

Why This Matters

This is the concept of displaced fear — terror that can't strike its real source, so it finds a substitute target. It's a psychologically powerful idea, and it explains something the "superstition" story can't: why the panic took the specific shape of hunting enemies within the community. The next slide connects this all the way back to Lecture 1.

Even Salem

The event taught as the most inward, self-contained episode in early New England turns out to have been shaped, at its core, by the pressure of Native power on the border.

The Throughline Indian power set the terms.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The same gold throughline box from the last two lectures returns — and this time it lands on the most surprising evidence yet.

Breaking It Down

"Even Salem": Of every event in this series, Salem is the one that looks most like a purely internal, self-contained Puritan story — girls, a village, a church, superstition. And yet, at its core, it was shaped by the pressure of Native military power on the colony's border.

"Indian power set the terms": The same sentence you've now seen after Metacom's War and the Pueblo Revolt. Three different events, three different regions, one recurring truth — and this is the least obvious case of the three.

Why This Matters

Here's the quiet radicalism of the whole lecture: if even Salem — the seemingly most inward-looking episode in early New England — was shaped by a war with Native peoples, then Native power reached almost everywhere in early America, including places we'd never think to look. That's the continental argument at its strongest, precisely because it shows up where you'd least expect it.

Spectral Evidence on Trial

Spectral evidence was testimony that an invisible specter of the accused did harm — something only the accuser could see.

Why it was catastrophic You cannot disprove an invisible attack. Throwing spectral evidence out in late 1692 is what effectively ended the trials — almost overnight.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide deepens one of the "complicating" causes from the cross-examination. Spectral evidence is a self-contained lesson in how a broken legal standard can manufacture a witch hunt regardless of the underlying fear.

Breaking It Down

What it was: Testimony that the accused's invisible "specter" — seen only by the afflicted accuser — had done harm. The evidence was, by definition, invisible to everyone but the accuser.

Why it was catastrophic: You can't disprove an invisible attack. If only the accuser can see the specter, the accused has no possible defense. That let accusations spiral without any brake.

The shutoff switch: When the court stopped accepting spectral evidence in late 1692, the prosecutions collapsed almost immediately.

Why This Matters

This is important for weighing Norton's claim fairly. The frontier war may explain the fear that fueled Salem — but the broken evidentiary standard helps explain why that fear ran so far out of control, and why removing one legal rule could stop it almost overnight. A full explanation needs both: the fear and the mechanism that let it spread.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide gives you the strongest competing explanation to Norton's frontier-war thesis — and asks you to weigh the two against each other yourself.

Breaking It Down

The Putnam faction: A striking number of Salem accusations trace back to one powerful family and its local grievances — land disputes, support for Reverend Parris, and old grudges (including against George Burroughs). Click the term for more.

The evidence scale: On one side, the frontier-war cause (refugee accusers, a frontier minister blamed for defeats). On the other, the local-feud cause (accusations that follow the Putnams' enemies). Both patterns are genuinely present in the record.

Why This Matters

This is the "how much weight can one cause bear?" skill made concrete. The local-feud explanation (associated with the classic study Salem Possessed) was the dominant scholarly account for decades before Norton. A strong answer doesn't pick one and discard the other — it recognizes that a single accusation could be driven by frontier trauma and local grudge at the same time. Real causation is layered.

How It Ended

  • Increase Mather warns against improper accusations — confessions stop
  • Governor Phips halts the court; spectral evidence is thrown out
  • The Superior Court later discards most of the trial evidence
  • 1706: Ann Putnam Jr. formally apologizes to her church
Notice the timing: the accusations collapsed once they threatened the powerful — and once the colony could no longer win with them. A fight is only useful while you can win it.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The final Independent Study slide, and it ties the ending of the crisis directly back to the "why fear turned inward" argument from the live lecture.

Breaking It Down

How it stopped: Increase Mather (Cotton's father) warned against improper accusations, and confessions dried up. Governor Phips halted the court and threw out spectral evidence. The Superior Court later discarded most of the trial evidence. In 1706, accuser Ann Putnam Jr. formally apologized to her church.

The revealing timing: The accusations collapsed once they started reaching powerful people — and once spectral evidence, the tool that made them unstoppable, was withdrawn.

Why This Matters

Connect this to the live lecture's argument: if the witch hunt was partly a "war the colony could win," notice that it ended right when it stopped being winnable — when it threatened the elite and lost its unfalsifiable weapon. A society reaches for an internal enemy when doing so is useful, and drops it when it isn't. That timing is itself evidence for what the accusations were really doing.

The Wabanaki

A confederacy of Algonquian-speaking Native peoples of present-day Maine and the Canadian Maritimes, allied with the French and at war with the English on the northern frontier through the 1670s–1690s.

Their sustained, successful raids on English settlements — York, Falmouth, and others — produced the refugees and the atmosphere of spiritual siege that Norton argues fed directly into the Salem crisis. To many Puritans, the Wabanaki were literally the Devil’s agents.

×
The Putnam Faction

A powerful Salem Village family whose members — including Thomas Putnam and the afflicted Ann Putnam Jr. — appear behind a remarkable share of the 1692 accusations.

Their involvement is the backbone of the older “local feud” explanation of Salem: accusations tracked the Putnams’ land disputes, their support of Reverend Parris, and old grudges (including against George Burroughs). This is the competing cause students must weigh against the frontier-war thesis — and both can be partly true at once.

Spectral Evidence

Testimony that the accused’s invisible “specter” — seen only by the afflicted accuser — had tormented them.

Because no one else could see the specter, such claims were essentially impossible to disprove, which let accusations spiral unchecked. When the court stopped accepting spectral evidence in late 1692, the prosecutions collapsed almost immediately — strong evidence that the broken evidentiary standard, not just the underlying fear, was what let the crisis run so far.

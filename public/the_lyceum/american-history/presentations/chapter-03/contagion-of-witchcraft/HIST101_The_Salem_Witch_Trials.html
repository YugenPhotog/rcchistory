Title: Malleus Maleficarum (The Hammer of Witches)
Authors: Heinrich Kramer and Jacob Sprenger (though Sprenger's contribution is debated)
Date: First published in 1487
Context:
The book was written during the European witch craze, a period from the late 15th century to the late 18th century when thousands of people — mainly women — were accused of witchcraft and often executed.
The "Malleus" was written against a backdrop of both religious (Catholic) concern about heresy and broader societal fears about witches' supposed malevolent powers.
Content and Structure:
The book is divided into three parts:
The reality and depravity of witches.
The powers witches could supposedly hold and the methods by which they carried out their malevolent actions.
The judicial procedures for trying and convicting suspected witches.
Key Themes & Notions:
Diabolical Pact: The central idea is that witches entered into a pact with the Devil. This pact granted them special powers but also bound them to serve Satan.
Gendered Nature of Witchcraft: The book emphasizes that women are more susceptible to the temptations of the Devil, leading to the disproportionately female face of the witch stereotype.
Sexuality: The "Malleus" often touches on the theme of sexuality, reflecting contemporary concerns about chastity and moral purity. Witches were frequently accused of sexual relations with demons or the Devil.
Legitimacy of Torture: The book justifies the use of torture for extracting confessions from suspected witches.
Signs of Witchcraft: The text lists supposed indications of witchcraft, such as specific marks on the body, leading to many innocent people being falsely accused.
Impact:
The "Malleus Maleficarum" played a significant role in the witch hunts in Europe. The book was reprinted multiple times, spreading its influence widely.
It provided the pseudo-legal and theological justification for the intense persecution of supposed witches.

Lancashire / Pendle Witch Trials (1612) — fuller briefing

Why this matters for Salem: English precedent. Colonists inherited English law, English demonology (including James I’s Daemonologie), and a working model of how witchcraft accusations could escalate through confession, rumor, and child testimony. Pendle is among the best-documented English witch trials of the seventeenth century.

Context

Pendle Forest, Lancashire — remote, poor, and long regarded by the authorities as a disorderly region with lingering Catholic recusancy after the Reformation. Village “cunning folk” (healers who used charms, herbal remedies, and counter-magic) were a familiar part of rural life; the same practices that neighbors might pay for in ordinary times could look like diabolism under a hostile magistrate.

England’s Witchcraft Act of 1604 (under James I) made contact with evil spirits and causing harm by witchcraft capital felonies. James had written Daemonologie (1597) and was known as a witch-hunting monarch; local justices had reason to take such cases seriously.

Two rival family networks of elderly healers stood at the center: Elizabeth Southerns (“Old Demdike”) and her Device kin at Malkin Tower, and Anne Whittle (“Old Chattox”) and her daughter Anne Redferne. Both matriarchs were blind and in their eighties. Competition over healing, begging, and protection-payments fed mutual accusations.

What triggered the case?

On 21 March 1612, Demdike’s granddaughter Alizon Device met pedlar John Law on the road and asked for pins (useful in charms and healing). After a refusal or quarrel, Law collapsed — likely a stroke. Alizon later confessed that she had cursed him and asked a familiar spirit to lame him. Magistrate Roger Nowell, already charged that year with listing Catholic recusants, opened an investigation.

What were they accused of doing?

  • Maleficium (harm by witchcraft): The twelve Pendle accused were charged with the murders of about ten people by witchcraft — not one mass killing, but a string of alleged deaths over years (e.g. Robert and Christopher Nutter; Henry Mitton after refusing Demdike a penny; Anne Townley; Anne Foulds; and others named in depositions).
  • Alizon Device: bewitching / laming John Law after the pin quarrel; she seems to have believed in her own power and confessed in court when confronted by Law.
  • Demdike & Chattox: confessions of long pacts with the Devil / familiar spirits; mutual accusations of murder by witchcraft stretching back years; clay images used to kill.
  • Elizabeth Device (Demdike’s daughter): multiple murder charges; her own children testified against her.
  • Malkin Tower meeting (Good Friday, 10 April 1612): After the first arrests, Elizabeth Device gathered friends at the family home. Rumor and testimony turned this into a witches’ sabbath: stolen sheep for a feast, plotting to free the prisoners — even an alleged scheme to blow up Lancaster Castle (historians treat that “gunpowder” detail cautiously; it may have been shaped by examiners’ questions after 1605).
  • Alice Nutter: relatively prosperous widow; convicted largely on presence at Malkin Tower and association — a reminder that not every accused fit the beggar stereotype.

How the trials worked

Jennet Preston was tried at York (27 July) and hanged. The rest went to Lancaster Assizes (18–19 August 1612) before judges Sir James Altham and Sir Edward Bromley; Nowell prosecuted. Star witness: Jennet Device, about nine years old — Alizon’s sister — who calmly testified against her mother, brother, and neighbors (child testimony of this kind was unusual in ordinary felony trials but facilitated by the special climate around witchcraft evidence). Elizabeth Device had to be removed from court for cursing her daughter.

Primary narrative source: court clerk Thomas Potts, The Wonderfull Discoverie of Witches in the Countie of Lancaster (1613) — detailed, but written for publication under the judges’ supervision and not a neutral transcript.

Outcomes (matches the slide’s numbers)

Twelve people from the Pendle circle were accused of murdering ten by witchcraft. Ten were hanged (nine at Lancaster on 20 August 1612, plus Jennet Preston at York). Old Demdike died in prison before trial. Alice Grey was found not guilty.

Accuracy note for you (not on the slide): the bullet says “12 women.” The twelve accused were not all women — among those tried were at least two men (James Device and John Bulcock). Of the eleven who reached trial, standard tallies are nine women and two men. The murder count (ten) and the 10 hanged / 1 died in prison / 1 acquitted outcome are sound.

Delivery tip

King Philip’s War

Terrifying impact on the colonists (1675–1676)

  • Bloodiest war New England colonists had yet faced — roughly a thousand English dead, many in sudden frontier attacks
  • Towns burned; survivors fled as refugees; everyday life on the frontier felt exposed and uncertain
  • Stories of massacre, captivity, and mutilation traveled with the refugees and lodged in colonial memory
  • Left a generation convinced that Indian war — and an unseen enemy who could strike without warning — was an existential threat

Tituba’s Role and Significance

Role

  • Participated in the counter-magic, i.e., the “witch cake”
  • Confessed to Witchcraft
  • Described sensational encounters with malevolent creatures and others witches
  • Implicated three women by name
  • Implied many others involved

Significance

  • Her confession validated the little girl’s accusation
  • Implied the existence of a conspiracy of witches and witchcraft
  • Fanned the flames of hysteria and started the witch hunt
Note – Tituba is blameless. As a slave, you would have been fearful of beatings or other punishment. And like everybody, she was afraid of being executed. She said what she thought they wanted to hear to save her life.

Gemeinschaft → Gesellschaft

Terms from sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies describing two kinds of social bond:

Gemeinschaft (“community”): face-to-face, personal, kinship- and neighborhood-based ties — duty, custom, and reputation bind people together. Think Salem Village: agrarian parish, known neighbors, moral surveillance.

Gesellschaft (“society”): impersonal, contractual, market-oriented relations — individuals pursue interest through law and exchange. Think Salem Town: commerce, harbor trade, more anonymous dealings.

On this slide, Gemeinschaft to Gesellschaft names the felt shift from traditional village community toward commercial society — a tension many historians connect to the Village/Town geography of accusation.

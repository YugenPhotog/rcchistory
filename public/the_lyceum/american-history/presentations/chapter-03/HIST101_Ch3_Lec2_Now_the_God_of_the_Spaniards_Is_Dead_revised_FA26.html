Five Years Later, a Thousand Miles West

Last time: Metacom’s War brought New England to the edge of destruction — and left colonists afraid of what the war had made them.

Now cross the continent. Different empire, different religion, different peoples — and autonomous Pueblo nations coordinating against a colonial order they found intolerable.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide is a bridge, not a fresh start. It deliberately ties today's lecture back to Metacom's War so you can see these aren't two unrelated stories — they're two pieces of one continental argument.

Breaking It Down

"Five years later": Metacom's War ended in 1676. The Pueblo Revolt breaks out in 1680 — roughly five years apart, but on opposite sides of the continent.

"Different empire, different religion, different people": New England was English and Protestant; New Mexico was Spanish and Catholic. Almost nothing about the setting is the same — which is exactly what makes the shared outcome so striking.

Why This Matters

If Native coalitions in two very different colonial worlds expose European weakness only a few years apart, that is more than coincidence. It is a continental pattern. The comparison does not make Wampanoag and Pueblo experiences identical; it shows that Native nations repeatedly constrained colonial power on their own terms.

A Mission in Crisis

  • Franciscan zeal slackening; priests refuse to learn Pueblo languages
  • Missionaries whip and shackle Pueblos for “infractions”
  • Drought settles in — as Apache and Navajo raids intensify
80,000 → 17,000
Pueblo population, 1598 to 1679

Adobe mission, Rio Grande region — modern woodcut; structure type often postdates 1680

📖 Understanding This Slide

Before we get to the revolt itself, we need to see the pressure building. Think of this slide as showing a system under strain from several directions at once — religious, environmental, and demographic.

Breaking It Down

Franciscan zeal slackening: By the late 1600s, fewer priests bothered to learn Pueblo languages and demanded Pueblos learn Spanish instead — a sign the mission project was becoming more about control than conversion.

Whipping and shackling: Missionaries who had arrived convinced of their own good intentions increasingly treated Pueblos as inferiors to be punished for offenses that, from a Pueblo view, were no offenses at all.

Drought plus raids: A prolonged drought hit at the same time Apache and Navajo raids intensified — material crisis stacked on top of religious crisis.

80,000 → 17,000: The Pueblo population collapsed by roughly three-quarters in about eighty years, mostly from disease. That's not a statistic — it's a society losing most of its people within living memory.

Why This Matters

The revolt didn't come out of nowhere. Like Metacom's decision last lecture, it was a response to a whole world being taken apart at once. Keep that parallel in mind — it's the continental pattern forming.

Sacred Authority in Crisis

Drought, disease, raiding, forced labor, and religious persecution undermined Spanish claims to sacred authority.

For diverse Pueblo communities, ceremonial autonomy became inseparable from political survival.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Do not reduce this crisis to a simple experiment in which Pueblo people compared two religions and chose whichever one produced rain. Pueblo ceremonial traditions were diverse, and decades of contact had produced selective conversion, adaptation, resistance, and religious blending.

Breaking It Down

Several crises at once: Drought, disease, raiding, forced labor, and religious persecution made Spanish promises of protection and sacred authority increasingly difficult to sustain.

No single Pueblo response: Some people converted, some blended Catholic and Pueblo practices, some resisted mission authority, and many moved among those positions. The shared issue was not a laboratory test between two gods; it was whether communities could preserve ceremonial and political autonomy under coercive rule.

Why This Matters

Religion still belongs at the center of the revolt, but not as a simplified contest between two uniform cosmologies. Spanish persecution made the defense of Pueblo ceremonial life inseparable from sovereignty, labor, land, and collective survival.

The Whipping of the Medicine Men

47 whipped
Pueblo medicine men, 1675 — charged with sorcery
3 hanged
a fourth took his own life

Among the whipped: Po’pay, of San Juan Pueblo — present-day Ohkay Owingeh. The punishment became a major catalyst and symbol for resistance.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is a major catalyst, not a moment of psychological certainty. The surviving record shows that punishment intended to enforce obedience became a powerful grievance around which resistance organized; it does not let us watch Po’pay’s inner outlook transform in a single instant.

Breaking It Down

47 whipped, 3 hanged, 1 suicide (1675): The Spanish publicly punished Pueblo medicine men for practicing their own religion. This was meant as a demonstration of Spanish power.

Po’pay: A religious leader from San Juan Pueblo, now called Ohkay Owingeh, who was among those punished. He later moved north to Taos Pueblo and helped organize the uprising across autonomous Pueblo nations and communities.

Why This Matters

The flogging is best treated as a catalyst and political symbol rather than a documented conversion point in Po’pay’s inner life. Colonial repression helped create the conditions for resistance, while Po’pay and other Pueblo leaders did the organizing.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Andrew L. Knaut – The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (1995)

Knaut's standard account traces exactly how decades of accumulating pressure — religious persecution, drought, disease, forced labor — converged in the 1670s, so that the whipping of the medicine men became the spark that lit fuel already long in place.

Why the Charge Was “Sorcery”

The Spanish weren’t prosecuting a labor dispute or a land claim. They prosecuted Pueblo religious practice itself as a demonic crime.

The charge reveals the Spanish theological frame: diverse Pueblo ceremonial traditions were recast as one demonic conspiracy.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Pay attention to the word "sorcery." The specific charge the Spanish chose tells you how they understood the conflict — and it wasn't as a political or economic dispute.

Breaking It Down

Not a labor or land charge: The Spanish could have framed Pueblo resistance as rebellion or refusal to work. Instead they prosecuted Pueblo religious practice itself as a demonic crime.

Why that framing matters: The charge shows how Spanish officials understood the conflict. It does not prove that all Pueblo nations shared one theology or understood the revolt as a single contest between two gods.

Why This Matters

Religious persecution made ceremonial autonomy a political question. The lecture title comes from one declaration preserved through a Spanish interrogation; it is powerful evidence of theological victory language, but not a transparent summary of every Pueblo community’s beliefs. Keep that mediation in mind when we reach the declaration.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Ramón Gutiérrez – When Jesus Came, the Corn Mothers Went Away (1991)

Gutiérrez shows how deeply religion, power, and daily survival were intertwined in colonial New Mexico — which is why an attack on Pueblo religious practice was experienced as an attack on the entire order of Pueblo life, not just on private belief.

📖 Understanding This Slide

Here's the uprising itself, in numbers. But the most important word on the slide is "coordination" — click it to see why.

Breaking It Down

~200 of ~1,000 Spaniards killed: A devastating blow to a small colonial population.

21 of 40 priests killed: More than half the colony's missionaries — a direct strike at the religious authority the whole conflict was about.

Coordinated, not homogeneous: Pueblo communities separated by great distances struck in a synchronized way. This required planning among autonomous nations and villages, but it did not erase their different languages, leaders, ceremonial traditions, or political goals.

Why This Matters

The coordination dismantles the stereotype of a spontaneous riot. It shows sophisticated political and military organization among autonomous peoples who created a shared campaign without becoming one homogeneous Pueblo polity.

— Declaration recorded in a Spanish interrogation, 1680 (Hackett, ed., 1942)

The churches burned. Spanish survivors fled Santa Fe down the Rio Grande to El Paso.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the line the entire lecture is named after. Read it slowly — it's a theological declaration of victory, not just a military one.

Breaking It Down

“The God of the Spaniards is dead”: In this recorded declaration, Spanish sacred power and the saints appear defeated while Pueblo sacred authority survives. The language announces theological as well as military victory, but it reaches us through translation and a Spanish interrogation rather than as an unmediated statement for every Pueblo nation.

The flight to El Paso: Spanish survivors abandoned Santa Fe and retreated hundreds of miles down the Rio Grande. The colonial capital was simply gone.

Why This Matters

Hold this quote loosely for now. In just a few slides, we're going to stop and ask a hard question about it: how do we actually know these were the Pueblos' words? Notice that even here, the line comes to us through a Spanish record. Keep that in the back of your mind.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Charles W. Hackett, ed. – Revolt of the Pueblo Indians of New Mexico (1942)

This quote survives because Hackett collected and published Spanish interrogation records. That written archive is indispensable and deeply complicated. The wording passed through captives, translators, scribes, and officials whose theological categories shaped what was recorded.

12 years
with no Spanish colonial government anywhere in New Mexico.

The most successful Indigenous revolt in North American history.

📖 Understanding This Slide

One number, and a big claim. Both deserve to be taken seriously rather than skimmed past.

Breaking It Down

Twelve years: For over a decade, no Spanish colonial government existed anywhere in New Mexico. That's not a temporary setback or a raid — it's roughly the length of an entire cycle of colonial administration, erased.

"Most successful in North American history": No other Indigenous uprising north of Mexico expelled a European colonial power from its territory this completely, for this long.

Why This Matters

Most survey courses barely mention this revolt, which is exactly why the "most successful" claim lands so hard. If the biggest Indigenous victory in North American history is also one of the least-taught events, that tells you something about whose successes the standard story tends to leave out.

Victory did not create one Pueblo future After 1680, autonomous Pueblo communities disagreed over leadership, diplomacy, religion, crops, and family life. Po’pay’s influence declined as local priorities reasserted themselves.

Shared victory did not erase distinct Pueblo political or ceremonial traditions.
(Full reconquest story → Independent Study)

📖 Understanding This Slide

The red caution box signals a necessary complication. The revolt achieved a shared expulsion, but each Pueblo remained an autonomous nation with its own leadership, traditions, and postwar priorities.

Breaking It Down

Po’pay’s influence declined: Continued drought, raiding, local rivalries, and disagreement over postwar leadership weakened his authority. The evidence supports political fragmentation; it does not require a simple claim that Pueblo people subjected him to the same religious “test” supposedly applied to Christianity.

Different postwar choices: Communities disagreed about Franciscan marriages, Spanish-introduced crops, diplomacy, ceremonial restoration, and relations with neighboring peoples. A shared revolt did not produce one uniform program.

Why This Matters

Coordination and uniformity are not the same thing. Autonomous nations can defeat a common enemy while retaining different goals and memories of the struggle. The full story of Spain’s violent return and continued Pueblo resistance is waiting in Independent Study.

Stop. How Do We Know Any of This?

The Problem With the Record

Much of the surviving written evidence about why Pueblo people revolted comes from declarations extracted from captives by the Spanish authorities the revolt had just defeated.

Other archives: oral traditions • archaeology • architecture • ceramics • rock art • landscape • Native scholarship

Josephe. Lucas. Pedro Naranjo. Three names. Three interrogations.

📖 Understanding This Slide

This is the lecture's turning point — the moment it stops telling the story and starts questioning how we know the story at all. This is a genuine historical-thinking skill, and it's arguably the most important thing you'll practice today.

Breaking It Down

The written-record problem: Much of the surviving written evidence about motives comes from statements given by captured Pueblo people to Spanish officials who were trying to understand and reverse their loss.

More than writing: Pueblo oral traditions, archaeology, architecture, ceramics, rock art, landscape, and Native scholarship provide additional archives. They preserve histories the Spanish record could not contain and remind us that the revolt had shared significance without one uniform Pueblo experience.

Josephe, Lucas, Pedro Naranjo: The three most-cited declarations, each extracted under interrogation. Click Naranjo's name for more on why his account is both invaluable and deeply compromised.

Why This Matters

Every source has an author and a purpose. When the author of your evidence is the losing side interrogating prisoners, you can't read that evidence as a neutral window into what happened. Learning to ask "who created this record, and why?" is a skill you'll use on every source in this course — and far beyond it.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

Daniel K. Richter – Facing East from Indian Country (2001)

Richter's whole method involves reading colonial records "against the grain" — mining sources written by Europeans for what they inadvertently reveal about Native perspectives the authors didn't intend to preserve. That's exactly the move this section of the lecture is teaching you to make.

Whose Words Are These, Really?

Be cautious

  • Naranjo describes demons that “emit fire” — Pueblo belief, translator’s choice, or Spanish theology hearing what it expected?
  • Answers shaped by interrogators looking for a “diabolical” conspiracy

But not worthless

  • A positive vision survives: abundant harvests, health, a world free of Franciscans
  • Lucas admits he knows only fragments — not what a coached confession invents

📖 Understanding This Slide

Source criticism isn't just "distrust everything." It's a two-sided skill: know what to doubt, and know what you can still reasonably trust. This slide models both sides at once.

Breaking It Down

Be cautious (left): When Naranjo describes demons that "emit fire," we can't tell how much is genuine Pueblo belief, how much is a translator's word choice, and how much is Spanish interrogators hearing the "diabolical" conspiracy their own theology expected. The interrogators were fishing for a devil — and may have caught the one they brought with them.

But not worthless (right): Some details cut against what a coached confession would produce. A positive religious vision — abundant harvests, health, freedom from Franciscans — isn't what interrogators were trying to extract. And Lucas openly admitting he knows only fragments is not what a fabricated clean confession looks like.

Why This Matters

Here's the transferable rule: details that work against the source-creator's interest are often the most trustworthy parts of a compromised source. An interrogator had no reason to invent a captive's uncertainty or the rebels' hopeful vision — so those details likely carry real information, even inside a coerced record.

Horses Before and After 1680

Indigenous peoples had incorporated horses across western North America before the Pueblo Revolt.

  • By the early 1600s: horses in Indigenous contexts on the Great Plains and northern Rockies
  • After 1680: Spain’s expulsion redistributed animals and may have accelerated existing Native networks

Later mounted scene — modern woodcut; not the origin of Indigenous horse cultures

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide corrects an older diffusion story. The 1680 revolt did not release the first horses that created Indigenous horse cultures. Archaeological, genomic, and Indigenous evidence now places horses in Native-controlled contexts far beyond New Mexico before the revolt.

Breaking It Down

Before 1680: The 2023 Science study found domestic horses in Indigenous contexts on the central Great Plains and northern Rockies by the first half of the seventeenth century at the latest.

After 1680: Pueblo people did seize or retain animals after Spain’s expulsion. Those horses may have enlarged and accelerated networks of trade, breeding, raiding, diplomacy, and cultural incorporation that Native communities had already begun building.

About the image: Any image here shows a later mounted scene. It illustrates one development within a longer history, not the moment Indigenous horse cultures began.

Why This Matters

The correction strengthens the continental argument. Native peoples no longer appear as passive recipients of animals accidentally released by Europeans in 1680. They appear as earlier and active participants in acquiring, breeding, exchanging, and integrating horses.

🎓 Historians Weigh In

William Timothy Treal Taylor et al. – “Early Dispersal of Domestic Horses into the Great Plains and Northern Rockies,” Science (2023)

This interdisciplinary study, created with Indigenous scholars and knowledge holders, places domestic horses in Indigenous contexts across the central Plains and northern Rockies before 1680. DOI: 10.1126/science.adc9691.

Breaking It Down

“Existing Native networks”: The revolt happened in one Spanish colony, but it entered a western world in which Indigenous people had already acquired horses and were building their own systems of movement, exchange, and power.

The Spanish Reconquest

  • 1692: negotiated submissions and Spanish claims of return
  • 1693: violent retaking of Santa Fe; executions and enslavement
  • 1694–96: organized resistance continues; revolt again in 1696
Rule returned, but not unchanged Pueblo resistance had exposed the limits of the earlier mission regime. Accommodation and coercion now existed together.

📖 Understanding This Slide

The live lecture compressed the Spanish return into a single line. The fuller story is not an uncomplicated reconquest in 1692–93, but a violent and uneven process followed by continued organized resistance.

Breaking It Down

1692: Diego de Vargas returned and secured negotiated or fragile submissions from some Pueblo communities. Spanish claims that New Mexico had been peacefully restored should not be confused with durable control.

1693 and after: The Spanish retaking of Santa Fe became violent; Spanish forces executed about seventy men and enslaved roughly four hundred women and children. Tewa and Tano resistance continued at Black Mesa, Jémez resistance continued, and Pueblo communities revolted again in 1696.

Limits remained: The Hopi were never restored to Spanish rule, and the post-reconquest mission regime operated differently because Pueblo resistance had demonstrated the limits of the earlier order.

Why This Matters

“The Spanish came back” is true; “everything went back to normal” is not. Reconstituted rule combined coercion with greater accommodation of Pueblo ceremonial and political life, not because colonial authorities became benign, but because the revolt had shown what the old mission regime could provoke.

Reading Naranjo, Line by Line

Two readings at once

Which phrases sound like Pueblo testimony — and which sound like a Spanish interrogator’s theology, hearing a “devil” wherever it already expected to find one?

📖 Understanding This Slide

This slide lets you practice the source-criticism skill from the live lecture on an actual passage. It's an exercise, not just information — try it.

Breaking It Down

What Naranjo's declaration describes: Three supernatural figures who gave Po’pay his orders, emitting fire from their bodies, directing a return to "the state of their antiquity" — the old ways before the Spanish.

The exercise: Read it twice. First, look for what likely reflects genuine Pueblo religious understanding. Then read it again as a Spanish interrogator would — someone primed to hear "the devil" in any Native religious vision. The same words can carry both readings at once.

Why This Matters

You can't cleanly separate the two voices — and that's the point. A coerced, translated, theologically-loaded source doesn't give you pure Pueblo testimony or pure Spanish invention. It gives you a negotiation between them, and learning to hold both possibilities at once is what real historical reading feels like.

Coordinated Uprising

The 1680 revolt was planned across Pueblo communities separated by considerable distance, striking in a synchronized way rather than erupting village by village.

This coordination matters because it refutes any picture of the revolt as a spontaneous riot. Tradition holds that runners carried knotted cords to mark the days remaining until the uprising — a deliberate regional act of political organization among autonomous Pueblo nations and communities.

Pedro Naranjo

A member of the Queres nation whose lengthy declaration, given under Spanish interrogation, is one of the most detailed surviving accounts of the revolt’s organization and religious vision.

His declaration is invaluable and deeply compromised at the same time: it preserves real detail about the uprising, but it was extracted by the defeated Spanish authorities, filtered through translation, and shaped by interrogators listening for confirmation of a “diabolical” conspiracy.

Not required today

  • Any prior study of religion
  • A faith of your own — or none
  • The ability to pronounce every term correctly

What you will learn

  • To interpret religious sources and practices
  • To compare traditions across real dimensions
  • To read religion in context

Curiosity is the prerequisite. Everything else we build together.

Delivery: Say this plainly and mean it. Students arrive worried in two opposite directions — that they know too little, or that their own commitments make them suspect. Name both and set them down.

The course objectives, in the syllabus's own terms — worth naming here since this is the "what you will learn" slide:

1. Interpret religious sources and practices — texts, images, rituals, material culture — by identifying form or genre, context, and the perspective of the community that produced or used them.
2. Compare traditions across at least two dimensions: ritual practice, conceptions of authority, ethics, salvation or liberation, community identity.
3. Analyze in context — how historical, social, and political settings shape religious change and diversity over time.
4. Describe and explain core beliefs, practices, texts, and historical development using accurate terminology.
5. Apply scholarly approaches to unfamiliar or emerging religious movements, including debates about classification and public perception.

The fifth one is worth flagging early — it is why the course ends with New Religious Movements rather than treating them as a curiosity.

The questions we keep coming back to

  • What does this tradition say is ultimately real?
  • What has gone wrong with us — and what would fix it?
  • Where does authority live — text, teacher, community, experience?
  • What does a person actually do? Ritual, ethics, daily practice.
  • How does a tradition change when history moves under it?
  • Who counts as inside — and who decides?
We are not collecting facts about seven religions. We are learning to ask the same good questions of each one — and to take the different answers seriously.

Delivery: Read these fast — they are a preview, not a lesson. The gloss at the bottom is the line that matters. Say it twice if you have to.

Stream & Discuss

What you do

  • Watch the documentary in Canvas — it must record at least 90% completion.
  • Initial post: normally three substantive paragraphs investigating topics from the film.
  • Two replies to classmates, normally a paragraph each, with citations for factual claims.

Why documentaries

Learn about a religious tradition from its adherents rather than primarily from its critics.
— Krister Stendahl's principle

These films let us meet religions through the people who actually practice them.

Discussions are time-sensitive. Post by the due date, then return during the following four days to reply. After that the discussion closes — there is no late version.

Delivery: The two-part deadline is the single most common place students lose points in the first month. Say it twice: the initial post has a due date, and the replies have their own window that closes.

The 90% completion tracking surprises people. Say it out loud — Canvas records watch time, and skipping to the end does not count as watching.

Stendahl's principle is worth a full minute. It is the methodological commitment of the whole course: you do not get to describe a tradition by its worst adherents or its loudest opponents. Students who grasp this write markedly better discussion posts.

Note that some assignments specify additional topics or paragraphs — the prompt in Canvas governs, and detailed grading criteria come with each one.

Two kinds of questions

Anchor Questions

An anchor points you at a specific passage. You should be able to recognize where an idea appears, what the passage discusses, and why it matters.

Review Questions

A review question asks you to show that you understand the larger issue the passage belongs to.

Find the evidence. Then say what it means.

Delivery: The pointing hand and the marginal gloss are the mental model students should walk out with. Anchor = location. Review = meaning. Both devices are borrowed from actual manuscript practice, which is a nice half-sentence aside if the room enjoys it.

Say this plainly: I am not trying to trick you into remembering an isolated fact. I want you to locate the evidence and understand what it tells us.

Optional live demo — worth the minutes. Write a term from Chapter 1 on the board — preunderstanding, or the dimensions of religion. Ask the room what section of the chapter they would expect it to sit in. Then ask what larger question a scholar of religion could build on it.

For preunderstanding you are fishing for: Why is a neutral description of religion so difficult? What does a scholar owe a tradition they do not belong to? Can you study something fairly from outside it? Then name the move you just made — the anchor located the evidence, the review question asked you to understand it.

Quiz mechanics to state out loud: the quizzes are short, multiple-choice, timed, completed online through Canvas, drawn from the chapter's Anchor and Review Questions, and worth 25 points each. Availability windows and due dates live in Canvas.

✔ Allowed as a learning aid

  • Brainstorming before you write
  • Clarifying an unfamiliar term
  • Asking for extra examples
  • Grammar and clarity checks on your own writing
  • Suggesting search terms

✘ Not allowed

  • Writing or rewriting what you submit
  • Generating discussion posts or replies
  • Answering Sacred Text reading questions
  • Summarizing readings instead of doing them
  • Any part of a quiz or exam

⁂ Sometimes required

Sacred Text assignments ask you to converse with an instructor-designed course chatbot — after your own reading and writing are done.

When an assignment permits or requires AI, its instructions override the general rule.

Do not assume that because AI is permitted in one activity it is permitted in another. If an assignment doesn't authorize it, do the work without it — or ask me first.

Delivery: Say this once, calmly. Resist the temptation to moralize — the policy is unusually reasonable and it argues for itself.

The honest version of the argument: AI can produce very convincing prose about religion. That does not mean it has read our textbook, sat in our classroom, or engaged the particular passage in front of you. The course values authentic learning, careful reading, and the development of your own analytical voice; AI should assist your thinking rather than replace it.

Two practical additions worth saying aloud. Disclosure: if permitted AI contributed to work you submit and it is not already built into the assignment, add a brief note — "AI assistance was used for brainstorming and grammar review." Routine use of the required course chatbot needs no separate disclosure unless the assignment asks. Verification: some assignments may require drafts, planning notes, reading responses, revision history, or a complete chatbot transcript. Altering or submitting an incomplete required transcript is itself an integrity issue.

Also worth one sentence: AI-generated information should be verified before you rely on it. In a course about seven traditions, confident-sounding errors about doctrine and history are extremely common.

The lens, defined

Worldview = your lens on reality

A worldview is the lens through which you interpret and act on the world.

  • Shaped by culture, upbringing, experience
  • Holds your beliefs, values, and assumptions together
  • No one gets a neutral or view-from-nowhere position

Glasses shape what you see. That does not make the world rose-colored.

Delivery: Lean on the lens metaphor and then immediately complicate it — you cannot take the glasses off, you can only learn what prescription you are wearing. That distinction does most of the work for the rest of the semester.

The point students miss: a worldview is not the same thing as a religion. A committed atheist holds one (philosophical naturalism) exactly as a committed Christian does (Christian theism). Neither seat in the room is the neutral seat — which is worth saying plainly and early, because roughly half the room assumes their own position is the default.

Say this: the goal is not to abandon your worldview. It is to become conscious enough of it to notice when it is doing the interpreting for you, and flexible enough to inhabit another one temporarily in order to understand it from inside.

Background — James W. Sire, The Universe Next Door: every worldview is an attempt to answer the same fundamental questions. Sire names seven (Christian theism, deism, naturalism, nihilism, existentialism, pantheistic monism, postmodernism), each answering differently on ultimate reality, human nature, death, and meaning. Mention only if a student pushes for a source.

The comparison framework for the whole semester

Every worldview answers these

  1. What is ultimate reality? (God, gods, cosmic principle, emptiness?)
  2. What is the origin and nature of the universe?
  3. What is the origin and nature of humanity?
  4. Does human life have a purpose?
  1. How do humans acquire knowledge?
  2. What is the basis of ethics and morality?
  3. What happens to humans after death?
Seven traditions. The same seven questions. That is how we compare without ranking.

Delivery: Do not read all seven aloud — read one and two, then let students scan. What matters is that they recognize this list when it reappears in every unit.

Question 1 is phrased deliberately. "Ultimate reality" rather than "God," because Buddhism and Daoism answer the question without a deity. Flagging the phrasing now heads off the assumption that every tradition is a variation on theism.

Worth naming: answers interlock. Question 2 — is the physical world real, or is it maya, as some Hindu philosophies hold? — changes what Questions 4 through 7 can even mean. Change one answer and others have to move with it. This is why cherry-picking a single belief out of a tradition distorts it.

Tie to the workshop: these seven are the questions the assignment is about to ask them, in plainer language, about themselves.

How this works

There is no “right” worldview here

You are not graded on having the correct one. The point is to identify the assumptions you already carry — including the ones you have never had to say out loud.

Why we do it in week one

In October, when we read the Qur'an or the Dao De Jing, this gives you something honest to compare against — instead of an invisible default you mistake for neutrality.

Worldview

A comprehensive framework of beliefs, values, and assumptions through which a person interprets all of reality. It includes positions on:

  • What exists (ontology)
  • How we know (epistemology)
  • What is right and wrong (ethics)
  • What it means to be human (anthropology)
  • Origins and purpose (cosmology)

Everyone holds one, whether or not they have ever put it into words.

Krister Stendahl

A Swedish New Testament scholar and bishop who taught at Harvard Divinity School. He is remembered for a set of rules for how to study someone else's religion fairly.

The one that governs this course: let a tradition be described by its own adherents rather than by its enemies — compare their best against your best, not their worst against your ideals.

It is why our documentaries feature practitioners, and why our readings are the traditions' own writings.

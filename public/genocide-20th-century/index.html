A Lecture Arc in Three Parts

Genocide in the
Twentieth Century

Genocide, crimes against humanity, and the problem of human evil

The century that gave us the word also gave us the thing it names, again and again. These three lectures begin with the Holocaust, widen to the pattern of mass killing from Armenia to Srebrenica, and end with the question every religious and ethical tradition has had to face since: what are human beings, that we do this to one another?

Seven cases studied across the arc. The list is not exhaustive; the century was not either.

IILecture Two

Century of Genocide

A crime without a name

Raphael Lemkin named the crime; this lecture follows it across the century — Armenia, the Holodomor, the Gulag and Pavel Florensky, Nanjing, Cambodia, Rwanda, Srebrenica — and asks who the perpetrators and the victims actually were. The required Pierian Spring extends to crimes against humanity: the Red Terror and Great Terror, the "comfort women," Unit 731, why death tolls differ, and how naming and denial work.

IIILecture Three

The Problem of Human Evil

God, evil, and human choice after the Holocaust

A hanging at Buna opens the question. Jewish and Christian thinkers answer it differently; Jedwabne and Le Chambon-sur-Lignon show what ordinary people chose when the choice was theirs; Kant's radical evil and Arendt's banality of evil give us two ways to name what we have seen. The arc ends not with an answer but with a responsibility.

The Word, Defined

Acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such. Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, Article II (1948)

Lemkin coined the word in 1944; the United Nations made it law in 1948. Much of the second lecture turns on what this definition includes, what it leaves out, and why the line matters to the people on either side of it.

Before You Begin

How to work through this arc

Read, then attend

The background essay for each lecture is written to be read before class. It carries the scholarship and the notes; the deck carries the argument. They are meant to be taken together.

Take the content notes seriously

These materials show and describe what happened. Nothing is included for shock, but nothing has been softened either. If you need to step away from a slide or a passage, do so and return.

Hold the religious question open

This is a World Religions course. The third lecture asks what Judaism, Christianity, and the philosophical tradition have said in the face of this history. Bring the first two lectures with you into that one.

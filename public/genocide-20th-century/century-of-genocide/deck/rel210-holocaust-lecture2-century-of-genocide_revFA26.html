Script

The twentieth century gave the world flight, antibiotics, and footprints on the moon. It also gave the world a new word. Again and again, in those hundred years, governments and the people who followed them set out to destroy whole groups of human beings. Armenians in the Ottoman Empire. Peasants in Soviet Ukraine. Prisoners in the Gulag. The people of Nanjing. Cambodians under the Khmer Rouge. Tutsi in Rwanda. The men and boys of Srebrenica. This is the century of genocide.

A word of care before we start. Some of what you will see and hear today is very hard: mass killing, starvation, and sexual violence. If you need to look away from a photograph, look away. You can still listen.

Script

On the morning of March 15, 1921, on Hardenbergstraße in the Charlottenburg district of Berlin, Germany, a twenty-four-year-old Armenian named Soghomon Tehlirian walked up behind a man out for a stroll and shot him once in the head.

The dead man was Mehmed Talaat Pasha. As interior minister of the Ottoman Empire, and later its grand vizier, Talaat had directed the destruction of the empire’s Armenians in 1915.

An Ottoman court-martial had sentenced him to death in absentia in July 1919, but he had fled to Germany and was living in Berlin under a false name. Tehlirian had lost his mother and most of his family in the deportations.

At his trial in June 1921 a German jury heard his testimony and the testimony of witnesses to the massacres, and on June 3 it acquitted him.

Script

In August 1941, while German shooting squads were murdering Jews by the tens of thousands in the occupied Soviet Union, Winston Churchill told a radio audience: “We are in the presence of a crime without a name.”

Lemkin gave it one. In Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, in 1944, he joined the Greek genos, people or tribe, to the Latin -cide, killing: “By ‘genocide’ we mean the destruction of a nation or of an ethnic group.”

Script

On December 9, 1948, meeting in Paris, France, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, one day before the Universal Declaration of Human Rights.

Lemkin’s word had become law. Its Article II is still the legal definition, and it is worth reading slowly, because every case today will be measured against it.

Definition · Genocide Convention, Article II (1948)

“genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such:

  1. Killing members of the group;
  2. Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group;
  3. Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part;
  4. Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group;
  5. Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.”

Two features of the definition

Genocide is defined by intent, and only four kinds of group are protected.

  • Intent, not numbers. Genocide is the aim to destroy a group “as such,” not a count of the dead.
  • A short list. National, ethnical, racial, religious. Political groups and social classes are not on it.
  • Early drafts included them; the Soviet delegation and several others objected, and they were dropped.
  • The law has a hole exactly where the Soviet and Cambodian killing of “class enemies” falls.

Two features of that definition matter for everything that follows.

First, genocide is defined by intent, the aim to destroy a group “as such,” and not by the number of dead.

Second, the list of protected groups is short. Political groups and social classes are not on it. Early drafts included them; the Soviet delegation and several others objected, and they were dropped.

Genocide vs. crimes against humanity

Genocide (Convention, 1948): acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such.

Crimes against humanity: the legal name for mass murder and persecution that fall outside that definition, for example the killing of political groups or social classes. The Nuremberg tribunal used the category in 1945–1946.

The difference is legal, not moral: a crime against humanity is not a lesser evil, only a different legal description.

Script

The Armenian Genocide was the destruction of the Ottoman Empire’s Armenian Christians by their own government in 1915 and 1916, during World War I. Armenians, an ancient Christian people, had lived on the Anatolian plateau for more than two thousand years, and some 1.5 to 2 million lived in this Muslim empire before 1914.

In 1908 a nationalist movement, the Committee of Union and Progress, known in Europe as the Young Turks, took power. By 1913 three men ran it: Talaat, a former telegraph clerk; the war minister, Enver Pasha; and the naval minister, Cemal Pasha. The empire entered World War I on Germany’s side in November 1914, and after a catastrophic defeat by Russia at Sarıkamış that winter, the leaders blamed the Armenians as a disloyal “internal enemy” and decided to remove them from Anatolia altogether.

Armenian men were rounded up and killed; women, children, and the elderly were driven on foot through the mountains toward the Syrian desert, where hundreds of thousands died of massacre, starvation, thirst, and exhaustion.

The map shows the scale: deportation routes and killing sites spread across the whole of Anatolia, all converging on the desert of Syria.

Script

The men first. Armenian men had been drafted into the Ottoman army. Early in 1915 they were disarmed and put into labor battalions, then killed in batches. In towns and villages the able-bodied men were rounded up, marched out of town, and shot or cut down.

Then the deportations. Women, children, and the elderly were ordered to leave on a few days’ notice, officially for “relocation” to the Syrian desert. The columns were marched for weeks and months through the mountains toward Aleppo and Deir ez-Zor, today in Syria. They were denied food and water, robbed by guards and local bands, and attacked again and again.

Women and girls were raped, abducted, or sold; children were taken into Muslim households and converted. Many massacres were carried out by the Special Organization, irregular killing squads that included convicts released from prison for the purpose. In 1916 a second wave of killing destroyed most of those who had survived the marches to the camps around Deir ez-Zor.

On the plain of Muş in eastern Anatolia, in the summer of 1915, villagers were herded into barns and stables and burned alive.

Let us gather up what was done to the Armenians. The men were taken out of their towns and shot, bayoneted, or cut down with axes and knives. The figure: range and source

In 1932 the state set grain quotas the republic could not possibly meet, and a law of August 7, 1932, made theft of collective property punishable by death or ten years in the camps. The Gulag was the Soviet Union’s system of forced-labor camps. 1. The Red Terror, 1918–1922

On the night of July 16–17, 1918, in the cellar of the Ipatiev House in Yekaterinburg, Russia, a Bolshevik firing squad shot the deposed tsar Nicholas II; his wife, Alexandra; their daughters Olga, Tatiana, Maria, and Anastasia, aged twenty-two down to seventeen; their thirteen-year-old son, Alexei; and four servants who had stayed with them. Bullets glanced off the jewels the girls had sewn into their clothing, and the guards finished them with bayonets. To the men who killed them they were not a family. They were a class to be erased.

Lenin and the Bolsheviks had seized power in Petrograd, Russia, in November 1917 (October by the old Russian calendar). Within weeks they created a political police, the Cheka, under Felix Dzerzhinsky. Lenin did not hide his methods. On August 11, 1918, he telegraphed the Bolsheviks of Penza, Russia, where peasants had risen against grain seizures: “You need to hang (hang without fail, so that the public sees) at least 100 notorious kulaks, the rich, and the bloodsuckers.” He added: “P.S. Use your toughest people for this.”

On August 30, 1918, Fanny Kaplan shot and wounded Lenin, and on the same day the head of the Petrograd Cheka was assassinated. On September 5 the Soviet government issued its decree “On the Red Terror,” which ordered “class enemies” isolated in concentration camps and anyone connected with White Guard organizations, conspiracies, and uprisings shot. Hostages were taken and shot in batches. Prisoners were killed in prison cellars and courtyards, usually with a single shot to the back of the head, or trucked out of town, bound and gagged, to be shot beside pits they were sometimes made to dig. Anti-Bolshevik investigators and émigré writers, such as Sergei Melgunov in The Red Terror in Russia (1924), recorded torture as well: people beaten, burned, scalped, and buried alive. Those sources were partisan, but the Cheka’s cruelty is abundantly documented in Soviet records too.

How many died? The Cheka’s own published count of executions for 1918–1920 was under 13,000, a figure no historian accepts. Modern estimates of executions by the Cheka and revolutionary tribunals in 1918–1922 run from about 50,000 to about 140,000, and to 200,000 or more when the crushing of peasant revolts is included.

2. The Great Terror and the Soviet total

On July 30, 1937, the head of the secret police (by then the NKVD), Nikolai Yezhov, signed Order No. 00447. It gave each region of the Soviet Union a quota: so many people to be shot, so many to be sent to the camps. Local chiefs asked for their quotas to be raised, and Moscow raised them. NKVD records count 681,692 people shot in 1937–1938, almost a thousand a day for two years, among about 1.5 million arrested. Old Bolsheviks confessed to absurd crimes at show trials. The Red Army lost most of its senior commanders.

The poet Osip Mandelstam, who in 1933 had written a short poem mocking Stalin and read it to a few friends, was arrested in 1934 and again in 1938; he died that December in a transit camp near Vladivostok, Russia. At least one NKVD officer in the Moscow region, Isai Berg, used a truck that piped its exhaust into a sealed compartment to kill prisoners on the way to execution, an echo of the gas vans the Germans would use in 1941.

A total often given for deaths in the USSR is 20 million. That is the figure of the historian Robert Conquest, also used in The Black Book of Communism (1997). Scholarly estimates depend on what is counted. Deliberate killing (executions, deaths in the camps and in deportation, and the famines) yields figures from about 6 to 10 million; counting the wider excess mortality caused by Soviet policy brings totals to 15 or 20 million. Nicolas Werth, who wrote the Black Book’s Soviet chapter, preferred about 15 million. On any count, the dead number in the millions.

3. All communist regimes

A death toll often given for the communist regimes of the twentieth century, from Lenin and Stalin to Mao Zedong and Pol Pot, is 100 million. 4. Imperial Japanese Atrocities: the “comfort women”

On August 14, 1991, a sixty-seven-year-old woman named Kim Hak-sun called a press conference in Seoul, South Korea. In 1941, she said, when she was seventeen, Japanese soldiers had seized her in China and forced her into a military brothel, where she was raped by soldier after soldier, day after day, until she escaped. She was the first survivor to speak publicly under her own name. Hundreds of women across Asia followed her: from Korea, China, the Philippines, Taiwan, Indonesia, and the Netherlands. A Dutch woman, Jan Ruff-O’Herne, taken from an internment camp on Java (then the Dutch East Indies, today Indonesia) at twenty-one, testified in Tokyo in December 1992.

The Japanese military set up its system of so-called “comfort stations” in the early 1930s and spread it across the empire after Nanjing, partly in the hope that controlled brothels would prevent mass rapes like Nanjing’s from stirring local resistance. Women and girls were recruited by deception, bought, or simply seized. Estimates of their number range from about 20,000 to 200,000; many did not survive the war. In 1992 the Japanese historian Yoshimi Yoshiaki published military documents showing the army’s direct role, and in 1993 the Japanese government’s Kōno Statement acknowledged it. Since 2018 South Korea has marked August 14 as a national day of remembrance. Kim Hak-sun died in 1997.

5. Imperial Japanese Atrocities: Unit 731

At Pingfang, outside Harbin in Manchukuo, the Japanese army built a vast walled compound, officially an “epidemic prevention and water purification” unit, under the army doctor Ishii Shirō. Unit 731 was the center of Japan’s biological-warfare program. Its scientists infected prisoners, Chinese, Koreans, Mongols, and Russians, with plague, anthrax, cholera, and other diseases, then cut them open, often alive and without anesthesia, to watch the disease at work. They froze prisoners’ limbs to study frostbite. In the unit’s language the prisoners were maruta, “logs.” An estimated 3,000 people were killed in experiments at Pingfang alone, and none of the prisoners there survived. Outside the compound, Unit 731 and related units dropped plague-infected fleas on Chinese cities, among them Ningbo in 1940 and Changde in 1941, killing thousands more; total deaths from these field attacks are unknown and disputed.

Some of the people who did this were very young. Shinozuka Yoshio joined Unit 731’s youth corps in 1939, at about sixteen. He later testified that he had helped prepare a prisoner infected with plague for vivisection, scrubbing the man’s body before the surgeons cut into him. After years in Chinese detention he came home to Japan and spent much of the rest of his life speaking publicly about what he had done.

Ishii and his senior scientists were never tried. In 1947–1948 American authorities granted them immunity in exchange for their data. At Nuremberg, in 1946–1947, the Americans tried Nazi doctors for human experiments; the doctors of Unit 731 walked free, and several went on to prominent careers in medicine and industry. The Soviet Union tried twelve members of the unit at Khabarovsk, USSR, in December 1949, and all had been sent home to Japan by 1956.

6. Counting the dead: why the numbers differ

Every figure in this lecture is an estimate, and students will meet very different numbers elsewhere. The reasons are worth knowing.

Records were destroyed or never kept. Perpetrators hide their crimes. The Khmer Rouge kept meticulous files at S-21 but almost none in the countryside. Ottoman deportation records are incomplete. The Japanese army burned documents in August 1945.

Documents versus demography. Historians count either documented killings, which undercounts, or the gap between the population before and after, adjusted for births and normal deaths, which depends on uncertain census data. The Holodomor figure of 3.9 million is a demographic estimate; the 681,692 executions of 1937–1938 is a documentary count.

Definitions. Does a famine death count as a killing? A soldier shot after surrendering? A deportee who dies in exile years later? The figures of 20 million Soviet dead and 100 million for all communist regimes depend heavily on such choices.

Politics. Death tolls are weapons in present-day arguments. Turkey minimizes the Armenian figures; Chinese official history fixes Nanjing at 300,000; Japanese nationalists deny it; communist totals have been both inflated and minimized. An honest account gives a range and says where it comes from.

7. Naming and denying

Every case in this lecture has its deniers. Turkey rejects the word genocide for the Armenians. Soviet officials denied the famine while it was happening, and Russian official history still resists the Ukrainian interpretation. Denial, the genocide scholar Gregory Stanton argues, is the final stage of genocide, because it continues the attack on the victims’ memory. Behind several of these disputes lies the drafting of 1948: the definition was narrowed to exclude political groups, partly at Soviet insistence, and the crimes of communist regimes fell largely outside it.

8. Justice and its failures

Compare the endings. Nuremberg tried the surviving Nazi leaders in 1945–1946 and the Nazi doctors in 1946–1947. Talaat was never tried by a court that could hold him; a survivor killed him. The Holodomor: Lemkin said genocide; historians still argue

Lemkin himself had no doubt. Speaking in New York in 1953, he said: “This is not simply a case of mass murder. It is a case of genocide, of destruction, not of individuals only, but of a culture and a nation.” In his view the famine was one blow in a wider assault on Ukraine as a nation, alongside the destruction of its intellectuals and its church.

Historians agree that Stalin’s policies caused the famine, that Ukraine was treated more harshly than other regions, and that the regime knew people were starving and stopped them from fleeing. They disagree about intent. Robert Conquest, Andrea Graziosi, Timothy Snyder, and many Ukrainian historians argue that Stalin used hunger deliberately to break the Ukrainian peasantry and Ukrainian national life, which meets the Convention’s “conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction.” R. W. Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft argue that the famine came from brutal, reckless policy pursued for the sake of industrialization, not from a plan to destroy Ukrainians as such. Anne Applebaum concludes that it fits Lemkin’s broader idea of genocide, the destruction of a nation, even where specific legal intent is harder to prove.

Ukraine recognized the Holodomor as genocide in 2006, the U.S. Senate by resolution in 2018, and Germany’s Bundestag and the European Parliament in 2022. The case shows how much rests on one word in the definition: intent. Nobody disputes the corpses. The argument is over what was in Stalin’s mind.

Nanjing, 1937–1938

Most scholars do not class Nanjing as genocide. The Japanese army did not set out to destroy the Chinese people “as such”; it murdered prisoners, raped and slaughtered civilians, and terrorized a captured capital to break China’s will to fight. Those are war crimes and crimes against humanity, and that is how the Tokyo tribunal judged them: it convicted the commanding general, Matsui Iwane, of failing to prevent the atrocities, and he was hanged on December 23, 1948.

Iris Chang’s best-selling book called Nanjing “the forgotten holocaust”; most historians prefer other words, not because the dead matter less, but because words are tools for understanding. “Not genocide” does not mean “less evil.” A girl raped and bayoneted in Nanjing suffered no less than a girl murdered for her ethnicity in Rwanda. The difference is in the intent of the killers, not in the worth of the victims.

Script

Most scholars do not class Nanjing as genocide. The Japanese army did not set out to destroy the Chinese people “as such”; it murdered prisoners, raped and slaughtered civilians, and terrorized a captured capital to break China’s will to fight. Those are war crimes and crimes against humanity, and that is how the Tokyo tribunal judged them: it convicted the commanding general, Matsui Iwane, of failing to prevent the atrocities, and he was hanged on December 23, 1948.

Iris Chang’s best-selling book called Nanjing “the forgotten holocaust”; most historians prefer other words, not because the dead matter less, but because words are tools for understanding. “Not genocide” does not mean “less evil.” A girl raped and bayoneted in Nanjing suffered no less than a girl murdered for her ethnicity in Rwanda. The difference is in the intent of the killers, not in the worth of the victims.

Cambodia, 1975–1979: the political-groups gap

Most of the Khmer Rouge’s victims were Khmer killed by Khmer: “new people,” “class enemies,” supposed traitors. That killing falls into the hole left in 1948, when political groups and social classes were dropped from the Convention; some scholars call it “autogenocide.”

Nuon Chea died in 2019 while his appeal was pending; Khieu Samphan’s conviction was upheld in 2022.

Rwanda, 1994: the textbook case

Rwanda is the case the Convention was written for, after the Holocaust. The killers said what they meant: the radio urged Hutu listeners to kill the “cockroaches,” and the victims were selected by the ethnic label on their identity cards. Intent was not hidden. Yet in Washington and at the UN, officials avoided the word “genocide” for weeks, because to name it might create an obligation to act.

The law came afterward. On September 2, 1998, the International Criminal Tribunal for Rwanda convicted Jean-Paul Akayesu, the mayor of the commune of Taba, of genocide. It was the first conviction for genocide by an international court, fifty years after the Convention, and the first judgment to hold that rape could be an act of genocide. Akayesu had been a schoolteacher and school inspector before he became mayor. For the first days of the killing he kept the militias out of Taba and protected its Tutsi; after a meeting with leaders of the interim government on April 18, 1994, he changed sides and began urging his neighbors to kill.

Why do you believe what you believe? Not the small things—not your opinion about a film or a candidate or a restaurant. The load-bearing beliefs. The ones underneath everything else: whether the universe has a purpose, whether you have a self, whether anything you do finally matters. If your entire belief system were projected onto a canvas and hung on a wall, would it hold together? Or would it look like a surrealist painting, full of quiet impossibilities you never noticed because you were standing inside them?

The Working Assumption: One Reality

Every argument on this page rests on one claim, and honesty requires calling it what it is: an assumption before it is a conclusion. There is one world—one reality that encompasses all of us. It exists as it is, regardless of what any of us believe about it, and it does not bend itself to suit our ideas.

"Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away." — Philip K. Dick

That assumption has been challenged, and challenged seriously—which is why this unit tests it rather than merely asserting it. But notice what follows if it holds: our world is a grand historical artifact handed to each of us, and it has one true explanation. One true set of answers. The question this page equips you to pursue is whether we can find them—and how we would know if we had.

What a Worldview Is

Dr. Kenneth Funk of Oregon State University defines a worldview as the set of beliefs about the fundamental aspects of reality that ground and influence everything a person perceives, thinks, knows, and does. The word doing the work in that definition is everything. Not your religious beliefs. Not your political beliefs. Everything: what you notice when you walk into a room, what strikes you as obviously true and what strikes you as absurd, what you feel guilty about, who you find it easy to dismiss. In plainer language, a worldview is your philosophy of life—and you have one whether or not you have ever sat down and thought it through. The unexamined worldview is not an absent worldview. It is simply one you have not yet met.

Walsh and Middleton, in The Transforming Vision, add a dimension worth holding onto: a worldview is never merely a vision of life. It is always a vision for life. A set of beliefs that does not actually lead a person into a particular way of living is not a worldview at all—it is a hobby. This is the sharpest diagnostic tool on this page, and it cuts: your real worldview is not the one you would write down on an exam. It is the one your calendar, your bank statement, and your reflexes would testify to under oath. Beliefs that cost you nothing and change nothing are not beliefs. They are decorations.

Why "Calibration"?

Think of your worldview as a lens. You never look at it—you look through it, at everything. An accurate worldview does what good glass does: it brings what can be known about reality into focus and delivers a whole, coherent image. An inaccurate one still delivers an image—confidently, continuously—and that image does not match what is in front of it.

But notice something about real instruments: you do not fix a lens once and call it fixed. You calibrate it. You put it on a bench, aim it at a known standard, and measure how far the image departs from the thing itself—repeatedly, across the working life of the instrument, because instruments drift. So do belief systems. The difficulty is that your images do not form on a wall where you can inspect them. They form out of sight, inside your own mind. That is why this unit exists: the questions below are the known standard, the three tests are the measurements, and the two assignments at the bottom of this page are the bench.

The Questions Every Worldview Answers

Every worldview, examined or not, answers the same short list of questions. These are not idle questions—each one carries stakes for how you live. And if you think you don't have answers, you do: even I don't know is an answer, and it cannot be known is a rather substantial one. The seven standard questions come first; the last two are additions this unit makes to the standard list, and the final pair may be the most revealing of all.

God

Is there a God or higher power? If so, what is that being like?

The answer that most strongly colors every answer after it.

Origins

What is the origin and nature of the universe? How did the world begin, and what is it made of—purely material, physical and spiritual together, or is the whole thing an illusion?

Humanity

What is the origin and nature of human beings? Created, or evolved? An animal among animals, or something higher? One with the universe, or a distinct component inside it?

Your answer here determines what you think a person is owed.

Purpose

Does human life have a purpose? If it does, what is it—and who assigned it?

Your answer here determines what you think a wasted life looks like.

Knowledge

How do we acquire knowledge at all? Through the senses? Through science? Through divine revelation? Or is truth relative to the culture that happens to be holding it?

Morality

What is the basis of ethics? Individual preference? Social convention? The character of a divine being? Or is there finally no distinction between good and evil—only distinctions we have agreed to enforce?

Death

What happens to us afterward?

Added in this unit

Agency

Are you actually the author of your own actions—or the last domino in a very long line?

Added in this unit

Diagnosis

What is wrong with the world—and what would fix it?

Answer this pair honestly and you will have described your worldview more completely than any of the others.

💡 Surfacing Your Basic Beliefs: The "Why" Drill

To find your own answers, keep asking why until you hit rock bottom. Why do I go to school? To get a degree. Why do I want a degree? To get a good job. Why a good job? To make money. Why money? To buy things I want. Why? To be happy.

Most people stop there and call it a basic belief: happiness is a priority for me. But that is a preference, not yet a belief about reality. So push once more: why is happiness worth prioritizing? What is it about being human that makes flourishing the right target? That question is two and a half thousand years old—and it has your name on it. You'll need this drill for the first assignment below.

Criteria That Cannot Do the Job

A glance around the globe reveals a great many takes on reality, and a striking number of them flatly oppose one another. If there is only one reality, how are so many people missing it? Part of the answer: most of us select our worldview using criteria that cannot possibly do the job. Instinct—it seems true to me. Feeling—it has emotional appeal. Wish fulfillment—I want it to be true. Every one of these is a real reason people believe real things, and every one will deliver you into literally any worldview on offer, because they measure the believer rather than the belief. They report the state of your appetite. They report nothing about the state of the world.

And there is one more, harder to see: the filter you forgot you screwed on. Every image you have ever formed has passed through the culture you happened to be born into, tinting everything uniformly—which is exactly why you stopped noticing it. Uniform error is the hardest kind to detect. What we need instead are criteria that watch how reality itself operates. Three of them do.

The Three Tests

Each test below is paired with the optical failure it corresponds to, because every one of these failures has a name in lens design—and each one teaches you something about how a belief system goes wrong. You will apply all three to your own worldview map in the reflection assignment.

1

Logical Consistency

Optical failure: ghosting

Aristotle called the law of non-contradiction the firmest of all principles: contradictory statements cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time. If I say God exists and you say God does not, we can both be sincere and both be decent people—but we cannot both be right.

In a lens, this failure is called ghosting: light reflects off an internal surface and the instrument returns two images of one subject, superimposed, both apparently solid. It is not confused and it is not hedging—it is confidently reporting two of something where there is one. Watch especially for self-defeating claims: "there are no absolutes" is itself an absolute and dies by its own standard. "All truth is relative to culture"—is that true across all cultures? "You should never impose your morality on anyone"—should I?

The question to ask: do my beliefs contradict each other?

2

Coherency

Optical failure: decentering

Consistency asks whether your beliefs fight each other. Coherency asks whether they know each other at all. Beliefs must be logically interconnected—one story running through your answers—not a random assortment of claims with a different narrator every time the subject changes.

The optical analogue is the subtlest failure a lens can have: decentering. A decentered lens has no defective part—every element is ground correctly, every element polished correctly. They simply are not sharing a common axis, and the image comes out sharp in one corner and soft in the opposite one with nothing you can point to and call broken. The failure does not live in any component. It lives in the relationships between them.

The question to ask: does the human nature I described actually support the morality I claimed? Does my theory of knowledge permit me to know what I said I know?

3

Correspondence with Reality

Optical measure: resolving power

The hardest test. A worldview can pass both earlier tests—perfectly consistent, beautifully coherent—and still not be fine enough for the world. In optics this is resolving power: point a poor lens at a double star and it returns a single smear. The lens is not lying. It is simply not fine enough to report what is there.

So ask what your worldview can actually resolve: the physical sciences, the social sciences, art, religion, grief. Seek the explanation that fits the most data with the least conflict.

The question to ask: what parts of human experience does my worldview render as a smear?

⚠️ The Fourth Failure: Flare

Bias has an optical name too. Flare is a bright source outside the frame—one you never pointed the instrument at and cannot see in the image—washing across everything and lowering contrast everywhere at once. You do not observe the source. You only observe its effect on every part of the picture. Pride and bias work the same way, and they grow back after every trimming. So ask the questions that hurt: Who am I to say what is true? Have my eyes probed the borders of the universe? Have they seen beyond the grave? Reality is not negotiating—which means we are the ones who move, willing to surrender even our most cherished beliefs in favor of what is true.

Where This Framework Comes From

Two disclosures, because a page that asks you to examine your commitments should be transparent about its own. First: when this unit says truth is conformity with reality, it is operating inside the correspondence theory of truth—one contested position among several. Philosophers have defended coherence theories and pragmatic theories seriously and at length; the Stanford Encyclopedia link below will take you into that debate. Correspondence is used here because it is the theory under which the other two tests do real work—and because it is the one that permits you to be wrong.

Second: the framework itself—the questions and the tests—is not neutral ground handed down from nowhere. It comes out of a specific twentieth-century tradition, largely a Christian one, running through writers like Walsh and Middleton, James Sire, and Ronald Nash. It is used here because it is clear, teachable, and applies to any worldview you care to run through it, including the ones its authors were arguing against. But you should know its address. A tool that hides where it was made is asking you to trust it on instinct—and instinct, as we established above, is not a valid criterion.

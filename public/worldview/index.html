Why do you believe what you believe? How do you know what you know? If your belief system could be projected onto a canvas, would it resemble a surrealist painting—full of incongruences, distortions, and impossibilities? This page is a basic introduction to worldview: what one is, what kinds of questions a worldview attempts to answer, and how to evaluate—and possibly revamp—your own.

Start with a simple observation: there is one world, and one reality that encompasses us all. That reality exists as it is, regardless of what any of us believe about it. It does not bend itself to suit our ideas.

"Reality is that which, when you stop believing in it, doesn't go away." — Philip K. Dick

Our world is the grand historical artifact presented to each of us, and it has one true explanation—one true set of answers. The question is whether those answers can be found, and how we would recognize them if we did. This page will be most helpful to those willing to undergo a few moments of critical self-examination and ask: What if I don't see life as it truly is? What if my beliefs don't stack up to reality?

What Is a Worldview?

Dr. Kenneth Funk of Oregon State University describes a worldview as the set of beliefs about the fundamental aspects of reality that ground and influence everything a person perceives, thinks, knows, and does. In other words, it is your philosophy of life—and it plays an enormous role in your life whether you have taken the time to think it through or not. Walsh and Middleton, in The Transforming Vision, add an important dimension: a worldview is never merely a vision of life—it is always a vision for life. A set of beliefs that does not actually lead a person in a particular way of life is no worldview at all.

Think of your worldview—the sum total of your basic beliefs—as a lens you view and live all of life through. An accurate worldview, like a working camera lens, brings what can be known about reality into focus: a whole, coherent picture of the world as it truly is. Inaccurate worldviews, like defective lenses, produce varying degrees of distortion—sending images to our minds that do not match reality. The difficulty is that these are not physical images we can scour for defects. They exist out of sight, within our minds.

The Questions Every Worldview Answers

God

Is there a God or higher power? If so, what is that being like?

Origins

What is the origin and nature of the universe? How did the world begin, and what does it consist of—purely material, physical mixed with spiritual, or illusory?

Humanity

What is the origin and nature of humankind? Created or evolved? An animal, or a higher being? One with the universe, or a component within it?

Purpose

Does human life have purpose? If so, what is it?

Knowledge

How do we acquire knowledge? Through the senses? Science? Divine revelation? Or is truth relative to one's culture?

Morality

What is the basis of ethics and morality—individual preference, societal convention, the character of a divine being, or is there no distinction between good and evil at all?

Death

What happens to us after death?

💡 Find Your Basic Beliefs: The "Why" Exercise

Everyone has answers to these questions—even if the answer is "I don't know" or "it cannot be known." To surface your own basic beliefs, keep asking yourself why until you hit rock bottom. Why do I go to school? To get a degree. Why do I want a degree? To get a good job. Why do I want a good job? To make money. Why do I want money? To buy things I want. Why? To be happy. Why do I want to be happy? When you can't answer anymore, you've found a basic belief—in this case: happiness is a priority for me.

Three Trademarks of Truth

A glance around the globe reveals many different takes on reality, many directly opposed to others. With only one objective reality, how can so many people be missing the mark? Truth is conformity with reality—and discerning what is true requires measuring truth claims against a valid standard. Instinct ("it seems true to me"), feelings ("it has emotional appeal"), and wish fulfillment ("I want it to be true") can land a person in any worldview—they are invalid criteria. Valid criteria observe how reality itself operates. Three stand out:

1

Logical Consistency

Aristotle's law of non-contradiction: contradictory statements cannot both be true at the same time. If you say God exists and I say God does not, we cannot both be right. A worldview's beliefs must not contradict one another—and beware of self-defeating claims like "there are no absolutes," which is itself an absolute statement and dies by its own standard.

2

Coherency

Beliefs must be logically interconnected, not a random assortment of claims. Do your individual beliefs cohere into one single vision of life, from origins all the way to what happens after death? Is there a single theme running through your story from beginning to end, or does the story change from answer to answer?

3

Correspondence with Reality

A worldview can be internally consistent and coherent yet still fail to fit the facts. How well does your worldview account for the full scope of human experience—the physical sciences, social sciences, aesthetics, and religion? Seek the explanation that fits the most data with the least conflict, and be willing to suspend your own bias long enough to evaluate it honestly.

A note on humility: developing an accurate worldview is not child's play. It involves a lifetime of disciplined, systematic scrutiny of one's beliefs—testing conclusions against incoming data, beating down personal bias and pride, and asking hard questions: Who am I to say what is true? Have my eyes probed the borders of the universe? Have they seen beyond the grave? Our perceptions won't change even the smallest facet of reality. We must be the ones to change—willing to surrender even our most cherished beliefs in favor of truth, no matter where it may take us.

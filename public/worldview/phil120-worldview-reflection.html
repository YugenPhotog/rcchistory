Your Reflection

Answer all three questions in your own words. These are about the process, not about ethics theory, and there are no wrong answers — only thin ones. "Nothing surprised me" is an acceptable answer only if you can explain why, in detail.

1. What surprised you?

Did the process of mapping your worldview reveal anything you hadn't thought about before? A belief you didn't know you held? A connection you'd never made? A tension you'd been quietly avoiding?

One paragraph minimum. Be concrete about what changed, not just that something did.

0 words

2. What was hardest?

Which layer was most difficult to articulate — what's real, how you know, what humans are, or what matters? Why that one? Was it hard because you don't know what you think, or because you do know and it's uncomfortable to say?

One paragraph minimum. Name the specific layer and the specific difficulty.

0 words

3. How did the map help?

Did seeing your worldview visually organized change how you understand your own thinking? If the map got something wrong about you, say so — that's a real finding, and explaining the mismatch counts.

One paragraph minimum. Point to something specific in the map you generated.

0 words

What Makes a Strong Reflection

  • Specific over general. "I realized my epistemology and my axiology don't agree about authority" beats "this made me think a lot."
  • Honest over impressive. Confusion described precisely is worth more than confidence performed vaguely.
  • About your thinking, not about the software. I don't need a review of NotebookLM. I need to know what happened in your head.
  • Willing to name discomfort. The most valuable sentence in this assignment is usually the one a student almost deleted.

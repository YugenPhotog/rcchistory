Purpose: Why Start Here?

Before we can think clearly about ethics, we need to understand how we already think—often without realizing it.

Every ethical judgment you make sits inside a larger framework of assumptions about reality, knowledge, human nature, and value. Philosophers call this a worldview. It's your mental operating system, running silently in the background, shaping what seems obvious, what seems questionable, and what seems impossible.

Here's the problem: worldviews are nearly invisible to the people who hold them. They feel like "just how things are" rather than one way of seeing things. This creates a serious obstacle to genuine ethical thinking. If you can't see your own assumptions, you can't examine them, and unexamined assumptions control your conclusions.

This assignment asks you to do something difficult: make your worldview visible. You'll identify the core beliefs and commitments that shape your moral intuitions, see how they connect to each other, and discover where they might be in tension. Only after this work can we productively engage the ethical theories and dilemmas we'll study this semester.

What you'll create: A visual map of your worldview built from written reflections on four fundamental questions. You'll write honest responses about what's real, how you know what's true, what humans are, and what matters, then use NotebookLM to generate a mind map that shows how those beliefs fit together.

STEP 1Answer the Reflection Questions

Answer in your own words in the boxes below. Write at least one full paragraph for each—more if you want the room. Be honest and be specific. The bullet points under each layer are prompts to get you started, not a checklist you must answer one by one.

Layer 1: What's Real? (The Foundation)

Philosophers call this "metaphysics" — your beliefs about the nature of reality

This is about what you think actually exists in the universe.

Prompts to consider:

  • Is there a God? Multiple gods? No god?
  • Is there anything beyond the physical world (souls, spirits, an afterlife)?
  • Is the universe just matter and energy following physical laws, or is there something more?
  • Do things like "justice" or "love" really exist, or are they just concepts humans made up?

Explain your position on what is fundamentally real. One paragraph minimum.

0 words

Layer 2: How Do I Know What's True? (Your Evidence Rules)

Philosophers call this "epistemology" — your theory of knowledge and justification

This is about what you trust when you're trying to figure out if something is true.

Prompts to consider:

  • Do you trust science and observation most?
  • Do you trust religious texts or teachings?
  • Do you trust your own reasoning and logic?
  • Do you trust your gut feelings or intuitions?
  • Do you trust what authorities (parents, teachers, religious leaders) tell you?
  • Do you trust tradition — "this is what people have always believed"?

Most people trust a combination of these, but usually rank them differently.

Explain which sources of knowledge matter most to you, in what order, and why. One paragraph minimum.

0 words

Layer 3: What Are Humans? (Your View of People)

Philosophers call this "philosophical anthropology" — your understanding of human nature

This is about what you think human beings fundamentally are.

Prompts to consider:

  • Are humans basically good, basically bad, or neutral?
  • Are we just very smart animals, or something more?
  • Do we have souls? Free will? A purpose we're supposed to fulfill?
  • What makes humans different from (or the same as) other animals?
  • What's most important about being human — our ability to reason? Our capacity to love? Our creativity? Our survival instincts?

Explain what you think humans are and what makes us matter. One paragraph minimum.

0 words

Layer 4: What Matters? (Your Values Base)

Philosophers call this "axiology" — your theory of value, including ethics

This is about what you think has value or importance.

Prompts to consider:

  • What makes life meaningful? (Relationships? Achievement? Happiness? Serving others? Serving God? Creating things? Learning?)
  • What do you think is most important in life?
  • Where do you think right and wrong come from? (God? Human reason? Culture? Evolution? Social agreement?)
  • What do you value most? (Freedom? Fairness? Loyalty? Compassion? Order? Progress?)

Identify what you value most and where you think moral rules come from. One paragraph minimum.

Connections & Tensions

This is the part that turns four separate answers into a worldview.

Explain at least one important connection among your beliefs and identify at least one place where your beliefs may not fit together perfectly.

  • A connection sounds like: "Because I believe X about reality, I therefore believe Y about knowledge, human nature, or value."
  • A tension is a place where two of your own beliefs pull against each other. Find a real one; a manufactured tension is easy to spot.

One paragraph minimum, covering both. Write more if you need the room.

What I'm Looking For

✓ Honest, thoughtful responses — not just what you think sounds good

✓ Specificity — actual beliefs, not vague platitudes like "I believe in being a good person"

✓ Clear connections between layers — show how your beliefs fit together

✓ A genuine tension — not one invented to check a box

✓ Evidence of real thinking in your final reflection

What I'm NOT Looking For

✗ The "right" worldview — there's no grade for having beliefs I agree with

✗ Perfect consistency — nobody's worldview is perfectly consistent

✗ Length for its own sake — one sharp paragraph beats three padded ones

✗ A "pretty" mind map — NotebookLM handles that; I'm grading your thinking

✗ Certainty about everything — "I'm not sure" and "I'm still figuring this out" are legitimate answers

Tips for Success

  • Be specific. NotebookLM can only map what you give it. If your writing is vague, the map will be too.
  • Use examples. If you say you value compassion, describe what that actually looks like in practice.
  • Don't perform. Write about the worldview you actually have, not the one you wish you had or think you should have.
  • The map is a tool, not the assignment. I'm grading your reflections and your thinking.
  • Write in one sitting if you can. Momentum helps on questions like these. But the page saves your work if you need to step away.

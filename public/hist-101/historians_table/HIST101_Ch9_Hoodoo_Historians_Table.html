HIST 101 | Chapter 9: Hoodoo and Rootwork

The Historian's Table

Was Hoodoo a Source of Power and Resistance for the Enslaved — or Primarily a Source of Vulnerability and Division?

Historical Question

To what extent did hoodoo and rootwork function as a source of power, protection, and resistance for enslaved people in the antebellum South — and to what extent did the evidence complicate or undercut that picture?

What You Are Doing

Historians do more than collect facts. They examine evidence, develop interpretations, listen to other historians, test their ideas, and decide what conclusions the evidence can support.

That is what you will do in this assignment.

Evidence → Your First Interpretation → Historians → Clio → Your Final Judgment

You will encounter the evidence — five firsthand accounts from enslaved people — before you encounter the secondary scholarship. This is intentional. Your first job is to decide what you think the evidence suggests.

Your responses do not need to sound like a formal essay until Step 5. What matters is that they show how your thinking develops as you encounter evidence, scholarship, and another voice at the table.

A note on the sources: The primary sources include dialect transcriptions, racial slurs, and language that is offensive by today's standards, presented unaltered because changing it would distort the historical record and the speakers' voices. Approach these texts with care and respect. Our goal is to analyze how language, power, and belief operated under slavery — not to reproduce harm. In discussion or notes, you may reference slurs indirectly (e.g., "the N-word") while keeping the written source text unchanged in your citations.

Step 1: Examine the Evidence

Begin with the five assigned primary sources, drawn from the packet Hoodoo in the Words of Enslaved People. Do not read the historiography reader yet.

For each source, use the worksheet to record notes on:

  1. How conjure was practiced — materials, rituals, objects.
  2. How the speaker viewed hoodoo — real, fraudulent, protective, dangerous.
  3. What community roles appear — conjure doctors, skeptics, and so on.
  4. How Christian belief and hoodoo overlap, conflict, or coexist in this account.
  5. What themes — resistance, protection, survival — connect to this source.

You might notice something about power, vulnerability, community trust, or gendered roles. Do not force every source into the same interpretation.

At this stage, there is no answer you are supposed to discover. Read the accounts and notice what seems historically important.

Primary Sources

Step 2: Make Your First Interpretation

After examining all five primary sources, pause before reading the historiography reader.

In the worksheet, answer in about 2–3 sentences:

Based only on the primary sources, does hoodoo look like a meaningful form of power and resistance for enslaved people, or more like a belief that offered little real protection and sometimes caused harm within the community? Why or why not?

This is your first interpretation. It is not supposed to be perfect. You will have a chance to reconsider it as other voices join the conversation.

Step 3: Invite the Historians to the Table

Now read Historians, Hoodoo, and the Question of Power, a historiography reader tracing how scholars — from Puckett and Hurston through Blassingame, Genovese, Levine, Raboteau, Rucker, Fett, Chireau, Anderson, and others — have argued about conjure and resistance over the past century.

Then answer three short questions in the worksheet:

  1. Which historian makes the strongest case that hoodoo functioned as a form of power, protection, or resistance — and what is that argument?
  2. Which historian raises the strongest caution or complication — a place where the reader shows conjure dividing the community, failing its believers, or serving purposes other than resistance?
  3. What did the historiography make you notice, reconsider, or question about your first interpretation?

You do not have to side with any one historian. Each interpretation is an additional voice at the table — and the historians disagree with each other as much as they might disagree with you.

Step 4: Invite Clio to the Table

Who is Clio?

Clio is our course AI participant. Clio has been provided with the same primary and secondary sources you are using. Think of Clio as another person sitting at the Historian's Table. Clio can ask questions, challenge your reasoning, point toward evidence you may have overlooked, and offer another interpretation.

Clio is not an answer key, and Clio may be wrong. Your job is to decide whether Clio's arguments actually fit the evidence.

Before opening Clio:

  1. Finish Steps 1–3 in your Historian's Table worksheet.
  2. Click Export for Clio in the worksheet.
  3. Save the text file generated by the worksheet.
  4. Open ClioBot using the button below.
  5. Upload your exported Historian's Table text file when Clio asks for it.
Open ClioBot

Clio will lead you through a short, five-round historical conversation. Clio may question your interpretation, challenge the connection between evidence and conclusions, introduce a competing interpretation, or ask you to reconsider a source.

You do not need to agree with Clio. What matters is that you evaluate Clio's reasoning against the evidence.

When the conversation is complete, download your Clio transcript. Then return to your Historian's Table worksheet and record:

  1. Clio's strongest challenge: What question, objection, or alternative interpretation made you think most carefully?
  2. Your response: Did you accept, reject, or partly accept Clio's challenge? What evidence led you to that decision?
Remember: Clio gets a seat at the table. Clio does not get the final word. You do.

Step 5: Leave the Table with Your Judgment

Return to your Historian's Table worksheet and answer the full historical question:

How did hoodoo and rootwork shape enslaved people's experiences in the antebellum South? In what ways did these practices offer healing, protection, or resistance, and why did they generate debate both inside and outside enslaved communities?

Write approximately 150–250 words. This is not a formal essay. It is your final historical judgment after hearing all of the voices at the table.

Your response should:

  • Clearly state what you now think.
  • Reference at least three of the five primary sources that matter most to your conclusion.
  • Incorporate at least one historian's argument from the historiography reader, by name.
  • Recognize at least one important limitation, complication, or counterargument.
  • Explain how the historiography or your conversation with Clio affected your thinking.

Your final judgment may be different from your first interpretation, partly different, or essentially unchanged. What matters is that you can explain why.

You are not being graded on whether you changed your mind. You are being graded on how thoughtfully you used evidence and evaluated the different interpretations at the table.

Citations

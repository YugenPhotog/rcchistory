Primary Sources

Uncle Tom

Loyalty, Resistance, and Martyrdom in Stowe's Own Words

HIST 101 · American History · Chapter 13

Primary-Source Student Reading Packet

Before You Read

Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was the best-selling American novel of the nineteenth century and one of the most powerful attacks on slavery ever written. Today, though, its hero's name is an insult. This packet sets the name aside and goes back to the man Stowe actually wrote.

You will read three short scenes from Tom's life. In Chapter 5, on a Kentucky farm, he learns he has been sold and refuses to run away. In Chapter 33, on Simon Legree's Louisiana cotton plantation, he refuses an order to whip a sick woman. In Chapter 40, he refuses to betray two women who have escaped, and he is beaten until he is near death. Watch how Tom's loyalty, his refusals, and his faith fit together, and decide for yourself who he is.

Stowe's language

The excerpts are quoted word for word from a public-domain edition. Stowe wrote her enslaved characters' speech in a period dialect (for example, "Mas'r" for "Master" and "an't" for "ain't"), and the novel uses the racial language of 1852, including slurs. These are kept for historical accuracy, not endorsed. When you quote the text, quote it exactly; in class discussion, do not repeat the slur aloud.

Reading difficult primary sources

These scenes include sale, family separation, and violent beatings. Read historically and closely. Ask what Stowe wanted her white Northern readers of 1852 to feel and do. Description is not always endorsement.

Key Terms

Term Working definition
Uncle / Aunt Forms of address white Southerners used for older enslaved people instead of "Mr." or "Mrs." They sounded friendly but withheld the respect owed to adults. Stowe used the custom in her title.
Mas'r / Missis Stowe's spelling of how enslaved characters say "Master" and "Mistress," the people who legally owned them.
Sold "down river" Sold south along the Mississippi to the cotton and sugar plantations of the Deep South, where work was harshest and families were rarely reunited.
Pass A written permission slip that let an enslaved person travel off the plantation. Tom has one in Chapter 5.
Driver An enslaved man whom the owner forced to supervise and whip other enslaved workers. Legree wants to make Tom one.
Simon Legree Tom's last owner, a brutal Louisiana cotton planter. His name became shorthand for a cruel boss.
Sambo and Quimbo Two enslaved men whom Legree uses as drivers and enforcers on his plantation.
Cassy and Emmeline Two enslaved women on Legree's plantation. Legree abuses Cassy and plans the same for Emmeline, then fifteen. They escape and hide in his garret, and Tom knows where.
Martyr A person who chooses to suffer or die rather than give up a belief or betray others.
Passive resistance Refusing to obey an unjust order without using violence, even when the refusal brings punishment.
Epithet An insulting name. By the 1910s and 1920s, "Uncle Tom" had become an epithet for a Black person accused of serving white power against other Black people.

Selected Readings

Read the three scenes in order. They trace Tom from loyalty, to resistance, to martyrdom. Notice what Tom is willing to give and what he refuses to give, and notice how Stowe, the narrator, steps in to tell you how to feel.

1 Loyalty: "Mas'r always found me on the spot"

Kentucky, past midnight. Tom's owner, Mr. Shelby, is in debt and has secretly sold Tom and Harry, the small son of Eliza, an enslaved woman in the Shelby house, to a slave trader named Haley. Eliza overheard the sale and is about to flee with Harry. Before she goes, she stops at the cabin of Tom and his wife, Aunt Chloe, to warn them. This is the scene most often blamed for the later insult.

Chapter 5 | Showing the Feelings of Living Property on Changing Owners

1

“Sold him?” echoed both, lifting up their hands in dismay.

2

“Yes, sold him!” said Eliza, firmly; “I crept into the closet by Mistress’ door tonight, and I heard Master tell Missis that he had sold my Harry, and you, Uncle Tom, both, to a trader; and that he was going off this morning on his horse, and that the man was to take possession today.”

3

Tom had stood, during this speech, with his hands raised, and his eyes dilated, like a man in a dream. Slowly and gradually, as its meaning came over him, he collapsed, rather than seated himself, on his old chair, and sunk his head down upon his knees.

4

“The good Lord have pity on us!” said Aunt Chloe. “O! it don’t seem as if it was true! What has he done, that Mas’r should sell him?”

[ . . . ]

5

“Well, old man!” said Aunt Chloe, “why don’t you go, too? Will you wait to be toted down river, where they kill niggers with hard work and starving? I’d a heap rather die than go there, any day! There’s time for ye,–be off with Lizy,–you’ve got a pass to come and go any time. Come, bustle up, and I’ll get your things together.”

6

Tom slowly raised his head, and looked sorrowfully but quietly around, and said,

7

“No, no–I an’t going. Let Eliza go–it’s her right! I wouldn’t be the one to say no–’t an’t in natur for her to stay; but you heard what she said! If I must be sold, or all the people on the place, and everything go to rack, why, let me be sold. I s’pose I can b’ar it as well as any on ’em,” he added, while something like a sob and a sigh shook his broad, rough chest convulsively. “Mas’r always found me on the spot–he always will. I never have broke trust, nor used my pass no ways contrary to my word, and I never will. It’s better for me alone to go, than to break up the place and sell all. Mas’r an’t to blame, Chloe, and he’ll take care of you and the poor–”

8

Here he turned to the rough trundle bed full of little woolly heads, and broke fairly down. He leaned over the back of the chair, and covered his face with his large hands. Sobs, heavy, hoarse and loud, shook the chair, and great tears fell through his fingers on the floor; just such tears, sir, as you dropped into the coffin where lay your first-born son; such tears, woman, as you shed when you heard the cries of your dying babe. For, sir, he was a man,–and you are but another man. And, woman, though dressed in silk and jewels, you are but a woman, and, in life’s great straits and mighty griefs, ye feel but one sorrow!

Editorial note: [ . . . ] marks omitted text. The cut in this excerpt is Eliza's longer account of what she overheard.

2 Resistance: "I'm willin' to work, night and day"

Years later. Mr. Shelby's son George has promised to buy Tom back, but Tom has been sold twice more and now belongs to Simon Legree, who bought him to make him a driver. At the evening weigh-in of cotton, Tom has secretly helped a sick, exhausted woman by putting cotton from his own sack into hers. Legree falsely calls her basket short. Cassy, mentioned at the start, is an enslaved woman Legree keeps in his house.

Chapter 33 | Cassy

1

“And now,” said Legree, “come here, you Tom. You see, I telled ye I didn’t buy ye jest for the common work; I mean to promote ye, and make a driver of ye; and tonight ye may jest as well begin to get yer hand in. Now, ye jest take this yer gal and flog her; ye’ve seen enough on’t to know how.”

2

“I beg Mas’r’s pardon,” said Tom; “hopes Mas’r won’t set me at that. It’s what I an’t used to,–never did,–and can’t do, no way possible.”

3

“Ye’ll larn a pretty smart chance of things ye never did know, before I’ve done with ye!” said Legree, taking up a cowhide, and striking Tom a heavy blow cross the cheek, and following up the infliction by a shower of blows.

4

“There!” he said, as he stopped to rest; “now, will ye tell me ye can’t do it?”

5

“Yes, Mas’r,” said Tom, putting up his hand, to wipe the blood, that trickled down his face. “I’m willin’ to work, night and day, and work while there’s life and breath in me; but this yer thing I can’t feel it right to do;–and, Mas’r, I never shall do it,–never!”

[ . . . ]

6

Legree looked stupefied and confounded; but at last burst forth,–“What! ye blasted black beast! tell me ye don’t think it right to do what I tell ye! What have any of you cussed cattle to do with thinking what’s right? I’ll put a stop to it! Why, what do ye think ye are? May be ye think ye’r a gentleman master, Tom, to be a telling your master what’s right, and what ain’t! So you pretend it’s wrong to flog the gal!”

7

“I think so, Mas’r,” said Tom; “the poor crittur’s sick and feeble; ’t would be downright cruel, and it’s what I never will do, nor begin to. Mas’r, if you mean to kill me, kill me; but, as to my raising my hand agin any one here, I never shall,–I’ll die first!”

[ . . . ]

8

“Well, here’s a pious dog, at last, let down among us sinners!–a saint, a gentleman, and no less, to talk to us sinners about our sins! Powerful holy critter, he must be! Here, you rascal, you make believe to be so pious,–didn’t you never hear, out of yer Bible, ‘Servants, obey yer masters’? An’t I yer master? Didn’t I pay down twelve hundred dollars, cash, for all there is inside yer old cussed black shell? An’t yer mine, now, body and soul?” he said, giving Tom a violent kick with his heavy boot; “tell me!”

9

In the very depth of physical suffering, bowed by brutal oppression, this question shot a gleam of joy and triumph through Tom’s soul. He suddenly stretched himself up, and, looking earnestly to heaven, while the tears and blood that flowed down his face mingled, he exclaimed,

10

“No! no! no! my soul an’t yours, Mas’r! You haven’t bought it,–ye can’t buy it! It’s been bought and paid for, by one that is able to keep it;–no matter, no matter, you can’t harm me!”

11

“I can’t!” said Legree, with a sneer; “we’ll see,–we’ll see! Here, Sambo, Quimbo, give this dog such a breakin’ in as he won’t get over, this month!”

Editorial note: The omitted passages describe the stunned reaction of the other enslaved workers and Legree's rage. The chapter ends as Sambo and Quimbo drag Tom away to be whipped.

3 Martyrdom: "I know, Mas'r; but I can't tell anything"

Cassy and Emmeline have escaped, and Legree's hunt with dogs and guns has failed. In fact, the two women are hiding in the garret of Legree's own house. Legree sends Quimbo to bring Tom from the cotton field. Tom quotes the Bible as he goes: "Into thy hands I commend my spirit" are the last words of Jesus in Luke 23:46. Tom dies a few days later, in Chapter 41.

Chapter 40 | The Martyr

1

Tom heard the message with a forewarning heart; for he knew all the plan of the fugitives’ escape, and the place of their present concealment;–he knew the deadly character of the man he had to deal with, and his despotic power. But he felt strong in God to meet death, rather than betray the helpless.

[ . . . ]

2

“Well, Tom!” said Legree, walking up, and seizing him grimly by the collar of his coat, and speaking through his teeth, in a paroxysm of determined rage, “do you know I’ve made up my mind to KILL YOU?”

3

“It’s very likely, Mas’r,” said Tom, calmly.

4

“I have,” said Legree, with a grim, terrible calmness, “done–just–that–thing, Tom, unless you’ll tell me what you know about these yer gals!”

5

Tom stood silent.

6

“D’ye hear?” said Legree, stamping, with a roar like that of an incensed lion. “Speak!”

7

“I han’t got nothing to tell, Mas’r,” said Tom, with a slow, firm, deliberate utterance.

8

“Do you dare to tell me, ye old black Christian, ye don’t know?” said Legree.

9

Tom was silent.

10

“Speak!” thundered Legree, striking him furiously. “Do you know anything?”

11

“I know, Mas’r; but I can’t tell anything. I can die!”

[ . . . ]

12

Tom looked up to his master, and answered, “Mas’r, if you was sick, or in trouble, or dying, and I could save ye, I’d give ye my heart’s blood; and, if taking every drop of blood in this poor old body would save your precious soul, I’d give ’em freely, as the Lord gave his for me. O, Mas’r! don’t bring this great sin on your soul! It will hurt you more than ’t will me! Do the worst you can, my troubles’ll be over soon; but, if ye don’t repent, yours won’t never end!”

13

Like a strange snatch of heavenly music, heard in the lull of a tempest, this burst of feeling made a moment’s blank pause. Legree stood aghast, and looked at Tom; and there was such a silence, that the tick of the old clock could be heard, measuring, with silent touch, the last moments of mercy and probation to that hardened heart.

14

It was but a moment. There was one hesitating pause,–one irresolute, relenting thrill,–and the spirit of evil came back, with seven-fold vehemence; and Legree, foaming with rage, smote his victim to the ground.

[ . . . ]

15

“He’s most gone, Mas’r,” said Sambo, touched, in spite of himself, by the patience of his victim.

16

“Pay away, till he gives up! Give it to him!–give it to him!” shouted Legree. “I’ll take every drop of blood he has, unless he confesses!”

17

Tom opened his eyes, and looked upon his master. “Ye poor miserable critter!” he said, “there ain’t no more ye can do! I forgive ye, with all my soul!” and he fainted entirely away.

[ . . . ]

18

“O, Tom!” said Quimbo, “we’s been awful wicked to ye!”

19

“I forgive ye, with all my heart!” said Tom, faintly.

Editorial note: The omitted passages include Legree's threat to take every drop of Tom's blood, and Stowe's own address to the reader about the whipping, which takes place offstage through the night. Paragraph 18 comes after Legree leaves, when Sambo and Quimbo take Tom down and try to revive him.

Thought Question 1
In Chapter 5, Tom refuses to run even though Aunt Chloe begs him to and Eliza is leaving that night. List the reasons he gives. Is he acting out of obedience to Mr. Shelby, keeping his word, protecting the other enslaved people on the place, or something else? Why does he call Eliza's escape "her right" but refuse it for himself?

Thought Question 2
Compare "Mas'r always found me on the spot" (Ch. 5, par. 7) with "I'm willin' to work, night and day ... but this yer thing I can't feel it right to do" (Ch. 33, par. 5). What has stayed the same in Tom, and what is new? What will Tom give a master, and what will he never give? Point to the exact words that show where he draws the line.

Thought Question 3
Tom tells Legree that his soul has been "bought and paid for" (Ch. 33) and later says, "I can die!" and "I forgive ye" (Ch. 40). How does Stowe use Christian faith to explain Tom's choices? Is his forgiveness of Legree, Sambo, and Quimbo a sign of weakness, a sign of strength, or both? Consider James Baldwin's charge, from Lecture 3A, that Stowe made Tom a symbol rather than a full human being.

Thought Question 4
By the 1910s and 1920s, "Uncle Tom" had become an insult for a Black person who serves white power against other Black people. Using at least one passage from each of the three excerpts, decide whether Stowe's Tom is a hero, a doormat, or something else. Then answer directly: does the text of the novel support the later insult, or did the culture that retold the story create it? Explain which passage most supports your answer and which passage most challenges it.

Source Note

Text: Harriet Beecher Stowe, Uncle Tom's Cabin; or, Life Among the Lowly, first serialized in the National Era (1851-1852) and published as a book by John P. Jewett, Boston, in 1852. The excerpts are taken from Project Gutenberg eBook #203, a public-domain transcription. Words, spelling, dialect, and Stowe's italics are kept exactly as printed there.

Classroom changes: paragraph numbers have been added for citation; omissions are marked [ . . . ]; and Stowe's long dashes are shown as shorter dashes to match this packet's typography. No words have been modernized or changed.

The purpose of this packet is close reading in an introductory American history course. It does not replace the full novel. Students should distinguish what Stowe wrote, how later audiences retold it, and their own evaluation.

Editorial principle

The packet does not settle the argument about Tom. The three scenes are chosen to show his loyalty, his refusals, and his death side by side, so that readers who call him a hero and readers who call him a doormat can both test their claims against the same evidence.

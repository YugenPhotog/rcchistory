Chapter Inquiry

The question this chapter answers

What did it mean to complete the American Revolution after independence?

The early republic had to do more than win a war and write a Constitution. Americans had to make national power work, learn whether organized opposition could be legitimate, decide what expansion meant for republican liberty and Native sovereignty, and determine when defending independence justified war.

Chapter throughline

The new republic survived by becoming more powerful, more partisan, and more expansive than many revolutionaries had expected. Each solution made the United States stronger while reopening the Revolution's unresolved question: how much power can a republic use in the name of liberty?

Lecture 1

The Constitution Had No Instruction Manual

Guiding question

How do you turn a written Constitution into a government people will obey?

Argument to watch for

The Constitution became real through precedent and capacity. Washington's choices created routines; Hamilton's financial system gave the federal government real reach; the Whiskey Rebellion tested whether that new power would be accepted as legitimate.

The Constitution supplied an office, not a complete operating manual.

Listen for: What did the title debate, Washington's Senate experiment, and the development of the Cabinet reveal about how constitutional meaning was created?

Stage II · Building Capacity

A government that cannot finance itself has little room to survive a crisis.

Listen for: Why did Hamilton care about public credit? How did assumption strengthen the national government? What did the bank debate reveal about implied constitutional power?

Stage III · Capacity Meets Citizens

Federal power becomes real when it reaches a port, a courtroom, a frontier, or a still.

Listen for: Why could the same whiskey tax look like national survival from Philadelphia and political extraction in western Pennsylvania?

A stronger state now had to prove that enforcement could remain republican.

Listen for: What did Bower Hill, the militia mobilization, the lack of a major battle, and Washington's pardons reveal about both the power and the limits of the new federal government?

Lecture 2

When Does Opposition Become Treason?

Guiding question

Can a republic survive people organizing to defeat the government in power?

Argument to watch for

Americans entered the 1790s fearing organized factions. Foreign crisis made domestic opponents look like enemies, but repression and the election crisis of 1800 forced a new principle into practice: an opposition party could defeat the government and remain loyal to the republic.

Open Lecture 2 deck →

Stage I · From Disagreement to Faction

Old revolutionary allies discover that each side thinks the other is betraying the Revolution.

Listen for: How did Hamilton, Madison, and Jefferson come to see the same government differently? Why did newspapers make organized opposition more powerful and more frightening?

Class activity · Make the case

Watchdog or Faction?

A group starts a newspaper, holds public meetings, circulates petitions, and tries to defeat the administration.

Which label is stronger in a republic?

Stage II · Foreign Crisis Makes Opposition Look Dangerous

France, Genêt, Jay's Treaty, and XYZ turn political disagreement into a security problem.

Listen for: How did foreign affairs change the meaning of domestic opposition? Why could Federalist fears look reasonable even as their response became repressive?

Stage III · The Sedition Test

A government that fears disloyalty decides whether criticism itself can become criminal.

Listen for: What did the Sedition Act criminalize? Why does Matthew Lyon's reelection from jail make the law's political problem impossible to ignore?

Class activity · Draw the boundary

Where Is the Line?

A republic has to protect itself. But it also has to allow opposition.

Boundary cases
  • A newspaper attacks the president.
  • A public meeting denounces a treaty.
  • A state declares a federal law unconstitutional.
  • A party tries to block a rival's election.

Stage IV · Opposition Becomes Government

The election of 1800 tests whether losing office means losing the republic.

Listen for: How did the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions complicate resistance? Why did the 73-73 deadlock matter? What precedent did the peaceful transfer of power establish?

Lecture 3

Can a Republic Have an Empire?

Guiding question

Can republican liberty expand across other peoples' lands without becoming empire?

Argument to watch for

The Louisiana Purchase solved a strategic crisis and seemed to secure a future of independent republican farmers. But the treaty changed claims faster than it changed sovereignty, exposing the tension between American liberty and Indigenous power.

Open Lecture 3 deck →

Stage I · New Orleans Creates a Crisis

One port could determine the economic and political future of the West.

Listen for: Why did closing the right of deposit at New Orleans frighten Jefferson? How did the failure of Napoleon's Caribbean project change what France was willing to sell?

Class activity · Constitutional dilemma

Would You Take the Deal?

You are Jefferson. France offers all of Louisiana, but the Constitution does not clearly say you can buy foreign territory.

Your choice

Stage II · Expansion Looks Like Liberty

Jefferson's constitutional problem was also a theory of the republic's future.

Listen for: Why did Jefferson accept power he had once wanted narrowly limited? Why could land expansion look like a way to preserve republican independence?

Stage III · The Map Is Not the Territory

France could sell an imperial claim. It could not make Native nations disappear.

Listen for: What is the difference between a treaty claim, effective control, and Native consent? What did Lewis and Clark's mission reveal about what the United States actually knew and controlled?

Class activity · Read the map against the story

If It's U.S. Territory, Why Are Guns Drawn?

In 1804, the expedition nearly came to violence with Teton Lakota leaders on the Missouri River.

Two pieces of evidence
  • The map says: this is now U.S. territory.
  • The encounter says: American sovereignty is not yet effective here.

Stage IV · Liberty for Whom?

The expedition succeeded inside Native diplomatic and commercial worlds it did not control.

Listen for: How did the expedition depend on Mandan, Hidatsa, Shoshone, and other Native communities? Why could expansion look like liberty to white settlers, dispossession to Native nations, and a wider future for slavery?

Lecture 4

Why Did a Republic That Feared War Choose War?

Guiding question

Why did Republicans who feared armies, taxes, debt, and concentrated power finally choose war?

Argument to watch for

War became thinkable only after diplomacy and economic coercion seemed to fail and after maritime pressure and western conflict converged into a broader crisis of sovereignty. The decision remained deeply contested.

Open Lecture 4 deck →

Stage I · A Republic Tries Not to Fight

Republicans feared the kind of government war could create.

Listen for: Why did impressment become a question of citizenship and national sovereignty? What did the Monroe-Pinkney Treaty and the Chesapeake-Leopard affair show about the limits of diplomacy?

Class activity · Presidential dilemma

Would You Fight Back?

A British warship has fired into an American warship and taken sailors. You are Jefferson.

Your choice

Stage II · Fighting Without Fighting

Jefferson tries economic pressure; Madison inherits the consequences.

Listen for: What was the logic of the Embargo? Why did avoiding war require more federal enforcement at home? How did the failure of embargo, non-intercourse, and later alternatives narrow Madison's choices?

Stage III · The West Has Its Own Crisis

Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa pursued an Indigenous project that Americans increasingly connected to Britain.

Listen for: What did Tecumseh and Tenskwatawa want? Why is it misleading to reduce Native resistance to British manipulation? How did Tippecanoe make western and maritime conflicts look connected?

Class activity · Connect the causes

Two Crises or One?

By 1811, Americans faced British maritime pressure and a western Indigenous resistance movement with British connections.

Which interpretation better explains why war became thinkable?

Stage IV · The Decision for War

War Hawks raise the temperature, Madison makes the case, and a divided Congress chooses war.

Listen for: Why did War Hawks matter without being the whole cause? How did Madison connect sea, West, and failed alternatives? What does the divided 79-49 House vote tell us about the phrase “America chose war”?

Reference

Anchor Terms

These are the terms that help you reconstruct the formal lecture arguments. They are not a list of everything in the textbook.

  • PrecedentLecture 1 A repeated governing practice that becomes an expected way of operating the Constitution.
  • CabinetLecture 1 The president's department heads acting as a group of advisers, a practice not spelled out in the Constitution.
  • Public creditLecture 1 Confidence that the government will honor its financial obligations and therefore be able to borrow.
  • AssumptionLecture 1 Hamilton's plan for the federal government to take responsibility for state Revolutionary debts.
  • Implied powersLecture 1 Powers not listed word-for-word but argued to be legitimate means for carrying out constitutional ends.
  • ExciseLecture 1 A tax on the production or sale of a particular good, here distilled spirits.
  • FactionLecture 2 An organized political group feared for placing its own interest above the common good.
  • Partisan pressLecture 2 Newspapers that did not merely report politics but helped organize and argue for political sides.
  • Jay's TreatyLecture 2 The 1795 agreement with Britain that reduced danger of war while intensifying partisan conflict at home.
  • XYZ AffairLecture 2 A diplomatic crisis with France that strengthened Federalist claims that the republic faced a real foreign threat.
  • Sedition ActLecture 2 The 1798 federal law used to punish certain critical writings against the government.
  • Peaceful transfer of powerLecture 2 The precedent established when the losing party surrendered office after the election of 1800.
  • Right of depositLecture 3 The ability of American shippers to store goods at New Orleans before transferring them to ocean-going trade.
  • Louisiana PurchaseLecture 3 The 1803 U.S. acquisition of France's claim to Louisiana.
  • Empire of libertyLecture 3 Jefferson's idea that territorial expansion could preserve a republic of independent landholding citizens.
  • SovereigntyLectures 3-4 Effective political authority: the power to govern, make claims, and have those claims recognized or enforced.
  • Corps of DiscoveryLecture 3 The Lewis and Clark expedition, which gathered geographic, scientific, commercial, and diplomatic knowledge for the United States.
  • ImpressmentLecture 4 British seizure of sailors claimed as British subjects, including sailors taken from American vessels.
  • Chesapeake-Leopard AffairLecture 4 The 1807 British attack on USS Chesapeake that turned maritime grievance into national humiliation.
  • Peaceable coercionLecture 4 The attempt to use economic restrictions rather than war to force Britain and France to respect U.S. rights.
  • EmbargoLecture 4 Jefferson's broad restriction on American overseas trade, intended as an economic weapon.
  • Tecumseh and TenskwatawaLecture 4 Shawnee brothers associated with an Indigenous movement resisting U.S. land expansion and dependency.
  • War HawksLecture 4 Militant Republicans who helped make war politically possible without single-handedly causing it.

Chapter Inquiry

The question this unit answers

How do historians know about the past, explain change, and disagree without treating every account as equally strong?

This short unit is not a survey of the Revolution. It uses episodes from the Revolution — King Street in 1770, the Stamp Act crisis of 1765, and rival interpretations of independence — to practice the discipline’s core moves: evaluating evidence, naming causal mechanisms, and judging competing interpretations.

Unit throughline

History is not “what happened” in the raw. It is disciplined argument from surviving traces. Different questions, scales, and evidence produce different accounts — and shared standards of evidence, mechanism, context, and revision make some accounts stronger than others.

Lecture 1

What Is History?

Guiding question

If history is simply “what happened,” why do accounts of the same event disagree?

Argument to watch for

Primary sources are not containers of neutral fact. They are people under pressure, writing for audiences. Disagreement is often about what the facts mean, not about whether basic checkable details occurred. Evaluation — sourcing, contextualizing, corroborating — is the discipline’s core skill.

Stage I · One night, two accounts

King Street, 5 March 1770 — Preston’s deposition and the Boston narrative.

Fragments are fine.

Watch for: What do the two accounts agree on? Where does the conflict actually sit — and why is that distinction important?

Lecture 2

What Makes History Happen?

Guiding question

Why did Boston explode in 1765 — and who made it happen?

Argument to watch for

A sequence is not a causal claim. Causation requires a mechanism, an assignment of weight, and an explanation of why the outcome was not automatic. Agency and structure are mutually constitutive — not a forced choice between “great men” and “impersonal forces.”

Stage I · The story first

14 August and 26 August 1765; repeal and the Declaratory Act — sequence without explanation yet.

Hold the story before you explain it.

Watch for: What happened, in order? What detail about the sheriff matters later? Why does the lecture stress that independence is eleven years away and nobody knows it?

Lecture 3

Why Do Historians Disagree?

Guiding question

Why can informed historians disagree even when they share many facts and professional standards — and how do we judge those disagreements without deciding that everything is just opinion?

Argument to watch for

Disagreement is normal because historians ask different questions, work at different scales, and privilege different evidence. It is not relativism: shared tests of question clarity, evidentiary fit, mechanism, contrary evidence, proportionality, and openness to revision make some accounts stronger than others.

Stage I · Why disagreement is normal

Different questions → different evidence → different scales.

Relevance is relational.

Watch for: How does the question a historian asks select the evidence that will count as central? How does scale change which causes look decisive?

Stage II · Three lenses

Beard / Progressive · Bailyn / ideological · Taylor / continental.

Match the tool to the question.

Watch for: Primary question, favored evidence, and characteristic risk for each lens. Where does each explain well — and where does it overreach?

Class activity · Rank & Defend

Which lens for escalation?

Suppose the question is: “Why did resistance to British policy escalate into a war for independence?” Rank the three lenses from most to least useful for that specific question. Defend the top and bottom with one sentence each.

Stage III · Judging fairly

Evaluation tests and the Five Historical-Thinking Questions as a common standard.

Same tests for every school.

Watch for: The six usable tests. How revision differs from denial. Why presentism comes in two directions.

Class activity · Judge the Claim

Improve or qualify this sentence

“The American Revolution was primarily a conflict over economic interests and the distribution of political power.”

Reference

Terms to Recognize and Use

You should be able to use these terms when explaining the unit’s arguments. Definitions and fuller explanations remain available in the Reveal decks and Study Buddy notes.

  • Primary sourceLec 1 A source created within the period or process under study.
  • Secondary sourceLec 1 A later work that interprets primary evidence and earlier scholarship.
  • ProvenanceLec 1 A trace’s own history: who made it, when, why, and how it reached you.
  • Sourcing / contextualizing / corroboratingLec 1 The three evaluation habits that make a claim checkable.
  • MechanismLec 2–3 The pathway by which a proposed cause produces an outcome.
  • AgencyLec 2 Capacity to make choices that shape outcomes, under constraint.
  • StructureLec 2 Durable patterns of rules, resources, or meanings that shape what people can do.
  • ContingencyLec 2 An outcome depended on a particular convergence and was not settled in advance.
  • ScaleLec 2–3 The geographic or temporal frame that organizes an account.
  • Progressive (Beard) interpretationLec 3 Emphasizes economic interests, property, and the distribution of power.
  • Ideological interpretationLec 3 Treats political ideas and constitutional language as genuine causal forces.
  • Continental interpretationLec 3 Widens the frame to multiple peoples, empires, and overlapping conflicts.
  • RevisionLec 3 Changing an interpretation in response to new evidence or stronger argument.
  • DenialLec 3 Protecting a required conclusion by selecting or dismissing evidence.
  • PresentismLec 1, 3 Reading the past primarily through present categories or political needs.
  • Five Historical-Thinking QuestionsUnit Cause · Comparison · Context · Consequence · Evidence.

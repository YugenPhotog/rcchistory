# Historian's Notebook

## Purpose

Create one student-facing **Historian's Notebook per chapter**, not one document
per lecture.

The Historian's Notebook is the student's in-class workspace for:

- following the central historical problem;
- taking selective notes while listening;
- completing authentic lecture activities;
- preserving group conclusions and individual judgments;
- connecting the chapter's lectures;
- conducting brief, optional retrieval after class;
- saving work in the current browser and device.

It is not:

- a transcript;
- a second textbook;
- a comprehensive exam guide;
- a required after-class workbook;
- a completion-based attendance mechanism;
- an artifact students are expected to print;
- a resource permitted during exams unless the instructor explicitly changes
  that policy.

## Default Assessment Policy

Assume students **may not use the Historian's Notebook during exams** unless the
instructor explicitly states otherwise.

Because it is not an exam aid:

- do not include Export to PDF;
- do not include a print button;
- do not create print-specific response mirrors;
- do not build a large comprehensive review packet;
- do not imply that every field must be completed.

The notebook should support learning during the chapter. Exam preparation should
occur through separate, deliberately limited synthesis or review materials.

## Primary Inputs

Use both of the following whenever available.

### 1. Approved professor background essays

These are the primary authority for:

- historical accuracy;
- causal explanation;
- interpretation;
- historiographical nuance;
- names, dates, evidence, and context;
- the deeper intellectual purpose of each lecture.

### 2. Final Reveal decks

These are the primary authority for:

- what students actually encounter;
- lecture sequence and pacing;
- student-facing wording;
- evidence emphasized in class;
- activities and their order;
- source excerpts, choices, comparisons, and debriefs;
- transitions among lecture sections.

The final Reveal deck controls the presence, sequence, and basic intellectual
task of student activities.

## Recommended Optional Inputs

When available, also use:

- the assigned textbook chapter or reading;
- chapter or module learning outcomes;
- exam blueprint or assessment objectives;
- module welcome page;
- course visual house style;
- explicit instructor directions about notebook completion or assessment use.

These materials may refine emphasis. They must not turn the notebook into an
encyclopedic record of everything mentioned in the course.

## Source Hierarchy

When sources differ, use this hierarchy:

1. **Historical accuracy:** approved background essay.
2. **What was taught and in what order:** final Reveal deck.
3. **Course boundaries and prerequisite knowledge:** assigned reading.
4. **Assessment emphasis:** exam blueprint or learning objectives.

If a Reveal deck conflicts materially with an approved background essay,
correct the notebook and report the discrepancy in the instructor design note.

Do not invent claims, quotations, statistics, interpretations, sources, or
activities unsupported by the supplied materials.

## Default Chapter Structure

Assume a chapter normally contains four approximately 35-minute lecture blocks
unless the supplied materials indicate otherwise.

Create one notebook containing:

- Chapter inquiry and lecture map
- Lecture sections in actual presentation order
- Authentic in-class activities embedded where they occur
- Optional Chapter Review in a collapsed section
- Terms to Recognize and Use
- Learning check

Adapt the number and architecture of lecture sections to the actual chapter.

## Governing Design Principle

The notebook must make it easier to attend to the lecture.

When a feature would make the notebook more comprehensive but would compete with
listening, participation, or classroom pacing, omit or reduce the feature.

When forced to choose between comprehensiveness and usability, choose usability.

## Student Framing

Near the beginning, include a brief instruction such as:

> Complete activity spaces when prompted. Use the note areas only for evidence,
> explanations, examples, and questions that will help you reconstruct the
> lecture later. Your notes are not checked for completeness.

Do not use language suggesting that:

- every box must be filled;
- the notebook is graded for completeness;
- an incomplete notebook will cause failure;
- the notebook replaces preparation, attendance, or participation.

## Complement Rather Than Repeat

Assume students completed the assigned reading.

Focus on the lectures' added value:

- interpretation;
- historical problems;
- evidence;
- causation;
- comparison;
- contingency;
- competing perspectives;
- historiographical disagreements;
- connections across topics.

Do not reproduce the textbook narrative or Study Buddy notes.

## Lecture-Pacing Architecture

### Follow the Reveal deck's sequence

The notebook must track the actual order of the lecture.

Do not place one large notes box before all activities. Instead, interleave
shorter note spaces and activity blocks at the points where they occur.

A lecture may use a structure such as:

1. Guiding question and argument
2. Opening notes
3. Activity 1
4. Evidence and explanation notes
5. Activity 2
6. Later notes or comparison
7. Activity 3
8. Closing takeaway

This is illustrative, not a fixed template. Derive each lecture's structure
from its own Reveal deck.

### Lecture title

Use the final title from the Reveal deck.

### Guiding question

State one question students should be able to answer by the end.

### Argument to watch for

Provide one short sentence identifying the central claim without giving a full
summary.

### Stage-specific note cues

Do **not** place a general **Listen for** list near the beginning of a lecture.

Students should see only the guiding question, the argument to watch for, and
the cue that applies to the current stage of the lecture. A separate opening
cue list duplicates instructions and forces students to decide which layer of
guidance matters at that moment.

Place one concise, stage-specific cue block beside each note area. The cue
should direct attention toward the material currently being presented, such as:

- significant evidence;
- a causal link;
- a comparison;
- change over time;
- perspective;
- historical significance;
- tension within the argument.

A stage cue may contain two or three closely related questions when the lecture
segment genuinely requires them, but it must remain scannable and tied to that
specific segment.

Do not:

- repeat the same cue in multiple places;
- use cues as disguised factual blanks;
- add a lecture-level **Listen for** list in addition to stage cues;
- ask students to track every important fact at once.

### Note areas

Use two or three note areas per lecture, placed at meaningful transitions.

Possible labels include:

- Opening Notes
- Evidence and Explanation
- Comparison Notes
- What Changed?
- Closing Takeaway

Each field should:

- support selective note-taking;
- use a clear visible label;
- include one stage-specific cue block;
- auto-expand as the student types;
- save locally;
- be large enough for useful notes but not so large that blank space implies a
  required essay.

A brief playful invitation line may appear above a note field. This voice is
encouraged when it makes the notebook feel approachable, for example:

- “Fragments are fine.”
- “Arrows encouraged.”
- “Future You forgets fast.”
- “Your box, your rules.”

Keep these messages short, varied, and clearly subordinate to the historical
cue. They may never replace the visible label or stage-specific instruction.

Do not create one enormous transcript box.

## Authentic Student Activities

Include every student activity from the final Reveal deck that students are
expected to complete. A lecture ordinarily contains at least two.

For each activity:

- place it exactly where it occurs in the lecture sequence;
- preserve its title when one exists;
- preserve or faithfully condense its directions;
- include required source material or a clear cue to the projected source;
- preserve individual, pair, group, or whole-class format;
- provide only the response structure necessary to complete the activity;
- give collaborative activities one compact space for the student's own group
  conclusion;
- add an **After the Debrief** field only when the deck explicitly asks students
  to revise, reconsider, or record a conclusion.

Do not:

- invent substitute activities;
- add generic reflections after every activity;
- add a limitation question merely because it sounds intellectually useful;
- convert an oral vote into a paragraph assignment;
- split one brief prediction into many separate writing boxes;
- require polished prose during a timed classroom activity.

### Match the response format to the task

Use the leanest useful format:

- radio buttons for a choice;
- one short justification field;
- a compact comparison table;
- ranking controls;
- claim-and-evidence fields;
- one before-and-after judgment when revision is central;
- a cause-and-consequence chain;
- source observation and inference;
- a small design-choice table;
- one group conclusion field.

Avoid generic textareas when structured controls reduce writing and increase
clarity.

## Content-Coverage Requirement

The notebook must reflect the full intellectual content of each lecture, not
only the activity slides.

For every lecture, identify its major sections or claims from the background
essay and Reveal deck. Ensure each important element appears in at least one of:

- a listening cue;
- a strategically placed notes label or helper prompt;
- an authentic activity;
- a closing takeaway;
- the optional retrieval question;
- the learning check.

Do not add long summaries simply to achieve coverage.

### Coverage matrix

Before writing the HTML, create an instructor-only internal matrix with:

- major lecture section or claim;
- where it appears in the notebook;
- whether it is represented through notes, activity, retrieval, or checklist.

Use the matrix to catch imbalances, especially when the activities cluster in
one portion of the lecture.

Do not display the matrix to students unless requested.

## Cognitive-Load Limits

For a typical four-lecture chapter:

- retain all authentic in-class activities;
- use no more than three listening cues per lecture;
- use no more than three note areas per lecture;
- use no more than one written response per ordinary activity stage;
- do not add automatic debrief fields;
- do not include written vocabulary assignments;
- do not include a comprehensive after-class workbook;
- do not include more than six optional review textareas;
- do not require continuous writing while the professor is speaking;
- do not imply that every blank field represents required work.

Activity fields are justified because class time is deliberately paused for
them. Note fields are justified because they reduce transcription pressure.
Additional fields must earn their place.

## Chapter Inquiry

Include:

- one central historical question;
- a two- or three-sentence orientation;
- a concise lecture map showing how the lectures build toward the question.

Do not summarize the entire chapter.

## Optional Chapter Review

Place review material inside a collapsed `<details>` element labeled:

**Optional Chapter Review**

Include an explicit note:

> This section is for self-checking after class. It is not required for notebook
> completion and the notebook is not used during exams.

The optional review should contain only:

### 1. One chapter-specific organizer

Choose the organizer that best fits the chapter's argument.

Possible structures include:

- Historical Problem → Forces or Claims → Tension or Consequence
- Cause → Turning Point → Consequence
- Competing Claim → Evidence → Limitation
- Region → Conditions → Social Result
- Policy Problem → Response → Unintended Consequence
- Continuity → Change → Historical Significance

Use one compact row per lecture or major chapter movement.

Do not impose the same organizer on every chapter.

### 2. One retrieval question per lecture

Each question should represent the whole lecture rather than merely repeating an
activity.

Use retrieval questions to capture important content that may not have appeared
in the activities.

Ask students to answer from memory first, then check their notes or Study Buddy
materials.

### 3. One optional synthesis question

The question should connect at least two lectures.

Label it clearly as optional. Do not make it a routine paragraph assignment.

### 4. Muddiest point

Include one brief field for a remaining question or unclear point.

### 5. Learning check

Include five to seven concise checkboxes beginning with:

**I can explain...**

Use meaningful historical performances:

- explain a causal relationship;
- compare interpretations;
- support a claim with evidence;
- identify a tension or consequence;
- connect lecture arguments;
- explain why an outcome was contingent rather than inevitable.

Avoid vague verbs such as know, understand, or be familiar with.

## Terms to Recognize and Use

Select five to eight terms for the entire chapter.

Present them as a compact list, not as response fields.

Use this instruction:

> You should be able to use these terms when explaining the chapter's
> arguments. Definitions and fuller explanations remain available in the
> Reveal decks and Study Buddy notes.

Do not require dictionary definitions or separate written significance
statements.

## Relationship to Study Buddy Notes

The two resources have distinct functions.

- **Historian's Notebook:** selective in-class notes, activity participation,
  conclusions, and optional retrieval.
- **Study Buddy notes:** fuller post-lecture explanation and clarification.

Do not duplicate Study Buddy material inside the notebook.

## Assessment Use

Default: the notebook is not permitted during exams.

Do not include:

- exam-access language;
- claims that notebook completion guarantees quiz success;
- comprehensive test coverage;
- print or PDF features designed to create an exam packet.

If the instructor explicitly changes this policy, adapt only after receiving
that instruction.

## HTML Delivery Requirements

Deliver one complete, deployable `.html` file suitable for Cloudflare Pages or
another static host.

Use:

- semantic HTML5;
- full CSS in a `<style>` element;
- full JavaScript in a `<script>` element;
- no build process;
- no database;
- no server-side code;
- no required external libraries;
- no analytics or response transmission;
- no cookies beyond browser storage required for function.

The file must work when opened locally and when hosted online.

## Student Controls

### Visible toolbar

The visible toolbar should contain only:

- **Save to This Device**
- save-status text

The toolbar may be sticky if it does not obscure content.

### Manage Saved Work

Place secondary controls inside a compact `<details>` element labeled:

**Manage Saved Work**

Include:

- Download Backup
- Restore Backup
- Clear Saved Work

This reduces interface clutter while protecting against device-specific storage
loss.

Do not include:

- Export to PDF;
- Print;
- browser-print instructions;
- print-specific controls.

## Local Saving

Implement saving with `localStorage`.

Requirements:

- save every textarea, text input, checkbox, radio button, and select field;
- use a stable, unique, versioned key containing course and chapter;
- restore work automatically on load;
- preserve line breaks;
- show saved, unsaved, and auto-saved status;
- warn that storage is browser- and device-specific;
- do not transmit responses to a server;
- require confirmation before clearing saved work.

Preferred behavior:

- mark unsaved changes after input;
- debounce auto-save;
- retain manual Save for reassurance;
- record the last save time.

## Backup and Restore

Provide a JSON backup download and restore function inside **Manage Saved Work**.

The backup payload must include:

- the notebook's exact versioned storage key or notebook identifier;
- the supported notebook version;
- the saved form-response data;
- an optional save timestamp.

Before asking permission to overwrite current work, the restore function must
parse and validate the file. It must confirm that:

1. the parsed value is an object;
2. `parsed.notebook` exactly matches the current notebook key or identifier;
3. `parsed.version` exactly matches the supported notebook version;
4. `parsed.data` exists, is an object, and is not an array.

Reject the file without applying any values when any validation check fails.
Show a clear student-facing error identifying it as an invalid or incompatible
backup for the current notebook.

Only after validation succeeds should the notebook:

- warn before overwriting current work;
- apply the restored form state;
- save the restored state to local storage;
- report successful restoration.

Do not silently accept arbitrary JSON, legacy unversioned objects, backups from
another chapter, or unsupported versions.

A backup is not a PDF and is not intended for reading or submission.

## No Print or PDF Functionality

Do not implement:

- `window.print()`;
- Export to PDF;
- print buttons;
- `@media print` layouts;
- print-only duplicate response elements;
- automatic PDF generation;
- instructions to save through a print dialog.

The notebook is designed for browser use.

## Editable Field Behavior

- Give every control a unique stable `id` and `name`.
- Associate visible labels with controls.
- Auto-expand textareas.
- Do not rely on placeholders as the only instruction.
- Maintain strong focus indicators.
- Use tablet-friendly touch targets.
- Avoid `contenteditable` when a native form control is more reliable.

## Accessibility

The notebook must:

- be fully keyboard usable;
- use proper heading order;
- provide visible labels;
- use native semantics whenever possible;
- meet accessible contrast standards;
- avoid color as the only signal;
- include a skip link for long pages;
- respect reduced-motion preferences;
- remain readable at 200% zoom;
- reflow cleanly on tablets and phones.

## Visual House Style

When the instructor supplies a course palette or reference page, follow it.

Otherwise use a restrained academic design with:

- a clear but unobtrusive masthead;
- distinct lecture sections;
- recognizable activity cards;
- neutral note fields;
- strong contrast;
- readable maximum width;
- responsive grids;
- generous but not excessive spacing.

Avoid:

- faux parchment;
- decorative clutter;
- oversized title regions;
- tiny type;
- excessive gradients;
- interface controls competing with the lecture content.

## What to Exclude

Do not include:

- long fill-in-the-blank sequences;
- copied slide sentences with missing words;
- written definitions for every term;
- repeated questions already answered on slides;
- extra reflections after every activity;
- paragraph writing during brief oral activities;
- busywork intended to prove attendance;
- trivia without interpretive purpose;
- a compressed background essay;
- every date, person, law, battle, or term mentioned;
- invented activities;
- identical response structures across chapters;
- server submission or tracking;
- PDF export or print functionality;
- completion grading language.

## Output Deliverables

By default, produce:

1. **Student Historian's Notebook HTML**
   - one self-contained `.html` file;
   - ready for Cloudflare hosting;
   - activities embedded in actual sequence;
   - interleaved selective note areas;
   - local autosave and manual save;
   - backup, restore, and clear controls under Manage Saved Work;
   - compact optional review;
   - no instructor answers;
   - no print or PDF feature.

2. **Instructor Design Note**
   - one page or less;
   - states the chapter inquiry;
   - explains the lecture-to-lecture arc;
   - confirms every authentic activity is included;
   - summarizes where each major lecture section appears;
   - identifies any source discrepancies;
   - reports the number of note fields, activity response fields, and optional
     review fields;
   - confirms the cognitive-load and technical audits.

Create an answer key only when explicitly requested.

## Quality-Control Audit

### Historical alignment

- Every substantive claim is supported.
- Names, dates, quotations, and causal claims are accurate.
- The notebook matches the lecture actually delivered.
- No unsupported interpretation has been introduced.

### Content coverage

- Every lecture's major sections are represented.
- Activities have not caused one portion of a lecture to crowd out the rest.
- Retrieval questions cover whole lectures.
- The learning check reflects the chapter's principal intellectual work.

### Activity alignment

- Every required Reveal-deck activity is included.
- Activities appear in correct order.
- Directions preserve the original task.
- Response structures are no larger than needed.
- Group activities leave a compact individual record.
- Debrief fields exist only when revision is explicit.
- No activity has been invented or expanded into busywork.

### Note-taking alignment

- Note fields are interleaved with lecture pacing.
- No lecture relies on one large transcript box.
- No more than three note areas appear per lecture.
- Notes are clearly optional and selective.
- Students can realistically listen while using the notebook.

### Cognitive-load audit

- No lecture-level **Listen for** list appears.
- Each note area contains only the cue needed for that stage of the lecture.
- Stage cues are concise, scannable, and not duplicated elsewhere.
- Writing occurs primarily during planned activities.
- Playful note-box messages remain brief and subordinate to the historical cue.
- No written vocabulary assignment appears.
- Optional review is collapsed and visibly optional.
- Optional review contains no more than six textareas.
- The notebook does not function as a comprehensive workbook.
- No language implies every field must be completed.
- Navigation and scrolling follow the lecture sequence.

### Technical audit

- HTML opens without errors.
- No external dependency is required for notebook function.
- Every control has a stable identifier.
- All controls save and restore.
- Save status updates correctly.
- Backup files contain notebook identifier, version, and response data.
- Restore rejects a backup whose notebook identifier does not match.
- Restore rejects an unsupported version.
- Restore rejects missing, invalid, or array-form response data.
- Restore validates before asking to overwrite current work.
- A valid restore applies the data and saves it locally.
- Clear requires confirmation.
- Data remains local.
- Textareas expand correctly.
- No print or PDF controls or code remain.
- Layout works on laptop, tablet, and phone.
- Page is keyboard accessible.
- Browser console contains no material errors.

### Variation audit

- The organizer fits this chapter.
- Note labels and activity formats follow the actual history.
- The notebook does not mechanically copy the previous chapter's structure.
- The ending reflects the chapter's argument.

## Final Instruction

Create a notebook students will keep open because it helps them listen, think,
participate, and preserve their work.

The Historian's Notebook succeeds when it disappears into the rhythm of the
class rather than becoming another assignment students must manage. Present
only the cue students need at the current lecture stage, preserve the
approachable note-box voice, and treat saved-work validation as a required
reliability feature rather than an optional enhancement.

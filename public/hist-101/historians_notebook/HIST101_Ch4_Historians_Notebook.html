Chapter Inquiry

How did war expose whose freedom, land, and promises an empire would protect?

How the lectures build

The chapter follows promises across four wartime settings: conditional freedom in Spanish Florida, kinship obligations in the Ohio Country, failed British diplomacy in 1755, and bargains that helped Britain win a global war. Victory did not settle whose claims counted; it magnified the disagreement.

The lecture map

  1. 1. The Town Called MoseA Spanish freedom offer becomes both refuge and weapon in an imperial borderland.
  2. 2. The RepublicsMultiethnic Ohio towns use kinship, trade, and diplomacy to create room between empires.

Lecture 1

The Town Called Mose

Guiding question

How could freedom serve enslaved people and an empire at the same time?

Argument to Watch For

Spanish Florida’s conditional freedom policy served imperial strategy, but freedom seekers used it to build real families, authority, and community at Fort Mose.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track why Spain offered conditional freedom, how the terms changed, and why strategic self-interest did not make legally recognized freedom unreal.

Jot the hinge, not the whole door.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record how Fort Mose functioned as community and garrison, and how Francisco Menéndez’s authority and re-enslavement show both the reality and fragility of status across empires.

Names, arrows, rough edges—all welcome.

Pause & Predict · Individual

Why become visible?

The Stono rebels were moving toward possible freedom. Why might they stop, display colors, beat drums, recruit, and make themselves loud rather than flee quietly?

Selective notes

At this stage

Track the southwest route, the limits of what the archive proves about Mose, Thornton’s inference about Central African backgrounds, and how war and the 1763 transfer ended the refuge.

Evidence beats polish.

Closing takeaway

A route toward freedom ran south, but its conditions, enforcement, and survival depended on imperial power that freedom seekers could use without controlling.

Lecture 2

The Republics

Guiding question

How did Ohio Country communities build security in a valley crowded with incompatible claims?

Argument to Watch For

Kinship and multiethnic town life created political leverage, but overlapping Native, colonial, commercial, and imperial claims made that security vulnerable.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record who lived in the Ohio Country, why the French called some towns “republics,” and how migration, trade, adoption, and intermarriage created multiethnic communities.

Sketch the relationships.

Predict · Individual

What can kinship defend?

What can a defense built on kinship and reciprocal obligation protect against well—and what can it not stop?

Selective notes

At this stage

Distinguish authority grounded in residence and kinship from French imperial claims, rival colonial charters and land companies, and contested Haudenosaunee assertions of authority.

Messy map, useful map.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track Hendrik’s grievances, why the broken Covenant Chain mattered in 1753, what Franklin’s plan would transfer, and why local power—not only missing identity—helps explain rejection.

Future You wants the why.

Closing takeaway

The valley was not an empty frontier or a two-sided contest; violence emerged from overlapping forms of authority that no common institution could reconcile.

Lecture 3

No Savage Should Inherit the Land

Guiding question

Why did Braddock’s powerful expedition collapse, and what did the defeat reveal about land and allegiance?

Argument to Watch For

Britain assembled a siege army without assembling a political coalition, and the result exposed the limits of material power in a contested human landscape.

Selective notes

At this stage

Separate Braddock’s alleged words, Shingas’s later retelling, and Charles Stuart’s retrospective record; use the passage as evidence of a diplomatic problem, not a verified transcript.

Source layers matter.

Predict · Individual

Numbers before the Monongahela

Braddock’s column numbered about 1,460; the French-led force about 891. What outcome do you expect on July 9, and what assumption drives your prediction?

Selective notes

At this stage

Track the road’s logistical meaning, weak Native alliance, the unexpected meeting engagement, terrain, officer losses, command breakdown, and coalition tactics—without repeating the myth of foolish Europeans.

Capture causes, not choreography.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record how frontier violence widened, why Pennsylvania struggled to respond, how Forbes paired adaptation with Easton diplomacy, and how Acadian removal reveals a different British attempt to govern contested land.

Leave a trail back to the argument.

Closing takeaway

Braddock’s road displayed imperial capacity, but Britain returned to the Forks only after military adaptation was paired with a political bargain.

Lecture 4

The Victory That Broke the Empire

Guiding question

How did Britain win through negotiation and then turn victory into a new imperial crisis?

Argument to Watch For

Britain mobilized colonial and Native cooperation through temporary bargains, then treated victory as authority to govern the costs and territory on terms its partners had never accepted.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track the disputes under Loudoun and the different mechanism Pitt used—requests, local officers, provincial finance, and expected reimbursement—without treating the bargain as colonial control of strategy.

A phrase is enough.

Discuss · Whole class

What kind of empire negotiates?

When wartime cooperation depends on negotiation, does that reveal a weak empire, a constitutional empire, or both? Keep one compact record of the class’s strongest conclusion.

Discuss · Whole class

Why did reimbursement work?

Why did Pitt’s reimbursement approach mobilize colonial resources more effectively than Loudoun’s demands, and what remained unresolved?

Discuss · Whole class

What was Easton?

Land settlement, wartime alliance, promise of restraint, or an unstable combination? Preserve the class’s best formulation.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track the linked military and diplomatic causes of victory and compare what 1763 meant to colonists, British officials, Native nations, and Fort Mose evacuees.

One victory, several memories.

Discuss · Whole class

Which consequence destabilized the empire?

Choose the strongest candidate—debt, removal of France, western land, standing army, or new confidence in central control—and keep one reason.

Closing takeaway

Victory rested on combination and temporary bargains; the postwar crisis began when imperial officials treated their own interpretation of those bargains as the only authoritative one.

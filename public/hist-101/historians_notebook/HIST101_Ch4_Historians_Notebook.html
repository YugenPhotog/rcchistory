Chapter Inquiry

How did competing claims to freedom, Native sovereignty, and legitimate authority make Britain’s victory in 1763 harder to govern?

How the lectures build

The chapter moves from freedom in Spanish Florida, to Native sovereignty in the Ohio Country, to religious authority under the Great Awakening, and finally to Britain’s continental victory. Each lecture asks who could make a claim, who could judge it, and what changed when imperial power expanded.

The lecture map

  1. 1. The Town Called MoseA Spanish freedom offer becomes both refuge and weapon in an imperial borderland.
  2. 2. The RepublicsMultiethnic Ohio towns build leverage through kinship, trade, and diplomacy before damaged alliances and competing claims produce a contingent military crisis.
  3. 3. Who Gets to Speak for God?The Great Awakening makes inward conversion a test of religious authority and widens the religious public.
  4. 4. Britain Wins a ContinentBritish victory redraws the map but leaves colonists, Native nations, and imperial officials with incompatible expectations.

Lecture 1

The Town Called Mose

Guiding question

How could freedom serve enslaved people and an empire at the same time?

Argument to Watch For

Spanish Florida’s conditional freedom policy served imperial strategy, but freedom seekers used it to build real families, authority, and community at Fort Mose.

Selective notes

Why become visible?

The Stono rebels were moving toward possible freedom. Why might they stop, display colors, beat drums, recruit, and make themselves loud rather than flee quietly?

Closing takeaway

A route toward freedom ran south, but its conditions, enforcement, and survival depended on imperial power that freedom seekers could use without controlling.

Lecture 2

The Republics

Guiding question

How did Native communities build political leverage in the contested Ohio Country, and why did that order break down into armed conflict in 1754?

Argument to Watch For

Kinship, trade, and multiethnic town life created political leverage, but competing claims, damaged alliances, and failed coordination narrowed that room for action and made armed escalation possible—not inevitable.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record who lived in the Ohio Country, why the French called some towns “republics,” and how migration, trade, adoption, and intermarriage created multiethnic communities.

Sketch the relationships.

Lecture 3

Who Gets to Speak for God?

Guiding question

How did the Great Awakening challenge established religious authority, and who gained a stronger voice in colonial religious life?

Argument to Watch For

The Great Awakening did not eliminate churches or clergy. It made credible conversion and spiritual fruit—not office alone—a test of authority, while widening participation within an unequal colonial society.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record the criticism of “dead formality,” the older Protestant emphasis on inward conversion, and how Jonathan Edwards’s Northampton revival of 1734 made that concern visible in one community.

What counted as genuine faith?

Selective notes

At this stage

Track George Whitefield’s itinerant preaching and print networks. Compare the authority of a settled parish minister with the authority of a traveling preacher whose audience chose to hear him.

How could authority become portable?

Selective notes

At this stage

Explain Gilbert Tennent’s challenge to an “unconverted ministry,” the New Light–Old Light dispute, and the importance of new colleges. Note how women, enslaved people, and Native converts entered a wider religious public without receiving equal legal or political rights.

Keep the change and its limits together.

Closing takeaway

By asking whether a minister showed genuine conversion, revivalists made inherited authority open to judgment. The Great Awakening created new religious networks and voices without making colonial society equal.

Lecture 4

Britain Wins a Continent

Guiding question

How could winning a war make an empire less stable?

Argument to Watch For

Britain turned its material advantages into victory by working with colonial assemblies and Native nations. But the Peace of Paris treated a populated continent as something an empire could simply redraw and govern.

Selective notes

At this stage

Explain why Britain kept losing despite its advantages, then track how William Pitt changed the war: he accommodated provincial officers, asked assemblies for troops and supplies, promised reimbursement, and directed British resources toward conquering New France.

Why did consent work better than coercion?

Closing takeaway

Britain won the continent in 1763. It did not win the right to govern everyone living on it: Native sovereignty persisted, colonists expected the rewards of partnership, and Britain faced a larger, more expensive empire.

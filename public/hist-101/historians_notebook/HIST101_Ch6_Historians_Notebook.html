Chapter Inquiry

How could Americans win independence, decide who could claim liberty, and create governments strong enough to rule but limited enough to obey?

How the lectures build

The chapter moves from military survival to the social claims released by revolutionary language, then to state and confederation experiments, and finally to the contested Constitution. Independence solved the problem of British rule while opening harder problems of power, belonging, and legitimacy.

The lecture map

  1. 1. Britain Kept Winning. Why Did the Revolution Not End?American survival, local allegiance, and foreign intervention turn British victories into an unwinnable political war.
  2. 2. Liberty Was Declared. Who Could Claim It?Revolutionary language creates claims whose outcomes depend on institutions, leverage, and coercive power.
  3. 3. The King Was Gone. Who Had the Right to Rule?State constitutions and the Confederation experiment with ways to turn resistance into binding authority.
  4. 4. What Could Destroy the Republic: Too Much Power or Too Little?The Constitution answers one diagnosis of crisis while ratification and amendment preserve the rival fear.

Lecture 1

Britain Kept Winning. Why Did the Revolution Not End?

Guiding question

Why was defeating American armies easier than restoring British authority?

Argument to Watch For

Britain could win battles and occupy cities, but it could not destroy the revolutionary state, protect exposed supporters, and concentrate enough power before the conflict became global.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track the difference among winning battles, occupying territory, destroying the Continental Army, and rebuilding legitimate authority across a decentralized society.

Separate the kinds of victory.

Pause & Predict · Individual

What does Washington need most?

He cannot hold New York or New Jersey and is losing men. Choose what he needs most right now.

Your choice

Selective notes

At this stage

Record why preserving the army mattered, how Trenton and Princeton changed expectations rather than territory, and why British inability to protect loyalists weakened restoration.

Small victories can move big politics.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track why Saratoga changed French calculations, how global theaters diluted British power, and why Yorktown required American endurance, French land and naval power, and British political exhaustion.

Connect scale to outcome.

Rank the Causes · Individual

Why did Cornwallis surrender?

Rank the five necessary conditions from 1 (most important) to 5, then defend your first choice in one sentence.

Closing takeaway

Independence resulted from a precarious convergence of military survival, local political control, British constraints, and foreign intervention—not inevitable national strength.

Lecture 2

Liberty Was Declared. Who Could Claim It?

Guiding question

When Americans declared universal liberty, who possessed enough power to make a claim enforceable?

Argument to Watch For

Ideals created real openings, but courts, legislatures, armies, property systems, and territorial power determined which claims became law and which claimants became threats.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track how constitutional language, wartime opportunity, litigation, legislation, and racialized nation-making connect ideals to outcomes without reducing the story to automatic progress or empty hypocrisy.

Follow the claim to the answer.

Selective notes

At this stage

Record the limits of Dunmore’s offer, Black movement toward both armies, regional emancipation paths, and why northern change and southern slaveholding power developed together.

Routes, risks, results.

Pause & Predict · Individual

The same claim?

Enslaved people used the Revolution to claim liberty. Native nations also used the Revolution. Predict whether they were asking for the same thing.

Your prediction

Selective notes

At this stage

Distinguish Black claims to freedom and membership from Native claims to collective sovereignty; then track women, Loyalists, and dissenters as further tests of selective transformation.

Keep the distinction sharp.

Rank · Individual or pair

Which claim could succeed?

Rank from 1 (least threatening to existing property and sovereignty) to 4. Then note what the ranking reveals about institutional priorities.

Closing takeaway

The Revolution expanded routes for claiming freedom while securing slavery, settler jurisdiction, patriarchy, and coercive national boundaries in uneven combinations.

Lecture 3

The King Was Gone. Who Had the Right to Rule?

Guiding question

How could Americans create governments they were morally obligated to obey after declaring their old governments illegitimate?

Argument to Watch For

Americans separated constituent power from ordinary government, but disagreed over whether liberty was safer through responsive institutions, balanced institutions, or a deliberately weak union.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track why independence did not authorize taxation or punishment by itself, and how a written constitution could place the people’s constituent authority above officeholders.

Elections are only the start.

You Are Writing a Constitution · Whole class vote

Which danger comes first?

Choose the danger you would design against: rulers insulated from the people, or a momentary majority that crushes others.

Your first fear

Selective notes

At this stage

Record each state’s primary fear, institutions, and constitution-making procedure; avoid treating Pennsylvania as simple democratic failure or Massachusetts as rejection of popular sovereignty.

Compare diagnoses, not winners.

Pause & Reflect · Individual

Who enforces higher law?

A constitution is supposed to bind the government operating under it. In 1780, who can enforce that limit?

Selective notes

At this stage

Track why the Articles made historical sense, what the Confederation accomplished and could not compel, and how equal statehood for settlers rested on claims over Native land.

Keep achievement and limit together.

Closing takeaway

Popular sovereignty became an institutional procedure as well as a slogan, yet ‘the people’ remained bounded and the anti-colonial republic acted imperially toward Native nations.

Lecture 4

What Could Destroy the Republic: Too Much Power or Too Little?

Guiding question

Did liberty face a greater threat from government too powerful to control or government too weak to govern?

Argument to Watch For

The Constitution strengthened national power in response to genuine problems, but its legitimacy depended on a contested ratification that forced limits into a settlement protecting property, slavery, and territorial expansion.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track how creditors, farmers, nationalists, and state majorities interpreted debt, hard-money taxes, relief laws, commerce, treaties, and public credit differently.

Whose crisis? Keep asking.

Decide and Defend · Individual

Resistance or destruction?

A Revolutionary veteran closes a courthouse to stop seizure of a neighbor’s farm. Is he defending the people from government or destroying a government the people made?

Your judgment

Selective notes

At this stage

Record the Regulators’ grievances and tactics, Springfield’s political uses, and how the Convention exceeded revision by creating a government acting directly on individuals.

Watch the change in scale.

Selective notes

At this stage

Track slavery’s structural place, Federalist and Anti-Federalist fears, close and negotiated ratification, and how the Bill of Rights shows constitutional losers reshaping the result.

The settlement stayed unsettled.

Closing takeaway

The Constitution did not settle whether power or weakness posed the greater danger; it institutionalized the argument through stronger government, divided authority, popular authorization, and written limits.

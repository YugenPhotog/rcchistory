Bridging from Lecture 3 — and Closing the Series

The first three lectures were about fear: holy war in New Mexico, total war in New England, and at Salem a multi-causal crisis partly shaped by frontier war. This final lecture turns fear inside out. It follows people captured and carried into the Native and French world that Puritans described as the Devil’s territory, then asks what their documented adult lives reveal about family, faith, community, and belonging. If the series has been about fear, it ends by examining forms of belonging that developed beyond Puritan New England.

Learning Objectives

  1. Describe the captivity experiences of Eunice Williams and Esther Wheelwright during Queen Anne’s War, including the 1704 Deerfield raid and its aftermath.
  2. Analyze the documented patterns by which some English captives built adult lives in Native or French Catholic communities rather than relocating permanently to New England.
  3. Evaluate how historians reconstruct the life of a person who left almost no words of her own, distinguishing what archival silence reveals from what Eunice Williams’s documented actions can establish.
  4. Synthesize the series’ continental argument: that borders among English, Native, and French worlds were porous and that captives developed different forms of belonging across them.
  5. Assess what these captivity stories reveal about the limits of colonial power — that a society could lose not only wars but its own people, to a world it insisted was inferior.

Section 1: Two Girls, Two Captures

Deerfield, 1704

This lecture is built as a paired portrait of two English Puritan girls captured separately during the same imperial war. Their lives belong together because both moved beyond Puritan New England, but they should not be forced into one identical pattern. Eunice was adopted into a Kanien’kehá:ka family and built her adult life at Kahnawake. Esther moved through Wabanaki family life, a French mission setting, and an Ursuline institution. Their different paths demand comparison rather than collapse.

The first is Eunice Williams. In February 1704, during Queen Anne’s War, a French and Native force attacked Deerfield, Massachusetts, before dawn. Fifty-seven people were killed and 112 were taken captive and marched north through winter toward Canada. Among them was seven-year-old Eunice, daughter of minister John Williams. Her mother was killed on the march. Eunice was taken to Kahnawake, a Catholic Kanien’kehá:ka (Mohawk) community near Montreal with its own political authority, kinship networks, and diplomatic life. There she was adopted into a Kanien’kehá:ka family.

Her father, John Williams, was eventually ransomed and returned to New England, where he wrote a famous captivity narrative, The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion. The title itself carries the Puritan assumption that captivity was a trial from which the faithful were ‘redeemed’ and returned home. But the title also carries a wound the book cannot close: John Williams was redeemed. His daughter was not.

Wells, and a Different Path

The second portrait is Esther Wheelwright, captured around 1703 near Wells, Maine, as a young girl during a Wabanaki raid. She was incorporated into a Wabanaki family, later moved through a French mission setting, and eventually entered the Ursuline convent in Quebec. Esther became a nun and rose to mother superior of one of French Canada’s most important religious institutions. Ann Little’s biography emphasizes these successive settings as ‘many captivities’: changing forms of kinship, dependency, institutional discipline, and belonging rather than one clean adult decision equivalent to Eunice’s life at Kahnawake.

Instructor note

Esther Wheelwright is the less familiar of the two; keep her portrait proportionally shorter in live delivery. Her function is not to duplicate Eunice’s ‘choice to stay’ but to demonstrate another form of belonging beyond Puritan New England. Preserve the differences: Eunice’s Kanien’kehá:ka adoption, marriage, family, and repeated returns to Kahnawake are not the same experience as Esther’s movement through Wabanaki, French mission, and Ursuline communities.

The Conceptual Payoff

Together, the two lives demonstrate different forms of belonging beyond Puritan New England. Their experiences show that borders among English, Native, and French worlds were crossable, but they do not describe one shared destination or one identical choice.

Section 2: Different Forms of Belonging

Eunice’s Documented Adult Life

The heart of Eunice Williams’s story lies in a durable pattern of adult action. At Kahnawake she was baptized Catholic, given the name A’ongote, adopted into a Kanien’kehá:ka family, married a Kanien’kehá:ka man, and raised a family. Her New England relatives sent emissaries, offered ransoms, and wrote letters in repeated efforts to relocate her permanently. Eunice visited Deerfield and nearby communities three times between 1740 and 1761. After each visit she returned to Kahnawake. Her marriage, family, repeated refusals to relocate, and returns document the durability of her Kanien’kehá:ka and Catholic life without requiring the historian to claim access to an unrecorded inner preference.

It is important to hold two truths at once. Eunice was taken as a child by force; the violence of the Deerfield raid and winter march should not be romanticized. Her adult conduct, however, repeatedly sustained family, community, and Catholic life at Kahnawake. The Puritan captivity narrative expected resistance, redemption, and permanent return. Eunice did not follow that expected arc. The evidence supports that behavioral conclusion more firmly than any claim about what she privately felt.

Esther’s Many Captivities

Esther Wheelwright’s path was not Eunice’s in parallel. She moved through several communities and forms of dependency: birth in a prominent Puritan family, capture and incorporation into a Wabanaki family, residence in a French mission environment, and life within the disciplined institution of the Ursuline convent. She took vows, became a Catholic nun, and eventually rose to mother superior. Her story demonstrates belonging beyond Puritan New England, but through a succession of relationships and institutions unlike Eunice’s family and community life at Kahnawake.

Discussion prompt

Write one sentence (1–2 min): Eunice was taken by force as a child. Her adult actions repeatedly sustained her life at Kahnawake. What is the difference between those two statements, and why does it matter?

The Conceptual Payoff

Captivity was forced. Adult belonging developed differently. Eunice’s documented choices sustained a Kanien’kehá:ka and Catholic family life at Kahnawake; Esther’s successive communities and institutions produced another form of belonging beyond Puritan New England. Comparison clarifies both lives only when their differences remain visible.

Section 3: The Silence of Eunice Williams

Here the lecture pauses on an archival problem. We have described what Eunice Williams repeatedly did, but she left almost no words of her own explaining why. Nearly everything that survives reaches us through others: her father’s narrative, relatives’ letters, and records produced by people trying to redeem her. Eunice herself is nearly silent in the surviving New England archive.

Side box

The Historian and the Silent Subject

John Demos’s The Unredeemed Captive (1994) is built openly around a difficult question: how can a historian write about a woman who left almost no record of her thoughts? Demos marks moments of inference and reconstruction instead of presenting them as documented fact. That methodological honesty matters. Eunice’s silence reveals the structure and limits of the archive: New England relatives and officials wrote extensively, while her own explanations were not preserved. Silence alone cannot prove misery, contentment, preference, or motive. The strongest evidence is behavioral—her marriage and family at Kahnawake, repeated refusals to relocate permanently, three visits to New England followed by three returns, and the durability of her Kanien’kehá:ka and Catholic life. The historian can document that pattern while remaining honest about the interior life the archive cannot recover.

The Conceptual Payoff

Archival silence is historically meaningful because it reveals whose voices were preserved and whose were not. It does not, by itself, disclose Eunice’s motives. The case for durable belonging rests most securely on repeated, documented action, while the limits of the archive remain part of the interpretation.

Instructor note

Keep this section contained—it is a single beat, not a second backbone. Lecture 2 used source criticism as its whole architecture; this side box is lighter and thematically different. Its purpose is to distinguish archival silence from behavioral evidence, then return to the belonging spine. Do not let it expand into a full methods lecture.

Discussion prompt

Quick write (1–2 min): What can Eunice’s silence reveal about the archive—and what can it not prove about her inner motives?

Section 4: What Belonging Reveals

The Border Was Porous

Eunice Williams and Esther Wheelwright matter beyond their individual stories because their lives expose the limits of a hard boundary between ‘colonial’ and ‘Indian’ worlds. English, Native, and French communities remained politically and culturally distinct, but people crossed among them. Some captives were incorporated into Kanien’kehá:ka or Wabanaki kin networks; others entered French Catholic institutions. Frontier movement was therefore not only a story of colonists expanding outward but also of colonists being absorbed into societies the colonies described as inferior.

This is why the captivity stories belong at the end of a series about Native and imperial power. They reveal a limit that wars and treaties do not fully capture. A colony could lose a battle and recover; it was harder to be unable, across decades of effort, to relocate the minister’s daughter permanently. Eunice’s adult life challenged Puritan claims of civilizational superiority through documented conduct: marriage, family, Catholic faith, repeated refusals to relocate, and returns to Kahnawake after each New England visit.

The Conceptual Payoff

One deep limit on colonial power was social as well as military: colonial authorities could not compel an adult life to conform to the captivity narrative’s expected ending. Eunice’s documented family, faith, community, and repeated returns to Kahnawake could not be ransomed away.

Discussion prompt

Connect across the series (1–2 min): Salem hunted the Devil amid fear of the frontier world. Eunice built her adult life within it. What do these responses tell you about how close that world was to New England?

Closing Synthesis — The Whole Series

This lecture ends a series built around one continental argument approached through four human stories. In New England, a shifting regional war brought colonial settlements near collapse. In New Mexico, autonomous Pueblo nations expelled Spain for twelve years; the revolt redistributed animals and may have accelerated horse-centered networks that Native peoples had already begun building before 1680. At Salem, frontier war partly shaped a multi-causal crisis. Here, at the end, Eunice Williams and Esther Wheelwright reveal different forms of belonging beyond Puritan New England.

The throughline holds across all four: Native nations constrained colonial power and shaped the terms of survival, expansion, and belonging. They did so through war, revolt, diplomacy, kinship, adoption, and the capacity of communities such as Kahnawake to incorporate outsiders into durable family and religious lives. Colonists were never as dominant, secure, or clearly superior as their rhetoric claimed. The continent remained overwhelmingly Native ground.

For students, the durable takeaway is larger than any one episode. Early America was not simply thirteen English colonies with a frontier at the edge, but a contested continent of many peoples in which Native power shaped colonial possibilities. Eunice Williams provides the final, quiet example: after three New England visits, she returned each time to Kahnawake, where her adult family, faith, and community remained.

Open analytical question

Across four lectures — a war, a revolt, a witch hunt, and a captivity — you have seen the same claim proven four different ways: that Native power set the terms in early America. Which of the four proofs do you find most convincing, and which most surprising — and what would it change about how you read the rest of U.S. history to keep this continental view in mind?

Independent Study Slide Candidates — Do Not Lose

Live delivery carries three short activities: distinguishing forced childhood capture from documented adult action, separating archival silence from evidence of behavior, and connecting Eunice’s life to the series-wide argument. As the finale, the Independent Study also consolidates the four lectures. Target: 3–6 slides.

  1. The Deerfield raid in depth — the 1704 attack, 57 people killed, 112 taken captive, the winter march north, and how one frontier raid produced the story anchoring this lecture. This supplies the violence compressed in live delivery so class time can focus on adult belonging and evidence.
  2. The captivity narrative as a genre — how accounts like John Williams’s The Redeemed Captive shaped New England’s self-image, and why Eunice did not follow the expected arc of resistance, redemption, and permanent return.
  3. Esther Wheelwright in full — her movement from the Wells raid through Wabanaki family life, a French mission setting, and the Ursuline convent, emphasizing Ann Little’s interpretation of several communities and forms of captivity, dependency, and belonging.
  4. Series synthesis slide — the four lectures on one screen, with Lecture 2 noting that Native horse networks predated 1680 and Lecture 3 presenting frontier war as one cause within a multi-causal crisis.

Suggested Readings for Instructor Preparation

John Demos, The Unredeemed Captive: A Family Story from Early America (1994)

The central account of Eunice Williams and an essential model of methodological honesty about reconstructing a silent subject. Use it while distinguishing the limits of archival silence from the stronger behavioral record of Eunice’s adult life.

Ann M. Little, The Many Captivities of Esther Wheelwright (2016)

The major modern biography of Esther Wheelwright and the key source for understanding her movement through several communities and forms of captivity, dependency, and belonging rather than treating her life as a duplicate of Eunice’s.

John Williams, The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion (1707)

The father’s own captivity narrative — the primary source whose title and framing the lecture interrogates. Valuable for reading Puritan assumptions about captivity, redemption, and belonging in their own words.

Evan Haefeli and Kevin Sweeney, Captors and Captives: The 1704 French and Indian Raid on Deerfield (2003)

The definitive scholarly account of the Deerfield raid itself; essential background for Section 1 and the Independent Study deep-dive slide.

June Namias, White Captives: Gender and Ethnicity on the American Frontier (1993)

Broader context on captivity, gender, and identity across the colonial frontier; useful for situating Eunice and Esther within the larger phenomenon of captives who did and did not return.

Daniel K. Richter, Facing East from Indian Country (2001)

The continental, Native-centered frame that has run through the entire series; directly supports the closing synthesis’s argument that these captivity stories reveal the porousness of the colonial border.

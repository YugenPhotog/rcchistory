Essential question

If virtue sustains democracy, the raising of children becomes a public concern. When did Americans decide that children had claims on society that could override their parents, and who gained the power to decide what a child needed?

Introductory Framing

The previous lectures traced reformers who tried to perfect adults through associations, institutions, and law. This lecture follows the same moral project to the next generation. If the republic depended on virtuous citizens, then the way children were raised could not be left entirely to private families. Over the course of the nineteenth century, Americans gradually came to see childhood as a distinct, vulnerable stage of life that deserved public protection, and to accept that the state and private societies could sometimes step between parents and children.

The lecture spans a long period, from Puritan family law in the 1640s to the juvenile court of 1899 and federal child labor law in 1938, so that students can see how antebellum reform ideas played out over time. Its argument runs along a double track. On one side, reformers made real gains: children were removed from adult prisons, abused children gained a legal remedy, and child labor was eventually outlawed. On the other side, every new protection also gave reformers, usually middle-class Protestants, new power over poor, immigrant, Catholic, Black, and Native families. Historians such as Anthony Platt, in The Child Savers (1969), stress the controlling side; others, such as Linda Gordon, in Heroes of Their Own Lives (1988), show that poor women and children themselves often sought out child-protection agencies for help. Both perspectives inform this essay.

Key Terms: Parens Patriae, Placing Out, and Child Saving

Parens patriae, Latin for “parent of the country,” is a legal doctrine holding that the state has the authority and duty to protect those who cannot protect themselves, including children. In English law it originally described the Crown’s guardianship over orphans and the mentally incompetent. American courts expanded it in the nineteenth century to justify intervening in families.

Placing out was the practice of sending dependent children to live and work in other households, sometimes under formal indenture. It was common in colonial America and was revived on a mass scale by the orphan trains.

Child saving is the term historians use, following the reformers themselves, for the late-nineteenth-century movement to rescue children from poverty, cruelty, vice, and crime through new institutions and laws.

The Colonial Family as a Little Commonwealth

Puritan New Englanders thought of the family as a “little commonwealth,” the phrase the historian John Demos took for the title of his classic study. The household was a miniature state, with the father as its governor, responsible for the religious instruction, discipline, and work of his wife, children, and servants. Good order in families was the foundation of good order in the colony, and magistrates could intervene when a household failed.

Massachusetts law reflected both sides of that idea. The Body of Liberties of 1641, the colony’s first legal code, gave children some protections: it allowed children to complain to authorities if parents willfully and unreasonably denied them a timely or convenient marriage or treated them with unnatural severity. A few years later the colony adopted the so-called Stubborn Child Law (1646), modeled on Deuteronomy 21, which made it a capital offense for a son of sufficient age and understanding to be stubborn and rebellious and to refuse to obey his parents. No one is known to have been executed under it, but it shows that obedience was understood as a matter of public order and salvation. Massachusetts laws of 1642 and 1647 also required parents and masters to teach children to read and required towns to maintain schools.

When families failed, communities stepped in. In New England, overseers of the poor “placed out” orphaned and poor children with other families, often as indentured servants or apprentices. In the Chesapeake, where high death rates left many children orphaned, orphans’ courts appointed guardians and supervised the management of orphans’ estates. Some towns used a “vendue,” auctioning the care of paupers, including children, to whoever would take them at the lowest public cost. By the late eighteenth century, religious and charitable groups began founding orphan asylums, such as the Ursuline orphanage in New Orleans (1720s) and the Charleston Orphan House (1790), marking a shift from kinship-based care toward organized benevolence.

New Ideas of Childhood

Two intellectual shifts changed how educated Americans thought about children. The first came from John Locke, whose Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690) described the mind at birth as something like a blank slate, a tabula rasa, and whose Some Thoughts Concerning Education (1693) urged parents to shape character through habit, example, and reasoned discipline rather than harsh beatings. If children were formed by experience, then environment and education mattered enormously, and the Calvinist emphasis on original sin lost some of its hold.

The second shift came from Jean-Jacques Rousseau, whose Emile (1762) celebrated childhood as a natural, innocent stage of life to be protected rather than hurried through. Romantic writers and artists turned the innocent child into a cultural ideal, and nineteenth-century reformers used sentimental images of children to stir public feeling. Together, Lockean environmentalism and Romantic innocence suggested that children were improvable, vulnerable, and worth saving, and that society had both the ability and the obligation to intervene when their surroundings failed them. These ideas joined the antebellum faith in institutions discussed in Lecture 2.

The Urban Crisis of Childhood

Industrialization and immigration made the problem visible. In mill towns, children worked long hours tending machines; in cities, poor children sold newspapers, matches, and flowers, scavenged, begged, and sometimes stole. Disease, accidents, and desertion left many without parents. Reformers called the children who lived and worked on the streets “street Arabs” and warned that they would grow into a dangerous criminal class.

In New York, the city’s chief of police, George W. Matsell, reported in 1849 that thousands of vagrant and idle children roamed the streets, and estimates of New York’s homeless or vagrant children in the 1850s ran into the tens of thousands. Such figures were impressionistic and often inflated for effect, but they captured a real alarm. Churches, missions, and charities mobilized, and civil society turned to children as a new field of reform.

Public Schools as a Children’s Right

Lecture 2’s Pierian Spring section covered Horace Mann and the common school. The new angle in this lecture is that, as the century went on, reformers increasingly framed schooling not just as a civic duty but as something society owed children, especially poor, immigrant, and working children who might otherwise be in factories or on the streets. That reframing helped justify compulsory attendance laws, beginning with Massachusetts in 1852, and connected the school to the larger child-saving movement.

Houses of Refuge and Ex parte Crouse

Before the 1820s, children convicted of crimes were held in the same jails and prisons as adults. Reformers in New York’s Society for the Prevention of Pauperism, renamed the Society for the Reformation of Juvenile Delinquents, argued that this turned young offenders into hardened criminals. On January 1, 1825, the society opened the New York House of Refuge, the first institution in the United States specifically for juvenile delinquents. Boston opened its own house of reformation in 1826 and Philadelphia a House of Refuge in 1828.

The houses of refuge reflected the moral laboratory logic of Lecture 2. They aimed to remold children through rigorous discipline, basic schooling, religious instruction, and hard work, often contract labor in shops making shoes, brushes, or chair seats. Their charters were broad. They admitted not only children convicted of crimes but also children found to be vagrant, idle, or disorderly, and children committed by their own parents as unmanageable. Inmates could be held until they reached adulthood and were often indentured out as servants or apprentices on release. Historians such as Robert Mennel and Steven Schlossman have documented the harsh discipline, punishments, and escapes that marked these institutions.

Ex parte Crouse

The legal foundation for this system came from Pennsylvania. Mary Ann Crouse was committed to the Philadelphia House of Refuge on a complaint by her mother that the girl was beyond her control. Her father sought her release by a writ of habeas corpus, arguing that she had been imprisoned without a jury trial. In Ex parte Crouse, decided by the Pennsylvania Supreme Court in early 1839 (the case is often dated to its 1838 term), the court rejected his claim. The House of Refuge, it held, was not a prison but a school, and the purpose of commitment was not punishment but reformation:

Primary source

“The object of the charity is reformation, by training its inmates to industry; by imbuing their minds with principles of morality and religion; by furnishing them with means to earn a living; and, above all, by separating them from the corrupting influence of improper associates.”

— Pennsylvania Supreme Court, Ex parte Crouse, 4 Wharton 9 (Pa. 1839)

The court then asked whether natural parents who were unequal to the task of education, or unworthy of it, might be superseded by the parens patriae, the common guardian of the community. Its answer was yes. Parental rights were not absolute; when parents failed, the state could step in for the child’s welfare, without the procedural protections required in a criminal trial.

The decision became the legal basis for juvenile reformatories, industrial schools, and eventually the juvenile court. It embodied the lecture’s central tension. The principle that children have interests the state must protect, even against their parents, was a genuine step toward children’s rights. But the same principle allowed children to be confined for vague offenses without due process, and it gave middle-class officials broad power over poor and immigrant families. The U.S. Supreme Court did not require basic due process protections in juvenile proceedings until In re Gault in 1967.

Analytical significance

Houses of refuge removed children from adult prisons, but they operated much like prisons themselves. Ex parte Crouse gave the state a parent’s authority over children, a doctrine that could protect children and could also be used to discipline families who did not share reformers’ values.

Girls, Moral Reform, and the Age of Consent

Reformers paid special attention to girls. Urban poverty pushed some young women and girls into prostitution, and in the 1830s evangelical women organized against it. The New York Female Moral Reform Society, founded in 1834, published the Advocate of Moral Reform, which reached a wide national readership, and grew into the American Female Moral Reform Society in 1839. Its members visited brothels, sometimes published the names of men who frequented them, campaigned for laws against seduction, and ran shelters and employment programs for women and girls. Similar societies and “Magdalen” asylums, named for Mary Magdalene, appeared in Boston, Philadelphia, and other cities. Carroll Smith-Rosenberg has shown that these women were bold critics of male sexual privilege and the double standard.

Their help came with conditions. Rescue homes demanded religious conversion, domestic training, and strict supervision; inmates could not always leave; and reformers often treated the girls as sinners to be redeemed rather than as victims of exploitation. Female moral reform thus blurred the line between protection and punishment, and it built some of the institutional machinery, rescue homes, reformatories, and placement networks, later used to control girls’ sexuality.

Later in the century, women reformers took up the legal age of consent, the age below which a girl could not legally consent to sexual relations. In the 1880s it was ten or twelve in most states and as low as seven in Delaware. After the British journalist W. T. Stead’s 1885 exposé of child prostitution in London, the WCTU and allied groups petitioned state legislatures across the country, and by the early twentieth century most states had raised the age to sixteen or eighteen. The campaign is a striking example of women exercising political power before they could vote.

Charles Loring Brace and the Orphan Trains

Charles Loring Brace (1826–1890) was the son of a prominent Connecticut family. He studied at Yale and then came to New York to study for the ministry, where missionary work among the poor horrified him. In 1853, at age twenty-six, he helped found the Children’s Aid Society. The society opened industrial schools for poor children, reading rooms, and, in 1854, a Newsboys’ Lodging House, where boys who sold papers could sleep, bathe, and save money for a small charge, so that they would not feel they were receiving charity.

Brace’s most famous program was “placing out,” revived on a national scale. He believed that the best place for a poor city child was not an institution but a farm family in the countryside, where fresh air, work, and Christian family life would remake the child. Beginning in 1854, when a group of children traveled to Dowagiac, Michigan, the society sent companies of children by train to rural towns in the Midwest and beyond, where local committees arranged for families to take them in. The New York Foundling Hospital, a Catholic institution founded in 1869 by the Sisters of Charity, ran its own placement trains, which sent children to Catholic families. Between 1854 and 1929, these “orphan trains” moved an estimated 200,000 to 250,000 children, many of whom had at least one living parent.

Brace explained his thinking in The Dangerous Classes of New York, and Twenty Years’ Work Among Them (1872). He described the city’s ignorant, destitute, and abandoned youth as the class most dangerous to its property, morals, and political life, and he argued that removing them from the city served both the children and society. Placement drained the city of children reformers feared and filled the rural demand for labor.

The results were mixed. Some children found loving homes and later remembered the program with gratitude. Others were treated as unpaid farm hands, separated from siblings, abused, or moved from family to family, and the society’s follow-up was often thin. Catholic leaders objected that Brace placed Catholic children in Protestant homes to convert them, which is one reason Catholic institutions launched their own placements. The lecture notes that orphan trains marked a significant shift: reformers were willing to place what they judged to be the interests of the child ahead of the claims of the birth family, a principle that could rescue children from neglect and could also break up poor families whose only offense was poverty.

The Shadow Side

The lecture insists that child protection was never color-blind. After the Civil War, several Southern states used apprenticeship laws to bind thousands of Black children, often without their parents’ consent, to white employers, frequently their former enslavers. Freedpeople protested, and the Freedmen’s Bureau and federal courts intervened in some cases; in In re Turner (1867), Chief Justice Salmon Chase, sitting on circuit, ruled that a Maryland apprenticeship of a Black girl violated the Civil Rights Act of 1866. Beginning with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in 1879, the federal government removed many thousands of Native children to boarding schools designed, in the words of Carlisle’s founder Richard Henry Pratt, to “kill the Indian in him, and save the man.” The same era that celebrated the rescue of white street children built systems that separated Black and Native children from their families in the name of uplift.

Analytical significance

Brace’s orphan trains embodied both the hopes and the prejudices of child saving: faith in rural Protestant family life, a desire to protect children, and a willingness to sever them from poor and Catholic families. Measures applied to Black and Native children in the same period show how race shaped who was “saved” and how.

The Mary Ellen Moment, 1874–1875

The mini-lecture on Mary Ellen Wilson tells one of the best-documented stories in the history of child welfare. Mary Ellen was born in New York in 1864 to Irish immigrant parents. Her father, a Union soldier, died in the war, and her mother, unable to support her, boarded her with a caretaker and eventually stopped paying. The city’s Department of Charities took custody of the child and, in 1866, handed her to Thomas and Mary McCormack, who claimed that Thomas was her father, without verifying the claim. After Thomas died, his widow married Francis Connolly. For years Mary Connolly beat the child, cut her with scissors, kept her locked indoors, and left her poorly fed and clothed.

In 1873 Etta Angell Wheeler, a Methodist mission worker in the tenements of what is now Hell’s Kitchen, learned of the child from neighbors and saw her condition. Police and charities told Wheeler they could do nothing without clear evidence of a crime. She turned to Henry Bergh, founder of the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (1866), who agreed to act as a private citizen. His lawyer, Elbridge T. Gerry, did not argue that Mary Ellen was an animal entitled to animal-cruelty protection, though that myth has circulated ever since. Instead, Gerry obtained an old common-law writ, de homine replegiando, allowing the court to remove a person held unlawfully. On April 9, 1874, Judge Abraham Lawrence of the New York Supreme Court issued the writ, and police brought Mary Ellen into court wrapped in a carriage blanket.

Her testimony the next day, reported in the New York Times and other papers, was devastating. She described being whipped nearly every day with a twisted rawhide whip, being struck with scissors, never being allowed to play with other children, and being locked in a room when Mrs. Connolly went out. Neighbors and Wheeler corroborated her account. In a separate criminal trial that April, Mary Connolly was convicted of felonious assault and sentenced to one year of hard labor. Mary Ellen was made a ward of the court; after a short stay in an institution for girls, she was raised by members of Etta Wheeler’s family in upstate New York. She married in 1888, raised children, named one of her daughters Etta, and died in 1956 at age ninety-two.

The case led directly to a new institution. In December 1874, Bergh, Gerry, and the philanthropist John D. Wright founded the New York Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children, incorporated by the state in 1875, generally described as the first child protection agency in the world. New York law gave such societies the power to investigate, bring complaints, and with police help remove children from abusive homes, and poor New Yorkers came to call its agents “the Cruelty.” Similar societies spread quickly across the United States and abroad. Private charity had acquired public authority.

Jacob Riis and Structural Reform

The Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children could rescue individual children from cruel adults. It could not change the conditions in which millions of children lived. Jacob Riis (1849–1914), a Danish immigrant who had known poverty himself in his first years in New York, became a police reporter for the New York Tribune in 1877 and covered the Lower East Side from the police headquarters on Mulberry Street. In 1887 he began using newly invented magnesium flash powder to photograph tenement interiors, cellars, lodging houses, and alleys at night. The flash was dangerous; it could start fires, and Riis later wrote of setting fire to things more than once.

Riis first presented his images in magic-lantern slide lectures and then in How the Other Half Lives (1890), one of the first American books to use halftone reproductions of photographs. Its introduction opens:

Primary source

“Long ago it was said that ‘one half of the world does not know how the other half lives.’ That was true then. It did not know because it did not care.”

— Jacob A. Riis, How the Other Half Lives: Studies Among the Tenements of New York (1890), introduction

Riis changed the question reformers asked. Instead of “which parents are neglecting their children?” his work asked how any parent could raise children in such conditions. His photographs and writing helped build support for New York’s Tenement House Act of 1901, which required windows, ventilation, and sanitation in new tenements, and for parks, playgrounds, and schools in crowded districts. Theodore Roosevelt, as New York police commissioner in the mid-1890s, became his friend and ally. Riis also had limits that students should notice: his book relied on crude ethnic stereotypes, and some of his photographs were staged. The lecture’s point stands, though: his work marked a shift from individual rescue to structural reform, a model the Progressive Era would follow.

The Juvenile Court, Child Labor, and the WCTU

The First Juvenile Court

On July 1, 1899, the Illinois Juvenile Court Act took effect, and the first juvenile court in the world opened in Cook County, with Judge Richard S. Tuthill presiding. The law was the product of years of campaigning by Chicago women reformers, including Lucy Flower, Julia Lathrop, and the residents of Jane Addams’s Hull House, working through the Chicago Woman’s Club and with the Chicago Bar Association. The court treated children not as criminals but as wards of the state. Proceedings were informal, without juries, and the judge acted as a kind of wise parent, in the parens patriae tradition of Ex parte Crouse. Probation officers supervised children at home where possible instead of sending them to institutions. Juvenile courts spread rapidly, and by the 1920s nearly every state had one.

Critics, then and later, noted that the court’s informality meant children lost due process protections, and that broad categories such as “delinquent” and “dependent” gave judges and probation officers enormous discretion over poor and immigrant families. Anthony Platt’s The Child Savers made this argument forcefully; David Tanenhaus’s Juvenile Justice in the Making (2004) offers a more balanced history.

Child Labor Reform

Child labor did not disappear in the nineteenth century; it grew with industrialization. The National Child Labor Committee, founded in New York in 1904, hired the sociologist and photographer Lewis Hine in 1908 to document children working in textile mills, coal breakers, canneries, glassworks, and street trades. His photographs, published in magazines and reports, became the movement’s most powerful evidence; among them is his 1910 image of Addie Card, a young spinner in a Pownal, Vermont, mill. Congress passed the Keating-Owen Act in 1916, banning interstate commerce in goods made by young children, but the Supreme Court struck it down in Hammer v. Dagenhart on June 3, 1918. A second law, based on the taxing power, was struck down in 1922. A proposed Child Labor Amendment passed Congress in 1924 but was never ratified. Only the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, upheld by the Court in United States v. Darby (1941), established lasting federal limits on child labor.

The WCTU and the “Do Everything” Policy

The lecture closes its chronology with the Woman’s Christian Temperance Union, introduced in Lecture 3. Under Frances Willard’s “Do Everything” policy, the WCTU worked for kindergartens, age-of-consent laws, prison reform, and suffrage as well as temperance, and it became the largest women’s organization of the late nineteenth century. Its campaigns illustrate how women’s moral networks, built in antebellum civil society, helped lay foundations for the twentieth-century welfare state.

Analytical significance

From Mary Ellen to Riis to the juvenile court and child labor law, child welfare moved from rescuing individual children toward changing the conditions that endangered children in general. Each step expanded public responsibility for children, and each step raised new questions about expert and state power over families.

Conclusion: Protection and Power

Return to April 1874, when a ten-year-old girl wrapped in a carriage blanket told a New York courtroom what had been done to her. Before that day, there was no agency whose job was to protect her. Within a year there was one, and within a generation there were juvenile courts, compulsory school laws, tenement codes, and the beginnings of child labor regulation. Americans had come to see childhood as a distinct and vulnerable stage of life and to accept that society owed children protection, even sometimes against their own parents.

But reform was never neutral. The same doctrine that rescued Mary Ellen allowed Mary Ann Crouse to be confined without a trial. The same faith in rural Christian homes that gave some orphan-train riders new lives separated others from parents who were merely poor. The same era that founded child protection societies bound Black children to former enslavers and removed Native children to boarding schools. Reformers saved many children, and they also imposed their own ideas of proper family life on families who did not share them. Those tensions run through today’s debates over child protective services, juvenile justice, and parents’ rights.

Analytical significance

Main argument: the children’s rights movement made childhood a matter of public concern rather than purely private family business. Its achievements were real, but every expansion of protection was also an expansion of power, and class, religion, and race shaped who was protected and who was punished.

Interpretive Synthesis: From the Little Commonwealth to the Child-Saving State

Colonial New England treated the family as a little commonwealth under paternal authority, with community oversight when households failed. Lockean and Romantic ideas, together with the urban crises of industrialization, reframed children as improvable and vulnerable. Antebellum reformers built houses of refuge, justified by Ex parte Crouse’s doctrine of parens patriae; women’s moral reform societies targeted girls; and Brace’s Children’s Aid Society placed tens of thousands of children with rural families. After the Civil War, the Mary Ellen Wilson case produced the first society for the prevention of cruelty to children, Riis exposed the structural conditions of tenement childhood, Chicago reformers created the juvenile court, and the long campaign against child labor culminated in 1938.

The open question is whether to read this history mainly as the expansion of children’s rights or mainly as the expansion of social control. Platt and Katz emphasize control; Gordon shows that poor mothers and children often used child-protection agencies for their own purposes; Tanenhaus and others stress the mix. The evidence supports holding both: the movement created protections children had never had, and it gave reformers and the state new power over the families of the poor and the marginalized.

Research Notes and Historiographical Guide

Instructor Notes on Accuracy and Teaching

Historical accuracy: The essay follows the deck’s order while correcting or qualifying several points. Omitted or paraphrased because they could not be verified: the Mary Englund, Addie Card, Tuthill, and Riis courtroom quotations; the exact wording of Mary Ellen’s testimony and of Brace’s “dangerous class” sentence; Willard’s “motherhood of the world” phrase. Corrected: orphan trains did carry Catholic children; the Mary Ellen case did not use animal-cruelty law; the juvenile court law came chiefly from the Chicago Woman’s Club, Hull House, and the bar rather than the WCTU; Hine was hired in 1908; the age of consent was ten or twelve in most states (seven in Delaware) in the 1880s; Pratt’s motto is quoted in his own words. Softened: the 30,000 homeless children estimate, the “40,000+” apprenticed Black children, “250 sister societies by 1910,” and WCTU age-of-consent counts.

Historiographical balance: The interpretation pairs social-control readings (Platt, Katz) with accounts that stress children’s and mothers’ own agency (Gordon) and balanced institutional histories (Tanenhaus, Mintz), and keeps race and religion central to the analysis of who was protected.

Narrative potential: Strong teachable scenes include the Stubborn Child Law, the opening of the New York House of Refuge on New Year’s Day 1825, Mary Ann Crouse’s father in court, the first orphan train arriving in Dowagiac, Mary Ellen in a carriage blanket before Judge Lawrence, Riis’s flash powder in a midnight tenement, and Lewis Hine posing as a Bible salesman or insurance agent to get into mills.

Historical-thinking spine: Context explains Puritan family order and Lockean and Romantic ideas; Cause links urban poverty and moral reform to child-saving institutions; Consequence appears in parens patriae, the SPCC, the juvenile court, and federal child labor law; Evidence can be modeled through Ex parte Crouse, Brace’s book, the 1874 newspaper coverage, and Riis’s and Hine’s photographs. The open question of rights versus control ties the lecture back to the chapter’s theme of reform’s two faces.

Turning a constitutional blueprint into a functioning national government

Essential question

How did Americans turn a written Constitution into a government people could recognize, obey, and regard as legitimate?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain why the Constitution of 1787 was not yet a complete operating manual for national government.
  2. Analyze how the presidential title debate, Washington's Senate visit, and the invention of the Cabinet created constitutional precedent through practice.
  3. Describe how the First Congress built departments, courts, and revenue machinery that made federal power workable.
  4. Evaluate the Bill of Rights as a legitimacy strategy that answered ratification-era fears without dismantling national capacity.
  5. Reconstruct the Whiskey Rebellion as a three-part test of federal enforcement and explain why successful state-building also created new political fears.

Introductory Framing

On April 30, 1789, George Washington took the presidential oath on the balcony of Federal Hall in New York. Crowds cheered, bells rang, and the most famous American alive became the first president under the Constitution. Washington's own words were less confident. He spoke of "weighty and untried cares." He was famous, but the office was new. There was no previous president, no settled cabinet routine, no agreed etiquette for how a republican executive should look, and no clear model for how the president should consult Congress.

This lecture follows one problem through selected moments: how Americans turned a written Constitution into a government people could recognize, obey, and regard as legitimate. The formal arc is deliberately narrow. It does not try to cover every action of the Washington administration. It follows three connected themes: precedent (routines created by choice when the text was silent), capacity (departments, courts, and revenue that made national power workable), and legitimacy (rights protections and the Whiskey Rebellion test of whether federal law could be enforced without destroying republican self-government).

Hamilton's public-credit program, assumption, and the Bank belong to the next lecture's question about what kind of republic those new powers should serve. Here the point is simpler and prior: before Americans could fight over the meaning of national power, they had to invent a government that could act at all.

I. Inventing the Presidency: Titles, Advice, and the Cabinet

Dignity Without Looking Monarchical

The first constitutional controversy of the new government was about a name. The Senate considered elevated forms of address; one proposal would have styled Washington "His Highness the President of the United States of America and Protector of the Rights of the Same." The House preferred the simpler constitutional title, "President of the United States." James Madison opposed grand titles because they sat poorly with "the nature of our government" and with American political expectations after a revolution against monarchy.

The argument was not silly ceremony. A title communicated what kind of executive the republic was creating. Too little dignity could make the office look weak; too much ceremony could make it look like elective kingship. Settling on "Mr. President" helped legitimate executive power by distancing it from court culture, while still treating the office as something more than a private citizen's job. Washington continued to wrestle with the problem in receptions, dress, travel, and access. Style was part of constitutional practice because no settled republican etiquette existed.

Washington's Awkward Senate Visit

The Constitution directs the president to make treaties with the "advice and consent" of the Senate. In August 1789 Washington took the word "advice" seriously and personally appeared before the Senate seeking guidance about negotiations with Southern Native nations. The encounter was awkward. Senators wanted documents, questions referred, and time to deliberate. The chamber treated his request like ordinary legislative business rather than a confidential council. Washington never again sought treaty advice from the Senate in that manner.

The later convention emerged from that failure: the president negotiates first and sends a completed treaty to the Senate for consent. The episode is a clean classroom example of how practice filled a textual gap. The Constitution named a relationship; experience taught people how that relationship would work.

Congress Built Departments; Washington Invented the Cabinet

Article II assumed that executive departments would exist and allowed the president to require written opinions from their principal officers. It named no cabinet and prescribed no cabinet meetings. The First Congress filled the statutory gap by creating Foreign Affairs (soon State), War, and Treasury in the summer of 1789. Washington experimented with individual written advice and other forms of consultation before gathering his department heads as a group. Lindsay Chervinsky's research shows that sustained collective cabinet practice developed gradually as foreign and domestic crises intensified.

Analytical significance

Early presidential power was invented as much as it was inherited. Titles, Senate consultation, and the Cabinet show constitutional meaning becoming real through repeated choices that later generations mistook for instructions that had always been there.

II. The First Congress Turns Sketches into Machinery

The Constitution created offices; Congress still had to build the machinery. Article I granted legislative powers, Article II created a president, and Article III provided for a Supreme Court and such inferior courts as Congress might establish. Those clauses were architecture, not an operating manual. The First Congress had to decide how money would be collected, how officers would be paid, how departments would work, how federal courts would function, and how ratification-era promises would become amendments.

In summer and fall 1789 the chronology is important. Revenue came first: the Tariff Act of July 4 and collection machinery later that month. Then came the executive departments. The Judiciary Act of 1789 built a workable federal court system out of the Constitution's sparse judicial language. Congress funded lighthouses, created compensation schedules, and began administrative routines that connected the capital to ports, courts, western posts, and local communities.

This was not mere housekeeping. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress often acted on states and depended on state compliance. Under the Constitution, the national government increasingly acted on individuals. Importers met customs officers. Litigants entered federal courts. Distillers later faced federal excise law. Citizens could be prosecuted for violating national statutes. The federal presence remained thin by later standards, but the principle was revolutionary: a national government could reach past state governments and make claims directly upon people.

Analytical significance

Constitutional meaning became practical through legislation, repeated choices, and routines people learned to recognize. Capacity was built statute by statute before anyone could prove that capacity in a crisis.

III. The Bill of Rights and Political Legitimacy

The Bill of Rights belongs in this lecture not as an amendment-by-amendment civics lesson, but as an institutional and political achievement. During ratification, many Anti-Federalists demanded amendments. Some proposals would have altered the structure or fiscal capacity of the new national government. Madison had once doubted that a bill of rights was necessary. By 1789 he regarded amendments as politically useful and substantively worthwhile, and he wanted to control their form.

Madison's strategy was to answer anxieties about rights without reopening the entire constitutional settlement. The amendments that emerged protected speech, religion, press, assembly, criminal procedure, and related liberties while leaving the government's core fiscal and administrative powers intact. Carol Berkin's account emphasizes that political dimension. The new government needed to reassure citizens if they were to regard it as legitimate. Capacity without trust would look like consolidation; rights protections without capacity would leave the Confederation's problems unsolved.

For the lecture's arc, the key point is legitimacy. Precedent created routines. Congress created machinery. The Bill of Rights helped make the newly empowered government politically bearable to people who had just finished a revolution against distant authority.

IV. A Small Federal Government Could Still Reach Citizens

Recent state-building scholarship cautions against equating power with bureaucratic size. The federal government of the early 1790s was tiny compared with later national administrations. Most Americans did not meet a federal officer every day. Yet the state was not imaginary. Its presence concentrated where revenue, commerce, law, diplomacy, and territory intersected.

Customhouses are the clearest example. The tariff supplied the overwhelming share of early federal revenue. Gautham Rao has shown how collectors, inspectors, merchants, paperwork, and remittances made national authority real in commercial life. Max Edling describes a government designed to be powerful in crisis while limiting ordinary peacetime intrusiveness. Customs duties were comparatively unobtrusive for many inland citizens because importers paid them at ports. Internal taxation was more visible. That difference becomes crucial when the government moved from waterfront revenue toward an excise on distilled spirits.

Students need this capacity picture before the Whiskey story. Revenue, courts, administration, diplomacy, and military authority gave the national government ways to affect ordinary lives even when the federal payroll was small.

V. The Whiskey Rebellion: Three Story Beats

Beat 1: The Excise Made Federal Power Visible at the Still

The federal government had taken on Revolutionary War debts and needed revenue to service them. At Alexander Hamilton's urging, Congress passed an excise on distilled spirits on March 3, 1791, the first nationwide internal tax under the Constitution. In western communities grain was bulky, roads were poor, and cash could be scarce. Distillation turned grain into a portable, marketable commodity. Larger commercial distillers could often navigate the tax more easily than small producers. What Treasury officials saw as legitimate national revenue could look in the backcountry like a policy written for eastern creditors and enforced against frontier farmers.

Resistance drew on a political language older than the Constitution: liberty, local autonomy, popular meetings, intimidation of collectors, and the legitimacy of protest against unjust taxation. Thomas Slaughter's description of the Whiskey Rebellion as a frontier epilogue to the American Revolution is useful here. The conflict forced Americans to confront a problem the Revolution had left unresolved: when does resistance defend liberty, and when does it threaten republican self-government?

Beat 2: In July 1794, Tax Resistance Became Armed Rebellion

In July 1794 U.S. Marshal David Lenox rode through the western counties serving writs that ordered unpaid distillers to appear in federal court in Philadelphia. Revenue inspector John Neville guided him. On July 15 their work collided with armed opposition. Neville's home at Bower Hill became a flashpoint. Gunfire left casualties; the property was burned. Crowds gathered; radicals, moderates, and people seeking negotiation mixed together. Armed resistance, casualties, and the burning of property turned a tax protest into a test of whether federal law could be defeated by force.

Beat 3: Washington Tried Law and Talks Before Sending the Militia

Washington met with his cabinet and Pennsylvania governor Thomas Mifflin on August 2. He could not simply send troops. The Militia Act of 1792 required a federal judge to certify that the courts and the marshal could not enforce the law. After that process, commissioners were also sent to seek peaceful submission. Only then did Washington call out approximately 13,000 militiamen from several states. He accompanied and reviewed the force, then returned east. Henry Lee commanded operationally, with Hamilton accompanying the expedition west. No major battle occurred. Many opponents dispersed; arrests followed; two men were eventually convicted of treason; Washington pardoned both.

The ending matters as much as the mobilization. Enforcement, legal process, and pardons all shaped the precedent. Federalists could say the government had proven that constitutional law was enforceable without creating a permanent dictatorship. Critics could answer that the response was disproportionate and that coercion itself created new fears. Both readings belong in the story because successful state-building also produced the political anxiety that drives Lecture 2.

Analytical significance

The Whiskey Rebellion proved that the Constitution had produced a government capable of acting. It also created new fears about who would control that power and how far it would reach. Capacity and distrust arrived together.

Closing Synthesis

By 1794 the written Constitution had become operational through three kinds of work. Precedent filled silences in the text: the presidential title, the failed experiment of seeking treaty advice in person, and the gradual invention of the Cabinet. Capacity made national authority workable: departments, courts, customs revenue, and administrative routines. Legitimacy made the new power politically survivable: the Bill of Rights answered ratification-era fears, and the Whiskey crisis asked whether enforcement could remain republican.

The Confederation had lacked dependable tools for raising force or acting directly against domestic resistance. In 1794 the federal government could levy an internal tax, use federal courts and marshals, invoke statutory militia procedures, and sustain an expedition across the mountains. The difference in capacity was real. So was the unfinished political question. Representative government does not eliminate conflict over when law deserves obedience.

The bridge to Lecture 2 is therefore not a list of additional Hamilton policies. It is the consequence of success. Once the federal government could act, Americans disagreed about what that power should accomplish, how broadly the Constitution should be read, and whether organized opposition to the people controlling national power could remain loyal to the republic.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Lindsay M. Chervinsky, The Cabinet: George Washington and the Creation of an American Institution (2020). Best single guide to how Washington invented collective executive advice through practice rather than from an explicit constitutional blueprint.

Kathleen Bartoloni-Tuazon, For Fear of an Elective King (2014). Shows why the presidential title controversy was a serious struggle over republican legitimacy and executive dignity.

Carol Berkin, The Bill of Rights: The Fight to Secure America's Liberties (2015). Accessible account of the political strategy behind amendments that reassured citizens without dismantling national capacity.

Max M. Edling, A Revolution in Favor of Government (2003). Places early national state-building in the world of fiscal-military capacity while explaining why Americans still feared European-style intrusion.

Gautham Rao, National Duties: Custom Houses and the Making of the American State (2016). Explains how a thin federal government became real through ports, paperwork, and revenue administration.

Thomas P. Slaughter, The Whiskey Rebellion (1986). Classic narrative framing western resistance as a frontier epilogue to Revolutionary arguments about liberty and taxation.

Max M. Edling and Peter J. Kastor, eds., Washington's Government (2021). Essays on the provisional, experimental character of the first federal administration.

1. Governing standards and scope

This audit follows the current canonical protocol and addenda. Research precedes scholarly understanding, pedagogical judgment, and presentation form. The textbook is the course baseline rather than its ceiling. Revision requires a consequential issue of accuracy, historiography, causation, context, interpretive balance, primary evidence, narrative opportunity, or historical thinking; minor style and citation imperfections do not justify reopening an internal preparation document.

2. Textbook baseline

The assigned chapter already carries the full chronological spine from imperial reform after 1763 through independence: the War of 1763–1766; Sugar, Currency, Quartering, and Stamp Acts; Stamp Act resistance and repeal; the Townshend program, Wilkes, and the Boston Massacre; partial repeal and continuing disaffection; divided loyalties and the widening language of liberty; the Tea and Coercive Acts; the First Continental Congress; war, the Second Continental Congress, and the turn to independence.

It also gives students more social and continental breadth than a narrow taxation narrative: Native sovereignty and the western boundary, imperial debt and administration, crowd action, nonimportation, women’s household labor, urban and rural divisions, loyalism, slavery, and the transition from resistance to war. Its review questions follow the six section-level crises; its critical-thinking questions ask students to reconcile property and liberty with destruction of private property and to imagine a conciliatory Grenville settlement.

Lecture therefore should not retell the acts in sequence. Its value lies in four analytical problems: whether Britain possessed the capacity and legitimacy to govern its victory; how people without office made a law unenforceable; how different actors appropriated ‘liberty’; and how resistance institutions acquired governing power. Those problems supply causation, contingency, evidence, human choices, and institutional transformation that the survey necessarily compresses.

One textbook sentence requires caution rather than direct repetition. It compresses proven British intent, Fort Pitt’s documented distribution of hospital items, and the later epidemic into a causal chain stronger than the evidence permits. The essay foundation is better: intention and the Fort Pitt act are documented; the episode’s epidemiological contribution to the wider outbreak cannot be established confidently.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

Imperial capacity, rival reform traditions, and Indigenous sovereignty

The first lecture is strongest when framed as a problem of capacity and incompatible constitutional expectations, not as a morality tale in which taxes mechanically produce revolution. Anderson and Dowd remain foundational for war, debt, western posts, Native diplomacy, and the postwar crisis. Amy Watson’s 2025 study adds a significant refinement: colonial ‘patriotism’ before 1763 belonged to a militant transatlantic British opposition tradition that imagined an expansive, Protestant, commercially free, and loosely confederal empire. The later imperial rupture was therefore a contest between rival reform projects, not simply liberty confronting reform.

Established synthesis: Fred Anderson, Crucible of War.

Indigenous-centered postwar interpretation: Gregory Evans Dowd, War under Heaven.

Recent rival-reform interpretation: Amy Watson, Patriots before Revolution.

The Royal Proclamation is best taught as an assertion of Crown control over land cession and settlement, combined with an effort to regulate an unstable frontier—not as a permanent humanitarian wall or proof that Native nations had surrendered sovereignty. Its visual and narrative counterpoint is a British map claiming continental authority while western posts were being attacked and British officials were negotiating from weakness.

Primary source: Royal Proclamation of 1763.

Nullification without office: print, resignation, commerce, and enforcement

Lecture 2 correctly distinguishes constitutional argument from the mechanics that made statutes fail. The Stamp Act collapsed through interlocking processes: petitions and declarations, printed coordination, crowd intimidation, officeholder resignations, merchant pressure, court and customs disruption, household nonconsumption, and parliamentary reconsideration. Adelman’s history of printers materially strengthens the existing account by showing that printers were commercial mediators who selected, repackaged, and circulated rhetoric rather than transparent pipes for elite ideas.

Print networks and commercial mediation: Joseph M. Adelman, Revolutionary Networks.

Consumer politics: T. H. Breen, The Marketplace of Revolution.

Jane Calvert’s 2025 Dickinson biography reinforces the essay’s insistence that organized resistance did not require an immediate embrace of independence. Dickinson’s constitutionalism, Quaker formation, and association with Anthony Benezet place principled nonviolent resistance, antislavery argument, and attachment to an improved British constitution inside the revolutionary coalition. The result is a more contingent story than a straight road from the Stamp Act to 1776.

Recent Dickinson reassessment: Jane E. Calvert, Penman of the Founding.

Primary constitutional claims: Resolutions of the Stamp Act Congress, 1765.

Contextual primary-source exhibit: Library of Congress, ‘No Taxation Without Representation’.

Contested liberty: allegiance, class, gender, Black freedom, and Native land

The chapter’s third lecture follows current historiography by treating ‘liberty’ as a portable political grammar rather than a fixed program or a simple exposure of patriot hypocrisy. Patriots, loyalists, neutrals, artisans, tenants, women, enslaved people, and Native nations could invoke or reject overlapping claims while pursuing different securities, authorities, and futures. Jasanoff and DuVal remain essential for loyalist and continental alternatives; Holton keeps elite decisions connected to Native resistance, debt, slavery, and popular conflict.

Scholarly foundation: Maya Jasanoff, Liberty's Exiles.

Scholarly foundation: Woody Holton, Forced Founders.

Scholarly foundation: Kathleen DuVal, Independence Lost.

Travis Glasson’s 2025 Nobody Men makes neutrality an active, unstable historical position rather than an absence of conviction. It reinforces the essay’s warning against treating later patriot and loyalist labels as fixed or exhaustive before war hardened categories.

Recent allegiance history: Travis Glasson, Nobody Men.

The clearest warranted addition concerns Phillis Wheatley. Carretta’s revised 2023 documentary biography incorporates discoveries made after the 2011 edition. Waldstreicher’s 2023 prize-winning reassessment goes further by placing Wheatley’s poetry, correspondence, publication decisions, and transatlantic reception at the center of revolutionary print and political culture. A new 2026 Oxford scholarly edition of the Occom letter foregrounds Wheatley’s argument that civil and religious liberty are inseparable. These works materially sharpen the lecture’s strongest primary-source anchor without changing its central interpretation.

Wheatley foundation: Vincent Carretta, Phillis Wheatley Peters, revised edition.

Wheatley foundation: David Waldstreicher, The Odyssey of Phillis Wheatley.

Wheatley foundation: Julia Jorati, ed., Wheatley's ‘Letter to Samson Occom’.

Primary source: Phillis Wheatley to Samson Occom, 11 February 1774.

Dunmore’s Proclamation should remain a limited, military, self-interested emancipation offer directed at enslaved men belonging to rebels who joined British forces. It transformed calculations of allegiance and made British lines a route toward freedom for thousands, but it did not abolish slavery, apply to loyalist-owned people, or by itself cause Virginia independence.

Primary source: Lord Dunmore's Proclamation, 7 November 1775.

From counterpower to alternative government

The fourth lecture is already aligned with the most important recent development. William Novak’s 2026 article demonstrates that provincial congresses and committees did not merely protest; they legislated, regulated trade and prices, administered relief and security, coordinated militia, and pursued public welfare. Their authority was extralegal in relation to imperial constitutions, but not lawless. This supports the essay’s move from counterpower to active emergency government and prevents the Declaration from becoming the moment institutions suddenly appeared.

Recent state-formation scholarship: William J. Novak, ‘Legislation, Regulation, and Administration in the American Revolution’.

The Continental Association is the most compact primary-source demonstration: Congress created rules, local enforcement machinery, monitoring, and sanctions in the name of collective political authority. Massachusetts supplies the strongest narrative scene when crowds closed county courts and compelled royal officials to cease acting; the surviving Provincial Congress journals then reveal resistance assuming documentary, administrative, and military form.

Primary source: The Continental Association, 20 October 1774.

Massachusetts institutional record: Journals of the Massachusetts Provincial Congresses.

Continental institutional record: Journals of the Continental Congress.

Paine and the Declaration belong late in this sequence. They widened and justified separation after resistance institutions had already displaced much royal authority. This keeps ideology consequential while preserving chronology and institutional causation.

Primary source: Thomas Paine, Common Sense.

Primary source: Declaration of Independence transcript.

Religion and the chapter boundary

Katherine Carté’s imperial history confirms that religion belongs in the professor’s continental context: imperial Protestant networks were real political infrastructure, and the Revolution disrupted them. It does not require a fifth lecture or the mechanical insertion of religion into every existing question. The current four-lecture arc can register religious language where it shapes Dickinson, Benezet, Wheatley, Occom, and allegiance while leaving denominational reorganization outside this chapter’s central problem.

Contextual scholarship: Katherine Carté, Religion and the American Revolution.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

Verdict: PASS

The roadmap complements rather than duplicates the textbook. Its arc is coherent and cumulative: imperial capacity and incompatible expectations; counterpower without office; contested ownership of revolutionary language; and resistance institutions becoming government. Each question is appropriate for first-year students because it turns chronology into a concrete problem of people, mechanisms, claims, and authority.

Each lecture also has a viable narrative engine. Lecture 1 can move from a map of British victory to besieged western posts and the Royal Proclamation. Lecture 2 can follow Andrew Oliver’s effigy, resignation, and the collapse of the Stamp Act’s human machinery while distinguishing the Hutchinson mansion attack. Lecture 3 can center Wheatley’s letter to Occom as a compact confrontation over who may interpret liberty. Lecture 4 can begin with Massachusetts court closings and follow the Continental Association from agreement to local enforcement.

The assignment carries the extended documentary analysis of changing power, so the lectures should equip rather than duplicate it. The Five Moves recur without quota: Cause and Context dominate Lecture 1; Cause, Evidence, and Consequence Lecture 2; Comparison, Context, and Evidence Lecture 3; Cause, Consequence, and Evidence Lecture 4. Fresh research reinforces rather than displaces the existing structure. No substantive roadmap adjustment is warranted, and the planning record remains unchanged.

6. Individual professor-background essay audits

Essay 1 — Can Britain Govern What It Won?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the fiscal-military state, debt, garrisoning, Indigenous diplomacy, War of 1763–1766, Proclamation, and smallpox evidence are responsibly stated. Historiography: Anderson, Dowd, Calloway, Edelson, Richter, and Watson supply both established and current interpretations; fresh research confirms the rival-reform frame. Primary sources: the Proclamation and Amherst/Bouquet record expose claimed authority, decision-making, and evidentiary limits. Balance and context: the essay preserves multiple British reform traditions and Indigenous sovereignty without reducing the crisis to taxation or inevitability. Narrative engine: British victory on paper against posts falling and Fort Pitt under siege. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Context, Comparison, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Evidence chronology: Bouquet Papers chronology and Amherst correspondence.

Essay 2 — How Do You Defeat a Law Without Holding Office?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the essay distinguishes constitutional protest from administrative nullification and correctly separates the 14 August Oliver action from the 26 August Hutchinson mansion destruction. Historiography: Maier, Morgan, Nash, Breen, Carp, Zabin, Adelman, and Calvert support a multimechanism account rather than a crowd-or-ideas binary. Primary sources: Stamp Act Congress resolutions, merchant agreements, newspapers, and resignation scenes reveal argument, coordination, uncertainty, and enforcement. Balance and context: coercion, household labor, commerce, print, officeholding, and parliamentary choice all matter. Narrative engine: an effigy becomes a resignation and a statute loses the people required to administer it. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Evidence, Consequence, and Context. Substantive change: none.

Boston Tea Party context: Benjamin L. Carp, Defiance of the Patriots.

Essay 3 — Who Gets to Use the Word Liberty?

Verdict: PASS WITH LIMITED SUBSTANTIVE ADDITIONS — COMPLETED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: allegiance remains fluid, popular and elite claims are differentiated, women’s participation is not equated with equal citizenship, Dunmore is treated with appropriate limits, and Native sovereignty is not translated into patriot liberty. Historiography: Bailyn, Nash, Holton, Jasanoff, DuVal, Taylor, Glasson, and Glover already made the essay broadly current. Primary sources: Wheatley’s Occom letter reveals an enslaved-turned-free Black intellectual claiming interpretive authority over liberty; Dunmore and Indigenous land conflicts show incompatible freedoms. Balance and context: the essay avoids both celebratory universalism and a flat hypocrisy morality play. Narrative engine: Wheatley’s 1774 letter quietly seizes revolutionary language from white patriots. Natural Five Moves: Comparison, Context, Evidence, and Consequence.

Substantive changes applied in place: added a short analytical paragraph incorporating Waldstreicher’s 2023 reassessment; updated Carretta to the revised 2023 edition; added Waldstreicher and the 2026 Jorati edition to the preparation apparatus; and added stable live links to all eleven web-accessible works in that apparatus. The essay’s structure, argument, learning objectives, primary-source anchor, and strong existing prose were preserved.

Essay 4 — When Does Resistance Become Government?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the essay distinguishes counterpower from alternative government and places Common Sense and the Declaration after much royal administration had already been displaced. Historiography: Maier, Raphael, Breen, Rakove, Reid, Armitage, Pincus, and Novak provide a strong institutional and ideological foundation. Primary sources: the Continental Association and provincial journals expose rules, monitoring, sanctions, administration, militia, and claims to public authority. Balance and context: extralegal authority is neither romanticized as spontaneous democracy nor dismissed as disorder; Massachusetts is identified as unusually developed rather than universal. Narrative engine: a court cannot sit, officials renounce royal authority, and a committee begins governing daily life. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Consequence, Evidence, and Context. Substantive change: none.

7. Primary-source and narrative shortlist

Lecture 1. Pair the Royal Proclamation with the Amherst/Bouquet/Fort Pitt documentation. The contrast lets students see assertion, intent, action, and the limit of causal proof rather than receiving one seamless summary.

Source: Royal Proclamation of 1763.

Source record: Bouquet Papers chronology and Amherst correspondence.

Lecture 2. Put the Stamp Act Congress’s constitutional resolutions beside the social mechanism of Andrew Oliver’s resignation. The law’s formal validity and practical failure can then coexist.

Source: Resolutions of the Stamp Act Congress, 1765.

Lecture 3. Let Wheatley’s sentence about the ‘love of freedom’ carry the lecture. It reveals biblical language, natural rights, irony, antislavery argument, and Black intellectual authority in one document. Dunmore then changes liberty from language into a dangerous wartime choice.

Source: Phillis Wheatley to Samson Occom, 11 February 1774.

Source: Lord Dunmore's Proclamation, 7 November 1775.

Lecture 4. The strongest chapter-wide narrative opportunity is the closing of royal courts in Massachusetts: crowds and militia did not merely denounce authority; they prevented it from functioning and required officials to renounce it. The Continental Association then turns that local confrontation into a continental system of rules and enforcement.

Source: Journals of the Massachusetts Provincial Congresses.

Source: The Continental Association, 20 October 1774.

8. Downstream issues to remember — do not implement in this audit

Reveal lecture decks. Preserve the smallpox distinction among documented intent, Fort Pitt’s action, and unproven epidemic causation. Preserve Dunmore’s limits and avoid making the proclamation a single cause of Virginia independence. Carry the Wheatley reassessment into images, quotations, and notes. Under the current Reveal addendum, every formal lecture must fit the 35–40 minute window, place overflow after a clear Independent Study divider, and contain two designated in-class historical-thinking activities with engaging student-facing titles.

Presenter notes / Study Buddy. Make the lecture mechanism explicit: capacity in Lecture 1, withdrawal of cooperation in Lecture 2, contested interpretive authority in Lecture 3, and institutional substitution in Lecture 4. Keep primary-source provenance and uncertainty visible.

Historian’s Notebook. Do not reduce the chapter to a tax-act chronology. Preserve rival reform projects, Native sovereignty, contingent allegiance, women’s labor, Black intellectual authorship, coercion within patriot mobilization, and the administrative work of committees.

Think Like a Historian practice. Use the smallpox evidence for Evidence and causal caution; Oliver’s resignation for Cause; Wheatley/Occom for Comparison and Evidence; and the Continental Association for Consequence and Context. These are natural reasoning opportunities, not a Five Moves quota.

Chapter assignment. Retain the revised Liberty! Stream and Discuss task as the major extended documentary encounter. Do not revive a redundant Rhetoric of Rights quiz or pre-answer the changing-power question in lecture.

Exam materials. Avoid questions that imply taxes alone caused revolution, that all colonists divided immediately into patriot and loyalist camps, that universal liberty language produced equal inclusion, that Dunmore abolished slavery, or that the Declaration created resistance government from nothing. Assess plural causation, mechanisms, contingency, contested evidence, and institutional consequence.

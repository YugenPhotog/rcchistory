The collapse of royal authority and the legitimacy of revolutionary administration

Introductory Framing

A revolution is not complete when people become angry or when they violate a law. It becomes a transformation of sovereignty when alternative institutions begin performing the work of government: collecting money, regulating trade, commanding armed force, administering justice, identifying enemies, and claiming obedience. Between 1774 and 1776, colonial resistance crossed that threshold unevenly. Massachusetts moved earliest and most dramatically, but similar institutions appeared across the rebelling colonies as royal authority weakened or fled.

The distinction from the previous lecture is essential. Counterpower prevents an existing government from working. Alternative government supplies rules, administration, and coercion after that government loses effective authority. Committees of correspondence began as communication networks. Committees of inspection enforced boycotts. Committees of safety and provincial congresses increasingly managed defense, finance, supplies, public order, and loyalty. The same movement that condemned arbitrary power created institutions with broad discretionary authority.

Recent scholarship makes this administrative dimension especially important. Pauline Maier and Ray Raphael demonstrated that independence grew from local action and popular pressure, not only congressional debate. T. H. Breen emphasized insurgent mobilization. William Novak's article, published online in 2025 and in the 2026 volume of Law and History Review, argues that provincial congresses and committees created robust precedents for public regulation and general-welfare governance. This work complicates the familiar story in which liberty naturally produced limited government.

From Obstruction to Substitution

The Threshold Problem

During the Stamp Act and Townshend crises, resistance organizations pressured officials and disrupted policy while royal governments continued to exist. Governors called assemblies, courts operated, taxes were collected, and imperial officers retained legal authority even when they faced opposition. The movement crossed a new threshold in 1774 when resistance organizations began instructing towns, closing courts, directing militia, withholding funds, and creating bodies that claimed to speak for entire colonies.

The shift was not announced in one document. It occurred through accumulated practice. A committee that published information exercised influence. A committee that inspected merchants and punished violators exercised regulatory power. A county convention that prevented a court from sitting challenged jurisdiction. A provincial congress that collected taxes and provisioned militia operated as a legislature. Sovereignty migrated through these acts before it was formally declared.

Legitimacy without Existing Law

Revolutionary institutions faced a paradox. They justified resistance in the name of constitutional rights, yet many lacked authorization under the colonial constitutions they defended. They therefore relied on town meetings, county conventions, elections, petitions, association, necessity, and claims of popular consent. Their authority was extralegal in relation to imperial law but not necessarily lawless in the eyes of supporters.

John Phillip Reid's constitutional history emphasizes the seriousness with which colonists understood inherited legal rights. Pauline Maier and Ray Raphael show how local political practice created new legitimacy. These perspectives should be joined. Revolutionaries did not discard constitutionalism; they argued that unconstitutional imperial action had returned authority to the people and made extraordinary institutions necessary.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Resistance became government when associations ceased merely to protest policy and began claiming the right to make, administer, and enforce rules for the community. The transfer of sovereignty was practical before it was textual.

Massachusetts: Royal Government Collapses before Lexington

The Massachusetts Government Act

The Coercive Acts were designed to isolate and discipline Massachusetts after the Tea Party. The Massachusetts Government Act altered the charter, increased royal control over the council, and restricted town meetings. General Thomas Gage became military governor. Instead of restoring obedience, the measures persuaded many inhabitants that ordinary constitutional remedies were closing.

County conventions and town committees responded by urging officials to refuse royal appointments and by organizing resistance to the new courts. In Worcester County, thousands of people compelled court officers to renounce the altered government, and courts ceased functioning. Similar actions spread. The collapse was not caused by a single Boston mob; it depended on broad mobilization through rural towns and county networks.

The Provincial Congress

When Gage dissolved the General Court in October 1774, elected representatives reconvened as the Massachusetts Provincial Congress. The body advised towns to withhold taxes from royal officials, appointed committees, supervised militia preparation, gathered military supplies, and created a Committee of Safety. It did not yet declare independence, but it claimed obedience on matters central to governance.

T. H. Breen and Ray Raphael stress how deeply local participation sustained this insurgency. The famous march to Lexington occurred after months in which many communities already obeyed revolutionary institutions rather than the governor. British attempts to seize military stores in April 1775 were therefore not merely preventive policing. They were efforts to restore authority in a province where effective government had already shifted.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Massachusetts was the most advanced case in 1774. Do not imply that royal government collapsed at the same time or in the same manner everywhere. The pace depended on local institutions, governors, military presence, and political divisions.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

In Massachusetts, the revolution in government preceded the opening battle. Lexington and Concord militarized an existing transfer of authority rather than creating political resistance from nothing.

The Continental Association as an Administrative Constitution

Congress Creates a Continental Rule System

The First Continental Congress did not possess a recognized executive, treasury, or police force. Its most consequential instrument was the Continental Association of October 1774, which established nonimportation, nonconsumption, and eventual nonexportation. The agreement reached into commerce, household behavior, leisure, funerals, manufacturing, and relations with violators. It was more than a boycott; it was a rule system for a political community not yet formally constituted as a nation.

Congress depended on local enforcement. Article eleven called for committees in every county, city, and town to observe conduct and publish the names of violators. Publicity would mark offenders as enemies of American liberty and cut them off from trade. The Association thereby linked a continental decision to local administrative machinery.

Primary Evidence: The Association

Primary source

We will, in our several stations, encourage frugality, economy, and industry, and promote agriculture, arts and the manufactures of this country ... and will discountenance and discourage every species of extravagance and dissipation.

- The Continental Association, October 20, 1774

The passage illustrates the breadth of revolutionary governance. Congress prescribed not only trade policy but moral economy and social conduct. It treated consumption, entertainment, clothing, and production as matters of public survival. The movement that resisted parliamentary intrusion was willing to regulate daily life extensively when it claimed that regulation arose from collective consent and emergency necessity.

The Association also generated a new political map. Local committees did not merely obey Congress; their existence gave Congress reach. They gathered information, judged compliance, and translated continental policy into community sanctions. Authority moved in both directions between localities and the intercolonial congress.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Continental Association functioned as an administrative constitution for resistance. It created common rules, delegated enforcement, and defined loyalty across colonial boundaries before Congress possessed the formal attributes of a national government.

Provincial Congresses and Committees of Safety

Emergency Government

As royal assemblies were dissolved or governors lost control, provincial congresses appeared in most rebelling colonies. Their names varied, and their authority remained provisional, but they legislated, raised troops, issued currency, organized supplies, supervised local committees, and communicated with the Continental Congress. Committees of safety exercised executive and military functions between sessions.

William Novak's recent study emphasizes that these bodies engaged in extensive regulation. They set prices or wages in some places, encouraged manufacturing, provisioned armies, inspected stores, managed public finance, controlled movement, monitored correspondence, and policed dissent. This record challenges a retrospective mythology in which the Revolution established government chiefly to protect private rights from regulation.

Coercion and the General Welfare

Revolutionary committees could summon suspects, require oaths, restrict trade, confiscate property, imprison opponents, and compel military service or material support. Patriots defended such powers as necessary to the safety of the people. Loyalists and neutrals experienced them as persecution. The conflict over legitimacy therefore persisted inside the revolutionary polity.

The principle salus populi, the safety or welfare of the people, helped justify extraordinary action. Emergency did not eliminate procedural concerns, but it widened discretion. Revolutionary government was simultaneously participatory and coercive: often more locally representative than the institutions it replaced, yet capable of intense surveillance and punishment.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The first revolutionary governments were not minimalist placeholders. They were active emergency states that mobilized resources, regulated society, and enforced loyalty. Their legitimacy rested on popular authorization and necessity, not on an absence of governmental power.

War, Independence, and the Public Justification of Sovereignty

War Changes the Constitutional Problem

The fighting at Lexington, Concord, Bunker Hill, and around Boston compelled the Second Continental Congress to coordinate an army while still professing loyalty to the king. The Olive Branch Petition and the Declaration of the Causes and Necessity of Taking Up Arms reveal a movement trying to distinguish resistance to ministers from separation from the Crown. George III's Proclamation of Rebellion and the hiring of German auxiliaries weakened that distinction.

War also sharpened internal coercion and allegiance. Royal governors fled or operated from ships. Patriots demanded oaths and military support. Dunmore's proclamation offered freedom to enslaved men belonging to rebels who joined British forces, intensifying white Virginia fears and linking independence to control over slavery and internal security. The state-building process cannot be separated from civil war.

Common Sense and the Change in Political Imagination

Thomas Paine did not invent the desire for independence, but Common Sense made separation accessible, urgent, and morally plausible. He attacked monarchy, rejected the inherited mystique of the British constitution, and argued that a continent should not be governed by an island. His pamphlet converted an institutional reality, colonies already governing through congresses and committees, into a program for permanent political independence.

The success of Paine's argument depended on timing. By early 1776, royal authority had collapsed in many places, fighting had continued for months, and reconciliation appeared less credible. The pamphlet offered language adequate to what people were already doing. It transformed provisional self-government from an emergency expedient into evidence that a new political order was possible.

Local Declarations and the Declaration of Independence

Pauline Maier identified scores of local and state declarations issued before July 1776. Towns, counties, juries, grand juries, and conventions instructed representatives or announced support for separation. These documents demonstrate that Congress did not simply impose independence from above. They also reveal variation in reasoning, emphasis, and political participation.

The Declaration of Independence then performed several tasks. It announced statehood to foreign powers, explained the break to domestic and international audiences, summarized grievances, and grounded legitimate government in popular consent and natural rights. David Armitage emphasizes its global form as a declaration among states. Steve Pincus argues that its grievances reveal an expectation of active government serving the public good. The document did not create revolutionary government, but it justified, consolidated, and internationalized it.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Independence named a transfer of sovereignty already produced by institutional collapse, administrative innovation, and war. Paine made separation imaginable; local declarations demonstrated authorization; the congressional Declaration turned de facto self-government into a public claim of legitimate statehood.

Closing Synthesis

The American Revolution was a change in government before it was a statement about government. Committees and conventions first obstructed royal authority, then assumed its functions. The Continental Association created common rules and local enforcement. Provincial congresses financed defense, raised troops, regulated markets, and policed loyalty. By the time Congress declared independence, many inhabitants had already lived under revolutionary institutions for months or years.

This perspective changes the role of famous texts. Common Sense and the Declaration remain essential, but they did not summon a new order from theory alone. They interpreted and legitimated an existing institutional transformation. Pauline Maier's local declarations, Ray Raphael's Massachusetts insurgency, T. H. Breen's popular mobilization, and William Novak's regulatory history reveal the administrative foundation beneath the intellectual drama.

The resulting Revolution contains a productive contradiction. Patriots resisted a distant legislature in the name of consent, yet revolutionary governments exercised broad emergency powers. Their claim was not that government should do little. It was that legitimate government derived from the people and served their safety. The open question is how a community can distinguish necessary revolutionary administration from the arbitrary power it has risen to oppose.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Essential Local Interpretation

Pauline Maier, American Scripture: Making the Declaration of Independence (1997).

Shows how local declarations and instructions prepared the way for Congress and explains how the Declaration became a revered national text. Essential for treating July 1776 as culmination rather than isolated inspiration.

Massachusetts Revolution

Ray Raphael, The First American Revolution: Before Lexington and Concord (2002).

Reconstructs the rural court closings and transfer of authority in Massachusetts during 1774. It provides the strongest narrative foundation for the claim that government collapsed before the first battle.

Popular Mobilization

T. H. Breen, American Insurgents, American Patriots: The Revolution of the People (2010).

Emphasizes ordinary colonists, local committees, communication, and the rapid spread of resistance. Useful for connecting local action to continental political authority.

National Political Development

Jack N. Rakove, The Beginnings of National Politics: An Interpretive History of the Continental Congress (1979).

A foundational study of Congress as a political institution. It clarifies the problems of representation, coordination, and authority faced by a body that initially lacked conventional sovereign powers.

Constitutional Interpretation

John Phillip Reid, The Constitutional History of the American Revolution, 4 vols. (1986-1993).

A demanding but indispensable account of the legal and constitutional assumptions underlying colonial resistance. Reid is the best corrective to interpretations that treat constitutional language as mere propaganda.

Declaration in Global Context

David Armitage, The Declaration of Independence: A Global History (2007).

Places the Declaration within the law of nations and the later global tradition of declarations of independence. Especially useful for explaining why Congress addressed the opinions of mankind.

Alternative Interpretation

Steve Pincus, The Heart of the Declaration: The Founders' Case for an Activist Government (2016).

Argues that the Declaration's grievances reveal a demand for energetic government promoting population, commerce, representation, and public welfare. It productively challenges readings that make the document a charter of minimal government.

Most Recent Scholarship

William J. Novak, "Legislation, Regulation, and Administration in the American Revolution," Law and History Review 44, no. 1 (2026): 73-102.

Examines provincial congresses and committees of safety as early sites of public law, economic regulation, provisioning, and police power. This open-access article substantially updates the administrative history of revolutionary government.

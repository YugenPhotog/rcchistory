HIST 101 • CHAPTER 9 • THE OLD SOUTH, 1790–1860

Slave Religion

Professor Background Essay • Lecture 2

Central historical question
How did enslaved people take a Christianity that slaveholders offered as a tool of control and remake it into a faith of hope, resistance, and community that outlasted slavery itself?

Research status: generated from Reveal deck and expanded with scholarship on African American religion, the plantation mission, the “invisible institution,” spirituals, and Nat Turner

Grounded in the Chapter 9 Lecture 2 materials and expanded with primary evidence and modern scholarship on African religious survivals, baptism and the plantation mission, Charles Colcock Jones’s catechisms, hush-harbor worship, the spirituals, enslaved preachers, and the Southampton rebellion of 1831.

Research Orientation

The Reveal deck moves through a clear three-part arc that its conclusion names directly: from control, to resistance, to cultural foundation. Enslaved Africans arrived with languages, music, and sacred ideas of their own. Slaveholders first feared that baptism might undermine slavery, then embraced a carefully managed Christianity that preached obedience. Enslaved people answered with a second, hidden church—the “invisible institution”—where they read the Bible from below, sang spirituals that carried double meanings, followed preachers chosen by their own communities, and, in Nat Turner’s case, found in scripture a call to armed revolt. The result was not a copy of the master’s religion but a distinctive African American Christianity that became the foundation of the Black church after emancipation.

The most important scholarly anchor is Albert J. Raboteau’s Slave Religion: The “Invisible Institution” in the Antebellum South (1978), which established that the enslaved did not simply receive Christianity but transformed it. Raboteau entered a long debate. The sociologist E. Franklin Frazier argued that slavery had largely stripped African Americans of their African heritage, while the anthropologist Melville Herskovits insisted that many African cultural forms survived. Most historians now hold a middle position: African traditions did not survive intact, but they shaped how enslaved people worshipped, sang, healed, and understood the sacred. Sterling Stuckey’s Slave Culture stresses the ring shout as a pan-African inheritance; Lawrence Levine’s Black Culture and Black Consciousness reads the spirituals as a sacred world view; Eugene Genovese’s Roll, Jordan, Roll shows religion as a space where the enslaved resisted the master’s claims to total authority; and Janet Duitsman Cornelius and Sylvia Frey and Betty Wood trace how slaveholders’ missions and enslaved converts shaped each other.

The resulting interpretation should avoid two easy stories. One treats religion simply as an “opiate” that made enslaved people accept their condition. The other romanticizes every hymn as a coded escape plan. The evidence shows something more interesting: the same Bible, the same hymns, even the same church buildings could serve the slaveholder’s purposes and the enslaved community’s purposes at the same time. Who controlled the meaning was the real contest.

Faith Carried Across the Water

Imagine the hold of a slave ship in the eighteenth century. The people chained there had been torn from homes in West and West-Central Africa—from Senegambia, the Bight of Biafra, the Gold Coast, Kongo and Angola. They spoke many languages and practiced many religions. Some were Muslims; some had encountered Catholic Christianity in the Kingdom of Kongo; most followed traditional religions that honored a high God, lesser spirits, and the ancestors. The Middle Passage took almost everything they owned. It could not take what they carried in memory and in their bodies: rhythms, songs, stories, healing knowledge, and ideas about how the living and the dead are connected.

Enslaved Africans were not “blank slates.” Their traditions changed under slavery, but they did not simply vanish. When drums were banned, as South Carolina did after the Stono Rebellion of 1739, people kept rhythm with hands, feet, and voices. When old languages faded, new creole languages and English carried old patterns of speech. When African priests and shrines were gone, healing knowledge lived on in rootwork and conjure. Survival through adaptation is the key idea: traditions bent, but they did not break.

Christianity entered this world slowly. For most of the colonial era, relatively few enslaved people in the British mainland colonies were Christians. The great change came with the evangelical revivals of the later eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, when Baptist and Methodist preachers offered an emotional, conversion-centered faith that welcomed Black converts, sometimes licensed Black exhorters, and, for a time, questioned slavery itself. Enslaved people heard in this faith a language of hope and justice—a God who saw suffering and promised deliverance.

What emerged was fusion. African musical structures such as call-and-response, in which a leader sings a line and the group answers, blended with Bible stories and Protestant hymns. The ring shout—a counterclockwise circle of shuffling feet, clapping, and song—turned worship into collective movement. The result was a new sound and a new style of worship that came from both Africa and America.

The enslaved did not merely adopt Christianity; they transformed it. — Key framing from the lecture, after Albert J. Raboteau, Slave Religion (1978)
[Instructor note: The quotation above is the deck’s paraphrase of Raboteau’s argument, not a verbatim line from his book. Present it that way. The Frazier–Herskovits debate over African “survivals” is a useful way to show students that historians disagree about continuity and change.]
The Conceptual Payoff: African American Christianity was not a copy of European religion. It was built by people who carried African sacred ideas and practices across the Atlantic and fused them with a faith first offered by those who enslaved them.

Religion as Control: Baptism, Missions, and the Catechism

Slaveholders did not always want enslaved people to become Christians. In the seventeenth century many English colonists believed that Christians should not hold other Christians as slaves, and some enslaved people sued for freedom on the grounds that they had been baptized. Colonial legislatures closed that door. Virginia declared in 1667 that baptism did not change a person’s condition from slavery to freedom, and other colonies passed similar laws. Even so, many planters worried that conversion would make enslaved people feel equal to their masters, encourage them to gather, or give them ideas from scripture.

Over time the planters’ fear turned into a strategy. If Christianity could not be kept from the enslaved, it could be managed. In the 1830s and 1840s—especially after Nat Turner’s rebellion in 1831—Southern churches organized a “mission to the slaves.” Missionaries promised slaveholders that properly taught Christianity would make enslaved people more honest, more obedient, and less likely to run away or rebel. Conversion was recast as “good discipline.”

The most influential figure in this movement was Charles Colcock Jones, a Presbyterian minister and slaveholder from Liberty County, Georgia. His Catechism of Scripture Doctrine and Practice, first published in 1837 and designed “for the oral instruction of coloured persons,” taught through memorized questions and answers. Oral teaching mattered: most Southern states made it illegal to teach enslaved people to read, so instruction was built around listening and repetition rather than reading the Bible for oneself. Slaveholders and missionaries emphasized passages such as Ephesians 6:5—“Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters”—and the so-called “Curse of Ham” from Genesis 9, which pro-slavery writers twisted into a claim that God had condemned Africans to servitude. Stories of liberation, above all the Exodus, were minimized.

“Who gave you a master and a mistress?” “God gave them to me.” — Question and answer from a slave catechism, as quoted in the lecture

Black preachers were sometimes allowed, but only under surveillance and with the expectation that they would preach submission. Plantation services were often led by a white minister, with the planter’s family seated apart and white listeners present to make sure nothing dangerous was said. The purpose was double: to make slavery look approved by God and to prevent the kind of independent gathering that might lead to collective organizing. In this sense, religion became part of the system that justified slavery.

The enslaved, however, were not passive listeners. Jones himself recorded a famous moment from the spring of 1845. Preaching on Paul’s letter to Philemon, he urged obedience and condemned running away. “One half of my audience deliberately rose up and walked off with themselves,” he wrote, and some of those who remained declared afterward “that there was no such Epistle in the Bible.”

[Instructor note: Some secondary sources date Jones’s Philemon sermon to 1833; Jones’s own report, published in 1846, places it in the spring of 1845. The walkout is excellent evidence that the enslaved judged sermons for themselves, even inside the supervised system.]
▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Document analysis: Read the catechism question and answer aloud. What does it want the listener to believe about God, masters, and the listener’s own place in the world? Then read Jones’s account of the walkout. What does the audience’s response tell you about whether the catechism worked?
The Conceptual Payoff: Slaveholders moved from fearing Christianity to using it. Supervised worship, selective scripture, and oral catechisms were designed to turn faith into a tool of control. But control was never complete: enslaved listeners judged what they heard.

The “Invisible Institution”: Hidden Faith and Theological Resistance

Alongside the supervised Sunday service, enslaved people built a second church that slaveholders could not see. Historians, following Raboteau, call it the “invisible institution.” Its meeting places were called hush harbors or brush arbors: clearings in the woods, ravines, or cabins where people gathered at night, out of earshot of the big house. Formerly enslaved people interviewed in the 1930s remembered turning an iron pot upside down—over a fire, inside a doorway, or on the ground—in the belief that it would catch or muffle the sound of praying and singing. Lookouts watched for patrollers. Here worship was loud, emotional, and full of African elements: rhythm and dance, call-and-response, and the experience of being filled with the Spirit.

The risks were real. Those caught at unauthorized meetings could be whipped, and laws in many states restricted Black gatherings without white supervision. Yet people kept meeting because the hush harbor offered what the supervised service could not: the freedom to pray for deliverance, to preach hope and dignity, and to speak honestly about suffering. These meetings built leadership, trust, and organizing skills that would later carry into independent Black churches.

In these spaces, enslaved Christians read the Bible “from below.” The Exodus became the central story: God had freed Israel from bondage in Egypt, and Pharaoh looked very much like the master class. “Let my people go” was not only an ancient command; it was a present-day hope. Jesus’ suffering showed that God understood pain and would bring justice. The creation story taught that all people bear God’s image, so slavery violated God’s design. The prophets showed a God who sided with the oppressed and would judge injustice. Songs about a “great gettin’ up mornin’” promised resurrection and a day of justice to come.

Formerly enslaved writers made the same point sharply. Frederick Douglass, in the appendix to his 1845 Narrative, drew a line between the faith of the slaveholders and the faith he embraced: “between the Christianity of this land, and the Christianity of Christ, I recognize the widest possible difference.” The enslaved were doing serious intellectual work—building a theology of freedom out of texts that others used to control them.

[Instructor note: Stress that the hush harbor and the supervised service often involved the same people. Many enslaved Christians attended the master’s church on Sunday morning and the hidden meeting on Sunday night. The difference was not who attended but who controlled the meaning.]

Spirituals as Resistance

The spirituals carried this theology into song. “Go Down, Moses” retold the Exodus with a refrain—“Let my people go”—that every listener could apply to the present. It became widely known in the North in 1861–1862, when it was published as “The Song of the Contrabands” after being heard among people who had escaped slavery to Union lines at Fort Monroe, Virginia. “Wade in the Water” spoke of baptism and the troubled waters of the Bible; later tradition linked it to escape, since walking through water could throw bloodhounds off a trail. “Swing Low, Sweet Chariot” sang of a chariot “coming for to carry me home”—a vision of rescue that could mean heaven, freedom in the North, or both.

Spirituals did double work. They were worship, and they could also send messages that insiders understood more fully than outsiders. Because they were shared orally, they spread quickly from plantation to plantation and created a community memory without written notes. They were theology lessons, survival guides, and protest in one.

[Instructor note: The claim that specific spirituals were coded escape instructions is contested. Strong evidence shows that enslaved people heard double meanings in Exodus and freedom imagery, and Frederick Douglass wrote that songs about Canaan could express a hope of reaching the North. But many popular claims—for example, that “Wade in the Water” was a set of Underground Railroad directions—come from later oral tradition rather than documents from the period. Teach the double meaning; flag the specific “code” claims as debated.]
▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Think-Pair-Share: Listen to “Go Down, Moses.” If you were an enslaved person singing it in 1850, and a slaveholder overheard, what might each of you have heard? Why was double meaning such a powerful tool?
The Conceptual Payoff: The invisible institution was the seedbed of the Black church. In hidden meetings and in song, the enslaved built a theology of freedom—reading Exodus, the prophets, and the suffering of Jesus as promises that God was on their side.

Preachers and Prophets: Leadership, Risk, and Revolt

Enslaved communities chose their own spiritual leaders. A slave preacher was recognized for gifts—powerful speech, wisdom, the ability to pray, sometimes healing—more than for formal schooling, which the law usually forbade. Preachers taught, counseled, settled disputes, shared news, and modeled courage. They lived in a constant tension. Owners pressured them to preach obedience; their congregations needed truth and hope. Messages that sounded “rebellious” could bring punishment, so preachers learned to speak through parables, biblical stories, and songs.

Some Black congregations gained a measure of institutional life even under slavery. Andrew Bryan, an enslaved man who later bought his freedom, led the First African Baptist Church in Savannah, Georgia, in the late eighteenth century despite whippings and imprisonment. In Richmond, Virginia, Black members of the city’s First Baptist Church formed the First African Baptist Church in 1841. Most of its members were enslaved, and by 1859 it counted more than 3,000 members. Virginia law required a white pastor, Robert Ryland, himself a slaveholder, but the congregation’s Black deacons and members shaped its life. After emancipation, churches like these became centers of education, politics, and community leadership.

Nat Turner: Prophet and Revolutionary

The most dramatic example of religion turning toward revolt came in Southampton County, Virginia, in 1831. Nat Turner, born in 1800, was literate and deeply religious. He studied the Bible, fasted, prayed, and reported visions; other enslaved people called him “the Prophet.” Turner came to believe that God had chosen him for a great work. He read natural events as signs: a solar eclipse in February 1831 and an unusual atmospheric darkening of the sun in August.

On the night of August 21, 1831, Turner and a small group of followers began moving from farm to farm, killing white men, women, and children. Their numbers grew to perhaps sixty or more. Over roughly two days they killed about fifty-five to sixty white people before militia and armed volunteers crushed the uprising on August 22–23. Turner hid for about two months before he was captured on October 30. He was tried, convicted, and hanged in Jerusalem, the county seat, on November 11, 1831. In the panic that followed, white militias and mobs killed many Black people, most of whom had nothing to do with the revolt, and dozens more were tried and executed or transported.

What we know of Turner’s own thinking comes mainly from The Confessions of Nat Turner, published by the white lawyer Thomas R. Gray, who interviewed him in jail. Historians read it carefully: it contains Turner’s voice, but filtered through Gray’s purposes. In it, Turner describes his revolt as obedience to a divine command, in the language of the Bible’s prophets and of the Book of Revelation.

The aftermath reshaped Southern religion and law. In 1831–1832 the Virginia legislature openly debated gradual emancipation and then rejected it. Instead, Virginia and other states passed stricter laws restricting literacy, Black preaching, and religious meetings without white supervision. The “mission to the slaves” grew partly in response, as slaveholders tried to replace independent Black religion with closely watched instruction.

[Instructor note: Casualty and execution figures for the Southampton rebellion vary by source. The number of Black people killed in the white retaliation is especially uncertain; estimates commonly run to more than one hundred. No authentic portrait of Nat Turner exists, so any image of his face is an invented likeness.]
▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Open discussion: Was Nat Turner a fanatic or a prophet? Thomas R. Gray presented him one way; many enslaved people remembered him another. How does your answer depend on how you weigh justice against order—and whose sources you trust?
The Conceptual Payoff: Enslaved preachers and prophets turned religion into leadership and, at times, into revolt. Their faith could counsel patience, but apocalyptic hope could also push people from waiting to action—and white Southerners responded by tightening control over Black religion.

Interpretive Synthesis: Faith as a Contest Over Meaning

Between the late eighteenth century and the Civil War, religion in the slave South was a battlefield over meaning. Slaveholders moved from fearing baptism to managing it, offering a Christianity of obedience through supervised services, selected verses, and oral catechisms. Enslaved people accepted the faith but not the slaveholders’ version of it. In hush harbors and in song they read Exodus as a promise, Jesus’ suffering as solidarity, and the prophets as a warning to the powerful. They chose their own preachers. A few, like Nat Turner, found in scripture a command to rise up.

This religion did more than help people endure. It preserved African ways of worship inside a Christian frame, created leaders and networks, and kept alive a sense of human dignity in a system built to deny it. As the lecture’s conclusion puts it, religion was the primary means by which enslaved people maintained their humanity in a system designed to dehumanize. After emancipation, the invisible institution became visible: independent Black churches, schools, and civic organizations grew from it, and the long tradition of faith-based Black activism—from Reconstruction to the civil rights movement—has roots here.

The open question is how to weigh religion’s double edge. Did Christianity, on balance, help enslaved people resist slavery, or did it also teach patience in ways that served slaveholders? The evidence supports both readings in different times and places. What it rules out is the idea that enslaved people were simply passive recipients of someone else’s faith.

Albert J. Raboteau, Slave Religion. The foundational study of the invisible institution and of how the enslaved transformed Christianity. Essential for the lecture’s central claim and for the African-survivals debate.

E. Franklin Frazier and Melville Herskovits. The classic poles of the debate over African cultural survivals—Frazier stressing loss, Herskovits stressing continuity. Useful for teaching historiographical disagreement.

Sterling Stuckey, Slave Culture. Argues for the ring shout as a unifying African inheritance at the heart of slave religion.

Lawrence W. Levine, Black Culture and Black Consciousness. Reads spirituals and folk belief as a sacred world view that sustained the enslaved.

Eugene D. Genovese, Roll, Jordan, Roll. Shows religion as a space where the enslaved contested the master’s authority within a paternalist system. Its paternalism framework is debated.

Janet Duitsman Cornelius; Sylvia R. Frey and Betty Wood. Trace the plantation mission and the evangelical encounter, showing how converts and missionaries shaped each other.

Patrick H. Breen and Kenneth S. Greenberg. Breen’s The Land Shall Be Deluged in Blood and Greenberg’s edited Nat Turner: A Slave Rebellion in History and Memory provide the best modern guides to Southampton and to reading Gray’s Confessions.

Deep-Dive Research Assessment

Historical accuracy: The essay preserves the deck’s chronology and claims while flagging contested points: the deck’s Raboteau framing is a paraphrase; the dating of Jones’s Philemon sermon varies in secondary sources; specific “coded” readings of spirituals rest partly on later tradition; and casualty figures for Southampton vary.

Historiographical balance: The interpretation follows Raboteau’s transformation thesis that structures the deck while presenting the Frazier–Herskovits debate and Genovese’s paternalism framework as points of disagreement. It avoids both the “opiate” reading of slave religion and an uncritical romance of coded songs.

Narrative potential: Strong teachable scenes include the hold of a slave ship, the catechism recited aloud, Jones’s congregation walking out, the overturned pot in the hush harbor, “Go Down, Moses” sung at Fort Monroe, the packed First African Baptist Church in Richmond, and Nat Turner reading the eclipse as a sign.

Historical-thinking spine: Context explains why slaveholders feared and then managed Christianity; Cause links the plantation mission to slaveholders’ fear of revolt; Consequence appears in the invisible institution, the Black church, and post-Turner repression; Evidence can be modeled through the catechism, Jones’s report, Douglass’s appendix, the spirituals, and Gray’s Confessions. The open question keeps the analysis from collapsing into a single verdict.

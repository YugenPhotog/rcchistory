What Is History?

Evidence, interpretation, and the difference between the past and history

Lecture Learning Objectives

  1. Distinguish “the past” from “history” and explain why the distinction matters for historical inquiry.
  2. Analyze history as an evidence-constrained act of selection, interpretation, explanation, and argument.
  3. Differentiate primary and secondary sources while recognizing that source status depends on the question being asked.
  4. Evaluate the limits and opportunities of surviving evidence, including archival silences, documentary abundance, institutional recordkeeping, and the problem of representativeness.
  5. Explain why disagreement among historians does not reduce history to arbitrary opinion.
  6. Distinguish the five-step workflow historians use to produce historical accounts from the five recurring historical-thinking questions students will use to analyze the past, and explain how the two frameworks reinforce one another.

Introductory Framing

The opening historiography lecture must dislodge two misconceptions without replacing them with a third. The first misconception is naïve positivism: the belief that history is simply a fixed body of facts waiting to be retrieved and repeated. The second is naïve relativism: the belief that because historians select and interpret evidence, every account is merely a personal opinion. The third, equally misleading position would be to describe history as “constructed” in a way that makes evidence appear secondary to narrative choice. A sound introduction must instead present history as a disciplined practice in which human beings formulate questions, work from records that are at once incomplete and sometimes overwhelming, test claims, and construct explanations constrained by what the evidence can bear.

The essential question—“If history is simply what happened, why do accounts of the same event disagree?”—therefore introduces an epistemological problem rather than a vocabulary exercise. Historians do not possess direct access to the past. They encounter remnants: documents, objects, landscapes, images, testimony, statistics, and earlier interpretations. Yet the absence of direct access does not mean the past is unknowable. Historical knowledge is inferential, provisional, and corrigible, but it is not arbitrary. The discipline’s standards of source criticism, corroboration, contextualization, citation, peer challenge, and openness to revision distinguish historical argument from memory, myth, propaganda, and fiction.

The course should name two complementary frameworks at the outset. The first, the Five Moves Historians Make, describes the workflow by which historians produce knowledge: ask a question, find sources, evaluate sources, interpret evidence, and construct and defend an explanation. The second, the Five Historical-Thinking Questions, identifies the recurring forms of reasoning students will practice: Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. The first framework answers how historians work; the second identifies what historians ask the evidence to help them understand. Keeping the labels distinct prevents conceptual confusion while allowing each framework to clarify the other.

The Past, Evidence, and History

The past as an irretrievable totality

The past consists of everything that has already occurred, not merely what was important, witnessed, recorded, or remembered. Most past actions left no durable trace. Most conversations vanished as soon as they ended. Many people were never named in surviving records. Yet other activities generated extraordinary documentary abundance: governments, courts, armies, churches, firms, and voluntary associations produced files on a scale no individual historian could ever exhaust. The past is therefore both determinate and irrecoverable. It happened in particular ways, but historians can neither reproduce it in its entirety nor simply absorb every surviving trace.

This distinction guards against treating “history” as a synonym for “everything that happened.” Historians study the past, but the products they create—articles, books, lectures, exhibits, documentaries, databases, maps, and arguments—are histories. A history is necessarily partial because it addresses a question, occupies a scale, and selects from available evidence. Partiality in this sense does not automatically mean distortion. A map of Illinois is incomplete because it does not display every blade of grass, yet its selectivity makes it useful. Historical accounts likewise gain intelligibility through principled omission.

Evidence is not the past itself

Evidence should be understood as a relationship between a surviving trace and a historical claim. A letter is a source; it becomes evidence when a historian uses it to support an inference. The same document may serve as evidence for several different questions. A soldier’s letter might illuminate battlefield conditions, literacy, masculinity, family relations, political belief, postal networks, or retrospective memory. Conversely, a source may be authentic yet poor evidence for a particular claim. A partisan newspaper can reveal what editors wished readers to believe, but it cannot by itself establish how most readers actually responded.

This relational understanding helps students move beyond the assumption that documents contain historical truth in ready-made form. Sources do not “speak for themselves.” They answer only the questions historians are able to pose, and they answer imperfectly. The historian must determine provenance, purpose, audience, context, genre, likely knowledge, silences, and the relationship between the source and other evidence. Such interrogation does not destroy the source’s usefulness; it establishes what kind of use is warranted.

Analytical Significance

The distinction among past, source, evidence, and history is foundational because it explains both the possibility and the limits of historical knowledge. The past constrains historians, surviving sources mediate access to it, evidence emerges through disciplined questioning, and history is the resulting argument rather than the untouched past itself.

History as Significant, True, and Storied

Significance: selection begins with a question

To call history significant is to recognize that historians do not simply gather everything they can find. They identify a problem worth explaining and determine which facts bear on it. Significance is not inherent in an isolated fact. It arises from the relationship between a fact and a question. A tax regulation may be marginal in a biography of an artist but crucial in a study of imperial administration. A merchant ledger may illuminate commercial networks, a church minute book may reveal institutional discipline and belief, a military order may expose command assumptions, and the experiences of one family may reveal migration, labor, kinship, or political allegiance.

Questions are shaped partly by the historian’s present, but no single political or moral agenda monopolizes that process. New work in political, diplomatic, military, economic, religious, social, cultural, environmental, Indigenous, women’s, labor, African American, disability, and business history has expanded inquiry by posing questions earlier scholarship neglected or framed differently. Present concerns can enlarge historical understanding, but they can also produce presentism when historians project contemporary categories, priorities, or moral vocabularies onto actors who understood their world differently. The discipline therefore requires a double obligation: ask questions that matter now while reconstructing the past on terms appropriate to its own context.

Truth: evidence constrains interpretation

The word “true” is necessary but requires precision. Historical truth is not omniscience, and it is rarely reducible to a single final account. Historians can establish many facts with very high confidence: a law was enacted, a battle occurred, a person signed a document, a census reported a number. More complex claims—about motive, causation, representativeness, or significance—require argument. They may be more or less persuasive depending on the range and quality of evidence, the logic connecting evidence to claim, and the treatment of counterevidence.

Interpretation does not suspend truth. It is the means by which historians move from discrete traces to claims about relationships and meaning. Two historians may agree on a body of facts yet disagree about what caused an event, how much weight to assign individual decisions, whether institutions or ideas mattered more, or which consequences were most significant. Their disagreement remains adjudicable. An interpretation that misquotes sources, suppresses contrary evidence, ignores chronology, treats outcomes as inevitable, or repeatedly makes claims that its evidence cannot support is worse than one that survives those tests. Professional standards emphasize evidence, argumentation, citation, peer review, and openness to revision because historical truth is pursued through accountable procedures rather than asserted by authority.

Story: narrative is a form of explanation

Calling history a story can be pedagogically effective, but it must be insulated from the implication that historical writing is fiction. Narrative gives temporal form to explanation. It establishes sequence, identifies change, connects actions to consequences, and distinguishes antecedent conditions from turning points. Even highly analytical history must decide where to begin, what to juxtapose, and how to relate events across time. These choices shape meaning.

Narrative can illuminate causation by showing how circumstances accumulated, how institutions constrained choices, how individuals altered possibilities, how chance and timing intervened, and how later outcomes emerged from earlier decisions. It can also distort by imposing inevitability, moral simplicity, artificial coherence, or a direct path from the past to the present. Historians therefore need both narrative skill and analytical self-consciousness. The most compelling story is not necessarily the best history; it must also respect chronology, contingency, complexity, and the limits of evidence.

Instructor Note: The three-part classroom formula—significant, true, and story—is useful because it is memorable. In the background essay, however, “story” should be understood as an organized explanatory account, and “true” as evidence-constrained rather than complete or infallible.

Analytical Significance

History is constructed but not invented. Selection gives an account focus, narrative gives it intelligibility, and evidentiary constraint gives it disciplinary credibility. The intellectual task is not to eliminate interpretation but to make interpretation answerable to sources, logic, and criticism.

How Historians Produce History: Five Interdependent Moves

Asking questions

Historical inquiry begins with a question, but strong questions are neither merely factual nor detached from evidence. “When did the Stamp Act pass?” can be answered by lookup. “Why did the Stamp Act provoke a broader imperial crisis?” requires explanation. The question directs research, but research also revises the question. Historians often discover that their initial categories were too narrow, that the expected evidence does not exist, or that sources reveal a different problem. Inquiry is therefore iterative rather than linear.

Locating sources

Historians locate evidence in archives, libraries, archaeological sites, oral histories, digital repositories, newspapers, legal records, quantitative datasets, material culture, landscapes, business records, church archives, military files, and administrative collections. What can be studied depends partly on what institutions created, classified, preserved, and later opened. Digitization has expanded access while introducing new forms of selection: searchable materials can appear more representative than uncatalogued or undigitized collections. The historian therefore confronts two opposite problems, scarcity and abundance. A fragmentary record demands cautious reconstruction; a massive record demands principled sampling, comparison, and selection.

Criticizing sources

Source criticism asks both external and internal questions. External criticism concerns authenticity, authorship, date, transmission, and material integrity. Internal criticism concerns meaning, purpose, reliability, audience, language, genre, and plausible knowledge. A forged document may still be historically valuable as evidence of the forger’s context, while an authentic document may be misleading about the event it describes. Reliability is therefore not a binary label attached to a source as a whole. A source may be dependable for one inference and weak for another.

The heuristics often summarized as sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration are especially valuable for novices. Sourcing identifies who produced the account and under what conditions. Contextualization reconstructs the world in which it was created. Corroboration compares it with independent evidence. Sam Wineburg’s research on historical thinking showed that these practices are not intuitive; readers commonly focus on what a document says before asking who produced it or why. Historical reading is “unnatural” because it requires disciplined resistance to immediate identification and present-minded judgment.

Interpreting evidence

Interpretation connects evidence to a larger claim. It involves inference, comparison, conceptualization, and judgments about representativeness. Historians must distinguish what a source directly states from what can reasonably be inferred. They must also avoid treating vivid evidence as typical evidence. A dramatic diary entry may illuminate an individual experience without proving that most people shared it. Quantitative evidence may reveal patterns while concealing meanings that only qualitative sources expose. Strong interpretation often depends on combining unlike forms of evidence.

Constructing and defending explanations

Historical writing turns inquiry into a public argument. Citation permits readers to locate evidence; exposition makes reasoning visible; peer criticism tests claims; revision responds to weaknesses. This public character matters. A private conviction about the past does not become scholarship until others can inspect how it was reached. The historian’s authority rests less on status than on the transparency and resilience of the argument.

The analytical toolkit: five historical-thinking questions

Cause asks why an event or development occurred and requires students to distinguish background conditions, immediate triggers, mechanisms, and relative causal weight. Comparison asks how two cases are similar or different on a consistent basis and guards against superficial analogy. Context asks what larger political, economic, institutional, religious, cultural, geographic, or chronological process shaped the case. Consequence asks what changed because of an event, for whom, over what period, and with what intended or unintended effects. Evidence asks which surviving sources or facts most directly support a claim and whether they are sufficiently reliable and representative for the inference being made.

Workflow and analytical questions are complementary

The two frameworks should not be collapsed into one list. A historian may begin the workflow by asking a question about cause, comparison, context, or consequence; locate and evaluate sources that might answer it; interpret those sources as evidence; and then construct a defensible explanation. Evidence appears in both frameworks because it is both a stage of inquiry and the discipline’s recurring test of every claim. The workflow describes the production of history across a project. The historical-thinking questions describe the intellectual operations that recur within that production and that students can practice in a lecture, document exercise, essay, or examination.

Instructor Note: Preserve the labels exactly. Call the process “The Five Moves Historians Make” and the analytical toolkit “The Five Historical-Thinking Questions.” Avoid calling both lists “moves.” Students should hear the compact distinction: historians follow five moves to produce an account, and within that process they ask five recurring questions.

Analytical Significance

The historian’s five-step workflow and the five historical-thinking questions describe different but interlocking dimensions of the discipline. The workflow makes historical knowledge public and revisable; the questions direct attention toward cause, comparison, context, consequence, and evidence. Together they show students that history is produced through a disciplined process and analyzed through recurring forms of reasoning, not through either fact collection alone or unconstrained interpretation.

Primary and Secondary Sources as Relational Categories

The conventional distinction

Primary sources are usually defined as materials created during the period or process under study, while secondary sources are later analyses that interpret primary evidence and prior scholarship. The distinction remains indispensable. It helps students recognize that a letter written in 1775 and a scholarly monograph published in 2025 perform different evidentiary functions. Primary sources provide traces of past actors and institutions; secondary works synthesize, contextualize, and argue.

Yet the categories are relational rather than permanent. A 1990 documentary is secondary evidence for the civil-rights movement but primary evidence for the history of civil-rights memory in the late twentieth century. A nineteenth-century edition of colonial documents may be secondary to the colonial events but primary for the history of archival practice and national commemoration. The historian’s question determines the source’s status.

Why primary does not mean better

Students often equate primary with direct, unbiased, and therefore superior. This is mistaken. A primary source offers proximity, not transparency. Eyewitnesses misperceive, forget, exaggerate, conceal, and interpret. Official records reflect institutional categories and priorities. Census schedules simplify identities; court testimony emerges under pressure; newspapers pursue political and commercial objectives. A primary source is valuable because it was embedded in the past process being studied, not because it escaped perspective.

Why secondary sources are evidence too

Secondary works do more than summarize. They formulate problems, organize bodies of evidence, test inherited claims, and make the historiographical conversation visible. They may also become evidence for how an event was understood in a later period. The most useful distinction is not “real evidence” versus “someone else’s opinion,” but source type and function within a specific inquiry. As recent teaching scholarship has emphasized, primary and secondary sources can both motivate, situate, evidence, and illustrate arguments; neither category monopolizes a single intellectual role.

Analytical Significance

Treating primary and secondary as relational categories prevents two common errors: fetishizing eyewitness materials as unmediated truth and dismissing scholarship as mere opinion. Historical reasoning depends on understanding what each source can establish for the question at hand.

Archives, Silences, Abundance, and Institutional Memory

Preservation and record creation are historical processes

The archive is not a neutral warehouse of everything that survived. Records were created, kept, classified, discarded, collected, and digitized through institutions with particular purposes. Governments preserved taxation, property, law, war, diplomacy, and administration. Churches preserved membership, doctrine, discipline, charity, and ritual. Businesses preserved transactions, contracts, correspondence, and personnel records. Armies preserved orders, logistics, reports, and casualty lists. Families, clubs, unions, and local associations created their own forms of memory. These records illuminate ordinary commercial, religious, political, military, and institutional life as well as the priorities of those who produced them.

The surviving record is therefore uneven in two directions. Some events and people scarcely appear at all; others generated mountains of paperwork. An event may never have been recorded, a record may have been destroyed, a catalogue may conceal it under inherited terminology, or a person may appear only through hostile description. At the same time, bureaucratic abundance can create a false sense of completeness: official files may preserve procedure in detail while obscuring informal practice, motive, emotion, or consequences beyond the institution’s field of vision. Historians must ask not only what is missing, but also why so much of a particular kind survives.

Reading records for both intended and unintended evidence

Historians routinely use records beyond the purposes for which they were created. A merchant ledger can reveal credit relations, trust, mobility, and consumption as well as prices. Church minutes can illuminate belief, discipline, conflict, gender roles, and community governance. Military correspondence can expose strategy, logistics, personality, institutional culture, and the limits of command. Court files and runaway advertisements, though produced by authorities, can preserve traces of enslaved people, workers, migrants, families, and others who left few self-authored records. Reading “against the grain” is therefore one method within a broader practice of asking what institutional records disclose unintentionally.

Such methods widen the recoverable past but do not abolish uncertainty. Responsible reconstruction distinguishes probability from possibility and identifies where the evidence ends. Quantitative abundance does not guarantee representativeness, and moral urgency does not license invention. The historian must combine imagination with restraint, test unusual cases against wider patterns, and remain alert to the possibility that both silence and abundance can mislead.

Instructor Note: Avoid reducing archival method to the slogan that “the powerful left the records.” Power shaped record creation and preservation, but powerful actors also destroyed evidence, nonelite communities sometimes maintained rich oral and material archives, and many institutional collections preserve the daily actions of ordinary people in great detail. The deeper point is that every archive has a history, a purpose, and a pattern of inclusion that must itself be investigated.

Analytical Significance

Archives shape historical knowledge not only through exclusion but through overrepresentation. Silences invite cautious recovery; abundance requires disciplined selection; institutional records reveal both the activities they were designed to document and assumptions their creators rarely intended to preserve. Understanding the archive as a historical product allows historians to use it critically without treating every record as either transparent truth or merely an instrument of power.

Interpretation, Objectivity, and Historical Disagreement

Between naïve objectivism and relativism

Modern historiography has repeatedly debated whether historians can be objective. The strongest answer avoids both the claim that historians can become viewless recording instruments and the claim that standards disappear because all historians possess perspectives. Historians choose questions and concepts from particular intellectual and social locations, yet they inherit durable professional obligations: accurate citation, source criticism, chronological discipline, contextualization, corroboration, fair representation of opposing evidence, and openness to correction. Objectivity is best treated as a practice of accountable inquiry rather than a claim to political innocence or total detachment.

Marc Bloch centered critical inquiry and the disciplined questioning of evidence. R. G. Collingwood stressed that historical knowledge reconstructs thought by posing questions to surviving traces. E. H. Carr emphasized the interaction between historian and facts, while Richard J. Evans defended the possibility of truthful historical knowledge against stronger postmodern skepticism. Their differences matter, but so does the continuity among them: none treats evidence as optional, and none makes the historian’s social location a substitute for argument.

Present concerns, presentism, and professional continuity

Historians inevitably approach the past with questions shaped by their own time. That fact can be intellectually productive: concerns about institutions, leadership, markets, religion, war, environment, race, class, gender, technology, or state power can reveal evidence earlier scholars overlooked. Yet present concerns become presentism when contemporary categories are treated as timeless, when historical actors are reduced to roles in current political debates, or when moral judgment replaces explanation. Contextualization requires historians to understand choices within the possibilities, languages, institutions, and expectations available at the time.

The persistence of professional standards across political and methodological eras is therefore crucial. Political history, social history, military history, cultural history, economic history, religious history, and other fields may ask different questions, but they are not exempt from common evidentiary obligations. Revision may move in many directions: quantitative evidence can overturn an ideological narrative; attention to belief can challenge material reductionism; institutional research can complicate stories centered on identity; social history can expose exclusions within elite accounts. No interpretive school receives automatic authority from being traditional, progressive, conservative, revisionist, or new.

Why historians can disagree honestly

Disagreement may arise because historians ask different questions, study different scales, privilege different causal mechanisms, or encounter different evidence. One historian may explain a political decision; another may reconstruct its social consequences. One may emphasize institutions, another ideas, material interests, leadership, contingency, geography, or culture. Their accounts can be complementary, but they can also conflict. Students often use “bias” as a universal solvent, yet a more useful vocabulary distinguishes standpoint, limitation, distortion, and bad faith. Perspective is unavoidable; distortion is not. Historical conclusions remain provisional because they are open to revision, not because all claims are equally unstable.

Analytical Significance

Historical objectivity is best understood as a commitment to fair evidence, contextual understanding, transparent reasoning, criticism, and revision. This model recognizes perspective without making perspective sovereign, protects the legitimate expansion of historical inquiry without endorsing presentism, and explains how historians working in different fields can disagree while remaining accountable to shared standards.

Closing Synthesis

Lecture 1 should leave students with a disciplined middle position. The past is not identical with history, because historians cannot recover everything that happened and must select from surviving traces that are sometimes scarce and sometimes overwhelming. Yet history is not merely a subjective story imposed on evidence. Questions, sources, criticism, contextualization, chronology, corroboration, and public argument constrain what historians may responsibly claim. The term “construction” should therefore denote intellectual labor, not fabrication.

The three-part classroom definition—history as significant, true, and storied—captures this middle position when carefully explained. Significance acknowledges selection; truth acknowledges evidentiary constraint; story acknowledges that explanation requires organization across time. The Five Moves Historians Make translate the definition into a workflow: ask, find, evaluate, interpret, and defend. The Five Historical-Thinking Questions identify the recurring analytical work within that workflow: Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. The primary-secondary distinction becomes an entry point into source criticism rather than a classification game, while the discussion of archives shows that historical knowledge is shaped by both silence and abundance, exclusion and institutional preservation.

This foundation is indispensable for the next two lectures. Once students understand that historians construct evidence-based accounts, they can examine how structures, ideas, individuals, institutions, and contingent events enter causal explanations, and how competing interpretations should be judged. The unresolved question that should remain is not whether historians interpret, but how they can ask questions rooted in the present without allowing present concerns to dictate what the past is permitted to mean.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Essential Starting Point — Marc Bloch, The Historian’s Craft. A concise and enduring meditation on historical inquiry, source criticism, time, causation, and the historian’s responsibility. Particularly useful for framing history as disciplined questioning rather than passive accumulation.

Classic Interpretation — E. H. Carr, What Is History?. The most influential short twentieth-century statement on the interaction between historian and facts. It remains useful for challenging naïve empiricism, though its confidence in progress and its limited engagement with social and cultural history require contextualization.

Philosophical Foundation — R. G. Collingwood, The Idea of History. Develops the view that historical knowledge involves reconstructing thought and asking questions of evidence. Useful for understanding why sources answer questions rather than yielding meaning automatically.

Major Defense — Richard J. Evans, In Defence of History. A sustained defense of truthful historical knowledge that acknowledges selection, interpretation, and linguistic mediation while rejecting the claim that historical accounts are indistinguishable from fiction.

Teaching Historical Thinking — Sam Wineburg, Historical Thinking and Other Unnatural Acts: Charting the Future of Teaching the Past. Explains why sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration are cognitively demanding and often counterintuitive. Essential for translating disciplinary habits into first-year instruction.

Methods Overview — John Tosh, The Pursuit of History. A broad introduction to historical method, evidence, interpretation, and the uses of history. Especially useful as a reference for explaining how historians combine source criticism with narrative and causal argument.

Professional Standards — American Historical Association, Statement on Standards of Professional Conduct. Clarifies the discipline’s commitments to evidence, citation, peer criticism, integrity, and the provisional character of historical knowledge. Useful for grounding objectivity in professional practice rather than abstract neutrality.

Disciplinary Outcomes — American Historical Association, History Discipline Core. A compact statement of the habits and competencies historians cultivate, including evidence, argumentation, continuity and change, multiple perspectives, and ethical practice. Useful for aligning the lecture with broader disciplinary learning goals.

What Could Destroy the Republic: Too Much Power or Too Little?

Postwar crisis, Shays’s Rebellion, and the road to the Constitution

Essential question

Did liberty face a greater threat from a government too powerful to control or one too weak to govern?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Analyze why postwar fiscal, commercial, diplomatic, and social conflicts generated competing diagnoses of republican crisis.
  2. Evaluate Shays’s Rebellion as both a challenge to governmental authority and a continuation of revolutionary protest traditions.
  3. Explain how the Constitution redistributed power in response to perceived weaknesses of the Confederation.
  4. Compare Federalist and Anti-Federalist understandings of representation, federalism, liberty, and political danger.
  5. Assess how slavery, economic interests, and popular ratification shaped the constitutional settlement and the adoption of the Bill of Rights.

Introductory Framing

The standard narrative of the 1780s presents a weak Articles of Confederation, a period of disorder, Shays’s Rebellion, and the inevitable rescue of the nation by the Constitution. That sequence identifies real problems but conceals the political argument. Americans did not agree on what counted as a crisis, what had caused it, or which remedies would preserve the Revolution. Creditors, debtors, merchants, farmers, veterans, speculators, state legislators, national officeholders, and Native nations experienced the postwar order differently. Measures that appeared to one group as necessary fiscal responsibility appeared to another as elite extraction or distant coercion.

The Constitution should therefore be taught as a contested solution to competing fears. Nationalists worried that the Confederation could not raise revenue, regulate commerce, honor public credit, enforce treaties, secure western claims, or prevent state majorities from violating property and contracts. Their opponents worried that the proposed government would combine taxation, military power, distant representation, executive authority, and federal courts in ways that could overwhelm the states and ordinary citizens. Both sides invoked the Revolution. They disagreed about whether its survival required more power or stronger barriers against power.

Shays’s Rebellion crystallized the dispute but did not mechanically cause the Constitution. The movement drew upon older practices of regulation and court closure, responded to concrete taxation and debt policies, and included veterans who believed republican government had betrayed its social obligations. Nationalists used the uprising as evidence that liberty had become license and that the Union needed authority. The Philadelphia Convention then exceeded a narrow revision of the Articles and proposed a new constitutional order ratified by state conventions. Ratification transformed elite drafting into a national public contest whose outcome required compromise, political organization, and promises of amendment.

I. The Postwar Crisis Had More Than One Meaning

War Debt, Public Credit, and Taxation

Independence left both Congress and the states burdened by debts to domestic creditors, soldiers, suppliers, and foreign lenders. The Confederation lacked an independent revenue stream and depended on state requisitions. Nationalists argued that reliable taxation was essential to honor commitments, establish public credit, and secure diplomatic credibility. Many state governments imposed taxes to meet obligations, often demanding payment in specie at a time when hard money was scarce.

The burden did not fall evenly. Small farmers could be asset-rich but cash-poor, and courts enforced private debts and tax collections through judgments, seizures, and foreclosures. Creditors and public securities holders feared depreciation, tender laws, and debtor relief. Debtors and rural communities often saw hard-money taxation as a transfer from producers to eastern merchants and speculators. Fiscal policy therefore became a struggle over who would pay for the Revolution and whose property claims the republic would protect.

Commerce, Diplomacy, and the Limits of the Union

British restrictions reduced access to imperial markets, while individual states adopted their own commercial policies and sometimes discriminated against neighbors. Congress could negotiate but lacked comprehensive authority to regulate interstate and foreign commerce. Britain retained western forts and cited American violations of the peace treaty, especially barriers to loyalist debt collection. Spain closed the lower Mississippi to American navigation, dividing commercial interests between western settlers and eastern diplomats. North African corsairs captured American vessels, revealing the costs of diplomatic and naval weakness.

Nationalists interpreted these problems as evidence that the United States possessed independence without effective sovereignty. Yet calls for commercial and fiscal power raised fears that national policy would favor merchants, creditors, and seaboard interests. The crisis was not simply government versus chaos; it was also a contest among political economies and regions over the purposes of government.

State Experimentation Was Not Mere Disorder

States responded with taxes, tariffs, paper money, tender laws, stay laws, debt relief, and other measures. Some policies produced instability or violated expectations of creditors, but they also represented attempts by elected governments to manage depression and scarcity. Woody Holton and Terry Bouton have emphasized that popular pressure and state-level relief were not irrational eruptions. They expressed competing visions of the Revolution’s economic meaning and forced nationalists to confront the power of democratic state politics.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The “critical period” was not one universally recognized collapse. It was a conflict over fiscal responsibility, public credit, democratic relief, commercial development, and regional power. Describing the period as simple disorder adopts the nationalists’ diagnosis before students have examined why other Americans regarded the proposed cure as a greater threat.

II. Shays’s Rebellion and the Meaning of Revolutionary Protest

Massachusetts Taxation and Rural Grievance

Massachusetts pursued an especially demanding fiscal policy to pay state debt and restore credit. Direct taxes payable in scarce specie bore heavily on rural communities. Courts processed debt suits and tax enforcement, increasing the danger of property loss and imprisonment. Petitioners sought tax reform, paper money, relocation or reduction of court costs, and other relief. When conventional petitions failed, organized groups of “Regulators” closed county courts in 1786 to prevent legal proceedings.

The movement drew on practices familiar from the colonial and revolutionary eras. Crowds had resisted imperial laws, intimidated officials, enforced boycotts, and claimed to act in defense of the community. Court closure was coercive and extralegal, but participants did not necessarily imagine themselves rejecting republican government. Many argued that they were compelling representatives to honor the public good. Daniel Shays became the best-known leader, yet the movement was broader than one man and varied by locality.

From Court Closures to Springfield

Governor James Bowdoin’s government defended the courts and raised a privately financed militia commanded by Benjamin Lincoln after Congress proved unable to provide an effective force. In January 1787, insurgents moved toward the federal arsenal at Springfield. State militia under William Shepard fired on them, killing several and dispersing the column. Lincoln’s force pursued the remaining insurgents. The rebellion collapsed, and Massachusetts eventually combined punishment with selective clemency and political change.

The Springfield confrontation gave nationalists a powerful symbol. George Washington, Henry Knox, and others feared that republican government lacked energy and that contagious uprisings could threaten property, credit, and union. Yet elections later removed Bowdoin and brought John Hancock back to office, while policy moderated. The political system absorbed much of the grievance. The aftermath can therefore support two conclusions: governmental weakness was alarming, and democratic politics remained capable of correcting policy without constitutional revolution.

Historiographical Contest

Earlier national narratives often treated the rebellion as evidence that the Articles had failed. Social historians recovered the insurgents’ economic grievances and revolutionary language. Leonard Richards emphasized that many participants were ordinary farmers under severe fiscal pressure. Woody Holton linked popular debtor politics to elite support for constitutional change, while Terry Bouton interpreted postwar conflict as an effort by ordinary Pennsylvanians and other citizens to preserve a democratic revolutionary vision against elite consolidation. More recent scholarship generally avoids reducing the movement to either lawless mob action or pure class heroism. Its importance lies in the collision of legitimate grievances, coercive tactics, and competing definitions of republican authority.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Do not call Shays’s Rebellion “the first American civil war.” The Revolution itself was a civil war, and the phrase obscures rather than clarifies the scale and character of the Massachusetts uprising.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Shays’s Rebellion mattered less because it proved one side correct than because it forced Americans to confront a recurring revolutionary dilemma: when does resistance defend the people against government, and when does resistance destroy the authority of a government created by the people?

III. Philadelphia: From Revision to a New Constitutional Order

The Nationalist Movement Before Shays

Efforts to strengthen the Union predated the Massachusetts uprising. Advocates sought congressional revenue, commercial authority, and greater compliance with national decisions. The Annapolis Convention of 1786, attended by only a minority of states, called for a broader meeting. Confederation Congress then endorsed a convention to revise the Articles. Shays’s Rebellion intensified urgency and shaped public rhetoric, but it did not create the nationalist project.

A Convention That Exceeded Its Mandate

Delegates meeting in Philadelphia in 1787 quickly abandoned simple amendment. The Virginia Plan proposed a genuinely national structure with a bicameral legislature, executive, and judiciary. The Convention ultimately created a federal system in which national authority would operate directly on individuals in important domains rather than only through state requisitions. The Constitution granted Congress powers to tax, regulate commerce, borrow, raise armed forces, and enact laws necessary and proper to execute enumerated powers. It created an independent executive and federal judiciary and made federal law supreme within its constitutional sphere.

The proposal also changed the rule of authorization. The Articles required unanimous state approval for amendment. The Constitution would take effect among ratifying states after approval by conventions in nine states. The framers appealed over the existing governments to the people acting through special conventions. This procedure drew upon state constitution-making experience and allowed Federalists to describe an illegal or extralegal break with the Articles as an act of popular sovereignty.

Representation and the Problem of Scale

The Convention had to reconcile the political identity of states with the claim to represent a national people. The House of Representatives would be apportioned by population; the Senate would give states equal representation. The presidency and judiciary would add institutions insulated to varying degrees from immediate electoral pressure. Federalists would argue that a large republic and divided powers could refine public opinion, control faction, and protect liberty. Critics would answer that distance and filtering could detach rulers from the people and privilege the wealthy and well connected.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Philadelphia Convention did not merely repair administrative weaknesses. It proposed a new source, scale, and structure of authority: a government grounded in popular ratification, operating directly on individuals, and powerful enough to govern. Its revolutionary character lay in both its institutional transformation and its appeal to constituent power above the existing Confederation.

IV. Slavery and the Constitutional Bargain

Union, Representation, and Human Property

Slavery was not a peripheral compromise added to an otherwise complete constitutional design. It shaped representation, taxation, commerce, fugitive policy, and the balance of power. The three-fifths clause increased southern representation by counting a portion of the enslaved population for apportionment while denying those people political rights. The Constitution prevented Congress from prohibiting the Atlantic slave trade before 1808, protected the recovery of people held to service who escaped across state lines, and allowed federal power to suppress insurrections.

Delegates used circumlocutions rather than the words slave or slavery, but the legal protections were substantive. At the same time, the document gave the national government powers that later antislavery actors would attempt to use. The constitutional settlement did not determine one inevitable future, but it created incentives, protections, and political leverage that strengthened slaveholding power within the early Union.

Historiographical Debate

Historians disagree over how to characterize the relationship between slavery and the Constitution. Some emphasize a deeply proslavery compact essential to securing southern participation; others emphasize contingency, federalism, and constitutional ambiguities that left room for later antislavery constitutionalism. The strongest teaching approach distinguishes these levels. Evidence clearly shows that protection of slavery was necessary to the bargain struck in 1787. Interpretation remains open concerning whether the Constitution should be described as fundamentally proslavery in its overall structure and how much weight to give later uses of national power against slavery.

The debate also requires attention to Native dispossession. National authority promised to manage western lands, treaties, and warfare more effectively, thereby strengthening the republic’s capacity for continental expansion. The Constitution’s power cannot be evaluated only within the Atlantic seaboard polity; it also reorganized the means through which the United States would confront Indigenous nations.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Constitution’s promise of effective republican government was inseparable from bargains over slavery and territorial power. Strengthening the Union did not merely solve neutral administrative problems; it redistributed authority in ways that protected human property, amplified slave-state representation, and enhanced the capacity of the settler state.

V. Federalists and Anti-Federalists: Rival Fears of Liberty’s Destruction

Federalist Diagnosis

Federalists presented the Constitution as the preservation of republican government. They argued that the Confederation could not secure credit, commerce, peace, treaties, or domestic order. A larger republic would contain multiple interests and make it harder for a single faction to dominate. Representation would refine public views; separation of powers and federalism would prevent concentration; an energetic executive would administer rather than legislate. Federalists insisted that the national government possessed only delegated powers and that the people, not the states as governments, authorized the Constitution.

Anti-Federalist Diagnosis

Anti-Federalists were a diverse coalition rather than a unified party. They feared that broad taxing and necessary-and-proper powers, federal courts, a standing army, the presidency, the Senate, and distant representation would consolidate authority. A vast republic, they argued, could not maintain genuine representation because rulers would not share the circumstances or knowledge of ordinary constituents. The proposed House was too small; the absence of a declaration of rights was dangerous; and federal supremacy could reduce states to administrative subdivisions.

Saul Cornell’s work has helped recover Anti-Federalism as a serious constitutional tradition rather than a collection of losing objections. Anti-Federalists defended local knowledge, participatory politics, juries, militia traditions, rotation, and restraints on centralized power. Their arguments exposed genuine ambiguities and helped produce the Bill of Rights. At the same time, localism could protect oppressive state practices, including slavery and restrictions on minorities. Neither scale possessed a monopoly on liberty.

The Labels and the Problem of Federalism

The labels can mislead. The Constitution’s supporters called themselves Federalists even though many sought a substantially more national government. Their opponents often defended a more traditional federal arrangement centered on sovereign states. The debate concerned not a choice between federalism and its absence but competing forms of federalism: how authority should be divided, which government would act directly on citizens, and which level could claim priority when powers conflicted.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Federalists and Anti-Federalists disputed the location of danger more than the value of liberty. Federalists feared impotence, faction, and disunion; Anti-Federalists feared distance, consolidation, and elite detachment. The constitutional debate remains intelligible only when both diagnoses are treated as responses to real features of republican government.

VI. Ratification and the Bill of Rights

A National Public Contest

Ratification unfolded state by state through specially elected conventions, newspaper essays, pamphlets, petitions, public meetings, private correspondence, and organized political pressure. The outcome was not predetermined. Federalists benefited from strategic advantages, including rapid action in favorable states, control of influential presses in some locations, and the support of prominent figures. Anti-Federalists mobilized substantial opposition and forced detailed discussion of the proposed government.

Pauline Maier’s narrative of ratification emphasizes contingency and the diversity of state debates. Delegates negotiated instructions, amendments, and declarations of rights. Close contests in Massachusetts, Virginia, and New York required Federalists to accept recommendations for amendment and to assure skeptics that the new system could be corrected. Ratification therefore did not represent simple popular endorsement of the unmodified document; it was a politically mediated authorization shaped by promises.

From Objection to Amendment

The original Constitution contained protections such as prohibitions on bills of attainder and ex post facto laws, but no comprehensive declaration of individual rights. Federalists initially argued that enumerating rights was unnecessary or dangerous because the national government possessed only delegated powers. Anti-Federalists answered that broad clauses and institutional power required explicit restraints. The political force of their criticism helped produce the first ten amendments, proposed by Congress in 1789 and ratified in 1791.

The Bill of Rights did not immediately apply to state governments and did not erase the Constitution’s exclusions. Its adoption nevertheless demonstrates that constitutional losers can reshape a settlement. Anti-Federalists failed to defeat ratification, but their insistence on rights, federalism, juries, and limits became part of the constitutional order. Ratification was thus both a victory for stronger national power and an acknowledgment that power required further restraint.

Fulfillment or Restraint of the Revolution?

Gordon Wood has interpreted the Constitution within a broad transformation of republicanism, while Charles Beard’s classic economic interpretation emphasized the material interests of property holders. Later scholars have refined, criticized, and combined ideological, social, economic, legal, and institutional explanations. Woody Holton and Terry Bouton foreground popular economic conflict; Max Edling emphasizes construction of a fiscal-military state capable of national action; Michael Klarman stresses the framers’ departures from democratic preferences; George William Van Cleve emphasizes the Confederation’s governmental incapacity; Pauline Maier restores the contingency and breadth of public ratification. The Constitution can plausibly be understood as both a fulfillment of popular sovereignty and a restraint on forms of popular politics that many framers distrusted.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Ratification transformed a convention’s proposal into a popularly authorized constitution, but authorization depended on intense political organization and promises of amendment. The Bill of Rights reveals that the settlement was negotiated rather than complete: stronger national power became legitimate partly because its opponents forced additional declarations of limitation.

Closing Synthesis

The Constitution arose from genuine weaknesses in the Confederation, but those weaknesses did not possess one politically neutral meaning. The postwar struggle concerned who would pay public debts, which property claims government would enforce, whose commerce would be favored, how state majorities should respond to hardship, and whether national authority would protect or threaten ordinary citizens. Shays’s Rebellion made these conflicts visible by placing revolutionary tactics against republican institutions.

Philadelphia answered the nationalists’ diagnosis by creating a government able to tax, regulate commerce, enforce federal law, administer policy, and act directly on individuals. It grounded that power in the constituent authority of the people while filtering participation through representation, federalism, separated institutions, and indirect selection. It also protected slavery and strengthened the territorial capacity of the United States. The new order therefore combined republican innovation with hierarchy and coercion.

Federalists and Anti-Federalists offered rival but durable understandings of political danger. One feared that liberty would perish when government could not govern; the other feared that liberty would perish when government became distant, consolidated, and militarily powerful. Ratification and the Bill of Rights did not resolve that tension. They institutionalized it. The enduring analytical question is not simply whether the Constitution was democratic or undemocratic, but which forms of popular power it enabled, which it restrained, and whose security it defined as the security of the republic.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Pauline Maier, Ratification: The People Debate the Constitution, 1787–1788 (2010). Essential narrative of the state ratifying conventions and public debate. Maier restores contingency, local variation, and the political work required to secure approval.

Saul Cornell, The Other Founders: Anti-Federalism and the Dissenting Tradition in America, 1788–1828 (1999). Major reassessment that treats Anti-Federalism as a coherent and influential constitutional tradition concerned with localism, participation, and restraints on centralized authority.

Woody Holton, Unruly Americans and the Origins of the Constitution (2007). Connects debtor relief, tax resistance, popular politics, and elite constitutional reform. Particularly useful for understanding why some nationalists sought to restrain state-level democracy.

Terry Bouton, Taming Democracy: “The People,” the Founders, and the Troubled Ending of the American Revolution (2007). Interprets postwar political conflict from the perspective of ordinary Pennsylvanians and argues that elite consolidation narrowed the democratic possibilities of the Revolution.

Leonard L. Richards, Shays’s Rebellion: The American Revolution’s Final Battle (2002). Accessible social and political reconstruction of the Massachusetts insurgency, with close attention to participants, taxation, and regional grievance.

Max M. Edling, A Revolution in Favor of Government: Origins of the U.S. Constitution and the Making of the American State (2003). Emphasizes the Constitution as construction of a national fiscal and military state. Useful for explaining what “energy” and revenue capacity meant to Federalists.

George William Van Cleve, We Have Not a Government: The Articles of Confederation and the Road to the Constitution (2017). A detailed argument that the Confederation’s structural weaknesses produced a genuine crisis of governance. Provides a strong counterweight to accounts centered primarily on elite reaction.

Michael J. Klarman, The Framers’ Coup: The Making of the United States Constitution (2016). A large-scale account emphasizing the framers’ nationalist agenda, the Convention’s departure from its mandate, and the political strategies used in ratification.

Robert A. McGuire, To Form a More Perfect Union: A New Economic Interpretation of the United States Constitution (2003). Revisits economic interpretation with quantitative methods and a more nuanced account than Beard’s original thesis. Useful for assessing material interests without reducing ideology to disguise.

David Waldstreicher, Slavery’s Constitution: From Revolution to Ratification (2009). A concise interpretation placing slavery at the center of constitutional development and the political bargains of union.

Gerald Leonard and Saul Cornell, The Partisan Republic: Democracy, Exclusion, and the Fall of the Founders’ Constitution, 1780s–1830s (2019). Situates the Constitution and Bill of Rights within the emergence of competing constitutional movements and highlights democracy, federalism, and exclusion.

Hannah Farber, “Political Economies,” in The Cambridge History of the American Revolution (2026). Recent scholarship emphasizing multiple competing political economies rather than a single constitutional economic settlement. Useful for updating older one-versus-two-model accounts of the founding era.

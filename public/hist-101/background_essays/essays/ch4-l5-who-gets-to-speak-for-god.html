Central Lecture Question

How did the Great Awakening turn a movement for personal conversion into a crisis over who possessed legitimate religious authority?

Learning Objectives

  1. Analyze the religious, institutional, and transatlantic conditions that produced the revivals of the 1720s through 1740s.
  2. Explain Jonathan Edwards’s Reformed theology of conversion and distinguish his theology from the stereotype of revivalism as anti-intellectual emotionalism.
  3. Evaluate how George Whitefield’s itinerancy, charisma, and use of print created a new kind of transcolonial religious authority.
  4. Compare New Light and Old Light arguments about conversion, clerical legitimacy, order, and religious experience, including the Presbyterian New Side–Old Side schism.
  5. Assess the social and institutional consequences of the Awakening and evaluate competing historical interpretations of its relationship to the American Revolution.

Essay Outline

Section 1: Provincial Religion Before the Awakening — Order, Respectability, and the Problem of Authenticity

Anglicization Did Not Mean Secularization

The Great Awakening makes the most sense when placed inside the broader transformation of eighteenth-century British America. The colonies were becoming larger, wealthier, more literate, and more closely connected to the British Atlantic. Imported consumer goods multiplied. Newspapers and books circulated more widely. Colonial elites copied British architecture, manners, educational practices, and political forms. The textbook describes this broad process as anglicization, and religion belonged to it. Clergy were increasingly educated, congregations more settled, and church life more institutional than it had been in the fragile founding generation.

None of this meant that religion was disappearing. The old “declension” narrative — the idea that the children and grandchildren of the founders simply became less religious — is too blunt. Patricia Bonomi’s work is especially useful here: colonial religion in the eighteenth century was vigorous, varied, and deeply woven into local society even when ministers complained that piety was cold. Congregational churches remained established by law in much of New England; the Church of England enjoyed legal establishment in colonies such as Virginia and South Carolina; Dutch Reformed, Presbyterian, Lutheran, Quaker, Baptist, and other communities flourished in the middle colonies. What changed was not the presence of religion but the conditions under which religious authority operated.

Those conditions varied sharply by region. New England inherited a dense network of towns, churches, schools, and colleges from its Puritan past. In the Chesapeake and lower South, Anglican parishes were geographically larger and clerical supply was thinner. The middle colonies were more pluralistic, with several confessions competing for adherents in the same towns and counties. Religious diversity could encourage toleration, but it also encouraged competition. A minister’s authority was less self-evident when another preacher, another congregation, or another denomination stood only a few miles away.

The Anxiety Behind “Dead Formality”

Revivalists did not usually claim that churches had ceased to exist; they claimed that churches could exist without saving religion. This distinction is crucial. A person might be baptized, attend worship, learn doctrine, behave respectably, and still lack the inward transformation that evangelicals called the “new birth.” The accusation of “dead formality” therefore attacked the possibility that religion could become socially respectable while spiritually empty.

New England had long wrestled with this problem. Seventeenth-century Puritans expected a visible church composed of people who could testify to a conversion experience, but the difficulty of maintaining that ideal led to compromises such as the Half-Way Covenant. In Northampton, Massachusetts, Solomon Stoddard went further than many Congregationalists by broadening access to communion and by presiding over repeated seasons of intense religious concern. His grandson and successor, Jonathan Edwards, inherited a congregation already familiar with periodic “harvests” of souls. Edwards did not invent revival from nothing; he inherited a local tradition that expected sudden collective awakenings as possible signs of divine action.

The same is true outside New England. Around 1720 the Dutch Reformed minister Theodorus Jacobus Frelinghuysen promoted experiential piety in New Jersey’s Raritan Valley. His preaching emphasized repentance, disciplined holiness, and a religion that could be known in personal experience rather than assumed from membership in a respectable church. Nearby Presbyterians watched closely. William Tennent, an Ulster immigrant who had moved from Anglicanism to Presbyterianism, trained his sons and other young ministers at the informal school later called the Log College in Neshaminy, Pennsylvania. His son Gilbert Tennent absorbed the conviction that ministers needed not only theological education and ordination but also personal conversion.

These Middle Colony revivals mattered because they reveal how many streams flowed into the phenomenon later called the Great Awakening. Dutch and German Pietist influences were important, but they were not the sole source. Reformed and Puritan traditions of conversion, Scots-Irish Presbyterianism, Moravian piety, early Methodism, and British and Scottish revival currents all contributed. By the 1730s, the Atlantic Protestant world already contained networks of people who expected sudden conversion, circulated reports of awakenings, and judged ministers by their capacity to awaken sinners.

A Transatlantic Revival Made Visible by Print

The revival was transatlantic in two senses. First, ministers and ideas traveled. John and Charles Wesley’s Holy Club at Oxford, Moravian missionaries, Scottish Presbyterians, English Methodists, New England Congregationalists, and Middle Colony Presbyterians were increasingly aware of one another. Second, reports traveled even faster than people. Newspapers, journals, letters, printed sermons, and conversion narratives allowed local episodes to be interpreted as parts of a larger work of God.

Frank Lambert’s Inventing the “Great Awakening” is especially helpful for lecture preparation because it explains why the movement could feel continental even though it was geographically uneven. No central organization planned a single revival from New England to Georgia. Instead, revival promoters used print to announce meetings, report conversions, defend preachers, answer critics, and connect events separated by hundreds or thousands of miles. The printed story of revival helped create the sense of a revival movement. In this respect the Great Awakening belonged to the same eighteenth-century world of print, mobility, consumption, and communication that also produced the Enlightenment and anglicization.

The label itself requires caution. Contemporaries certainly spoke of “awakenings” and of extraordinary works of God, but they did not operate under a single organization officially named the Great Awakening. Nineteenth-century histories, especially Joseph Tracy’s 1842 The Great Awakening, helped consolidate many revivals into a single capitalized event. Jon Butler later used this fact to question whether historians had imposed too much unity on a series of diverse local revivals. Lambert’s answer is more subtle: the unity was not merely invented by later historians, because eighteenth-century revivalists themselves were already using print to make scattered events appear interconnected. The “Great Awakening” is therefore best understood as both a real network of related revivals and a historical interpretation of those revivals.

Instructor note

Avoid the simple classroom binary “Enlightenment versus Great Awakening.” The same print culture, literacy, voluntary choice, and transatlantic communication that carried Enlightenment ideas also carried revival religion. The useful contrast is not reason versus faith but competing ways of recognizing truth and legitimate authority.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Before class, consider: if the revivals depended on newspapers, travel, consumer print, and voluntary choice, should the Great Awakening be treated as a rejection of eighteenth-century modernization — or as one of its religious forms?

The Conceptual Payoff

The Great Awakening was not a retreat from the modernizing eighteenth-century world. It used the new world of print, mobility, competition, and choice to reopen an old Protestant question: how can anyone know whether outward religion is inwardly genuine?

Section 2: Jonathan Edwards — Conversion, Grace, and the Evidence of Authentic Religion

A Reformed Theologian, Not an Anti-Intellectual Enthusiast

Jonathan Edwards is often remembered through a single classroom image: a grim Puritan preacher dangling sinners over hell. That caricature obscures why he matters. Born in East Windsor, Connecticut, in 1703, Edwards entered Yale as a teenager and developed into one of the most formidable theologians and philosophers in the British Atlantic. He read deeply in Reformed theology, natural philosophy, and contemporary thought, including John Locke. His mind was analytical, systematic, and intensely interested in how human beings perceive beauty, truth, desire, and divine action.

Edwards’s theology remained recognizably Calvinist. Human beings were not morally neutral agents capable of earning salvation by making themselves good. Sin had disordered the will. Salvation depended on God’s grace rather than on human merit. Justification came through faith, and genuine conversion involved a “new birth” in which the sinner acquired new spiritual affections — new loves, desires, and perceptions oriented toward God. This made the difference between outward respectability and saving religion fundamental. A person could know doctrine, attend church, and behave decently without possessing the transformed spiritual disposition that Edwards regarded as evidence of grace.

When a dramatic revival began in Northampton in 1734, Edwards interpreted it through this theological framework. The event was not important merely because people cried, fainted, or became frightened. What mattered was whether sinners experienced conviction, turned toward God, and exhibited enduring changes in character. Edwards described the Northampton revival in A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God, published in London in 1737. The work circulated widely in Britain and helped place a Massachusetts town inside a transatlantic evangelical conversation. John Wesley read the Narrative and was impressed by its account of sudden conversions.

Emotion as Evidence — and the Problem of False Evidence

Edwards’s defense of revivalism was more intellectually demanding than the familiar contrast between emotion and reason suggests. He believed genuine grace necessarily affected the emotions, but he did not believe that intense emotion automatically proved grace. A person could tremble, cry out, fall down, feel joy, or experience visions and still be self-deceived. The central problem was epistemological: if God’s grace works inwardly, how can either the convert or the community distinguish true conversion from temporary excitement?

This problem became more urgent as the revivals spread. Critics pointed to bodily convulsions, loud outcries, impulsive judgments, and people who seemed converted one week but returned to old behavior later. Edwards conceded much of the criticism. In Some Thoughts Concerning the Present Revival of Religion in New England (1742) and later in A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections (1746), he argued that neither unusual bodily effects nor intense feelings were reliable by themselves. The strongest evidence of authentic conversion was a durable transformation of the person’s affections and conduct. His project can therefore be described, as the textbook insightfully suggests, as a rational defense of religious emotion rather than an abandonment of reason.

“Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God”: What the Terror Is Doing

The sermon that has come to stand for Edwards’s revival preaching was delivered at Enfield, Connecticut, on July 8, 1741. That date and place matter. The famous Enfield sermon came after the Northampton revival of 1734–1735 and after George Whitefield’s dramatic New England tour of 1740. It belongs to a moment when revival expectations were already widespread. The audience knew what an “awakening” could look like, and Edwards preached into that charged environment.

“The God that holds you over the pit of hell, much as one holds a spider … abhors you.” — Jonathan Edwards, Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God, Enfield, Connecticut, 1741

The spider image is memorable because it condenses Edwards’s theology into a physical picture. The sinner is not standing safely on moral ground and deciding whether to step toward God. The sinner is already suspended over destruction, unable to secure himself. The only reason he does not fall is God’s restraining hand. The sermon’s horror therefore has a doctrinal purpose: it makes human dependence on grace emotionally imaginable. Fear is not the final goal. The sermon eventually turns toward the possibility of mercy, urging hearers to flee toward salvation while the opportunity remains.

This is why “fire and brimstone” is an incomplete description. Edwards wanted listeners to feel the instability of self-reliance. A respectable church member could not take security from ancestry, baptism, morality, intelligence, or social standing. The awakened sinner had to confront the possibility that all of those external securities were inadequate. In a society in which family, office, education, and reputation organized public status, the theology of conversion introduced a disturbing alternative standard: the most important status a person possessed might be invisible to ordinary institutions.

Terrifying Language, Restrained Delivery

Students often assume that a terrifying sermon must have been delivered theatrically. Edwards provides a useful contrast between rhetorical content and physical performance. His normal preaching practice was manuscript-centered, carefully composed, and comparatively restrained. He was not famous for pacing the stage, imitating voices, or using dramatic gestures. Contemporary accounts of the Enfield sermon describe the audience crying out so loudly that the service was interrupted, but the emotional eruption belonged primarily to the hearers rather than to a flamboyant performance by Edwards.

The distinction matters because it helps students see two different revival styles. Edwards sought to make doctrine vivid through tightly constructed argument and image. Whitefield, by contrast, became famous for theatrical voice, gesture, impersonation, tears, and physical presence. Both could produce intense responses, but they represent different technologies of persuasion. Edwards’s case also prevents the mistaken assumption that revival religion was anti-intellectual. His revivals rested on one of the most philosophically sophisticated theological systems in colonial America.

Instructor note

The safest classroom phrasing is “terrifying words, restrained delivery.” Avoid claiming that Edwards never varied his voice or never looked up from a manuscript; the evidence is more complicated. The important correction is that he was not Whitefield-style theatrical entertainment.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Before class, consider: if Edwards insisted that emotional intensity by itself did not prove conversion, why did he deliberately use imagery designed to produce fear? What distinction was he making between emotion as an instrument and emotion as evidence?

The Conceptual Payoff

Edwards turned revival into a problem of evidence. True religion had to be experienced inwardly, but inward experience had to be interpreted and tested. The authority question therefore moved inside the believer: not “Did I perform the right forms?” but “What would count as evidence that grace has actually transformed me?”

Section 3: George Whitefield — Itinerancy, Print, Celebrity, and Portable Authority

From Oxford Methodism to Open-Air Preaching

If Edwards explains the theology of conversion, George Whitefield explains how revival became a mass public phenomenon. Born in Gloucester, England, in 1714, Whitefield attended Oxford and joined the circle around John and Charles Wesley known derisively as the “Methodists” because of their methodical religious discipline. Whitefield was ordained in the Church of England, but he developed a strongly evangelical ministry centered on the new birth. Although he remained connected to Anglicanism, he eventually differed from John Wesley on predestination: Whitefield remained more Calvinist while Wesley moved toward an Arminian understanding of grace and free response.

Whitefield’s early career also tied him to Georgia. He traveled there as a young minister and founded the Bethesda Orphan House near Savannah. To raise money for the project he returned to Britain, where he discovered his extraordinary ability to draw crowds. When suspicious clergy closed their pulpits to him, he preached outdoors. Fields, commons, courthouse steps, and other public spaces became religious venues. What might have been an institutional defeat — exclusion from established pulpits — became a strategic advantage, because outdoor preaching allowed audiences far larger than any church building could hold.

The American Tours, 1739–1741

Whitefield’s second major trip to British America in 1739 transformed the scale of the revivals. He landed in the Delaware region, preached through Philadelphia, New Jersey, and New York, then traveled south through the Chesapeake and South Carolina before returning north for a major New England tour in 1740. He met Benjamin Franklin, Gilbert Tennent, and Jonathan Edwards. Newspapers reported his movements. Admirers traveled ahead or behind him. Ministers invited him into some pulpits and barred him from others. Wherever he went, the controversy itself generated publicity.

Crowd figures must be treated cautiously. Eighteenth-century observers did not take ticket counts, and revival supporters had an incentive to magnify success. Yet even skeptical observers accepted that Whitefield could attract extraordinary audiences. Benjamin Franklin, who admired Whitefield’s oratory without sharing his theology, famously experimented with the preacher’s acoustical range in Philadelphia.

“He had a loud and clear voice, and articulated his words and sentences so perfectly.” — Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, recalling George Whitefield

Franklin walked backward from the courthouse while Whitefield preached and estimated how many listeners might fit within the area in which the voice remained intelligible. His calculation suggested an audience of more than thirty thousand was physically possible, which made newspaper claims of crowds in the tens of thousands seem less absurd to him. The calculation should not be treated as a census, but it reveals how contemporaries themselves tried to understand Whitefield as a new mass phenomenon.

Print and the Creation of a Transcolonial Celebrity

Whitefield’s authority rested on a powerful combination: ordination, a compelling conversion message, theatrical skill, constant travel, and print publicity. His journals and sermons circulated in cheap editions. Newspapers announced his arrivals and departures. Critics published attacks, which gave revivalists more material to answer. Benjamin Franklin printed Whitefield’s writings because they sold well, even though Franklin remained religiously independent. The relationship is a reminder that religious revival and commercial print were not opposites. The evangelical message traveled through the same market institutions that sold political pamphlets, almanacs, advertisements, and consumer news.

Harry Stout’s description of Whitefield as a “divine dramatist” captures the performance dimension. Whitefield had acted as a youth and brought unusual vocal and bodily expressiveness to preaching. He could shift characters, weep, whisper, thunder, and make biblical scenes feel immediate. Contemporary listeners sometimes described his pronunciation itself as emotionally irresistible. His authority therefore looked different from Edwards’s. Edwards asked whether the hearer had experienced genuine grace; Whitefield added the spectacle of a preacher whose perceived spiritual authenticity seemed confirmed by thousands of voluntary listeners.

This voluntarism was institutionally disruptive. A parish minister possessed authority because he had been ordained and settled over a congregation. Whitefield possessed authority because people chose to hear him, newspapers publicized him, other ministers endorsed him, and his preaching appeared to produce conversions. If a layperson left his own parish to hear Whitefield in a field, that act implicitly compared two kinds of authority. The local minister could reasonably ask whether orderly church government could survive if charismatic outsiders entered any community, judged the resident clergy, and invited people to follow them elsewhere.

Whitefield also created a shared religious conversation across provincial boundaries. A Bostonian, Philadelphian, Charlestonian, and New Yorker could all have an opinion about the same preacher. This did not create American nationalism in the 1740s, and Whitefield himself was an Englishman operating within the British Atlantic. But it did demonstrate that a public figure could become famous from Georgia to New England through a combination of travel and print. Long before committees of correspondence or revolutionary pamphlet networks, colonial readers were already capable of participating in a transcolonial controversy.

Spiritual Inclusion and the Contradiction of Slavery

Whitefield also reveals one of the central limits of revivalist “equality.” He believed enslaved Africans possessed souls that required the gospel, and in 1740 he publicly rebuked southern masters for cruelty and for withholding Christian instruction. The letter was printed by Benjamin Franklin and addressed to slaveholders in Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, and South Carolina.

“I think God has a quarrel with you, for your abuse of and cruelty to the poor negroes.” — George Whitefield, Letter to the Inhabitants of Maryland, Virginia, North and South Carolina, 1740

The sentence sounds radical until its limits are made explicit. Whitefield did not conclude that slavery itself must end. He argued that Christianized enslaved people could remain servants and that masters should treat them more humanely. Within a few years he became an active advocate for legalizing slavery in Georgia, largely because he believed the Bethesda Orphan House could not become economically secure without enslaved labor. He acquired enslaved workers and died a slaveholder. His ministry thus extended the promise of salvation across the color line while leaving the legal structure of bondage intact.

That contradiction is pedagogically valuable because it prevents an easy equation between spiritual equality and social equality. Evangelicalism could tell a slaveholder and an enslaved person that both stood equally in need of grace before God, yet still permit one to own the other. The gap between spiritual claims and material institutions would become a recurring problem in American history. Some later evangelicals would turn Christian equality against slavery; others would use Christianity to stabilize slaveholding. Whitefield’s own career contains both the possibility and the limit.

Instructor note

Do not turn Whitefield’s slavery position into a detached morality tale. It belongs directly to the authority theme: the same preacher who crossed parish and denominational boundaries could radically widen the audience for salvation while accepting the coercive social order of slavery. Religious voluntarism and social hierarchy could coexist.

The Conceptual Payoff

Whitefield made religious authority portable. A preacher could now build legitimacy across parish and colonial boundaries through reputation, media, performance, and voluntary audiences. That development weakened the monopoly of local institutions without automatically producing social or political equality.

Section 4: From Revival to Institutional Crisis — Tennent, Chauncy, Schism, and New Colleges

Gilbert Tennent and the “Unconverted Ministry”

Gilbert Tennent pushed the authority problem farther than either Edwards or Whitefield needed to do. Born in County Armagh in 1703 and brought to British America with his family in 1718, Tennent was trained by his father William at the Log College and became a Presbyterian minister in New Brunswick, New Jersey. He learned from the Dutch Reformed revivals associated with Frelinghuysen and became one of the strongest defenders of experiential conversion among colonial Presbyterians.

On March 8, 1740, at Nottingham, Pennsylvania, Tennent preached The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry. The sermon argued that a minister could possess formal education, ordination, theological vocabulary, and ecclesiastical office while remaining spiritually unconverted. Tennent compared such ministers to the Pharisees and warned congregations about entrusting their souls to men who lacked the very experience they were supposed to cultivate in others.

“An ungodly ministry is a great curse and judgment. These caterpillars labor to devour every green thing.” — Gilbert Tennent, The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry, 1740

Tennent was not abolishing the ministry. He believed faithful ministers were essential. What made his sermon explosive was the standard by which faithfulness would be judged. If ordination could not prove conversion, then church courts and educational credentials could not settle the question. Ordinary hearers might have to decide whether their own minister was spiritually alive. In practice, that could justify seeking another preacher, inviting an itinerant into a parish, or abandoning a settled minister who appeared “dead.” The doctrine of conversion had become a public test of office.

The Presbyterian Schism: New Side and Old Side

Presbyterian government made this conflict especially visible because ministers belonged to presbyteries and synods that claimed authority over ordination and discipline. Revivalist ministers associated with the Log College sometimes preached across presbytery boundaries or questioned the spiritual condition of ministers who opposed the revivals. Critics complained that they were violating church order, intruding into other pastors’ charges, and weakening educational standards for the ministry.

The crisis produced the Old Side–New Side schism of 1741. The precise institutional sequence is worth keeping straight in instructor preparation. In 1741 the Synod of Philadelphia fractured and the revivalist New Brunswick group was excluded from participation. In 1745 the Presbytery of New York joined with revival sympathizers in forming the Synod of New York, the principal New Side body. The two sides eventually reunited in 1758. The reunion itself is significant: the controversy did not end with the permanent destruction of Presbyterianism. The denomination absorbed much of the revivalist emphasis on experiential religion while restoring standards of education and church order.

For first-year students, the terminology can be simplified without falsifying the history. Presbyterians usually spoke of New Side and Old Side. New England Congregationalists more commonly spoke of New Lights and Old Lights. The labels are not identical, but they name parallel conflicts: supporters of revival emphasized conversion and welcomed itinerant preaching; opponents emphasized order, learned ministry, and suspicion of religious “enthusiasm.” Students do not need four factions. They need to understand one recurrent problem expressed through different denominational vocabularies.

Charles Chauncy and the Case Against “Enthusiasm”

The strongest classroom opponent for the revivalists is Charles Chauncy, minister of Boston’s First Church and a leading Old Light. Chauncy was not irreligious, and he was not simply defending cold formalism. He feared that revivalists were treating bodily excitement as proof of divine action, undermining parish government, and allowing impulsive people to claim direct guidance from God. His 1743 Seasonable Thoughts on the State of Religion in New-England documented swooning, screaming, bodily agitation, itinerant intrusions, and the tendency of some revivalists to condemn established ministers as unconverted.

“Religion, of late, has been more a Commotion in the Passions than a Change in the Temper of the Mind.” — Charles Chauncy, Seasonable Thoughts on the State of Religion in New-England, 1743

Chauncy’s criticism cannot simply be dismissed as elite resistance to popular religion. Revival excesses gave him evidence. The itinerant James Davenport, for example, denounced ministers, encouraged separation from established congregations, claimed unusual spiritual impressions, and participated in public acts that critics regarded as fanaticism. Davenport later repudiated some of his own behavior. Such episodes made the Old Light fear of “enthusiasm” — the belief that one’s strong impulses came directly from God — more than a straw man.

Edwards’s mature defense of revivalism is powerful precisely because he shared part of Chauncy’s concern. Edwards agreed that bodily effects and emotional intensity could not prove grace. The disagreement concerned what should be concluded from that fact. Chauncy emphasized the danger of disorder and the need for reasoned religious control. Edwards argued that genuine grace necessarily engaged the affections and that the presence of excess did not invalidate the entire revival. The controversy was therefore not faith versus reason. It was a dispute over what kinds of evidence could identify authentic religion and what institutions had the right to police that evidence.

Schism Produced Institutions, Not the End of Institutions

One of the most important long-term consequences of the Great Awakening was institutional multiplication. If older schools and church structures were judged insufficiently evangelical, revivalists built alternatives. The College of New Jersey, founded in 1746 and later known as Princeton, is the clearest example. New Side Presbyterians wanted an institution that could provide learned ministers without surrendering the demand for experimental religion. The college therefore embodied a compromise that is easy to miss: revivalists did not reject education; they wanted education joined to conversion.

Other colleges emerged in the religiously competitive world strengthened by the revivals. The College of Rhode Island, later Brown University, was chartered in 1764 with strong Baptist sponsorship. Queen’s College, later Rutgers, was founded in 1766 within the Dutch Reformed world. Dartmouth College, chartered in 1769, grew from Eleazar Wheelock’s evangelical and missionary projects. These institutions were not all direct products of the revival of 1740, and they should not be taught as if Whitefield founded them during one tour. They belong to the longer institutional afterlife of evangelical competition and denominational expansion.

The contrast institutions need equal care. King’s College in New York, chartered in 1754, had a clear Anglican identity. The College of Philadelphia, later the University of Pennsylvania, should not be described simply as an Anglican “counter-college.” Benjamin Franklin envisioned it as broadly nonsectarian and practical. The larger historical pattern is not one evangelical educational system confronting one Anglican system. It is a crowded religious marketplace in which denominations and civic leaders increasingly founded institutions capable of training their own ministers and elites.

Denominational realignment also unfolded over decades rather than overnight. New England Congregationalists divided into New Light and Old Light camps; some revivalists became Separates and later Baptists. Presbyterians reunited but retained a stronger evangelical component. Baptists expanded especially in the later eighteenth century. Methodism’s major American growth came still later, particularly in the revolutionary and early national periods. The Awakening did not instantly redraw the denominational map, but it accelerated a transition toward a more competitive, voluntary, and evangelical Protestant culture.

Instructor note

The live lecture does not need the 1741/1745 Presbyterian institutional sequence unless a student asks. The preparation value is that it prevents the common shortcut “the church split in 1741 and the New Side immediately became the Synod of New York.” The schism began in 1741; the New York synod formed in 1745; reunion came in 1758.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Before class, consider: once congregants are told that they may judge whether an ordained minister is genuinely converted, what prevents religious communities from fragmenting indefinitely? Why did revivalism generate new institutions instead of simply destroying institutions?

The Conceptual Payoff

The crisis of authority became institutional. Revival did not eliminate clergy, churches, colleges, or denominational structures; it forced them to compete, divide, reform, and multiply. The long-term result was not less organization but a more pluralistic religious marketplace.

Section 5: Significance of the Great Awakening — Authority, Institutions, Participation, and Political Culture

“Behold, I Make All Things New”: What Actually Changed?

The language of renewal that surrounded eighteenth-century revivalism invited expansive claims about its consequences. Revelation 21:5 — “Behold, I make all things new” — captures the confidence of revivalists who believed that God was renewing churches, ministers, and individual believers. For historians, however, the phrase also poses a useful question: what, exactly, did the Great Awakening make new? The strongest answer is not that the revivals overturned colonial society or created a democratic nation ahead of schedule. Their significance lay in a series of more specific changes: they made inherited religious authority more contestable, strengthened a distinctive evangelical culture, generated new institutions, widened participation in religious life, rearranged denominational loyalties, created transcolonial networks, and contributed to a political culture in which appeals to conscience and challenges to established authority became more familiar.

These changes were consequential precisely because they were partial. Revivalists did not abolish churches, clergy, social hierarchy, slavery, patriarchy, or colonial rule. They did not agree on politics, and they did not imagine themselves as founders of an American nation. Yet the revivals changed the terms under which religious authority operated. They taught large numbers of colonists that office, education, inherited status, and institutional legitimacy were not by themselves sufficient evidence of spiritual authenticity. Once that premise entered colonial religious culture, it could be carried into institutions, denominations, public controversy, and eventually political argument.

1. A Challenge to Established Religious Authority

The most immediate significance of the Great Awakening was its challenge to the authority of established churches and settled clergy. Revivalists did not abolish the ministry. Edwards, Whitefield, Tennent, and their allies all believed that faithful ministers were essential to Christian life. What they denied was that formal credentials alone could prove that a minister possessed genuine spiritual authority. Education, ordination, denominational office, and social standing might establish a minister’s institutional legitimacy, but they could not demonstrate that he had experienced the new birth.

This distinction gave ordinary believers a larger role in judging religious leadership. If conversion was the indispensable sign of authentic Christianity, then congregants could ask whether their minister preached with spiritual power, whether his life displayed the fruits of grace, and whether his ministry produced genuine conversions. Tennent’s The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry made the logic explicit: a properly ordained minister might still be spiritually unfit. In practical terms, this encouraged laypeople to compare ministers, travel to hear itinerants, support revivalist preachers, or leave congregations they regarded as spiritually dead.

The old shorthand that revivalism “downgraded the clergy” therefore needs refinement. Revivalists did not make clergy unimportant; they changed the criteria by which clergy were judged. The minister’s office mattered, but conversion and spiritual effectiveness mattered more. This shift also helps explain why the revivals contributed to arguments about religious liberty. If individuals possessed a duty to seek authentic religion, then coercing them to support a church they considered spiritually false became harder to defend. Dissenters increasingly framed liberty of conscience as a natural or “unalienable” right and resisted taxation for religious establishments they did not accept.

Bernard Bailyn’s discussion of dissenting Protestantism is useful here because it connects religious controversy to the wider ideological language of liberty without collapsing the two. He noted that dissenters defended liberty of conscience so strongly that some preferred imprisonment or loss of property to paying taxes for churches they rejected, while others moved toward explicit arguments for separation of church and state. The Great Awakening did not invent liberty of conscience, but it intensified the practical circumstances in which colonists had to decide whether inherited religious authority deserved obedience.

Instructor note

For the live lecture, the clean formulation is: revivalism did not eliminate clergy; it made clerical authority conditional. That distinction preserves the authority thesis without suggesting an anti-institutional religious revolution.

2. A More Distinctive Colonial Evangelical Culture

The revivals also helped transform inherited European Protestant traditions into a more distinctive colonial evangelical culture. The older lecture phrase that the Great Awakening “turned European churches into American churches” captures a real process but states it too absolutely. Colonial churches remained deeply connected to British, Dutch, German, Scottish, and other Atlantic traditions. Whitefield was English, Presbyterianism remained transatlantic, Congregationalists inherited Puritan theology, and revivalists read European authors and followed European controversies. The Awakening was not a declaration of religious independence from Europe.

What changed was the environment in which those traditions operated. Colonial evangelicalism placed extraordinary emphasis on the new birth, accessible preaching, personal testimony, voluntary participation, and the practical evidence of conversion. Itinerant ministers crossed parish and colonial boundaries. Religious allegiance became more mobile. A believer might judge a preacher by spiritual effectiveness rather than by the prestige of his institutional office. This encouraged a religious culture that was competitive, mobile, and increasingly voluntary.

The term “frontier religion” should be used cautiously in this context. The First Great Awakening was not the camp-meeting religion of the early nineteenth century, and it should not be merged with the Second Great Awakening. Yet some of the habits associated with later American evangelicalism are visible here in early form: itinerancy, portable authority, suspicion of overly formal institutions, emotional conversion, and the willingness to create new congregations or institutions when older ones seemed spiritually inadequate.

The most defensible formulation is therefore that the Great Awakening strengthened a distinctive colonial evangelical culture. It did not sever American Protestantism from Europe; it altered the way European Protestant traditions functioned in a colonial setting characterized by distance, denominational competition, print circulation, migration, and voluntary choice.

3. New Institutions and an Emphasis on Education

The Great Awakening’s challenge to older institutions did not produce an anti-intellectual or anti-educational religious culture. On the contrary, revival conflict stimulated institutional construction. When revivalists believed that existing colleges or church courts did not adequately support experiential religion, they founded alternatives. The clearest example is the College of New Jersey, chartered in 1746 and later known as Princeton. New Side Presbyterians wanted ministers who were both learned and genuinely converted. The college therefore embodied an evangelical synthesis rather than a rejection of education.

Other institutions emerged from the broader denominational world strengthened by revival. The College of Rhode Island, later Brown University, was chartered in 1764 with strong Baptist sponsorship. Queen’s College, later Rutgers University, was founded in 1766 within the Dutch Reformed tradition. Dartmouth College, chartered in 1769, grew out of Eleazar Wheelock’s evangelical missionary work and his earlier school for Native students. These institutions were not all direct products of the revival wave of 1740–1741, and they should not be taught as if Whitefield’s tours immediately produced a chain of college foundings. They belong to the longer institutional afterlife of religious competition and evangelical expansion.

The older lecture’s contrast between evangelical colleges and supposed Anglican “counter-colleges” also needs qualification. King’s College in New York, chartered in 1754 and later renamed Columbia, had a clear Anglican identity. The College of Philadelphia, later the University of Pennsylvania, was different. Benjamin Franklin envisioned it as broadly nonsectarian and practical, not simply as an Anglican answer to revivalism. The larger pattern is more important than a denominational scoreboard: eighteenth-century religious competition encouraged churches, denominations, and civic leaders to create institutions capable of educating ministers and elites on their own terms.

The educational consequence also corrects the old phrase that revivalists emphasized “piety over intellectualism.” Revivalists certainly insisted that intellectual credentials did not prove conversion. But figures such as Edwards, the Tennents, and the founders of Princeton did not believe learning was irrelevant. Their argument was that learning without piety was insufficient. The Great Awakening therefore did not replace education with emotion; it helped create institutions that sought to join education to evangelical conviction.

4. Revival Religion Reached Beyond the Traditional Religious Elite

The revivals widened religious participation beyond the world of established white male clerical authority, but the nature of that widening must be stated carefully. Women had long been central to colonial church life and in many communities outnumbered men among committed members. The Great Awakening did not suddenly discover female piety. What it provided were additional forms of religious speech and networking. Women recorded conversion narratives, corresponded with ministers, hosted meetings, testified to spiritual experience, and sometimes exhorted others. Such participation could give women greater religious influence without changing the overwhelmingly male structure of ordination and formal church government.

The same distinction matters for enslaved people. Whitefield and other evangelicals insisted that Africans were capable of conversion and should hear the gospel. That claim could challenge assumptions of spiritual inferiority, and enslaved hearers could appropriate evangelical teaching in ways masters did not control. Yet early revivalism did not produce a general abolitionist movement. Whitefield himself exposes the contradiction most clearly. He rebuked southern masters for cruelty and neglect of enslaved people’s souls, but he did not conclude that slavery itself had to end. He later advocated legalization of slavery in Georgia and became a slaveholder.

Native participation likewise demonstrates both the reach and the limits of evangelical networks. Samson Occom, a Mohegan born in 1723, converted during the Great Awakening, studied with the New Light minister Eleazar Wheelock, and became a minister, teacher, diplomat, and intellectual. His life demonstrates that Native converts could become active religious agents rather than merely objects of missionary work. Yet evangelical missions were still embedded in colonial structures of power and assimilation. The same networks that created opportunities for Occom also helped build institutions that increasingly served white colonists.

The correct conclusion is therefore not that the Awakening created egalitarian democracy. It is that the revivals widened the religious public. They addressed women, enslaved Africans, Native people, artisans, servants, and elites as sinners equally dependent on grace. That theological equality could destabilize assumptions about who was capable of religious knowledge or whose conversion testimony mattered. But it did not automatically alter property law, voting rights, coverture, racial slavery, land seizure, or clerical office.

Spiritual inclusion was not social equality. The Great Awakening widened participation faster than it changed colonial legal or political structures.

5. Denominational Boundaries Became More Fluid — and New Divisions Appeared

Another long-term consequence was the reorganization of denominational identity. Revivalists often cooperated across existing boundaries because they shared an emphasis on conversion and evangelical experience. An itinerant preacher might enter a community outside his own denomination, and supporters could regard revivalists elsewhere as spiritual allies. Pulpits, correspondence networks, and printed sermons crossed institutional lines. In such settings salvation could seem more important than strict conformity to liturgical or denominational distinctions.

Yet it would be misleading to say simply that the Awakening “blurred denominational lines.” The same movement that created cross-denominational evangelical solidarity also produced bitter conflict within denominations. Congregationalists divided into New Light and Old Light camps. Presbyterians divided into New Side and Old Side factions. Some Congregational revivalists became Separates and later Baptists. Denominational boundaries became more fluid in one direction while new revivalist–anti-revivalist boundaries hardened in another.

This apparent contradiction is one of the Awakening’s most important institutional effects. A revival-minded Congregationalist might feel more affinity with a revival-minded Presbyterian than with an Old Light minister in his own denomination. The revivals therefore did not erase denominational difference; they added a new axis of religious identity. Whether one accepted the revival, itinerancy, and conversion-centered religion could matter as much as inherited denominational affiliation.

Over the longer eighteenth century, this competitive environment contributed to the growth of evangelical denominations, especially Baptists and Presbyterians. Methodism would expand dramatically later, particularly during and after the Revolutionary era. The Great Awakening should not be treated as an instant denominational realignment, but it accelerated the movement toward a more voluntary and competitive Protestant marketplace.

6. A Shared Transcolonial Experience

The revivals also helped create one of the earliest large-scale cultural experiences shared across several British colonies. That claim should not be confused with the stronger statement that the Great Awakening created American nationalism. In the 1740s there was no independent American nation, and most colonists still understood themselves within local, provincial, and British identities. Yet the movement demonstrated that people separated by hundreds of miles could participate in the same public controversy.

George Whitefield was central to this development. A Bostonian, Philadelphian, New Yorker, or Charlestonian could all know his name, read accounts of his sermons, discuss his methods, and argue about his legitimacy. Newspapers reported his movements and the reactions he provoked. Printed sermons, journals, attacks, defenses, and conversion narratives made revival a shared topic. These networks did not eliminate provincial differences, but they made colonial readers increasingly aware that events in distant colonies could matter to them.

The Awakening therefore altered the sense of colonial separateness without producing nationalism in the later political sense. It created transcolonial networks and common reference points. The same media environment that carried news of Whitefield and revival controversy could later carry political pamphlets, reports of imperial policy, petitions, and arguments over resistance. The causal connection should not be overstated, but the communications infrastructure and habits of intercolonial attention were real.

This is the strongest version of the old lecture claim that the Awakening “contributed to American nationalism.” It is more precise to say that the movement contributed to an intercolonial consciousness: colonists could increasingly imagine themselves participating in a common controversy that crossed provincial borders.

7. Was the Great Awakening a Proto-Revolutionary Event?

The final significance claim is also the most controversial. The Great Awakening has often been interpreted as a “proto-revolutionary” event because it challenged inherited authority, encouraged popular participation, defended liberty of conscience, created voluntary networks, and accustomed colonists to mobilizing across provincial boundaries. Those parallels are real enough to demand explanation. They are not strong enough to establish direct causation by themselves.

The weakest argument is simply chronological: the Awakening happened before the Revolution. Chronology alone proves almost nothing. The main revival wave of the 1730s and early 1740s preceded the imperial crisis that began after 1763 by roughly a generation. A causal argument therefore requires a mechanism connecting religious experience to later political behavior.

Alan Heimert supplied one of the strongest such mechanisms in Religion and the American Mind: From the Great Awakening to the Revolution (1966). Heimert challenged accounts that made Enlightenment rationalism the primary intellectual source of the Revolution. He argued that revivalist Calvinism fostered a more radical, participatory political culture and that evangelical religion helped shape revolutionary ideology. In this interpretation, the revival pulpit carried habits of popular judgment and moral opposition into the political world.

Heimert’s thesis was influential because it restored religion to the history of the Revolution. It was also too neat in places. Religious alignments in the 1740s did not map cleanly onto Patriot and Loyalist alignments in the 1770s. Charles Chauncy, one of the most prominent Old Light critics of revival enthusiasm, became an ardent Patriot. Some revival supporters remained loyal to Britain. New Light theology did not automatically produce revolutionary politics.

Patricia Bonomi offered a more cautious and institutionally grounded interpretation. Rather than claiming that revival theology directly caused republican ideology, she emphasized the political significance of religious competition, popular participation, denominational organization, and an “ideology of dissent.” Congregations learned to petition, organize, challenge establishments, build networks, and defend claims of conscience. In this version, the most important link between Awakening and Revolution lies not in one doctrine but in a repertoire of collective action and a habit of treating authority as contestable.

Bernard Bailyn’s work places this religious dissent within a broader ideological world. Bailyn did not argue that the Great Awakening caused the Revolution, but he recognized that dissenting Protestant arguments about liberty of conscience and coercive establishments contributed to the language of liberty later used in revolutionary debate. Religious dissent joined, rather than replaced, English constitutional argument, commonwealth thought, and inherited claims about the rights of Englishmen.

Jon Butler’s 1982 article, “Enthusiasm Described and Decried: The Great Awakening as Interpretative Fiction,” challenged the category itself. Historians, he argued, had made a fragmented and geographically uneven set of revivals carry too much explanatory weight. Butler’s critique remains a warning against teaching the Awakening as a single wave that transformed every colony in the same way or as an automatic prelude to democracy.

Frank Lambert later complicated Butler by showing that the “invention” of a unified Awakening began in the eighteenth century. Revival promoters used print to connect local revivals, circulate reports, and persuade readers that God was carrying out one extraordinary work across the Atlantic. The movement’s unity was therefore constructed, but constructed by contemporaries as well as by later historians. Thomas Kidd’s synthesis similarly preserves the reality and significance of the revivals while resisting the claim that they created a single political trajectory.

Paul Johnson’s A History of the American People (1997) offers the strongest version of the older argument and gives it its most memorable classroom phrase:

“The Great Awakening was thus the proto-revolutionary event.”
— Paul Johnson, A History of the American People, 1997

Johnson emphasizes democratization of religious experience, weakened clerical authority, reduced concern for parish and denominational boundaries, transcolonial unity, and the capacity of popular enthusiasm to be redirected toward political goals. Those are the essential arguments of the older lecture, and they remain useful if stated as a thesis rather than a fact. The language of “all men are created equal” should not be presented as a direct product of revival religion, but the broader habit of judging authority and defending conscience does provide a plausible cultural connection.

The most defensible conclusion is intermediate. The Great Awakening did not cause the American Revolution. Revivalists were trying to save souls, reform churches, and identify genuine religion, not create a new nation. Immediate revolutionary causation came from imperial warfare, taxation, constitutional conflict, western land issues, parliamentary policy, and British coercion after 1763. If the Awakening belongs in the causal story, it belongs primarily at the level of political culture.

The revivals may have helped some colonists become accustomed to:

  • judging inherited authorities rather than accepting them automatically;
  • defending liberty of conscience;
  • organizing voluntary associations;
  • participating in public controversy;
  • building networks across provincial boundaries;
  • mobilizing popular enthusiasm around shared ideas.

Those habits were transferable. They could later be channeled toward political purposes that Edwards, Whitefield, or Tennent did not intend. That is a stronger and more historically defensible formulation than treating the Awakening as a direct rehearsal for independence.

Instructor note

Preserve the “proto-revolutionary” language because it gives students a clear historiographical problem. Present Johnson as the strongest version, Heimert as the major scholarly predecessor, Bonomi as a more cautious institutional and cultural mechanism, and Butler as the corrective against over-unification and direct causation.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Before class, consider: which consequence of the Great Awakening offers the strongest plausible bridge to later revolutionary politics — liberty of conscience, voluntary association, challenges to clerical authority, transcolonial networks, or habits of popular mobilization? What evidence would be needed to demonstrate that the connection was causal rather than merely chronological?

The Conceptual Payoff

The Great Awakening mattered because it changed the religious terms of authority, participation, institution building, and public connection. It widened the religious public without creating social equality, and it created habits of judgment and dissent without causing political revolution. Its deepest significance lies in making authority more conditional: inherited institutions increasingly had to demonstrate why they deserved allegiance.

Closing Synthesis

The Great Awakening is easiest to misunderstand when it is reduced to emotion. Its deeper history is a struggle over authenticity and authority. Eighteenth-century colonial religion had become more institutional, more educated, and more connected to the British Atlantic. Revivalists did not reject that modernizing world; they used its print networks, travel routes, schools, and voluntary associations to insist that outward religion was not enough. The central evangelical claim was that genuine Christianity required inward conversion — a new birth that could not be guaranteed by family, social status, church membership, education, or ordination.

Jonathan Edwards supplied the theological architecture. His terrifying images dramatized dependence on grace, but his measured defense of revival insisted that emotion alone was not proof. George Whitefield supplied mobility, performance, and mass publicity, creating a form of authority that could travel outside parish boundaries. Gilbert Tennent made the institutional challenge explicit by arguing that an ordained minister might be spiritually unqualified. Charles Chauncy and other Old Lights responded with a serious defense of order and with evidence that revival excess could become disruptive. The resulting conflicts divided churches, created new colleges and networks, and helped produce a more competitive evangelical culture.

The movement’s reach exceeded the world of white male clergy, but its social consequences remained constrained. Women, enslaved people, and Native people participated in evangelical networks and could appropriate the language of conversion for their own purposes. Yet slavery, patriarchy, and colonial power survived. That same mixture of spiritual inclusion and institutional inequality explains why the political legacy of the Awakening remains contested. The revivals may have taught some colonists to evaluate authority, defend conscience, join voluntary networks, and imagine connections beyond provincial borders. They did not tell colonists to declare independence.

The lecture’s strongest closing formulation: The Great Awakening did not cause the American Revolution. Its significance was broader and more indirect: it helped create a religious culture in which authority was increasingly conditional, institutions had to compete for allegiance, ordinary believers participated more visibly in public religious life, and some colonists became practiced at asking whether inherited authority was legitimate — and at acting when they concluded it was not.

Open Question for Lecture Preparation

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — If the Great Awakening’s most important legacy was the right to test authority rather than a specific political doctrine, how should we distinguish between religious dissent that destabilizes institutions and religious dissent that ultimately strengthens them by forcing reform?

Suggested Readings

The following works provide the most useful scholarly grounding for this lecture. Together they cover the theology of revival, transatlantic print culture, Whitefield’s performance, institutional and political consequences, and the historiographical debate over whether there was one coherent “Great Awakening.”

Thomas S. Kidd, The Great Awakening: The Roots of Evangelical Christianity in Colonial America (2007).

The best single-volume synthesis for this lecture. Kidd treats the revivals as the formation of early evangelicalism rather than as one perfectly unified event, and he follows both famous leaders and lesser-known participants. Especially useful for social reach, denominational diversity, and the tension between spiritual equality and persistent hierarchy.

Frank Lambert, Inventing the “Great Awakening” (1999).

Essential for understanding print, publicity, and the construction of a transatlantic revival consciousness. Lambert explains how promoters used newspapers, journals, and printed narratives to make dispersed local revivals appear to be one extraordinary work of God. This is the strongest scholarly foundation for the lecture’s connection between Chapter 4 print culture and Whitefield’s celebrity.

Harry S. Stout, The Divine Dramatist: George Whitefield and the Rise of Modern Evangelicalism (1991).

The classic study of Whitefield as performer, itinerant, and religious celebrity. Stout is especially valuable for explaining why Whitefield’s delivery was not merely decoration but part of a new evangelical method of persuasion. Use alongside newer scholarship to keep Whitefield’s relationship to slavery in view.

George M. Marsden, Jonathan Edwards: A Life (2003).

The standard modern biography of Edwards. Marsden places Edwards’s theology, pastoral work, revivalism, intellectual life, and social world in context and is useful for correcting the classroom caricature of Edwards as simply a “fire-and-brimstone” ranter. Particularly helpful for distinguishing the sophistication of Edwards’s theology from the emotional effects of his preaching.

Patricia U. Bonomi, Under the Cope of Heaven: Religion, Society, and Politics in Colonial America (1986; updated ed. 2003).

A foundational account of religion as a central institution in colonial life rather than a declining remnant of the seventeenth century. Bonomi’s chapters on the Awakening, denominational conflict, and the “political awakening” provide a more cautious route from religious dissent to revolutionary politics than Heimert’s stronger thesis.

Alan Heimert, Religion and the American Mind: From the Great Awakening to the Revolution (1966).

The major scholarly predecessor to strong “proto-revolutionary” interpretations. Heimert argues that evangelical Calvinism played a radical role in shaping the intellectual and political world of the Revolution. The book is demanding and its alignments are too neat for current consensus, but it remains indispensable for understanding why historians connect revival religion to independence.

Jon Butler, “Enthusiasm Described and Decried: The Great Awakening as Interpretative Fiction,” Journal of American History 69, no. 2 (1982): 305–325.

The classic revisionist challenge. Butler argues that historians had imposed too much unity and too much explanatory weight on geographically uneven revivals. It is the best counterweight to Heimert and Johnson, and it prepares the way for a post-revisionist synthesis in which the revivals are real and important without becoming the master cause of American democracy or revolution.

Primary Sources Worth Keeping at Hand

Jonathan Edwards, A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God (1737); Edwards, Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God (1741); Gilbert Tennent, The Danger of an Unconverted Ministry (1740); Charles Chauncy, Seasonable Thoughts on the State of Religion in New-England (1743); Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, passages on Whitefield; George Whitefield, Letter to the Inhabitants of Maryland, Virginia, North and South Carolina (1740). These sources let the lecture move from summary to evidence without requiring students to master the full theological literature.

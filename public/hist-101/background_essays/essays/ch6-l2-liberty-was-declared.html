Liberty Was Declared. Who Could Claim It?

Revolutionary language and the people who tested its limits

Essential question

When Americans declared universal liberty, who possessed enough power to claim it?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain how revolutionary language became a resource that different historical actors used to pursue distinct and sometimes incompatible goals.
  2. Analyze the choices enslaved and free Black people made between British and Patriot opportunities during the war.
  3. Evaluate the Revolution’s uneven effects on slavery, emancipation, and racial ideology across regions.
  4. Interpret Native alliances and resistance as strategies for preserving sovereignty rather than as secondary attachments to the imperial combatants.
  5. Compare the expansion of rights for some Americans with the continued exclusion, dispossession, or displacement of others.

Introductory Framing

The Declaration of Independence announced propositions about natural rights and human equality whose language could travel far beyond the immediate dispute with Britain. Yet declarations do not enforce themselves. The Revolution created openings through war, state-building, legal change, migration, military service, and political argument, but the ability to convert those openings into freedom depended on access to institutions and leverage. Enslaved people could flee to an army, negotiate service, petition, sue, or seek manumission. Native nations could mobilize warriors, negotiate alliances, defend boundaries, and exploit imperial rivalries. Women could assume new economic and civic responsibilities, organize consumption, petition governments, and invoke the logic of consent. Loyalists could appeal to imperial rights and protection. Each claim confronted counterclaims grounded in property, conquest, patriarchy, local majority rule, or racial hierarchy.

The lecture’s central formulation—revolutionary ideals created claims, but power determined outcomes—avoids two opposite simplifications. The first celebrates universal ideals as though their articulation automatically liberated those excluded from political citizenship. The second dismisses the Revolution as empty hypocrisy because its leaders failed to apply equality consistently. Both miss historical change. Revolutionary language had real effects: it destabilized inherited justifications, enabled new lawsuits and petitions, contributed to northern emancipation, weakened some forms of religious establishment, and expanded public discussion of women’s education and civic importance. At the same time, independence strengthened settler sovereignty, preserved slavery, and gave white Americans new institutional power to define belonging.

Black freedom and Native sovereignty offer the strongest paired cases because they expose contrasting relationships to the Revolution. Enslaved people often sought inclusion in the promise of liberty, though some pursued freedom by leaving the United States altogether. Native nations generally did not seek inclusion as individual American citizens. They defended collective political independence, territorial jurisdiction, and diplomatic standing. Treating both as generic “minority experiences” would erase the difference between a claim to equal freedom within a polity and a claim that the polity had no legitimate authority over one’s nation.

I. Revolutionary Language as a Contested Political Resource

Universal Claims and Particular Communities

Revolutionary Americans inherited multiple languages of liberty. English constitutionalism emphasized the rights of subjects and limits on arbitrary power. Republican thought associated liberty with civic virtue, independence, and resistance to corruption. Natural-rights arguments asserted rights possessed by human beings prior to government. Protestant traditions framed liberty through conscience and covenant. These languages could reinforce one another, but they did not produce a single democratic program. A propertied white man might denounce parliamentary taxation as slavery while owning enslaved people, not because words had no meaning to him, but because he located political slavery in subjection to arbitrary public authority while treating chattel slavery as legitimate private property and racial order.

The Revolution intensified the problem by making consent and equality central tests of legitimacy. Critics could ask why a principle used against Parliament did not also apply to masters, husbands, established churches, landlords, or settlers claiming Native land. Such analogies were rhetorically powerful but never self-executing. Existing institutions decided which analogies would be recognized. Courts could interpret a state constitution against slavery; legislatures could enact gradual abolition; military authorities could promise freedom; and communities could tolerate or suppress petitioners. The reach of revolutionary language was therefore mediated by law, war, property, race, gender, and political organization.

Agency Without Romanticization

Emphasizing the agency of enslaved people, Native nations, women, loyalists, and religious dissenters should not imply that all actors possessed equal choices. Agency means purposeful action within constraints, not sovereign control over outcomes. An enslaved person deciding whether to flee faced surveillance, violence, family separation, disease, and the possibility that a military promise would be revoked. A Native council choosing an alliance weighed old rivalries, trade access, settler pressure, diplomatic promises, and the limits of unity within a confederacy. A loyalist family might choose exile only after local harassment or confiscation made neutrality impossible. Restoring such decisions to the narrative makes the Revolution more human without minimizing coercion.

Robert Parkinson’s work on the creation of a “common cause” demonstrates another dimension of revolutionary language: Patriot unity was partly constructed by defining dangerous outsiders. Newspaper stories and political rhetoric linked British power to enslaved rebels, hostile Native peoples, and foreign mercenaries. This helped disparate colonies imagine themselves as one people while reinforcing racial boundaries. Universal political claims and exclusionary nation-making could develop together.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Revolutionary ideals mattered because they supplied arguments that historical actors could deploy, but the new nation did not begin from a level field. Institutions and coercive power determined which analogies were accepted, which claims became law, and which claimants were redefined as threats to the revolutionary community.

II. Black Freedom in an Imperial Civil War

Dunmore’s Proclamation and the Politics of Escape

In November 1775 Virginia’s royal governor, Lord Dunmore, proclaimed martial law and offered freedom to enslaved people belonging to rebels who were able and willing to join British forces. The measure was limited: it did not free all enslaved people, did not apply to those owned by loyalists, and arose from military necessity rather than a general commitment to abolition. Even so, it transformed the conflict. It offered an official route by which enslaved Virginians might turn imperial war into personal liberation, while terrifying slaveholders who understood that British power could destabilize the labor system on which their wealth and authority rested.

Primary source

“All indented Servants, Negroes, or others, (appertaining to Rebels,) free that are able and willing to bear Arms.”

— Lord Dunmore’s Proclamation, 7 November 1775

The most important subject is not Dunmore’s motive but the response of enslaved people. Men, women, and children moved toward British lines throughout the war, sometimes individually and sometimes in family groups. They carried knowledge of roads, waterways, households, and local economies. Some served as soldiers, laborers, pilots, guides, sailors, laundresses, or servants. British camps were dangerous environments marked by disease, scarcity, exploitation, and shifting policy, but they could still represent a better chance than remaining under an owner committed to the Patriot cause.

Later British proclamations broadened the opportunity. Sir Henry Clinton’s Philipsburg Proclamation of 1779 promised protection to people who deserted rebel masters, without requiring military service. The resulting movement of Black refugees was one of the Revolution’s largest emancipatory processes. At the end of the war, several thousand Black Loyalists evacuated from New York to Nova Scotia and other parts of the British Empire, their names and claimed status recorded in the Book of Negroes. Their later experiences reveal both achievement and betrayal: some secured freedom, but many faced discrimination, poverty, land delays, and continued imperial inequality.

Black Service for the Patriot Cause

Black men also served in Patriot forces. Policies varied by colony and state, changed over time, and distinguished inconsistently among free men, enslaved men, and substitutes. Washington initially restricted Black enlistment after taking command in 1775, then permitted reenlistment of free Black soldiers as the manpower crisis deepened. Northern units and militias included Black soldiers, and Rhode Island authorized a regiment that recruited enslaved men with compensation to owners and promises of freedom to recruits. Black sailors served in state and Continental naval forces and on privateers, where maritime labor practices could be more fluid than army policy.

Patriot service could produce freedom, wages, pensions, community standing, or legal claims, but outcomes were inconsistent. Some enslaved men served as substitutes for masters; some were promised emancipation; some returned to bondage. Military service did not erase racism, yet it created evidence that Black men had defended the republic and therefore possessed claims upon it. Judith Van Buskirk’s work is especially useful because it follows African Americans through wartime movement and military labor rather than treating them only as objects of white policy.

Choice, Family, and the Geography of Opportunity

The binary labels “Black Loyalist” and “Black Patriot” can obscure the practical character of decision making. Allegiance might be secondary to freedom, family preservation, food, safety, or mobility. A person could assist one side, move between jurisdictions, and later present a different account to officials deciding emancipation or compensation. Choices were also geographic. British occupation created opportunities in Virginia, the Chesapeake, coastal Georgia and South Carolina, New York, and other zones at different times. The likelihood of escape depended on proximity, military movement, local surveillance, and kin networks.

Recent scholarship on enslaved women makes the family dimension more concrete. Karen Cook Bell shows that women comprised roughly one-third of Revolutionary-era runaways and often planned flight around children, kin, disguise, mobility, and the breakdown of wartime surveillance. The British evacuation record known as the Book of Negroes also exposes the limits of imperial promises: it registered about three thousand Black refugees bound from New York to Nova Scotia, but it records the case of Betty, whom British officials returned to Thomas Smith after he asserted ownership. Her case turns the lecture’s central claim into a human scene. A military proclamation could open a route toward freedom, yet an official’s decision about evidence and property could close it again.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Enslaved and free Black people did not wait for either side to apply revolutionary ideals consistently. They used war, mobility, military labor, and legal argument to pursue freedom. Their actions made emancipation one of the Revolution’s real outcomes while exposing the narrow and instrumental character of many British and Patriot promises.

III. Emancipation, Slavery, and the Racial Boundaries of the Republic

Northern Abolition as a Revolutionary Process

The Revolution accelerated antislavery change in the North, but the process was uneven and gradual. Vermont’s 1777 constitution prohibited adult slavery in principle, though enforcement and the status of children remained complicated. Pennsylvania’s Gradual Abolition Act of 1780 did not immediately free those already enslaved. It ended the hereditary transmission of lifetime slavery for children born after the law, who instead owed lengthy terms of service. Massachusetts did not adopt a gradual statute; freedom suits invoking the 1780 constitution’s declaration that all men were born free and equal helped render slavery judicially unenforceable. Other northern states followed with gradual measures in later years.

Gradual abolition reflected both the power and limits of revolutionary change. It challenged perpetual hereditary slavery and created a path toward extinction, but it protected existing property claims and prolonged coerced labor for decades. Enslaved people, free Black activists, Quakers, ministers, lawyers, and legislators all shaped the process. Elizabeth Freeman’s suit and the Quock Walker cases in Massachusetts illustrate how constitutional language could acquire consequences through litigation. The cases did not simply reveal an already obvious legal meaning; litigants and judges made equality operative within a specific institutional setting.

The South: Destabilization Without Abolition

In the Upper South, wartime disruption and revolutionary rhetoric encouraged private manumissions, especially after Virginia liberalized manumission law in 1782. Some slaveholders acted from religious or ideological conviction; others responded to economic circumstances or individual relationships. Free Black communities grew. Yet no southern state adopted general emancipation. Slavery remained deeply embedded in landholding, credit, household authority, and political power, while racial fears intensified through wartime flight and British recruitment.

It is anachronistic to explain the immediate postwar survival of southern slavery primarily through the later cotton boom. Cotton would transform the scale and geography of slavery after the 1790s, but in the 1780s tobacco, rice, indigo, mixed agriculture, debt, inherited property, and planter authority already sustained bondage. The Constitution’s protections for the Atlantic slave trade until 1808, fugitive recovery, and representation based partly on enslaved population would further secure slaveholding influence within the Union. The Revolution opened antislavery possibilities without dislodging the political economy of slavery.

Race and the Limits of Universalism

The war encouraged both emancipatory argument and sharper racial categorization. White Patriots confronted the fact that British policy could mobilize enslaved people and Native nations against the rebellion. Political rhetoric often transformed these groups into evidence of British tyranny rather than recognizing their independent grievances. Freedom increasingly became identified with white citizenship even as Black petitioners invoked the same natural-rights language. Benjamin Quarles’s foundational synthesis established Black participation as central to the war; later scholars such as Douglas Egerton and Sylvia Frey have emphasized self-emancipation, mobility, and the broader Atlantic context of Black resistance.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Revolution weakened slavery in the North and created concrete routes to freedom, but it did not produce a national antislavery settlement. Instead, emancipation and racial exclusion developed together, helping create a republic in which universal language coexisted with increasingly sectional and racialized systems of bondage.

IV. Native Sovereignty and the American Peace

Why Native Nations Chose Sides

Native nations did not enter the war as dependents of Britain or the United States. They possessed their own diplomatic traditions, territorial priorities, internal divisions, and memories of earlier imperial relationships. Many communities judged that American independence would intensify settler expansion. British officials had often failed to restrain colonists, but the imperial government at least recognized Native nations as diplomatic and military powers and had attempted after 1763 to regulate western settlement. Patriot victory threatened to remove even that imperfect barrier.

The Haudenosaunee Confederacy split under wartime pressure. Oneida and Tuscarora communities generally supported the Americans, while many Mohawk, Seneca, Cayuga, and Onondaga people allied with Britain. These alignments reflected missionary ties, local diplomacy, old rivalries, and assessments of settler danger. The division produced a devastating civil war within the confederacy. The Sullivan-Clinton campaign of 1779 destroyed numerous Haudenosaunee towns, food stores, and orchards, demonstrating that American strategy targeted the material foundations of resistance as well as warriors.

In the South, Cherokee and Creek leaders likewise pursued policies shaped by territorial defense and internal debate. Some Cherokee towns attacked frontier settlements in 1776, prompting destructive Patriot campaigns. Creek diplomacy under leaders such as Alexander McGillivray continued after independence as communities sought to preserve autonomy among competing Spanish and American pressures. Treating “the Indians” as a unified side misses the diversity of Native politics; treating alliances as mere reactions to European decisions misses Native strategic agency.

The Treaty of Paris and the Fiction of Transfer

The 1783 peace negotiations excluded Native nations. Britain recognized the United States to the Mississippi River, but the treaty could not legitimately extinguish Native title simply because one European power conceded claims to another. From the perspective of the United States, victory over Britain established sovereignty within generous boundaries. From the perspective of many Native nations, Britain had transferred lands it did not own and abandoned allies without securing their consent.

The contradiction became visible immediately. The United States expected western nations to accept the status of conquered peoples, while Native confederacies insisted that they had not been defeated as a whole and retained their lands. Warfare in the Ohio Country continued, culminating in major American defeats and eventually the Northwest Indian War. Alan Taylor’s work on the divided ground and Colin Calloway’s reconstruction of Indian Country show that the Revolution did not end Indigenous diplomacy. It reorganized the contest by replacing imperial rivalry east of the Mississippi with a settler republic claiming continental sovereignty.

Inclusion Versus Independence

The language of rights can blur the political nature of Native claims. Many Native leaders were not primarily asking the United States to treat them as equal individual citizens. They asserted the sovereignty of nations, the validity of treaties, and collective jurisdiction over territory. An inclusive American republic could still be colonial if it denied the right of Native polities to remain independent. Ned Blackhawk’s continental synthesis is useful here: Indigenous affairs were not peripheral consequences of the founding; they shaped the imperial crisis and the territorial state that emerged from it.

Instructor note

Instructor note: In classroom language, distinguish “rights within the United States” from “the right not to be ruled by the United States.” The latter captures the core of Native sovereignty more accurately than a generic inclusion framework.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

American independence expanded self-government for settlers while accelerating a claim of jurisdiction over Native homelands. The Peace of Paris ended the Anglo-American war but did not settle sovereignty in the West. For many Native nations, the revolutionary republic appeared not as liberation but as a more immediate settler empire.

V. Uneven Revolutions: Women, Loyalists, and Religious Liberty

Women’s Labor and Civic Significance

War altered women’s responsibilities across political allegiances. Women managed farms, shops, estates, and households; traveled with armies as cooks, laundresses, nurses, and sutlers; produced homespun; participated in boycotts; gathered intelligence; petitioned officials; and protected families amid displacement. These activities were not merely “contributions” added to a male war. They sustained economies and armies while exposing the dependence of republican independence on labor performed outside formal citizenship.

The postwar language later called republican motherhood elevated women’s responsibility for cultivating virtue in future citizens. It supported expanded female education among some social groups and acknowledged that the republic depended on domestic formation. Yet it did not grant women equal political rights. The ideology could enlarge civic significance while reinscribing women’s authority within marriage and motherhood. Exceptional stories such as Deborah Sampson’s military service can attract attention, but they should illuminate rather than substitute for the structural contradiction between indispensable participation and formal exclusion.

Loyalists and the Coercive Boundaries of the New Nation

Loyalists included officeholders, merchants, tenant farmers, artisans, enslaved people seeking freedom, recent immigrants, pacifists, and others whose motives varied. Some defended monarchy; some feared social disorder; some relied on imperial networks; some opposed local Patriot enemies; some attempted neutrality and were forced into a side. Patriot governments imposed loyalty oaths, confiscated property, restricted civil rights, and sometimes tolerated mob violence. Tens of thousands left the United States during or after the war, resettling in Canada, Britain, the Caribbean, Sierra Leone, and elsewhere.

Maya Jasanoff’s study of loyalist refugees interprets their diaspora as part of the remaking of the British Empire. Exile demonstrates that the revolutionary nation was constituted not only through inclusion but through expulsion and confiscation. The right of a people to revolution did not settle the rights of minorities who rejected the revolution. Loyalists’ losses complicate a simple story of expanding liberty without requiring a false equivalence between imperial and Patriot political projects.

Religious Freedom and Disestablishment

Religious change was among the Revolution’s more durable expansions of liberty. Wartime pluralism, dissenting mobilization, and hostility to coercive establishments weakened state-supported churches. Virginia’s Statute for Religious Freedom, enacted in 1786, rejected compelled support for religion and articulated a strong principle of conscience. Change remained uneven: some states retained religious tests or tax support, and toleration did not immediately erase anti-Catholic or other prejudice. Even so, disestablishment provided an example of revolutionary principles becoming institutional reform without requiring social equality in every domain.

A Pattern of Selective Transformation

Across these cases, the Revolution redistributed authority without abolishing hierarchy. Women gained recognition without citizenship; dissenters gained conscience rights while other exclusions persisted; loyalists lost rights in the name of popular sovereignty; Black people secured freedom through multiple routes while slavery survived; Native nations confronted a republic that universalized its own sovereignty. The outcomes were neither a coherent democratic program nor a simple preservation of the colonial order. They were products of conflict among actors with unequal leverage.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Revolution broadened the languages and institutions through which freedom could be claimed, but it also defined a national community through exclusion, exile, patriarchy, racial hierarchy, and territorial expansion. Its social consequences were selective because political power remained unevenly distributed.

Closing Synthesis

The Revolution’s promises should be understood as historically consequential claims rather than as completed achievements. Enslaved and free Black people transformed military conflict into opportunities for escape, service, litigation, manumission, and community formation. Their actions helped drive northern abolition and expanded the political meaning of liberty. Yet slaveholding survived, gained constitutional protection, and became increasingly associated with regional and racial power.

Native nations confronted a different problem. Their central claim was often not equal membership in the American republic but recognition of sovereignty outside it. Wartime alliances, internal divisions, and continued diplomacy reveal strategic efforts to protect land and political independence. The Treaty of Paris exposed the settler republic’s imperial character by treating Native territory as transferable between Britain and the United States without Native consent.

Women, loyalists, and religious dissenters further demonstrate that revolution simultaneously opens and closes possibilities. The analytical question to carry forward is not whether the Revolution was “really” about liberty or “really” hypocritical. It is how revolutionary principles interacted with institutions, coercion, and unequal power—and why some claims became enforceable while others were denied, deferred, or turned against the claimants.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Benjamin Quarles, The Negro in the American Revolution (1961). Classic foundational synthesis that established Black military participation, loyalty, and freedom-seeking as central to the Revolution. Later scholarship has revised terminology and expanded the geographic and social analysis, but Quarles remains an essential historiographical starting point.

Douglas R. Egerton, Death or Liberty: African Americans and Revolutionary America (2009). A broad account centered on Black action, resistance, military service, litigation, and institution building. Egerton is especially useful for connecting the Revolution to the emergence of free Black communities and the contradictions of the new republic.

Judith L. Van Buskirk, Standing in Their Own Light: African American Patriots in the American Revolution (2017). Reconstructs Black Patriot service and wartime mobility with attention to individual lives and the instability of racial and military policy. It balances histories that focus primarily on British emancipation.

Sylvia R. Frey, Water from the Rock: Black Resistance in a Revolutionary Age (1991). Places southern Black resistance and the Revolution within a wider struggle over slavery. Particularly valuable for understanding self-emancipation, British lines, wartime disruption, and the relationship between military conflict and social transformation.

Robert G. Parkinson, The Common Cause: Creating Race and Nation in the American Revolution (2016). Explains how Patriot leaders used stories about enslaved rebels, Native enemies, and foreign mercenaries to create intercolonial unity. It shows how nation-making and racial exclusion developed alongside universal language.

Colin G. Calloway, The American Revolution in Indian Country: Crisis and Diversity in Native American Communities (1995). A foundational Native-centered study organized around communities rather than European campaigns. Calloway recovers the diversity of Indigenous choices and the internal crises produced by war.

Alan Taylor, The Divided Ground: Indians, Settlers, and the Northern Borderland of the American Revolution (2006). Traces the postwar struggle over sovereignty, land, and loyalty in the northern borderlands through figures such as Joseph Brant and Samuel Kirkland. It is especially useful for showing that the peace of 1783 did not settle the Revolution in Indian Country.

Ned Blackhawk, The Rediscovery of America: Native Peoples and the Unmaking of U.S. History (2023). A major continental synthesis that places Native nations at the center of the imperial crisis and the growth of the United States. It offers a current framework for teaching the Revolution as part of a longer history of Indigenous power, dispossession, adaptation, and survival.

Maya Jasanoff, Liberty’s Exiles: American Loyalists in the Revolutionary World (2011). Follows loyalist refugees across the British Empire and interprets their diaspora as a global consequence of American independence. Useful for integrating exile, imperial reconstruction, and the coercive boundaries of the new nation.

Karen Cook Bell, Running from Bondage: Enslaved Women and Their Remarkable Fight for Freedom in Revolutionary America (2021). A major study of enslaved women’s fugitivity that recovers family strategy, geography, and the gendered risks of wartime escape. Bell’s work supplies the strongest recent narrative and analytical correction to accounts centered mainly on male military service.

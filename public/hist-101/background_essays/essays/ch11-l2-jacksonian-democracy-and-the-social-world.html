Essential question

The Jacksonian era called itself the age of the common man. What did that democracy look like from the factory floor, the boardinghouse, the immigrant neighborhood, and the Black church?

  5. Evaluate how urban workers, Lowell mill women, and free Black communities used petitions, strikes, newspapers, and institutions to make their own claims on American democracy.

Introductory Framing

This lecture changes the lens. Earlier lectures in this course built the political and cultural story of the early republic: parties, presidents, courts, newspapers, revivals. This one asks a different question. Instead of starting with what leaders promised, it starts with what ordinary people experienced. The lecture first defines social history and explains why historians use it. It then sketches what historians mean by Jacksonian Democracy and immediately names its central contradiction: democracy grew for white men while it shrank or stayed closed for nearly everyone else. From there it moves into daily life: the market revolution that pulled households into wage work and clock time, the self-made-man ideal that promised success and blamed failure on the individual, the immigrants who crowded into cities, the workers who organized to win shorter hours, the young women of Lowell who struck and petitioned, and the free Black communities that built churches, newspapers, and a political voice of their own. It ends with a set of paradoxes and asks students which lens best explains the era.

Historians have argued about Jacksonian Democracy for a long time. Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr.’s The Age of Jackson (1945) read it as a movement of Eastern workers and farmers against business power. Later historians such as Lee Benson argued that ethnic and religious loyalties, not class, shaped how people voted. Edward Pessen found that the “age of the common man” was in fact an age of growing inequality. Social historians since the 1970s, including Sean Wilentz, Thomas Dublin, Christine Stansell, and Gary Nash, have studied artisans, mill women, the urban poor, and free Black communities to see how democracy looked from below. Charles Sellers built his account around a market revolution that disrupted older ways of life, while Daniel Walker Howe has argued that improved communication and transportation brought more benefits than Sellers allowed.

This essay avoids two easy stories. One celebrates the era as the triumph of the common man. The other dismisses its democracy as a fraud. The evidence supports a harder conclusion: participation really did expand, and ordinary people really did use it, but the same society that opened politics to white men tightened the boundaries of race and gender, and the market that created opportunity also created new forms of insecurity.

Key Terms: Social History, Jacksonian Democracy, and the Market Revolution

Social history is a historiographical approach that centers the lives of ordinary people: how they lived, worked, worshiped, and socialized, and how big historical forces shaped their daily experience. It focuses on family and household, labor and workplaces, belief and leisure. It analyzes class, gender, race, and community. And it uses “street-level” evidence such as diaries, letters, account books, wage records, petitions, census returns, and court records. Its method is to test big claims against everyday experience. If the era claimed equality, social historians ask: equal for whom, and in what ways?

The lecture is careful to say that a new lens is not a new story. It is a way of seeing the same era. Political history looks at institutions, laws, leaders, and the state. Cultural history looks at symbols, media, identity, and performance. Economic history looks at markets, labor systems, and inequality. Intellectual history looks at ideas and debates. Environmental history looks at land, climate, and the relationship between people and nature. Each lens shows different things. None of them changes the past; they change what we can see in it.

Jacksonian Democracy is historians’ name for the political world of roughly 1828 to 1848, named after Andrew Jackson, president from 1829 to 1837. It refers to wider voting rights for white men, the rise of mass political parties, and an ideal that celebrated the “common man” and distrusted privilege, banks, and concentrated power.

The market revolution is the shift, in the decades after 1815, from an economy of households and local exchange toward one of wage labor, long-distance trade, and consumer goods, made possible by canals, railroads, steamboats, banks, and factories. It is a historian’s term, popularized by Charles Sellers, and some historians prefer to speak of a longer, more gradual transformation. Either way, it changed how Americans worked, what they bought, and how they measured time.

Setting the Scene: March 4, 1829

On March 4, 1829, Andrew Jackson took the oath of office on the east portico of the Capitol before a crowd of thousands. Many had traveled hundreds of miles to see the hero of New Orleans, a man born in the backcountry of the Carolinas, orphaned as a boy, and scarred by a British officer’s sword during the Revolution. After the ceremony Jackson rode to the White House, and the crowd followed him. The reception that afternoon was open to the public, and the public came: farmers, laborers, office seekers, women, children, and Black and white Washingtonians pushing into the rooms. People climbed on furniture in muddy boots to get a look at the president; glasses and china broke; staff hauled tubs of punch onto the lawn to draw the crowd outside. Jackson himself, pressed against a wall, slipped away.

Margaret Bayard Smith, a leader of Washington society who admired Jackson, described the scene in a letter a few days later:

Primary source

“The Majesty of the People had disappeared, and a rabble, a mob, of boys, negros, women, children, scrambling fighting, romping.”

— Margaret Bayard Smith, letter describing Jackson’s inaugural reception, March 1829

Supreme Court Justice Joseph Story, no friend of Jackson, put it more briefly: “The reign of King ‘Mob’ seemed triumphant.” Jackson’s supporters saw the same scene and drew the opposite conclusion: the people had finally taken possession of their government. Notice who Smith saw in the crowd: boys, women, children, Black Washingtonians. All of them were there. Very few of them could vote. That gap between who appeared in the democratic scene and who held democratic power is where this lecture begins.

Why a Social-History Lens?

Political history tells us what leaders promised; social history asks what ordinary people experienced. For most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, history meant mainly the history of presidents, generals, and laws. In the 1960s and 1970s a generation of historians began asking new questions about workers, women, enslaved people, immigrants, and families, people who rarely left memoirs or held office. The British historian E. P. Thompson stated the goal in a famous sentence:

Primary source

“I am seeking to rescue the poor stockinger, the Luddite cropper, the ‘obsolete’ hand-loom weaver, the ‘utopian’ artisan . . . from the enormous condescension of posterity.”

— E. P. Thompson, The Making of the English Working Class (1963), preface

Thompson wrote about England, but his approach shaped how historians studied American workers too. Social historians found that ordinary people left plenty of evidence if you knew where to look: boardinghouse rules, mill payrolls, strike proclamations, church records, city directories, and census lists. They learned to read these “tiny traces” for patterns of power, discipline, and dignity. That is what the lecture’s two labs ask students to do.

Analytical significance

Approaches are lenses. A social-history lens does not replace the political story of Jacksonian America; it tests that story’s biggest claims, about equality, opportunity, and democracy, against the evidence of everyday life.

What Was Jacksonian Democracy?

In 1800 most states limited voting to men who owned property or paid taxes. By the late 1820s nearly all of them had dropped or loosened those requirements for white men. New states in the West entered with broad suffrage from the start, and older states rewrote their constitutions in conventions where reformers argued that a man who paid taxes, served in the militia, or simply worked deserved a vote. At the same time, more states let voters, rather than legislatures, choose presidential electors. By 1832 South Carolina was the only state whose legislature still chose them.

The results showed up at the polls. In the presidential election of 1824, only about a quarter of eligible voters cast ballots. In 1828, when Jackson defeated John Quincy Adams, turnout more than doubled, to well over half. In 1840 it reached about 80 percent. Parties organized to win these new voters. Martin Van Buren of New York, Jackson’s great organizer, believed that disciplined national parties were good for the republic, and he helped build the Democratic Party from local committees up to national conventions. Jackson’s opponents, angered by his use of presidential power, organized in the mid-1830s as the Whig Party, named after the British party that opposed royal power. For the next two decades, Democrats and Whigs competed in nearly every state. Rallies, parades, barbecues, songs, and the penny and partisan press made politics a form of mass participation, and sometimes a mass entertainment.

The Jacksonian Ideal

Jacksonians described their cause as the cause of the common man. They believed that ordinary white men, farmers, mechanics, and laborers, should be able to rise by merit and hard work, and that government should not hand special privileges to the rich and well connected. They were suspicious of banks, monopolies, and corporate charters, and of a federal government that might use its power to favor some citizens over others. Jackson put the idea plainly in July 1832, when he vetoed the bill to recharter the Second Bank of the United States:

Primary source

“It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes. . . . the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers—who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government.”

— Andrew Jackson, veto message on the bill to recharter the Bank of the United States, July 10, 1832

Jacksonians also believed in “rotation in office”: government jobs should not belong to a permanent class of officials but should be open to ordinary citizens, and to the winning party’s supporters. Critics called this the spoils system, after New York senator William L. Marcy’s 1832 remark that “to the victors belong the spoils.”

Visitors noticed the change. In 1831 a young French aristocrat named Alexis de Tocqueville arrived in the United States with his friend Gustave de Beaumont to study American prisons. They traveled for nine months, and Tocqueville came home to write Democracy in America (1835 and 1840), still one of the most quoted books about the country. He opened it with the observation that struck him most:

Primary source

“Among the novel objects that attracted my attention during my stay in the United States, nothing struck me more forcibly than the general equality of condition among the people.”

— Alexis de Tocqueville, Democracy in America, vol. 1 (1835), Henry Reeve translation

Tocqueville meant equality of manners and expectations as much as of wealth: Americans did not bow to their social superiors the way Europeans did. But he also wrote at length about slavery and the treatment of Native peoples, and he worried that a new “manufacturing aristocracy” could grow out of the factories. His book is a useful reminder that even admiring observers saw the limits of American equality.

Analytical significance

Jacksonian Democracy was real: property barriers fell, turnout soared, and mass parties pulled ordinary white men into politics. Its ideal of the common man and its hostility to privilege gave ordinary people a language to criticize the powerful. The next question is who counted as “common men.”

The Central Contradiction

In the fall of 1821, delegates gathered in Albany to write a new constitution for New York. They were reformers, and they swept away the old property requirements that had kept many white men from voting. In the same document they did something else. Black men, who had been able to vote in New York on the same property terms as white men, would now have to own a freehold worth $250 and meet a longer residence requirement, standards most white men no longer had to meet at all. Because few Black New Yorkers owned that much property, the change disfranchised nearly all of them. One convention, one document: democracy expanded for white men and contracted for Black men at the same time.

New York was not unusual. The lecture lists four groups for whom Jacksonian “democracy” meant exclusion or domination.

Women were denied the ballot. In New Jersey some propertied women had voted under the state’s 1776 constitution, but a law of 1807 limited voting to white male taxpayers, closing the door on both women and Black men. No state allowed women to vote in this period. Women did take part in politics in other ways: they attended rallies, sewed banners, wrote for newspapers, and signed petitions, especially antislavery petitions, by the tens of thousands. In 1848 the women and men who met at Seneca Falls, New York, demanded the vote among their resolutions.

Free Black men were restricted or disfranchised in many states. Connecticut limited voting to white men in 1818, Rhode Island in 1822, Tennessee in 1834, and North Carolina in 1835. In 1838 Pennsylvania, home to the largest free Black community in the North, added the word “white” to its voting clause. Black Philadelphians protested in a pamphlet called the Appeal of Forty Thousand Citizens, Threatened with Disfranchisement, written largely by the abolitionist Robert Purvis. By the 1840s Black men could vote on the same terms as white men only in Massachusetts, Maine, New Hampshire, Vermont, and, after 1842, Rhode Island. Every state admitted to the Union between 1819 and the Civil War, except Maine, limited voting to white men.

Native nations faced removal under federal and state pressure. Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act in May 1830, and over the next decade the federal government forced tens of thousands of Cherokee, Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole people from their homelands in the Southeast to lands west of the Mississippi. The Cherokee resisted through the courts and won a favorable ruling in Worcester v. Georgia (1832), but neither Georgia nor the president enforced it. In 1838 and 1839, U.S. soldiers marched about 16,000 Cherokees west; perhaps 4,000 died during the roundup, imprisonment, and journey known as the Trail of Tears. For many white farmers, removal meant cheap land and opportunity. For Native peoples, the democracy of the common man arrived as dispossession.

Slavery deepened and spread. The enslaved population of the United States grew from about 1.5 million in 1820 to about 3.2 million in 1850, and cotton plantations spread across Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Texas. Many of the white men celebrated as self-made, Jackson among them, rose by buying land and enslaved people. In the South, white men’s political equality rested on the forced labor of Black people.

These exclusions were not accidents, left over from an older order that democracy had not yet reached. Historians such as Alexander Keyssar have shown that, in many states, the same movement that broadened white male suffrage drew sharper lines of race and sex. As class barriers fell, race and gender became the main boundaries of citizenship. The lecture’s summary is blunt: inclusion for some, domination of others.

Analytical significance

Jacksonian Democracy expanded participation for white men while women, free Black Americans, Native nations, and enslaved people were excluded or dominated. The expansion and the exclusion were connected: as property stopped defining citizenship, race and gender defined it more sharply.

The Market Revolution Transforms Life

Picture a farm family in western New York around 1815. They grow most of their own food, spin and weave some of their own cloth, trade butter and eggs with neighbors, and sell a little surplus grain when they can get it to market. Their day follows the sun and their year follows the seasons. Now picture their children twenty years later. The Erie Canal, finished in 1825, runs a few miles away, and canal boats carry the family’s wheat to New York City and bring back factory-made cloth, iron stoves, and clocks. One son works for wages in a Rochester flour mill; a daughter has gone to Massachusetts to tend looms; the youngest buys a cheap shelf clock from a peddler, and for the first time the household knows the time to the minute.

That shift is what historians call the market revolution. Canals came first: the Erie Canal’s success set off a canal-building boom across the North and West. Railroads followed. The Baltimore and Ohio began construction in 1828, and by 1840 the country had about 3,000 miles of track, more than all of Europe. In 1844 Samuel F. B. Morse sent the first long-distance telegraph message from Washington to Baltimore, a line from the Bible: “What hath God wrought.” News that had once taken weeks to cross the country could soon cross it in minutes. Together these technologies knit regions into a national market.

From Household to Wage Labor

The market revolution changed work. Under the old system, many goods were made in households or small shops, where a master craftsman worked alongside journeymen and apprentices who might one day become masters themselves. Increasingly, merchant capitalists and manufacturers broke production into simpler tasks, paid workers by the piece or by the day, and sold goods in distant markets. Shoemaking in Lynn, Massachusetts, for example, was divided between men who cut and finished leather in central shops and women who stitched uppers at home for piece rates. Wage labor, once a temporary stage of life for young men, became a permanent condition for many.

Time Discipline

The market revolution also changed time. Farm work followed daylight, weather, and seasons, with long hard days at harvest and slower periods in winter. Factory work followed the clock and the bell. E. P. Thompson called this “time discipline”: the process by which employers, schools, and churches taught people to measure work in hours and minutes and to treat wasted time as a moral failing. Cheap clocks, mass-produced in Connecticut by makers such as Eli Terry, Seth Thomas, and Chauncey Jerome, spread into ordinary homes in the 1820s and 1830s. Employers rang bells to start and end the workday, and some fined workers who were late.

The lecture’s summary is a pair: more opportunity and more insecurity. Farm families could sell more and buy more. Young people could leave home and earn their own money. But a worker who depended on wages could lose everything when a factory closed, and a farmer who depended on distant markets could be ruined by a price collapse in Liverpool, as the Panic of 1819 had shown and as the Panic of 1837 would show again.

Analytical significance

Canals, railroads, and the telegraph built a national market and pulled households into wage labor, consumer buying, and clock time. The same changes that opened opportunity also made ordinary people dependent on employers and distant markets they could not control.

The “Self-Made Man” Ideal

In February 1832, Henry Clay, defending his American System on the floor of the Senate, answered critics who said protective tariffs only made rich manufacturers richer. The factory owners he knew, he said, were not born rich at all:

Primary source

“In Kentucky, almost every manufactory known to me is in the hands of enterprising and self-made men, who have whatever wealth they possess by patient and diligent labor.”

— Henry Clay, speech in the U.S. Senate in defense of the American System, February 1832

Clay’s phrase caught on. The self-made man became one of the era’s central ideals: a man who rose not by birth or inheritance but by his own effort, talent, and character. Benjamin Franklin’s autobiography, widely read in the period, served as a model. The ideal had real democratic force. It told white men without famous fathers or family land that they could become anything, and Jackson himself, the orphan who became president, seemed to prove it. (Jackson’s opponents, ironically, embraced the ideal too; Whigs were especially fond of celebrating self-made businessmen.)

But the ideal also had a darker side. If success came from effort and character, then failure must come from a lack of them. Poverty, which earlier generations often treated as a misfortune, could now be read as a personal failing, a sign of laziness, drink, or bad habits. Scott Sandage has shown how business failure came to be read as a failure of character, and how the new credit-reporting agencies of the 1840s rated a man’s habits and reputation along with his accounts.

The reality check is that most Americans did not become rich, and most rich Americans had not started poor. Edward Pessen studied tax records in New York, Boston, and Philadelphia and found that wealth in the big cities was highly concentrated and became more concentrated in the Jacksonian decades, and that most of the very rich had been born into wealthy or comfortable families. The myth of the self-made man still shaped policy. It encouraged reformers to treat education as the ladder of mobility; Horace Mann, who led Massachusetts’s new Board of Education from 1837, later called education “the great equalizer of the conditions of men.” And it shaped how cities treated the poor, distinguishing the “deserving” from the “undeserving” and sending many to workhouses designed to teach discipline.

Analytical significance

The self-made-man ideal shifted the measure of success from birth to effort, which opened doors for white men without pedigree. It also recoded hardship as personal failure, even though most Americans did not rise far and wealth was growing more concentrated.

Immigration, Cities, and Nativism

In the 1840s a potato blight destroyed the main food crop of Ireland. During the Great Famine that followed, beginning in 1845, about a million people died and more than a million emigrated, a large share of them to the United States. They arrived after weeks in crowded, disease-ridden ships, many with nothing but the clothes they wore, and most settled in port cities such as Boston, New York, and Philadelphia. In the same years, hundreds of thousands of Germans left the German states because of crop failures, economic change, and the failed revolutions of 1848.

Immigration had been growing since the 1820s, but in the mid-1840s it became a flood. Between 1820 and 1860 about five million immigrants arrived, most of them Irish or German. In 1850, the first census to record birthplaces, nearly 10 percent of the population had been born abroad, and in cities such as New York and Boston the share was far higher. The two groups tended to find different places in the economy. Many Irish immigrants, poor and rural, became urban laborers: they dug canals and railroad beds, loaded ships, and worked as domestic servants. Many Germans arrived with some money or a craft and became artisans, shopkeepers, or farmers in the Midwest.

Cities strained to absorb them. Immigrants crowded into cheap tenements and cellars in neighborhoods such as New York’s Five Points. Clean water and sewers lagged far behind population. Cholera, a waterborne disease, swept through American cities in 1832 and again in 1849, killing thousands, and many native-born Protestants blamed the immigrant poor for the disease rather than the conditions they were forced to live in.

Nativism

The new arrivals met hostility, called nativism: the belief that native-born Protestant Americans should be protected against immigrants, especially Catholics. Many Protestants feared that Catholic immigrants would obey the pope rather than American law. In 1834 a Protestant mob burned the Ursuline convent in Charlestown, Massachusetts. In 1835 the painter and future telegraph inventor Samuel F. B. Morse published Foreign Conspiracy Against the Liberties of the United States, warning of a Catholic plot.

The worst violence came in Philadelphia in 1844. Catholic Bishop Francis Kenrick had asked that Catholic children in the public schools be allowed to read their own Douay version of the Bible instead of the Protestant King James version. Nativists claimed Catholics wanted to drive the Bible out of the schools. In May 1844 rioting broke out in the Irish neighborhood of Kensington; mobs burned homes and two Catholic churches. In July fighting erupted again in Southwark, where militia and rioters exchanged gunfire. Around two dozen people died across the two outbreaks. The lecture calls this democracy’s violent edge: in a country where majorities ruled, a majority could also turn on a minority.

Analytical significance

Mass immigration supplied the labor that built canals, railroads, and cities, and it made American cities more diverse and more crowded. Nativist violence showed that the democratic majority could define itself against newcomers, especially Catholics, as sharply as it defined itself against Black Americans.

Working-Class Life: Inside the Factory

Before dawn, a bell rings. In a New England mill town in the 1830s or 1840s, that bell woke workers, called them to the mill gates, sent them to breakfast and back, and released them in the evening. A typical workday ran twelve to thirteen hours, six days a week. Inside, hundreds of machines ran off a single water wheel or turbine through belts and shafts overhead, and the noise of looms made conversation nearly impossible. Workers repeated the same tasks all day, tending spindles, tying broken threads, or watching several looms at once. The air was hot, humid (to keep threads from breaking), and full of cotton dust. Moving belts and gears could catch hair, clothing, or fingers, and fire was a constant danger in buildings full of oil and cotton.

Pay came with deductions. In company towns, employers often charged for room and board in company housing and deducted the cost from wages; some fined workers for lateness, poor work, or broken rules. Boardinghouses, especially for young women, came with curfews, matrons, and expectations of church attendance and proper behavior. Work was divided by gender: men held most of the skilled, supervisory, and heavy jobs, such as machinist, mechanic, and overseer; women filled most of the jobs tending textile machines, at lower pay.

The lecture’s illustration is honest about its source: an engraving of power-loom weaving after Thomas Allom, from Edward Baines’s History of the Cotton Manufacture in Great Britain (1835). It shows a British mill, used to suggest what a factory floor of the era looked like. American mills differed in details, but the rhythms of machine work were similar on both sides of the Atlantic. The lecture’s summary line fits both: opportunity and insecurity grew together on the factory floor.

Early Labor Politics: Organizing Claims

In the spring of 1827, journeymen carpenters in Philadelphia walked off the job. They demanded a ten-hour day, instead of the sunrise-to-sunset schedule that could stretch to fourteen hours in summer. The strike failed, but it led to something new. Later that year Philadelphia’s journeymen formed the Mechanics’ Union of Trade Associations, one of the first citywide federations of different trades in the country. In 1828 its members helped launch a Working Men’s Party, which ran its own candidates for city and state offices. In 1829 a Working Men’s movement in New York elected a carpenter to the state assembly, and similar tickets appeared in dozens of towns over the next few years.

These first labor parties did not last long. Internal quarrels, attacks from the major parties, and the major parties’ adoption of some of their demands broke most of them up by the early 1830s. But their platforms show what workers wanted from democracy. They called for free public schools, so that workers’ children would not grow up in ignorance; an end to imprisonment for debt; mechanics’ lien laws, which gave workers a legal claim on a building or property so they could collect unpaid wages; an end to compulsory militia service, which fell hardest on men who could not afford fines; and limits on banks and monopolies.

The Ten-Hour Movement

Above all, workers wanted time. The ten-hour movement argued that a shorter workday was not only about rest. It was about citizenship. A man who worked from dawn to dark had no time to read, attend lectures, educate himself, or take part in public life, and a republic of exhausted workers could not be a republic of informed citizens. In 1835 Boston’s building tradesmen published a circular demanding the ten-hour day, and in Philadelphia that June a strike that began among coal heavers on the Schuylkill River docks spread across the city’s trades. Marchers demanded work “from 6 to 6,” with two hours for meals, and within weeks the city government and many private employers agreed. The deck’s broadside, printed for Philadelphia’s journeymen shipwrights, joiners, caulkers, and mast makers in 1835, comes from that campaign. In 1840 President Martin Van Buren ordered a ten-hour day for laborers on federal public works.

Tools and Claims

Workers built other tools too. Mutual aid societies collected small dues and paid benefits when a member was sick or died, so that a family could afford a doctor or a decent burial. A labor press, including the New York Working Man’s Advocate, edited by George Henry Evans from 1829, gave workers a voice. Trade unions spread in the mid-1830s, and in 1834 delegates from several cities formed the National Trades’ Union, an early attempt at a national labor federation. Workers organized petitions and strikes among printers, carpenters, shoemakers, tailors, and others. Their newspapers described equality not only as the right to vote but as a fair share of time, dignity, and education.

The Panic of 1837 crushed most of these unions, as unemployment made strikes nearly impossible. But the movement left a legacy. In Commonwealth v. Hunt (1842), the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled that unions were not, in themselves, illegal conspiracies. And the language of the ten-hour movement would return in every later struggle over the length of the workday.

The lecture makes a subtle point here. Americans liked to describe their republic as classless, a country without the fixed ranks of Europe. Workers could not easily say “class” without sounding un-American. So they invented a language of class without the word: “producers” against “nonproducers,” honest “mechanics” against “aristocrats,” “monopolies,” and “the money power.” Sean Wilentz calls this outlook “artisan republicanism”: the belief that the independence and dignity of working men were the foundation of the republic, and that the new wage system threatened both.

Lab: Doing Social History with Urban Workers

The lecture’s first lab gives students short excerpts from workers’ sources, such as petitions, slogans, and labor newspapers, and asks three questions. Beyond wages, what do they demand? What does this imply about democracy from the shop floor? And which single word in each source signals a value claim? The pattern students usually find is that workers asked for more than money. They asked for time, learning, dignity, and a civic voice. That ties the lab back to the self-made-man ideal: workers accepted the promise that effort should bring advancement, and insisted that they could not advance, or be citizens, without time to improve themselves.

Analytical significance

Early labor politics turned shop-floor grievances into political claims. Workingmen’s parties, the ten-hour movement, mutual aid, lien laws, and a labor press defined democracy as time, education, and dignity as well as the ballot. In a republic that claimed to have no classes, workers built a language of class without the word.

The Lowell Mill Women

In 1813 a group of Boston merchants led by Francis Cabot Lowell formed the Boston Manufacturing Company, and in 1814 at Waltham, Massachusetts, they opened a mill that combined spinning and power-loom weaving under one roof. After Lowell’s death in 1817, his partners chose a site at the falls of the Merrimack River for a much larger enterprise and named the new town Lowell, incorporated in 1826. By the 1840s Lowell had dozens of mill buildings and was the largest cotton-textile center in the country.

The owners faced a problem. Americans associated English factory towns with misery and vice, and New England farm families would not send their daughters to such places. So the companies built what became known as the Lowell or Waltham system. They recruited young, unmarried women, most between about fifteen and thirty, from farm families across New England. They housed them in company boardinghouses run by respectable matrons, often widows, with curfews (usually 10 p.m.), rules against improper conduct, and the expectation of church attendance. Wages, paid in cash, were higher than most other work open to women, and many women saved money for a dowry, a brother’s schooling, or their own education. Most stayed only a few years.

Work and Rhythm

Mill days ran about twelve to thirteen hours, governed by bells. The work was repetitive and loud, and the hot, humid rooms were thick with cotton dust that many blamed for lung disease. Women tended spinning frames or looms under male overseers. Wages came minus the cost of room and board, and there were fines for breaking the rules. Yet many mill women also found new freedoms: money of their own, friendships, lectures, libraries, and “improvement circles” where they read and discussed their writing. From 1840 to 1845 some of them published the Lowell Offering, a magazine of stories, poems, and essays written by mill workers, which visitors such as Charles Dickens praised as proof that factory work need not degrade its workers.

Turning Out

The respectability that made the Lowell system work also gave mill women leverage. In February 1834, after the companies announced a wage cut, about 800 women “turned out,” the period’s term for a strike, and issued a proclamation headed “Union is Power.” The strike failed within days, but it was one of the first strikes by women factory workers in the United States. In October 1836, when the companies raised the charge for board, which effectively cut wages, a much larger turnout followed: by one estimate twelve to fifteen hundred women marched through the streets singing. One of them was eleven-year-old Harriet Hanson, a doffer (a child who replaced full bobbins) whose widowed mother kept a boardinghouse. More than sixty years later, as Harriet Hanson Robinson, she remembered the moment her room hesitated:

Primary source

“I don’t care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether any one else does or not.”

— Harriet Hanson Robinson, recalling the Lowell turnout of October 1836, in Loom and Spindle; or, Life Among the Early Mill Girls (1898)

She marched out, and the others followed. “As I looked back at the long line that followed me,” she wrote, “I was more proud than I have ever been since at any success I may have achieved.” The company punished her mother by turning her out of her boardinghouse. Robinson, writing as a woman suffragist in old age, also recorded the song the strikers sang, a parody of a popular tune: “Oh! isn’t it a pity, such a pretty girl as I— / Should be sent to the factory to pine away and die?” Historians have found that at least some companies backed down on the board increase, but Robinson herself remembered that “so far as results were concerned this strike did no good,” and that wages kept falling.

The Lowell Female Labor Reform Association

By the 1840s conditions had worsened. Companies sped up the machines and assigned each woman more looms or spindles, while wages stagnated. In 1845 Lowell women founded the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association (LFLRA), with Sarah G. Bagley, a weaver from New Hampshire, as its first president. The association gathered signatures for petitions asking the Massachusetts legislature to limit the workday to ten hours. In response, a legislative committee held hearings in 1845, among the first government investigations of working conditions in American history, and several mill women, including Bagley, testified about long hours, poor ventilation, and exhaustion. The committee, chaired by Lowell’s own representative, William Schouler, concluded that the remedy did not lie with the legislature. The LFLRA campaigned against him when he next stood for election.

The association also helped publish a labor newspaper, the Voice of Industry, and Bagley publicly attacked the Lowell Offering for avoiding criticism of the corporations. Mill women gave speeches, wrote essays, and organized branches in other New England mill towns. Their arguments joined the ten-hour movement’s claims about time and citizenship to claims about women’s moral and educational dignity: respectable, intelligent women deserved time to read and improve themselves. Massachusetts did not pass an enforceable ten-hour law for women and children until 1874, and by the 1850s Irish immigrant women were replacing many of the Yankee farm daughters in the mills. But before most male factory workers had organized, young women had petitioned, struck, testified, and published.

Robinson’s memoir offers an interesting twist on the self-made-man ideal. Looking back at the later lives of her fellow mill girls, she wrote that they had been “self-made in the truest sense,” and added: “A woman ought to be as proud of being self-made as a man.”

How did gender expectations shape the women’s tactics? What counted as “respectability” leverage? Which phrase reframes what democracy means? The lecture’s notes suggest a comparison: urban male workers spoke the language of producer-citizenship, the independent mechanic as the backbone of the republic; Lowell women spoke the language of virtue and respectability, educated daughters of New England who deserved better treatment. Both demanded time and a voice.

Analytical significance

The Lowell system offered young women wages and independence under paternal oversight and bell-driven discipline. Mill women answered with turnouts, petitions, testimony, and newspapers, using the language of respectability and education to claim a voice they could not cast at the ballot box.

Free Black Communities in the North

One Sunday in Philadelphia, probably in the late 1780s or early 1790s, Black worshipers at St. George’s Methodist Church knelt to pray in a section of the gallery where white church officials did not want them. According to the account Richard Allen later wrote, an official pulled the minister Absalom Jones up off his knees in the middle of the prayer. When the prayer ended, the Black members walked out together. Allen, a former slave who had bought his freedom and become a Methodist preacher, went on to found Bethel Church in 1794 in a converted blacksmith shop. In 1816, after years of struggle with white Methodist leaders over control of the congregation, Bethel and other Black churches formed the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) Church, the first independent Black denomination in the United States, with Allen as its first bishop.

Free Black communities grew in Northern cities in the decades after the Revolution, as gradual emancipation laws slowly ended slavery in the Northern states and free people and fugitives from the South moved to Philadelphia, New York, Boston, and smaller towns. Philadelphia had the largest. These communities were mostly poor: Black men worked as laborers, sailors, waiters, barbers, and porters, and Black women as laundresses and domestic servants. A small middle class of ministers, teachers, shopkeepers, and craftsmen emerged, along with a few wealthy men such as the Philadelphia sailmaker James Forten.

Institutions as Infrastructure

The church was at the center. Black churches were places of worship, but also schools, meeting halls, relief agencies, and political headquarters. Around them grew mutual aid societies, literary and debating societies, Masonic lodges, and schools. In 1817 nearly three thousand Black Philadelphians gathered at Bethel and voted overwhelmingly to reject the American Colonization Society’s plan to send free Black Americans to Africa; this was their country, they insisted. In 1830 Bethel hosted the first National Negro Convention, with Allen presiding, the start of a convention movement that met through the antebellum years to debate education, self-help, emigration, and civil rights.

Black Americans also built their own press. In March 1827, in New York, Samuel Cornish and John Russwurm launched Freedom’s Journal, the first newspaper owned and edited by African Americans. Its first editorial explained why:

Primary source

“We wish to plead our own cause. Too long have others spoken for us.”

— Samuel Cornish and John B. Russwurm, “To Our Patrons,” Freedom’s Journal (New York), March 16, 1827

Voices: David Walker and Maria Stewart

In Boston, a used-clothing dealer named David Walker, born free in North Carolina, published his Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World in 1829. It was a furious attack on slavery, racism, and colonization, and it called on enslaved people to resist. Walker used sailors and other contacts to carry copies into Southern ports, and Southern states responded with new laws against “seditious” writings and against teaching enslaved people to read. Walker rejected the idea that Black Americans belonged anywhere else: “America is more our country, than it is the whites—we have enriched it with our blood and tears.” He died in Boston in 1830.

Walker’s Appeal inspired Maria W. Stewart, a young Black widow and former domestic servant in Boston. In 1831 the abolitionist William Lloyd Garrison published her essay Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality, and in 1832 and 1833 she gave public lectures in Boston, among the first by any American woman to audiences that included both men and women. She demanded education and dignity for Black women in particular:

Primary source

“How long shall the fair daughters of Africa be compelled to bury their minds and talents beneath a load of iron pots and kettles?”

— Maria W. Stewart, Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality (Boston, 1831)

Stewart faced criticism, including from within her own community, for speaking in public as a woman, and she gave a farewell address in 1833 before leaving Boston. But she had shown, earlier than the white women abolitionists who are often credited, that a woman could claim the public platform.

Limits Even in “Free” States

Freedom in the North had sharp limits. As we have seen, Black men lost the vote in state after state. In most Northern states Black people could not serve on juries, and some states, including Ohio, Indiana, and Illinois, passed “Black laws” that restricted Black settlement or required bonds of good behavior. Schools, churches, theaters, streetcars, and steamboats were often segregated. Skilled jobs were usually closed to Black men, and in the 1830s and 1840s Irish immigrants competed with Black workers for unskilled labor, sometimes violently.

Mob violence was common. In 1829 white mobs in Cincinnati attacked Black neighborhoods, and many Black residents left for Canada. In Philadelphia, white rioters attacked Black homes and churches in 1834, 1838, and 1842; in May 1838 a mob burned Pennsylvania Hall, a new meeting hall built for abolitionists, days after it opened. Like the anti-Catholic riots of 1844, these attacks showed that in Jacksonian cities, a crowd could claim to speak for “the people” while terrorizing a minority.

Yet the institutions free Black communities built in these years became the infrastructure of abolitionism. Black subscribers kept Garrison’s Liberator alive in its first years. Black churches and vigilance committees sheltered fugitives from slavery. Black speakers, writers, and organizers, from Stewart to Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838, brought the experience of slavery and Northern racism into public debate.

Analytical significance

Free Black communities faced disfranchisement, segregation, and violence even in “free” states. They responded by building their own institutions, churches, schools, mutual aid societies, newspapers, and conventions, which became the infrastructure of abolitionism and a democratic public of their own.

Conclusion: Paradoxes of Jacksonian America

Go back to the crowd at Jackson’s White House reception in March 1829. Margaret Bayard Smith saw boys, women, children, and Black Washingtonians, along with the farmers and mechanics who had voted Jackson into office. Through the social-history lens, everyone in that crowd has a story. The white mechanic could now vote, and might join a Working Men’s Party or a ten-hour strike. The young woman might soon leave her family farm for a Lowell boardinghouse and learn to live by the bell. The Black man might attend a Bethel convention, read Freedom’s Journal, and watch his right to vote disappear. And the immigrant who arrived a few years later might dig a canal, survive cholera, and face a nativist mob.

The lecture sums up the era as a set of paradoxes. Participation and exclusion: white male democracy grew alongside the subordination of others. The market revolution: it brought both opportunity and precarity. Class: Americans talked about equality while living in an increasingly stratified society. Culture: popular forms, from rallies and songs to penny papers, shaped political feeling. None of these paradoxes is a contradiction we need to resolve. They are the shape of the period itself.

Discussion prompt

Exit thought: Which lens, political, cultural, or social, best explains the Jacksonian era, and why? Choose one, and name one piece of evidence from this lecture that the other lenses would miss.

Analytical significance

Main argument: Jacksonian Democracy really did open politics to ordinary white men, but seen from below it was a society of paradoxes. Expanding participation, a growing market, and the self-made-man ideal brought opportunity and insecurity together, while race and gender drew new boundaries around the “common man.” Ordinary people, including those shut out of the ballot box, answered with strikes, petitions, newspapers, and institutions of their own.

Interpretive Synthesis: Democracy from Below

Between the 1820s and the 1840s, American politics became more democratic for white men than almost anywhere else in the world. Property barriers fell, turnout soared, and Democrats and Whigs built mass parties that drew ordinary men into rallies and campaigns. Jacksonians celebrated the common man and attacked privilege, as in Jackson’s Bank veto. But the same years saw the disfranchisement of free Black men, the exclusion of women, the removal of Native nations, and the expansion of slavery. The boundaries of the political nation were redrawn around race and gender as property ceased to define them.

Social history shows how the era looked from below. The market revolution brought canals, railroads, wages, and cheap goods, but also clock discipline, dependence on employers, and exposure to panics. The self-made-man ideal opened doors and blamed the poor for their poverty. Immigrants supplied the labor for a growing economy and met nativist suspicion and violence. Urban workers organized parties, unions, and the ten-hour movement and defined democracy as time, education, and dignity. Lowell mill women struck and petitioned, using respectability as leverage. Free Black communities built churches, newspapers, and conventions, the foundation of abolitionism.

So what did democracy look like from the factory floor, the boardinghouse, the immigrant neighborhood, and the Black church? It looked unfinished. Ordinary people believed the promise of equality enough to demand that it be kept, and their demands, for shorter hours, free schools, equal suffrage, and an end to slavery, would shape American politics for the rest of the century. The open question, which the exit thought asks, is how much weight to give to the expansion and how much to the exclusion. The evidence supports neither celebration nor dismissal alone.

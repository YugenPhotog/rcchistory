Learning Objectives

After reading this essay and delivering this lecture, the instructor should be able to:

  1. Evaluate Charles Stuart's captivity account as retrospective evidence and explain what Shingas's reported speech reveals — and cannot prove — about British-Native diplomacy before Braddock's defeat.
  2. Explain how British assumptions about land, allegiance, and military authority weakened the 1755 campaign for the Ohio Country.
  3. Analyze the Battle of the Monongahela as a sudden meeting engagement shaped by terrain, coalition warfare, command breakdown, and Native tactical skill rather than by a simple opposition between "European" and "American" warfare.
  4. Trace how Braddock's defeat widened the Pennsylvania frontier war without reducing later violence to a single road, decision, or diplomatic failure.
  5. Compare the defeat on the Monongahela with the Acadian deportation as distinct consequences of British assumptions about land and allegiance.

Section 1: A Captive's Memory and a Diplomatic Warning

The Source Before the Battle

In November 1755, a Lenape war party captured Pennsylvania settler Charles Stuart and his family during an attack in the Great Cove. During his captivity, Stuart heard the Lenape leader Shingas explain why many Ohio Country people had abandoned neutrality or moved toward the French alliance. Stuart's surviving narrative is retrospective; historian Beverley W. Bond Jr. published it from manuscript in 1926. Stuart was not present at the meeting he described, and his account is the only surviving source for this particular exchange. It is therefore unusually valuable evidence of Shingas's retrospective political explanation, but not a stenographic transcript of a conversation in General Edward Braddock's tent.

According to Stuart's account of Shingas's retelling, Lenape, Shawnee, and Mingo leaders had asked Braddock what would happen to the Ohio Country if Britain expelled the French. Braddock allegedly replied that the English would "inhabit & inherit the Land" and that "No Savage Should Inherit the Land." When the chiefs answered that they would not fight for land on which they had no liberty to live, Braddock reportedly said he did not need their help.

The passage contains at least three layers: Braddock's alleged words, Shingas's later retelling, and Stuart's recollection and transcription. No independent record confirms the exchange in precisely this form, and British and colonial witnesses described Braddock's relations with Native delegations differently. Although the wording cannot be independently authenticated, the speech articulates grievances documented elsewhere in Ohio diplomacy: fear of settler occupation, fraudulent or disputed land purchases, and uncertainty over whether British victory would leave Native communities a territorial future.

What the Warning Reveals

The source should not be used to make one sentence cause an entire war. It should be used to expose the political question beneath the campaign: would a British victory protect Native homelands or accelerate colonial occupation? Shingas's reported demand joined land and liberty. Assistance was conditional on a future in which Native communities retained places to live, hunt, trade, and govern themselves. Lenape, Shawnee, Mingo, and other Ohio communities did not share a single position; their decisions reflected local politics, kinship, trade, inter-Indigenous rivalries, and changing assessments of French and British intentions. Richard S. Grimes's study of western Delaware nationhood is especially useful here because it treats Lenape migration and political consolidation as historical processes rather than assuming a timeless or homogeneous "Delaware" position.

Analytical Significance

The reported speech matters less as a perfect transcript than as evidence of a diplomatic failure. It preserves Shingas's retrospective explanation rather than Braddock's verified wording. British commanders approached the Ohio Country as territory to be secured; Native leaders approached the same campaign as a negotiation over who would possess land and exercise authority after the fighting ended.

Section 2: Braddock's Expedition

From Great Meadows to an Imperial Offensive

George Washington's surrender at Great Meadows in July 1754 transformed a contested Ohio encounter into an imperial emergency. In 1755 Britain sent Major General Edward Braddock with the 44th and 48th Regiments and authority as commander in chief. His expedition toward Fort Duquesne was one part of a broader campaign that also aimed at French positions in Nova Scotia, the Lake Champlain corridor, and Niagara.

Braddock's force was neither a comic opera nor an army wandering blindly into wilderness. British regulars brought artillery, engineers, wagons, stores, and siege doctrine appropriate to the objective of reducing a fort. Provincial troops, wagoners, laborers, women, servants, and enslaved and free Black workers made the campaign possible. Friction arose because military rank, supply contracts, local knowledge, and provincial political authority did not fit neatly inside Braddock's chain of command.

Braddock's Road as an Imperial Achievement

To move artillery and hundreds of wagons west from Fort Cumberland, soldiers and laborers widened older paths, including the route associated with the Native guide Nemacolin, into a road roughly twelve feet wide. The work was slow and punishing, but it was a major logistical accomplishment. Braddock eventually divided the army, advancing with a lighter column while Colonel Thomas Dunbar followed with much of the baggage.

The road embodied the campaign's strength and its danger. It demonstrated Britain's capacity to move organized force across the mountains, but it also advertised a durable corridor into the Ohio Country. For Native observers, a road built to expel the French could readily become a road for traders, land companies, soldiers, and settlers.

Diplomacy was the expedition's narrower supply line. Braddock did seek Native assistance, and a small number of Native scouts remained with the column, but he failed to secure the broad Ohio alliance he needed. Potential allies did not act as a bloc; their choices reflected inter-Indigenous politics as well as assessments of French and British intentions. Gift shortages, inadequate interpreting and mediation, status disputes, colonial land ambitions, and the belief that regular troops could compensate for weak relations left the army with limited intelligence and little political legitimacy among communities whose territory it entered.

In retrospective biographical remarks composed about 1786–1788, Washington remembered the retreat through "the dead, the dying, the groans, lamentations, and crys of the wounded for help." The sentence captures both the expedition's human catastrophe and Washington's later memory of it, but the larger failure began before the shooting: Britain had assembled an army without assembling a coalition.

Analytical Significance

Braddock could build a road and transport a siege army, but material capacity could not substitute for consent, intelligence, and alliance. The campaign's central weakness was not that Britain brought an army; it was that Britain treated diplomacy as an accessory to an operation whose political future depended on Native decisions.

Section 3: The Battle of the Monongahela

A Sudden Encounter, Not a Scripted Ambush

On July 9, 1755, Braddock's advance column — approximately 1,460 regulars and provincials — crossed the Monongahela a few miles from Fort Duquesne. A French-led force of approximately 891 moved out to meet it: 36 officers, 72 French regulars, 146 Canadian militia, and 637 Native warriors. The two forces encountered one another unexpectedly. Calling the battle a planned ambush imposes order on what began as a meeting engagement.

French commander Daniel-Hyacinthe-Marie Liénard de Beaujeu was killed early, but Captain Jean-Daniel Dumas and other leaders restored momentum. Native warriors moved through open woodland, used trees and folds in the ground for cover, and pressed the British flanks. British advance units fell back into troops still coming forward. Smoke, noise, officer casualties, friendly fire, and the compression of men along the road made command increasingly difficult.

The outcome was devastating. Of approximately 1,460 British and provincial personnel who crossed the river, roughly two-thirds were killed, wounded, or missing; published totals vary slightly according to roster and counting practice. The French-led force suffered only a few dozen casualties. Those figures show tactical mastery and command collapse, but they do not prove that disciplined formations were inherently useless in North America. The defeat accelerated an uneven process of tactical and institutional adaptation in which regular formations increasingly operated alongside light infantry, rangers, scouts, provincial forces, artillery, and Native allies.

Beyond the Myth of "European Stupidity"

Braddock was an experienced officer, and his column had taken precautions. The defeat came from interacting failures: too little intelligence, a sudden collision, terrain that rewarded dispersed movement, the rapid loss of officers, poor coordination between advance and main bodies, and the effectiveness of a coalition in which Native warriors supplied most of the combat power.

Instructor note

Avoid reenacting the older contrast between foolish redcoats in straight lines and naturally invisible Native marksmen. Emphasize that both coalitions adapted, that the battlefield was open woodland rather than impenetrable forest, and that Native tactics were learned military practices rather than racial traits.

Analytical Significance

The Monongahela was not simply a triumph of "American" warfare over "European" warfare. It was a coalition victory in which Native numbers, discipline, mobility, and knowledge turned a sudden encounter into a British catastrophe.

Section 4: The War After the Defeat

A Road into the Settlements

Braddock's defeat left Fort Duquesne in French hands and shattered the expectation that a large regular force would quickly secure the Ohio Country. The road did not cause the raids or create Native mobility, since Native communities already possessed extensive routes and geographic knowledge. It nevertheless left an improved military corridor across the mountains, exposed the permanence of British ambitions, and could be used by multiple belligerents. Abandoned stores and captured equipment also strengthened the victors.

By autumn 1755, Lenape, Shawnee, Mingo, and allied war parties struck settlements in Pennsylvania, Maryland, and Virginia. The attacks killed and captured colonists, displaced thousands, and revealed how thinly defended the backcountry had been. They were not simply French commands carried out by Native clients. Particular leaders and communities pursued their own war aims, including checking settlement, avenging losses, protecting regional influence, and forcing colonial governments back into diplomacy.

Pennsylvania's Divided Response

Pennsylvania did not enter the crisis with a standing provincial army or a mature frontier fort system. Its Quaker-influenced political culture distrusted compulsory militia service, while the assembly and proprietary governors were locked in a struggle over taxation and authority. After the attacks, the colony funded defenses, passed a voluntary militia law, and built a chain of forts, but the response remained politically contested and uneven.

The crisis also intensified racialized retaliation. Colonial governments offered scalp bounties; militias attacked Native towns; and Colonel John Armstrong's 1756 expedition destroyed Kittanning, a Lenape town and staging point for raids. Such campaigns could rescue captives or disrupt war parties, but they also widened cycles of destruction and endangered people who were neutral, divided, or seeking peace.

Washington's memory of "shocking Scenes" described soldiers on the retreat, yet comparable suffering spread outward to Native towns, colonial farms, refugee roads, and captive households. The frontier war was not the aftershock of one battle. It was a struggle over settlement and sovereignty that Braddock's failure made harder to contain.

The eventual movement toward negotiations culminating at Easton in 1758 confirmed the lesson that the 1755 expedition had ignored: no military solution in the Ohio Country could be separated from land policy and diplomatic recognition.

From Defeat to a Different Imperial Strategy

British and colonial adaptation was uneven rather than sudden. Rangers and light troops gained prominence, commanders cultivated Native intelligence more seriously, and William Pitt later offered money and military concessions that encouraged provincial mobilization. The successful Forbes expedition of 1758 advanced more deliberately and was paired with the Treaty of Easton. Michael N. McConnell's reconstruction of the Forbes campaign emphasizes that success came from a hybrid regular-provincial force, negotiated colonial participation, logistics, and diplomacy rather than from a simple tactical correction to Braddock's mistakes.

That later success should not be narrated as Britain finally learning how to conquer alone. It depended on reducing French support, repairing some diplomatic relationships, mobilizing provincial manpower, and persuading important Native communities that neutrality served their interests.

The contrast is crucial: Braddock's road survived his army, but Britain's path back to the Forks of the Ohio required a political bargain.

Analytical Significance

Defeat widened the war because it exposed British weakness and confirmed Native fears about land. Britain recovered only when it combined adaptation in the field with diplomacy beyond it.

Section 5: The Other 1755

Fort Beauséjour and the Acadian Oath

While Braddock marched west, British regulars and New England provincial troops attacked Fort Beauséjour on the contested isthmus connecting peninsular Nova Scotia to the mainland. The fort surrendered on June 16, 1755; nearby Fort Gaspereau surrendered the following day. Beauséjour's fall weakened French power in the region and exposed Acadian communities that had long tried to navigate between British rule, French connections, and Mi'kmaq alliances.

British authorities demanded an unconditional oath of allegiance. Many Acadians had previously accepted qualified oaths that they understood to preserve neutrality, especially a refusal to bear arms against France or the Mi'kmaq. Officials increasingly treated that position as intolerable in wartime. Some Acadians had supplied or assisted the French, while others had avoided direct participation; British policy collapsed those differences into a collective problem of allegiance.

On July 28, the Nova Scotia council ordered deportation. The decision was not an automatic consequence of a law that clearly made all Acadians foreign enemies. It was an executive wartime policy justified through claims of military necessity, unreliable allegiance, and the supposed impossibility of policing scattered communities. Officials also understood that removal would make Acadian farms available to loyal Protestant settlement. Recent work by Richard LeBlanc has reopened both the terminology and chronology of the Acadian tragedy, distinguishing descriptive and legal categories such as deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing, and genocide while locating the removals within longer settler-colonial pressures that predated 1755. That framework usefully broadens the chronology without eliminating the contingency of the council's wartime decision.

Removal, Mortality, and Diaspora

In the first phase, roughly 6,000 to 7,000 Acadians from the Bay of Fundy region were deported to Britain's mainland colonies. Removals continued through the war; by the early 1760s approximately 10,000 people had been forcibly displaced. Estimates of the broader prewar Acadian population vary according to whether historians include only the communities targeted in 1755 or also Acadians in Île Saint-Jean, Île Royale, and adjacent regions. Others escaped, hid, or endured refugee camps. Disease, shipwreck, hunger, exposure, and family separation killed many, although exact mortality totals remain contested.

Analytical Significance

Braddock's defeat and the Acadian deportation were not equivalent events, and the peoples involved occupied different political and legal positions. Native nations asserted sovereignty and territorial rights outside British subjecthood; Acadians were conquered inhabitants whose disputed status was expressed through oaths and wartime allegiance. The crises nevertheless exposed two distinct ways British officials tried to convert contested human landscapes into governable imperial territory: by demanding Native cooperation without recognizing an enduring Native territorial future, and by making unconditional allegiance the price of continued Acadian residence.

Closing Synthesis

The year 1755 revealed two faces of British imperial power. In the Ohio Country, Britain possessed regiments, artillery, and engineers but lacked the coalition needed to make victory politically possible. In Acadia, Britain won a fort and then used administrative and military force to remake the population of a region.

The phrase "No Savage Should Inherit the Land" survives through a layered and imperfect source whose exact wording cannot be authenticated. It nevertheless condenses a durable imperial assumption visible in other evidence: officials could imagine land as a prize whose future did not require the consent of those already living on it. In the Ohio Country that assumption weakened diplomacy with sovereign Native communities; in Acadia a different legal and political logic converted contested allegiance into removal and opened occupied farms to new settlement.

The open question this lecture leaves is not whether Britain was strong or weak. It is what kind of power Britain exercised when it could build roads, move populations, and claim territory — yet still depended on peoples whose rights it was unwilling to recognize.

Suggested Readings for Instructor Preparation

David L. Preston, Braddock's Defeat: The Battle of the Monongahela and the Road to Revolution (2015)

Preston's deeply researched reconstruction replaces the familiar tale of a foolish general and an inevitable ambush with a coalition history of diplomacy, logistics, terrain, and combat. It is the most important guide to the battle and to the provenance of the Shingas-Braddock exchange.

John Mack Faragher, A Great and Noble Scheme: The Tragic Story of the Expulsion of the French Acadians from Their American Homeland (2005)

A broad narrative of Acadian society and the deportation. Faragher is especially useful for reconstructing official planning and the moral logic by which British administrators described coercive dispersal as a necessary and even improving policy.

Christopher Hodson, The Acadian Diaspora: An Eighteenth-Century History (2012)

Hodson follows displaced Acadians across the Atlantic world and prevents the story from ending at the docks in 1755. His emphasis on labor, migration, and failed imperial settlement projects complicates a simple Acadia-to-Louisiana trajectory.

Matthew C. Ward, Breaking the Backcountry: The Seven Years' War in Virginia and Pennsylvania, 1754–1765 (2003)

Ward explains how raids, captivity, refugee movement, forts, and provincial politics transformed the backcountry after Braddock's defeat. It is essential for connecting a single battlefield to the longer regional war.

Geoffrey Plank, An Unsettled Conquest: The British Campaign Against the Peoples of Acadia (2001)

Plank places Acadians and Mi'kmaq within the same contested region and shows that British conquest remained incomplete and negotiated long after formal claims to Nova Scotia. The book is particularly valuable for avoiding an Acadian story detached from Indigenous history.

Stephen Brumwell, Redcoats: The British Soldier and War in the Americas, 1755–1763 (2002)

Brumwell reconstructs the British army as an adaptive institution rather than a rigid European relic. His treatment of regular soldiers, tactics, and military learning supports a more accurate explanation of the Monongahela and its aftermath.

Michael N. McConnell, A Country Between: The Upper Ohio Valley and Its Peoples, 1724–1774 (1992)

McConnell centers the Native communities of the upper Ohio and explains their diplomacy, migrations, and shifting wartime decisions on their own regional terms. It supplies the political world that makes Shingas's reported speech intelligible.

Michael N. McConnell, To Risk It All: General Forbes, the Capture of Fort Duquesne, and the Course of Empire in the Ohio Country (2020)

McConnell's detailed study of the 1758 Forbes campaign explains why Britain succeeded where Braddock failed without reducing the contrast to tactics alone. It is particularly useful for understanding the hybrid regular-provincial force, negotiated colonial participation, logistics, diplomacy, and the limits of imperial coordination.

Richard S. Grimes, The Western Delaware Indian Nation, 1730–1795: Warriors and Diplomats (2017)

Grimes reconstructs western Lenape migration, political consolidation, warfare, and diplomacy across the Seven Years' War and revolutionary eras. The book helps place Shingas and other leaders within changing Lenape institutions rather than treating the "Delawares" as a timeless or politically uniform people.

Richard LeBlanc, "A Terminological History of the Acadian Tragedy" (2025)

LeBlanc examines the changing vocabulary used to describe the Acadian removals, including deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing, and genocide. The article is useful for separating historical description, legal classification, commemoration, and moral judgment rather than allowing one term to settle every interpretive question.

Richard LeBlanc, "Settler Colonialism and the Elimination of the Acadian: 1708–1748" (published online 2025)

LeBlanc extends the chronology backward from 1755 and situates the deportation within longer British efforts to resolve the Acadian presence as an obstacle to Protestant settlement and imperial security. The article complements studies centered on the wartime decision by showing the deeper structures that made removal imaginable.

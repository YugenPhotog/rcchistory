Essential question

What kind of republic did the Revolution create, and can organized opposition remain legitimate?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain Hamilton's public-credit logic and why Jefferson and Madison feared it rebuilt British corruption.
  2. Analyze the Bank debate as a conflict over the Necessary and Proper Clause and the reach of national power.
  3. Trace how Genêt, Jay's Treaty (bargain and backlash), Washington's Farewell, and the Adams succession turned foreign policy into party identity.
  4. Evaluate the XYZ Affair and Sedition Act as an emergency politics that asked whether criticism could become a federal crime.
  5. Assess why the election of 1800 established that an opposition party could become the government without destroying the republic.

Introductory Framing

Lecture 1 showed that Americans could make the Constitution operational. Lecture 2 asks what kind of republic that newly capable government would become. Alexander Hamilton and Thomas Jefferson both claimed the Revolution. They feared different ways liberty could be lost. Hamilton feared a government too weak to tax, borrow, or sustain independence. Jefferson and James Madison feared a government tied to creditors, banks, and executive patronage that might rebuild the British system Americans had resisted.

Their disagreement became organized parties. Foreign crisis then made opposition look like disloyalty. Genêt, Jay's Treaty, Washington's Farewell, the Adams succession, the XYZ Affair, and the Sedition Act raised a sharper question: can organized opposition remain legitimate in a republic that has not yet accepted the idea of a loyal opposition? The election of 1800 forced an answer into practice when an opposition party defeated the government and power still changed hands.

This essay follows the formal lecture's history-first path. It begins with public credit and the Bank, then moves through foreign crisis, emergency politics, and the transfer of 1801. Optional enrichment topics such as Fries's Rebellion or the full fairness controversy over debt certificates appear only briefly, if at all, because they are not required for the formal argument.

I. Public Credit and Competing Visions of the Republic

Hamilton's Problem Was Credibility

Hamilton's January 1790 Report on Public Credit treated debt not as a list of bills but as a problem of national reputation. The United States had won independence while accumulating obligations to foreign governments, soldiers, suppliers, and domestic creditors. Under the Confederation, Congress lacked a dependable independent revenue stream. A government that could not reliably pay interest or principal would borrow only with difficulty and would have little room to survive war or crisis.

Hamilton therefore argued that adequate provision for the debt was important to the "honor and prosperity" of the United States. He wanted funded debt: obligations backed by reliable revenue and serviced predictably. He did not embrace perpetual debt as an unconditional public good. He wanted creditors to believe the government would honor contracts. In an eighteenth-century international system, borrowing capacity could determine whether a state remained independent.

Assumption of state Revolutionary debts intensified the conflict because state circumstances differed. Some states had paid down much of their debt; others had not. Assumption redistributed burdens and benefits, but Hamilton's purpose went beyond accounting. Transferring state debt to the national government would make federal revenue more important and link a larger class of creditors to national fiscal stability. The conceptual chain mattered for students: state debts became federal debt; federal debt required federal revenue; federal revenue strengthened national political gravity.

Jefferson and Madison Feared a British System Reborn

In 1790-1791 Jefferson (Secretary of State) and Madison (in the House) broke with Hamilton's program. They did not advocate no government. They feared dependence and corruption: permanent public debt, a national bank, and executive patronage that might create a permanent coalition of creditors and officeholders. From the Republican perspective, that resembled the British model in which debt, taxation, financial institutions, and executive influence reinforced one another.

Both sides could plausibly claim the Revolution. Hamilton thought interdependence between government and creditors was a source of stability. His opponents feared it was machinery of aristocracy. The argument was about political economy: what kinds of economic relationships would make republican liberty secure?

Analytical significance

The first party conflict did not begin as a quarrel over ballots. It began as a quarrel over what national power should do once the Constitution made national power real.

II. The Bank Debate and Necessary and Proper

In 1790-1791 Hamilton proposed a Bank of the United States to manage federal funds, issue notes, and stabilize credit. Congress passed a bank bill. Washington asked for constitutional opinions before signing. Jefferson and Madison argued that nowhere did the Constitution expressly authorize a bank. If "necessary and proper" meant merely convenient, Jefferson warned, implied power could expand into a "boundless field of power." The issue was larger than banking. It was a fight over how broadly the Constitution could be read in practice.

Hamilton answered with a different theory of government. Sovereign powers, he argued, necessarily carry implied means appropriate to legitimate constitutional ends. Necessary could not sensibly mean absolutely indispensable, because governments routinely choose among useful means. If Congress could tax, borrow, regulate commerce, and manage public finances, then a bank reasonably connected to those powers could be constitutional even without an explicit banking clause.

Washington did not rubber-stamp Hamilton. He solicited written opinions from Attorney General Edmund Randolph and from Jefferson, both of whom objected, then requested Hamilton's response. The president's decision helped establish a broad practical interpretation of federal power, but the episode also shows governance by argument. Cabinet officers were becoming constitutional advisers. A textual ambiguity was being resolved through institutional practice.

III. Competing Visions Become the First Party System

By the mid-1790s Hamilton's supporters and Jefferson and Madison's opponents of the fiscal program sorted into lasting coalitions. Federalists defended credit, commercial development, executive energy, and closer relations with Britain. Republicans defended agrarian independence, suspicion of financial privilege, stricter constitutional reading, and warmer identification with the French Revolution's early promise. Americans still condemned "faction" in theory while building durable parties in practice.

Partisan newspapers mattered, but they should not swallow the lecture. Printers organized information, ridicule, and loyalty across distance. Supporters saw watchdogs; opponents saw dangerous factions. The deeper point is institutional: once national power could act, disagreement about its ends produced organized opposition even though republican theory said a virtuous people should share a common good.

IV. Genêt Turns a Foreign Revolution into a Domestic Loyalty Test

In April 1793 Edmond-Charles Genêt arrived as minister from revolutionary France. Washington had just issued the Neutrality Proclamation: the United States would stay out of the European war between France and Britain. Genêt outfitted privateers, appealed directly to American public opinion, and treated popular enthusiasm for France as a resource he could use against Washington's policy. The episode made foreign allegiance look like a domestic political weapon.

Democratic-Republican societies formed in this atmosphere. Their stated principles included discussion of public conduct and preservation of republican government. Federalists saw clubs that resembled French Jacobinism and threatened social order. The French Revolution thus changed the meaning of American disagreement. Policy dispute could be framed as loyalty or subversion.

V. Jay's Treaty: Bargain First, Backlash Second

The History of the Bargain

Teach the crisis before the street theater. British troops still occupied northwestern posts after the Revolution. British naval practice impressed American sailors and seized ships. War with Britain threatened commerce and perhaps the republic's survival. John Jay negotiated a treaty that secured British evacuation of the posts, improved commercial arrangements, and reduced the immediate danger of war. The treaty left impressment and other grievances unresolved. It was a bargain to avoid war, not a triumph that settled every American complaint.

The Backlash That Made Parties Dig In

When Jay's Treaty became public in 1795, Republican newspapers and public meetings denounced it as a humiliating tilt toward Britain and a betrayal of France and the Revolution. Demonstrators burned Jay in effigy. Federalists defended the treaty as hard-headed preservation of peace and credit. Foreign policy became party identity. The treaty's terms and the reaction to those terms did different political work: one reduced the chance of war; the other deepened the sense that opponents were not merely wrong but dangerous.

VI. Washington's Farewell and the Adams Succession

In September 1796 Washington published his Farewell Address as he declined a third term. He warned against permanent foreign attachments that could drag the republic into European wars, and against the spirit of party that put faction above the public good. The warning was not abstract wisdom floating above politics. Washington had watched Genêt appeal to American crowds, seen Jay's Treaty tear the public apart, and watched cabinet conflict harden into party organization. He named the system already emerging even as he hoped to restrain it.

John Adams, a Federalist, narrowly defeated Thomas Jefferson in 1796 and took office in March 1797. It was the first contested presidential succession after Washington. Adams inherited Hamilton's fiscal system, the unfinished French crisis, and a republic already divided by party. Under the original Constitution, electors did not cast separate votes for president and vice president, so Jefferson became vice president. Name the president and the years before XYZ appears. Students need to know who held the office when emergency politics arrived.

VII. XYZ, Sedition, and the Line Between Opposition and Disloyalty

The XYZ Affair Made Federalist Fear Plausible

Adams sent envoys to calm relations with France. In 1797-1798 French agents (later labeled X, Y, and Z in published dispatches) demanded a bribe and a loan before talks. Publication of the insult produced a wave of patriotic anger, a Quasi-War at sea, and a temporary collapse of Republican credibility. The Alien and Sedition crisis did not arise in peacetime from imaginary threats. Federalists faced a hostile revolutionary France, naval conflict, and domestic opponents who had celebrated the French cause. Emergency made repression politically available.

The Sedition Act

In 1798 Federalist-controlled Congress passed the Alien and Sedition Acts. For the formal lecture, focus on the Sedition Act: it made it a federal crime to publish "false, scandalous, and malicious" writings against the government, Congress, or the president. Federalists framed the law as wartime self-defense. Republicans framed it as a criminalization of opposition. Prosecutions followed. Matthew Lyon, a Republican congressman from Vermont, was convicted for published attacks on Adams, served about four months in jail, and won reelection while imprisoned. His reelection made the law's political problem impossible to ignore: voters could defy repression at the ballot box.

Republican-led legislatures answered with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions. Madison's Virginia Resolutions argued that states should "interpose" against unconstitutional federal acts. Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions went further toward nullification language. The resistance defended liberty and also created a new constitutional problem: if states could judge federal law, what held the Union together?

Analytical significance

Sedition made the lecture's question literal. A republic that fears disloyalty must decide whether criticism itself can become criminal, and whether voters may still reverse that decision.

VIII. 1800: Opposition Becomes Government

By the campaign of 1800 each party had a coherent nightmare. Federalists warned that Jefferson meant French-style radicalism, irreligion, and social disorder. Republicans warned that Federalists meant monarchy, standing armies, heavy taxes, and Sedition. Each side believed defeat could destroy the republic.

Under the original Constitution, electors did not vote separately for president and vice president. Jefferson and his running mate Aaron Burr each received 73 electoral votes, tying for the presidency. The contest went to the House of Representatives and produced weeks of deadlock before Jefferson prevailed. The Constitution had not been designed for parties, and the tie exposed that gap.

On March 4, 1801, Jefferson took the oath and Adams left office. In his First Inaugural Jefferson said, "We are all republicans: we are all federalists." The line did not erase ideological conflict. The durable precedent was narrower and more important: an opposition party could defeat the government, take power, and remain inside the constitutional order. Loyal opposition became practical before it became comfortable theory.

Closing Synthesis

What kind of republic did the Revolution create? By 1801 the answer was unsettled but newly constrained by practice. It was a republic capable of funding debt, chartering a bank under a broad reading of "necessary and proper," and fighting over whether that capacity preserved independence or corrupted liberty. It was a republic whose foreign crises turned domestic disagreement into loyalty tests. It was a republic that criminalized certain criticism under Sedition and then watched voters and an electoral transfer refuse to let emergency politics become permanent.

Competing visions produced parties. Parties learned, painfully, that they could transfer power. That achievement did not end conflict over expansion, slavery, empire, or war. It did establish that losing office need not mean losing the republic. Lecture 3 begins where that political settlement meets continental ambition: who belongs in an expanding republic, and who pays the price?

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Joanne B. Freeman, Affairs of Honor (2001). Reconstructs the unfamiliar political culture of rumor, honor, and partisan fear before Americans treated parties as normal.

James Roger Sharp, American Politics in the Early Republic (1993). Clear account of why persistent opposition was so hard to accept and how the 1790s crisis produced the first party system.

Jeffrey L. Pasley, The Tyranny of Printers (2001). Shows newspapers as active organizers of party politics rather than mere commentators.

Gordon S. Wood, Empire of Liberty (2009). Broad synthesis of the early republic useful for placing public credit, parties, and 1800 in a longer story of democratic transformation.

Stanley Elkins and Eric McKitrick, The Age of Federalism (1993). Dense but still essential narrative of Federalist statecraft, diplomacy, and the unraveling of Federalist political authority.

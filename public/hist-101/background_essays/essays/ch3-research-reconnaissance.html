Audit disposition. The four detailed lecture questions are substantively strong, but the chapter does not yet possess one operationally coherent upstream sequence. The four outlines, the four revised professor essays, and the published three-lecture schedule represent different architectures. This document preserves the strong work, records the necessary alignment adjustment, revises only the Pueblo Revolt essay, and identifies the authoritative essay copies.

1. Governing standards and audit scope

This audit follows the current HIST 101 Y1 Canonical Course Production Protocol v1.1 and its addendum. Research precedes pedagogical judgment and presentation form; the textbook is a student baseline rather than a ceiling; no standard formula, word count, section quota, or historiographical quota is imposed. The audit threshold is substantive: consequential accuracy, historiography, causation, context, interpretive balance, primary-source depth, narrative opportunity, and the Five Historical-Thinking Moves. Minor style and citation-format differences are not grounds for reopening an internal professor-preparation essay.

The assigned textbook chapter was read once as a whole. The four outline documents and four revised professor essays were then read individually. Existing decks, notebooks, assignments, and schedules were consulted only to understand intended emphasis and operational fit; they were not audited or changed.

2. Textbook baseline

Chapter 3 gives students a broad 1670–1720 chronology. It opens with the violent crises of the 1670s and 1680s—Metacom’s War, the Pueblo Revolt, Virginia’s Indian war and Bacon’s Rebellion, and pressure on New France—then contrasts Spanish and French negotiated empires with England’s Atlantic and settlement empires. It covers New Mexico after revolt, the French ‘middle ground,’ Canada, Illinois, Louisiana, Texas, mercantilism and the Navigation Acts, the Dominion of New England, the Glorious Revolution in England and America, Salem, household settlement, Queen Anne’s War, the Yamasee War, and the persistence of a Native-controlled interior.

The reading already carries the major names, chronology, institutional vocabulary, and comparative imperial frame. Its review questions emphasize simultaneous violence, negotiated alliance, the Glorious Revolution, and household expansion; its critical-thinking questions ask students to confront the power/liberty paradox, changing racism, and comparative imperial resilience. Lecture should therefore add human-scale narrative, Native political plurality, contested evidence, multicausal explanation, and explicit comparisons rather than retell the sections.

Two textbook claims need caution in teaching. First, the Pueblo Revolt killed roughly four hundred Spaniards—including twenty-one Franciscan missionaries—and expelled approximately two thousand survivors, not roughly two hundred of a population of one thousand. Second, the familiar story that horses entered Indigenous Plains networks only after the 1680 revolt is no longer tenable. The revolt enlarged Pueblo control of horse herds and accelerated existing networks; it did not create Indigenous horse culture from nothing.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

War as a crisis of kinship, jurisdiction, and Indigenous politics

The strongest current framing of Metacom’s War does not reduce the conflict to colonists versus Indians or to a single land grievance. Kin networks, Native towns, Christian communities, contested jurisdiction, diplomacy, food systems, and divergent Indigenous strategies mattered. Weetamoo’s political and military role and the unstable position of praying towns give the lecture human actors without turning the war into a simple morality play. The revised Metacom essay already embodies this approach.

Current scholarship: Lisa Brooks, Our Beloved Kin.

Narrative reconstruction: James D. Rice, Tales from a Revolution.

The Pueblo Revolt: coordinated sovereignty, coerced evidence, and horses

The revolt’s causes remain multicausal: suppression of ceremonial life, labor and tribute demands, food insecurity, drought, raiding, and colonial jurisdiction interacted. Spanish interrogations of Josephe, Lucas, and Pedro Naranjo are indispensable but coerced and translated evidence; they reveal Pueblo reasoning through a record shaped by Spanish theological expectations. Archaeology and material culture help recover community action, regional variation, rebuilding, and the limits of a single-leader story.

Foundational narrative: Andrew L. Knaut, The Pueblo Revolt of 1680.

Religion, gender, and power: Ramón A. Gutiérrez, When Jesus Came, the Corn Mothers Went Away.

Archaeological scholarship: Robert W. Preucel, ed., Archaeologies of the Pueblo Revolt.

The most consequential new finding is the 2023 Science study combining archaeological, genomic, isotopic, radiocarbon, and Indigenous-knowledge evidence. It places horses in Indigenous networks of the central Plains and northern Rockies during the first half of the seventeenth century. The Pueblo Revolt remains important because it placed additional herds beyond Spanish control and accelerated exchange; it is no longer defensible as the singular origin of Native horse dispersal.

Current interdisciplinary study: William Timothy Treal Taylor et al., “Early Dispersal of Domestic Horses into the Great Plains and Northern Rockies”.

Bacon’s Rebellion and the construction of racial slavery

The Jamestown outline is strongest when it keeps several processes in view at once: elite faction, franchise restriction, frontier security, land hunger, Native dispossession, class grievance, and the political economy of labor. Bacon’s language against Indigenous peoples is not incidental to the rebellion’s popular appeal. Nor should the aftermath be turned into a single-event origin story for racial slavery. The rebellion belongs within a longer legal, demographic, imperial, and gendered consolidation of hereditary racial bondage.

Documentary context and primary sources: Bacon’s Rebellion documentary overview (Encyclopedia Virginia).

Recent scholarship on slavery, gender, and political economy: Jennifer L. Morgan, Reckoning with Slavery.

Legal-historical reassessment: Holly Brewer, “Creating a Common Law of Slavery for England and its New World Empire”.

Negotiated empires and the Native-controlled interior

The comparative-imperial outline is well founded. Spain and France did not simply choose ‘soft’ strategies while Britain chose a ‘hard’ one; demography, geography, commerce, missionary institutions, household settlement, and Native military and diplomatic leverage structured the available relationships. The French middle ground names a historically contingent practice of accommodation and mutual invention, not an atmosphere of permanent harmony. Newer continent-scale work strengthens the conclusion that European empires depended on Native power well into the eighteenth century.

Foundational framework: Richard White, The Middle Ground.

Borderlands context: David J. Weber, The Spanish Frontier in North America.

Continental political history: Michael Witgen, Seeing Red.

Information and diplomacy: Alejandra Dubcovsky, Talking Back.

Glorious Revolution, Protestant empire, and Salem

The constitutional outline properly links metropolitan and colonial events without treating 1688 as a clean democratic revolution. The Dominion of New England, colonial uprisings, the English Bill of Rights, confessional politics, war with France, and new imperial administration belong to the same story. Salem is strongest as an evidentiary and causal problem shaped by war trauma, political uncertainty, local conflict, gender, religious fear, and changing legal rules. Mary Beth Norton’s war thesis is illuminating but should be tested against other causes, exactly as the revised essay does.

Protestant imperial formation: Carla Gardina Pestana, Protestant Empire.

Post-1688 empire: Owen Stanwood, The Empire Reformed.

Salem synthesis: Emerson W. Baker, A Storm of Witchcraft.

Captivity, conversion, and belonging

Eunice Williams/Kanenstenhawi and Esther Wheelwright should not be collapsed into one conversion story. Their trajectories allow comparison among forced capture, childhood, kinship, adoption, religious vocation, community belonging, gender, and later colonial memory. The strongest evidentiary discipline is to separate adult behavior from inaccessible interior belief. This material is an excellent chapter narrative, but its place in the operational lecture sequence must be deliberately chosen.

Paired-biography scholarship: Ann M. Little, The Many Captivities of Esther Wheelwright.

Primary narrative and colonial memory: John Williams, The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion (Library of Congress record).

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

Verdict: PASS WITH SUBSTANTIVE ADJUSTMENTS

As an intellectual architecture, the four outlines are strong. They complement rather than duplicate the textbook; ask questions worth live lecture time; move coherently from simultaneous continental violence to Bacon’s Rebellion and racial formation, comparative imperial power, and constitutional empire; provide viable narrative engines; and distribute Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence without converting the Five Moves into a checklist. The slavery workshop carries extended analysis that Lecture 2 should frame but not pre-solve.

The substantive adjustment is operational alignment. Before downstream deck production, the course must designate one canonical Chapter 3 sequence and make the roadmap, professor essays, and published schedule agree. If the four detailed outlines remain authoritative, the scholarly foundation needs aligned preparation for Bacon’s Rebellion, comparative empires, and the Dominion/Glorious Revolution lecture, while Salem can be retained within the final lecture. If the revised ‘Frightened Continent’ essay sequence is adopted instead, the conflicting outlines and three-block agenda must be formally reconciled. Do not mix labels from one sequence with the research foundation of the other.

Because no standalone roadmap document exists, no roadmap file was altered. Per the audit instruction, the approved adjustment is recorded here and in the completion report. The four outline files themselves were preserved unchanged.

6. Individual professor-background essay audits

Essay 1 — Metacom’s War

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: chronology, naming, Native political plurality, praying towns, the Great Swamp, Native allies, Weetamoo, slavery, and the war’s human and political consequences are sound. Historiography: Richter, Lepore, and Brooks provide an established and current foundation; fresh research reinforces rather than displaces the interpretation. Primary sources: colonial providential accounts, diplomacy, and the fate and display of Metacom’s remains create evidentiary tension. Balance and context: the essay integrates land, jurisdiction, kinship, religion, security, alliance, and contingency without false equivalence. Narrative engine: Metacom’s head displayed at Plymouth for roughly two decades, with Weetamoo’s fate and the instability of Native alliances as counterpoints. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Essay 2 — Pueblo Revolt

Verdict: REVISE SUBSTANTIVELY — COMPLETED AND LOCKED

Accuracy: the revolt’s casualty and expulsion scale required correction to roughly four hundred Spaniards killed, including twenty-one missionaries, and approximately two thousand survivors driven south. Historiography: the older singular horse-dispersal story was materially outdated by the 2023 interdisciplinary study. Primary sources: Josephe, Lucas, Naranjo, and Otermín remain exceptionally valuable precisely because they require attention to coercion, translation, and colonial theological framing. Balance and context: the revised essay now holds ceremonial suppression together with labor, tribute, drought, food insecurity, raiding, and jurisdiction, and it distinguishes coordinated Pueblo action from a simple Popé-centered command story. Narrative engine: the coordinated August 1680 uprising and the interrogations that followed. Natural Five Moves: all five.

Substantive changes applied in place to the authoritative Google Doc: corrected the casualty paragraph; recast the horse section so 1680 accelerates pre-existing Indigenous networks rather than originating them; preserved the revolt’s continental consequence and Native agency; refined the reconquest compression; added the Taylor study; and supplied clickable links for every web-accessible work in the suggested-reading apparatus. The revised document was re-read after editing and its link structure verified.

Essay 3 — Salem

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: Mercy Lewis, George Burroughs, spectral evidence, political transition, and the chronology of accusations and trials are handled responsibly. Historiography: the essay uses Mary Beth Norton’s war-and-frontier thesis as a proposition to test rather than a master explanation and integrates local, gendered, religious, legal, and political interpretations. Primary sources: depositions, examinations, petitions, and execution narratives expose fear, procedure, and evidentiary change. Balance and context: multicausal without dissolving accountability or uncertainty. Narrative engine: the accusation and execution of Burroughs, or Mercy Lewis carrying frontier trauma into Salem. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Context, Comparison, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Essay 4 — Captives

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the paired biographies of Eunice Williams/Kanenstenhawi and Esther Wheelwright are carefully distinguished. Historiography: recent biographical work is already integrated, and the essay correctly avoids treating ‘captivity’ as a stable identity or assuming that behavior reveals private belief. Primary sources: John Williams’s redemption narrative, material objects, later visits, family records, and institutional evidence expose both colonial memory and archival silence. Balance and context: coercion, adoption, kinship, conversion, gender, and adult belonging remain in productive tension. Narrative engine: Eunice returning to Deerfield as an adult Kanien’kehá:ka woman, or the visual comparison between her later life and her father’s published story. Natural Five Moves: Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

7. Primary-source shortlist

These sources offer contemporary reasoning, decision-making, disputed evidence, or human experience that summary alone cannot supply:

Pueblo Revolt interrogations and reconquest record: Hackett and Shelby, Revolt of the Pueblo Indians of New Mexico and Otermín’s Attempted Reconquest.

Spanish official report written during the 1680 retreat: Antonio de Otermín’s 1680 report (Library of Congress).

Bacon’s public indictment of Berkeley and its anti-Indigenous politics: Nathaniel Bacon’s Declaration in the Name of the People (1676).

A law exposing the rebellion’s franchise politics: Virginia act restoring the freemen’s franchise (1676).

Metropolitan judgment after the rebellion: Royal proclamation concerning Bacon’s Rebellion (1677).

A constitutional statement useful for testing the limits of 1688: English Bill of Rights (1689), Avalon Project.

Searchable Salem court records and transcriptions: Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive and Transcription Project.

A colonial redemption narrative that made captivity into public memory: John Williams, The Redeemed Captive Returning to Zion (Library of Congress record).

Objects and community history complicating that printed redemption story: Kanenstenhawi/Eunice Williams and the Arosen objects (Historic Deerfield).

A museum interpretation centered on Native continuity: Historic Deerfield’s permanent exhibit on Native survival and continuity.

8. Narrative opportunities by existing outline

Outline 1 — Why Did America Explode? Open with three dates and one mystery—1675 in New England, 1676 in Virginia, 1680 in New Mexico—then follow the different coalitions and political choices that turned local crises into simultaneous continental war. Metacom’s displayed head and Pueblo coordination provide unforgettable human anchors without making the wars equivalent.

Outline 2 — Jamestown in Flames. Use Bacon’s Declaration as an unstable document: it voices popular grievance, elite rivalry, frontier fear, and genocidal policy at once. Jamestown burning is the reversal; the post-rebellion construction of racial slavery is the consequence to investigate rather than assume.

Outline 3 — Why Couldn’t Europeans Just Take Over? Let one map do argumentative work: European colors hug waterways while Native nations control the interior. Follow a French governor acting as Onontio, then test where accommodation ends. The lecture can make comparison visible without turning empires into personality types.

Outline 4 — Could the King Just Do Whatever He Wanted? Open with Andros under arrest in Boston as news of the Glorious Revolution crosses the Atlantic. The English Bill of Rights, colonial uprisings, imperial war, and Salem then become evidence for a central dilemma: limiting royal power could coexist with expanding imperial power.

9. Downstream issues to remember — do not implement in this audit

Reveal lecture decks and presenter notes / Study Buddy: first resolve which four-lecture sequence is canonical. Then correct every Pueblo casualty figure and every claim that the 1680 revolt originated Native horse dispersal. Preserve the revised formulation: additional herds accelerated networks already operating.

Historian’s Notebook and Think Like a Historian practice: ensure that evidence questions explicitly address coercion and translation in the Pueblo interrogations, perspective in Bacon’s Declaration, the legal status of spectral evidence, and the difference between colonial narrative and adult behavior in the captivity materials.

Chapter assignments: keep Slavery in Colonial America as the major assignment if the current course sequence stands. Lecture 2 should establish Bacon’s Rebellion, dispossession, labor systems, and legal change without duplicating or pre-answering the workshop. Clarify whether Witch Hunt remains an online activity rather than a second major assignment.

Exam materials: avoid items that reproduce the textbook’s lower Pueblo casualty figure; treat horse dispersal as pre-1680 Indigenous networking accelerated by the revolt; do not imply that Bacon’s Rebellion single-handedly caused racial slavery, that the French middle ground was harmony, or that 1688 made the empire democratic.

Scheduling and file authority: the working agenda currently allows three lectures plus the major-assignment workshop, while the detailed roadmap and essay set each contain four lectures but do not correspond to one another. Resolve this before deck revision, and archive or relabel superseded planning files only after that choice.

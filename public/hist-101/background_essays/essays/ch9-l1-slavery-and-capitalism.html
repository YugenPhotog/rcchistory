HIST 101 • CHAPTER 9 • THE OLD SOUTH, 1790–1850

Slavery and Capitalism

Professor Background Essay • Lecture 1

Central historical question
How did a brief late-eighteenth-century moment of uncertainty about slavery’s future give way to the massive expansion and intensification of bondage that powered industrial capitalism on both sides of the Atlantic?

Research status: generated from Reveal deck and expanded with scholarship on the Second Slavery, the cotton economy, and the New History of Capitalism

Grounded in the Chapter 9 lecture materials and expanded with primary evidence and modern scholarship on manumission, the Haitian Revolution, the cotton gin, Dale Tomich’s “Second Slavery,” the Atlantic cotton web, financial instruments, and the Mississippi River system.

Research Orientation

The Reveal deck presents a clear narrative arc: the Upper South experienced a genuine but fragile period of economic uncertainty and voluntary manumission in the 1790s; the Haitian Revolution and Gabriel’s Rebellion closed that window; the cotton gin and British industrial demand then produced the “Second Slavery”—an expansion and intensification of bondage in the U.S. South, Cuba, and Brazil precisely while slavery was being abolished elsewhere; and this system was fully integrated into Northern free-labor industrialization, British textile manufacturing, modern finance, and riverine infrastructure. The result was a global cotton web in which enslaved labor was not a pre-modern relic but a modern, profitable, and central engine of capitalism.

The most productive scholarly framing is Dale Tomich’s concept of the Second Slavery. It rejects the older assumption that slavery was declining or becoming obsolete in the nineteenth century. Instead, slavery modernized and expanded under the pressure of industrial demand for cotton and sugar. Sven Beckert’s Empire of Cotton supplies the global commodity history; Walter Johnson’s River of Dark Dreams shows how the Mississippi system turned both cotton and people into circulating capital; Edward Baptist’s The Half Has Never Been Told emphasizes the domestic slave trade and the intensification of labor (though his productivity claims remain contested). Ira Berlin’s generational framework helps distinguish the Upper South manumission moment from the later Cotton Kingdom. Counterpoints from economic historians such as Gavin Wright caution against overstating cotton’s contribution to overall U.S. growth while still acknowledging slavery’s regional and institutional importance.

The resulting interpretation should not become a simple claim that “slavery built capitalism” without qualification, nor a denial that the cotton economy was deeply integrated with industrial and financial systems. The deck’s central insight holds: Northern industrialization and British factory production were predicated on Southern enslaved cotton. The free North / slave South binary obscures that interdependence. At the same time, the scale of the transformation was contingent—on technology, markets, political reaction to rebellion, and the willingness of enslavers to close the door on manumission once profitability returned.

The Peculiar Institution in Transition

By the 1780s the economic logic that had sustained slavery in the Chesapeake for more than a century was under severe strain. Tobacco depleted nitrogen rapidly and required constant movement onto virgin land. Prices fell under European competition while soil exhaustion reduced yields. Rice cultivation in the Lowcountry faced parallel difficulties. Planters shifted toward less labor-intensive crops—wheat, mixed farming, livestock—that did not require the same scale of gang labor. The question that emerged was practical: if the crops that justified large enslaved workforces were declining, did slavery still pay?

This was not abstract speculation. A Virginia planter in 1790 surveying exhausted fields confronted a concrete calculation. Wheat farms needed far fewer hands. Some enslavers concluded that maintaining large enslaved populations might cost more than it returned. The period therefore produced both economic diversification and a genuine, if limited, reconsideration of the future of bondage.

[Instructor note: Students often treat the late eighteenth century as a moment of inevitable decline for slavery. Emphasize that the decline was real in the Upper South but highly contingent and regionally limited. The Deep South saw almost no parallel movement.]

The late eighteenth century also witnessed the largest wave of voluntary manumissions in American history, concentrated in the Upper South. Three forces converged. Religious awakening—Quakers, some Methodists under Francis Asbury, and Baptists—produced moral opposition; Robert Carter III freed more than 450 people between 1791 and 1803. Revolutionary ideology created a rhetorical contradiction that some enslavers and enslaved people exploited. Economic calculation mattered as well: when slavery appeared less profitable, manumission could be cheaper than continued maintenance. Virginia’s 1782 law allowing private manumission without legislative approval removed a key barrier.

Virginia’s free Black population grew from roughly 2,000 in 1782 to nearly 13,000 by 1790 and more than 20,000 by 1800. In Delaware nearly three-quarters of Black people were free by 1810. Yet the movement was limited, fragile, and regionally specific. Manumissions often protected economic interests—gradual schedules, freedom only for those past prime working years, or requirements that freed people leave the state. The Deep South experienced virtually none of this trend. The moment was real; it was never secure.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Think-Pair-Share: If a Virginia planter in 1795 was shifting from tobacco to wheat and calculating that fifty enslaved people were more expensive to maintain than to free, what does that reveal about the relative weight of economic versus moral motives in the manumission wave?
The Conceptual Payoff: Slavery’s future in the 1790s was not predetermined. A combination of soil exhaustion, crop shifts, religious conviction, and Revolutionary language produced the largest voluntary manumission wave in American history—but only in the Upper South, and only while the economic rationale for large-scale gang labor remained weak. The institution was adaptable; it was not yet locked in.

Revolution and Reaction: Haiti and Gabriel’s Rebellion

Saint-Domingue was the richest colony in the world. It produced roughly 40 percent of the world’s sugar and 60 percent of its coffee. Nearly 500,000 enslaved people—about 90 percent of the population—lived under brutal conditions. In 1791 they launched a revolution that defeated French, Spanish, and British forces. On 1 January 1804 Haiti declared independence, the only successful large-scale slave revolution in history and the first Black republic in the Americas.

The Haitian Revolution proved that enslaved people could overthrow their enslavers and establish an independent state. White Americans absorbed the news as a warning, not an inspiration. — Contemporary American reaction, summarized from refugee accounts and official correspondence, 1790s–1800s

Thousands of white French colonists fled to the United States, especially to Louisiana and Charleston, carrying stories that white Americans internalized as evidence of what “another Haiti” would look like. Free people of color also migrated, bringing radical political ideas. Jefferson refused to recognize Haitian independence and imposed a trade embargo. His fears about slave rebellion shaped attitudes toward western expansion; control of the Mississippi and the Louisiana Purchase were in part efforts to limit Caribbean influence and contain potential insurrection.

[Instructor note: Jefferson’s refusal to recognize Haiti is often under-emphasized. It is a concrete example of how revolutionary ideology stopped at the color line. Use it to complicate simple narratives of the Founders’ antislavery credentials.]

Gabriel Prosser, an enslaved blacksmith in Richmond, Virginia, was literate, politically informed, and inspired by both the American and Haitian Revolutions. He organized a conspiracy involving hundreds of people recruited through artisan networks. The plan was military: seize Richmond, take Governor James Monroe hostage, and negotiate for freedom. Weapons were stockpiled; targets were identified. A violent thunderstorm and betrayal by two enslaved informants prevented execution. Gabriel and twenty-five others—twenty-six men in all—were hanged.

The conspiracy terrified Virginia’s enslavers precisely because it occurred during the supposed “softening” of slavery. Enslaved people were organized, politically sophisticated, had absorbed Revolutionary ideology and claimed it for themselves, and were willing to use violence. Combined with the Haitian example, the rebellion ended the tentative emancipation moment. Virginia tightened restrictions on free Black people and manumission; new laws required freed people to leave the state within a year. Southern states strengthened slave codes and patrol systems. Pro-slavery ideology began to shift from “necessary evil” toward “positive good.” Manumissions in Virginia dropped from thousands per year in the 1790s to hundreds by the 1810s.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Open discussion: Why did Gabriel’s Rebellion terrify enslavers more than earlier, smaller revolts? What does the timing—during a period of partial manumission and Revolutionary rhetoric—reveal about the limits of gradualism?
The Conceptual Payoff: Enslaved people did not wait for gradual, controlled emancipation. Haiti and Gabriel’s Rebellion demonstrated that they claimed Revolutionary principles for themselves and were prepared to fight. White reaction was to close the door: harsher laws, restricted manumission, and a hardened defense of slavery. The brief window of uncertainty closed not because slavery became less profitable, but because its victims refused to wait.

The Cotton Gin and the Emergence of the Second Slavery

The bottleneck in cotton was not cultivation but processing. Long-staple Sea Island cotton had silky fibers that separated easily from seeds, but it grew only in limited coastal zones. Short-staple cotton could grow across the Deep South, yet its seeds clung tenaciously to the fiber. One person could clean roughly one pound per day by hand—economically unviable for large-scale production. In 1793 Eli Whitney, a Yale graduate tutoring in Georgia, invented a machine that used wire teeth on a rotating cylinder to pull fiber through narrow slots while the seeds fell away. Productivity rose approximately fifty-fold: one person could now clean fifty pounds a day. Whitney patented the gin in 1794.

The results were explosive. U.S. cotton production rose from about 2 million pounds in 1791 to 40 million in 1801, 160 million by 1820, and 2.3 billion pounds by 1860. Cultivation spread across Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Texas. The Cotton Belt—or Black Belt, named for its dark fertile soils and the density of its enslaved population—became the richest agricultural region in the world. By 1840 cotton constituted roughly 60 percent of all U.S. exports; by 1860 the United States produced about 75 percent of the world’s cotton.

The Second Slavery refers to the expansion and intensification of slavery in the nineteenth century—in the U.S. South, Cuba, and Brazil—precisely during the period when slavery was being abolished elsewhere in the Atlantic world. — Summary of Dale Tomich’s concept, as framed in the lecture

Tomich’s concept challenges the assumption that slavery was a declining, pre-modern institution. In the nineteenth century, while Britain abolished the slave trade and then slavery itself, and while northern U.S. states completed gradual emancipation, slavery expanded and modernized in the U.S. South, Cuba, and Brazil. It was more brutal and intensive than earlier forms, driven by industrial capitalism’s demand for raw materials, highly profitable, technologically “modern,” and fully integrated into global financial markets. It created new patterns of forced migration—the domestic slave trade that moved hundreds of thousands of people from the Upper South to the Cotton South, breaking families and communities.

The paradox is central: slavery expanded and intensified because of industrial capitalism, not despite it. The cotton gin plus textile mills plus global markets produced unprecedented demand for enslaved labor. By 1860 the slave systems of the U.S. South, Cuba, and Brazil were generating enormous wealth and were central to the global economy. This reframes slavery not as a relic being swept away by progress, but as a modern, rational, capitalist institution that required violent abolition to end.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Document analysis: Look at the production numbers (2 million pounds in 1791; 2.3 billion in 1860). What does the scale of that increase imply about the demand for both land and enslaved labor? How does it challenge the idea that slavery was becoming obsolete?
The Conceptual Payoff: The cotton gin did not merely increase efficiency; it restructured an entire regional economy and locked in an expanding slave system at the exact moment when other Atlantic societies were abolishing bondage. The Second Slavery was modern, profitable, and more brutal—not a holdover from the past, but a product of industrial demand.

The Atlantic Cotton Web: Industry, Finance, and Infrastructure

The British Industrial Revolution began with textiles, and cotton textiles specifically. Mechanized spinning (the spinning jenny and later power looms) and the factory system created an insatiable demand for raw cotton. British imports of raw cotton rose from 2.5 million pounds in 1760 to 366 million pounds by 1837—a 146-fold increase. Employment in cotton manufacturing grew from roughly 7,900 workers in 1770 to 331,000 by 1833. Manchester and Liverpool reshaped the economy of Mississippi and Alabama.

In the United States the same pattern appeared in New England. The Lowell mills, built by the Boston Associates along the Merrimack River, employed thousands of young women from New England farms under a system promoted as an industrial utopia of boarding houses, moral improvement, and literary culture. The reality was twelve-to-fourteen-hour days, dangerous machinery, lint-filled air, and wages of two to four dollars a week. Those workers processed cotton picked by enslaved people. Northern “free labor” industrialization was predicated on Southern slavery; it could not have existed in the same form without the raw material slavery supplied. The free North / slave South binary obscures this deep economic complicity. Both regions formed one capitalist system.

[Instructor note: The New History of Capitalism scholarship (Beckert, Baptist, Johnson) emphasizes this interdependence. Some economic historians (Wright, Olmstead) argue that cotton’s contribution to overall U.S. growth has been overstated and that Northern industry had multiple drivers. Present the deck’s argument clearly, then flag the debate so students see that the claim is contested rather than settled.]

Plantation agriculture required enormous capital. A medium-sized cotton plantation in the 1830s–1850s could require $60,000–$115,000 (roughly $2–4 million in today’s dollars) for land, enslaved people, equipment, and buildings. Most planters lacked that cash and needed credit. Northern and British banks, cotton factors, and merchants advanced loans against future harvests. Enslaved people were used as collateral for mortgages—human beings treated as financial assets that could be leveraged, insured, and depreciated. Marine insurance protected cotton shipments; life insurance policies were written on individual enslaved people so that an owner could be compensated if a valuable worker died. Cotton futures were traded on commodity markets. Major financial houses—Barings Bank, Brown Brothers, and the Lehman brothers in Alabama—invested heavily in the cotton economy. Modern financial instruments did not grow up at a distance from slavery; they developed inside it.

Before railroads became extensive, rivers were the only practical way to move bulk goods. The Mississippi system and its tributaries offered more than 12,000 miles of navigable waterways connecting the interior to New Orleans and the Gulf. Transportation costs by river were twenty to fifty times cheaper than overland wagon rates, making plantation agriculture in the interior possible. Steamboats carried both cotton bales and enslaved people. New Orleans was the entrepôt of the Cotton Kingdom—the place where American cotton met global markets. International merchants, brokers, and bankers kept offices near the levee. The same city contained the largest slave markets in North America. Auction houses and “slave pens” operated as rationalized businesses: systematic inspection, pricing, and marketing of human beings. The physical infrastructure of “internal improvements” was built in large part to serve the slave economy.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Think-Pair-Share: If Northern mills, British factories, New York banks, and New Orleans slave markets all depended on the same enslaved labor force, what does that do to the claim that the Civil War was simply a conflict between a free, industrial North and a slave, agricultural South?
The Conceptual Payoff: The Atlantic cotton web was a single integrated system. Northern industrialization, British factory production, financial innovation, and the Mississippi River infrastructure all rested on enslaved labor in the Cotton South. Slavery was not an obstacle to modern capitalism; it was one of its foundational engines.

Cotton, Capital, and the Making of Modern Capitalism

The wealth generated by the slave-cotton economy was staggering. By 1860 enslaved people constituted the single largest category of private wealth in the United States—approximately four million people valued at an average of roughly $800 each, for a total of about $3.2 billion (common estimates range from $3 billion to $3.5 billion). That figure exceeded the combined value of all U.S. railroads (~$1.8 billion), manufacturing capital (~$1 billion), and bank capital (~$400 million). Enslaved people represented more wealth than all railroads, factories, and banks combined. That capital accumulation helped fund subsequent industrial expansion and created templates for mass production, global supply chains, and financialization.

World-systems analysis, developed by Immanuel Wallerstein, provides one framework for understanding the resulting structure. Core regions (Britain, the northern United States) dominate through capital and technology; periphery regions supply raw materials and cheap or coerced labor. In this reading the U.S. South functioned as a periphery: coerced labor and resource extraction feeding industrial cores. The interdependence was real, but so were the power asymmetries. These structural relationships did not vanish in 1865. Sharecropping, convict leasing, and later forms of economic subordination in the South, as well as broader patterns of raw-material extraction from the Global South, followed similar logics of core-periphery extraction. Marxist and left-leaning historians emphasize historical injustice and continuing structures of domination. Liberal-institutional economists counter that differences in institutions, governance, human capital, and innovation better explain divergent outcomes, pointing to the mobility of former peripheries such as South Korea and Taiwan.

▶ DISCUSSION PROMPT — Open discussion: Wallerstein’s core-periphery model treats the cotton South as a periphery supplying the industrial core. Does that framing help explain why emancipation did not immediately produce economic equality in the South, or does it understate the agency and internal dynamics of Southern society?
The Conceptual Payoff: Slavery generated more private wealth than the nation’s railroads, factories, and banks combined and supplied the raw material that powered industrial cores on both sides of the Atlantic. The patterns of extraction and capital accumulation it established were not erased by emancipation; they were adapted. Understanding the Second Slavery is therefore essential to understanding both the origins of modern capitalism and the durability of its inequalities.

Interpretive Synthesis: Slavery as the Engine, Not the Obstacle

Between 1790 and 1850 the institution of slavery in the United States moved from a period of genuine uncertainty to a position of unprecedented economic centrality. Soil exhaustion and crop shifts in the Upper South, combined with religious and Revolutionary pressures, produced the largest wave of voluntary manumission in American history. That window closed when enslaved people themselves—through the Haitian Revolution and Gabriel’s Rebellion—demonstrated that they would not wait for gradual, controlled emancipation. White reaction hardened the institution just as a technological innovation and an industrial transformation made it more profitable than ever.

The cotton gin solved the processing bottleneck for short-staple cotton; British and Northern textile mills created insatiable demand; financial markets turned enslaved people into collateral and insured assets; and the Mississippi River system moved both cotton and human beings to global markets. The result was the Second Slavery: an expansion and intensification of bondage in the U.S. South, Cuba, and Brazil precisely while slavery was being abolished elsewhere. Northern free-labor industrialization and British industrial capitalism were not alternatives to this system; they were predicated upon it. The wealth locked in enslaved bodies exceeded the combined value of the nation’s industrial and transportation capital.

The structures built by this slavery-capitalism complex did not disappear in 1865. They were transformed and, in important respects, reproduced. The open analytical question that remains is whether the centrality of enslaved labor to nineteenth-century industrial capitalism means that modern capitalism is inseparably bound to histories of coercion, or whether subsequent institutional and technological changes have rendered that earlier dependence contingent rather than constitutive. The evidence of the cotton web forces the question; it does not by itself settle the answer.

Deep-Dive Research Assessment

Historical accuracy: The essay preserves the deck’s central chronology and causal claims while noting the major historiographical debate over the scale of cotton’s contribution to U.S. growth. Manumission numbers, Haitian and Gabriel timelines, cotton production statistics, and the financial/infrastructural integration are standard and well-supported.

Historiographical balance: The interpretation follows the New History of Capitalism framing that structures the deck (Beckert, Baptist, Johnson, Tomich) while explicitly flagging counter-arguments from economic historians (Wright and others). It avoids both triumphalist “slavery built everything” rhetoric and the older view of slavery as a pre-modern obstacle to capitalism.

Narrative potential: Strong teachable scenes include the exhausted tobacco fields and manumission deeds of the 1790s, refugee accounts from Saint-Domingue, Gabriel’s forge and the storm that betrayed the conspiracy, Whitney’s gin, the Lowell mill floor processing slave cotton, a life-insurance policy on an enslaved worker, and the New Orleans levee and slave market. These carry the analytical argument without requiring a seminar on world-systems theory.

Historical-thinking spine: Context explains why the 1790s looked like a possible decline; Cause links rebellion, technology, and industrial demand; Consequence is visible in the Second Slavery and the Atlantic web; Evidence can be modeled through production statistics, Tomich’s definition, and the financial instruments that treated people as assets. The open question at the end keeps the analysis from collapsing into a single moral verdict.

Bridging from Lecture 1

Five years before this revolt, Metacom’s War pushed New England to the edge of destruction and left English colonists questioning what their own conduct in the war had made of them. This lecture crosses the entire continent to a different empire, a different religion, and a different people — but the underlying claim is the same one Lecture 1 established: Native power, not European initiative, set the terms of contact in this period. Metacom’s War proved that claim in New England. The Pueblo Revolt proves it again, a thousand miles away, five years later — evidence that the pattern was continental, not regional.

Learning Objectives

  1. Explain the religious and material causes of the 1680 Pueblo Revolt, distinguishing the drought and violence that triggered open uprising from the generation of pressure that preceded it.
  2. Analyze the Pueblo declarations of Josephe, Lucas, and Pedro Naranjo as coerced interrogation testimony, evaluating what they reveal about Pueblo motives and what the circumstances of their extraction conceal.
  3. Evaluate the claim that the Pueblo Revolt was “the most successful Indigenous revolt in North American history,” including Popé’s later loss of authority and the Spanish reconquest of 1692–93.
  4. Trace how the revolt enlarged the supply of horses under Pueblo control and accelerated already-existing Indigenous horse networks across the Great Plains and Great Basin, previewing the continental argument this course builds toward.
  5. Connect the Pueblo Revolt to the continental argument organizing this lecture series: that Native power, not European initiative, set the terms of contact across North America in this period.

Section 1: Holy War in New Mexico

A Mission in Crisis

By the final decades of the seventeenth century, the Spanish missionary project in New Mexico was unraveling on every front at once. Franciscan zeal, once the engine of the colony’s religious ambitions, had slackened considerably; fewer priests bothered to master Pueblo languages, insisting instead that Pueblo people learn Spanish. Missionaries who had arrived convinced of their own benevolence increasingly treated Pueblo people as children and savage inferiors, whipping or shackling them for infractions that, from a Pueblo perspective, were no infractions at all. Disease compounded the damage: the Pueblo population had collapsed from roughly 80,000 in 1598 to only about 17,000 by 1679, a demographic catastrophe that hollowed out labor, kinship, and religious life all at once.

Material pressure and religious assault arrived together, not separately. A prolonged drought settled over New Mexico just as Apache and Navajo raiders intensified their attacks on Pueblo communities already weakened by disease and forced labor. For a people whose religious life was built around ensuring rain, fertility, and communal survival, watching Franciscan Christianity fail to prevent drought, disease, and raiding was not a minor disappointment. It was evidence. Many Pueblos concluded that abandoning Christianity and resuming their own forms of worship was not defiance for its own sake but a rational response to a religion that appeared, by its own fruits, to be failing them.

The Whipping of the Medicine Men

The Spanish response to this religious reassertion was brutal and specific. In 1675, colonial authorities whipped forty-seven Pueblo medicine men for the offense of sorcery and hanged three of them; a fourth took his own life rather than submit. Among those whipped was Popé, a medicine man from San Juan Pueblo. Rather than breaking him, the punishment radicalized him. Popé moved north to Taos Pueblo, outside the reach of the officials who had punished him, and began organizing what would become the most successful Indigenous revolt in North American history.

It is worth pausing on the specific charge: sorcery. The Spanish were not merely suppressing a labor dispute or a land conflict. They were prosecuting Pueblo religious practice itself as a criminal, demonic offense — which means that from the Spanish side as much as the Pueblo side, this was understood as a contest between two rival cosmologies, not merely a contest between two political powers. Whichever god’s rain came, whichever god’s crops grew, would settle the argument.

The Conceptual Payoff

Religious suppression was central, but the “holy war” interpretation is strongest when held alongside labor demands, tribute, food insecurity, drought, raiding, and colonial jurisdiction. Pueblo actors fought to restore ceremonial authority and communal survival; Spanish officials treated that restoration as both political rebellion and demonic error.

Discussion prompt

Open discussion: The Spanish charge against the medicine men was specifically sorcery, not rebellion or labor refusal. What does that specific charge tell you about how the Spanish themselves understood what was at stake in Pueblo religious practice?

Section 2: The Most Successful Revolt in American History

A Carefully Timed Uprising

In August 1680, Popé and Pueblo leaders across New Mexico executed a coordinated uprising involving communities separated by considerable distance — a level of inter-village coordination that itself refutes any characterization of the revolt as a spontaneous riot. Pueblo fighters killed roughly four hundred Spaniards, including twenty-one Franciscan missionaries, and drove approximately two thousand survivors south toward El Paso. They destroyed or raided Spanish structures across the province and desecrated the churches they could reach. The declaration recorded from the uprising is worth reading in full:

Primary source

Now the God of the Spaniards, who was their father, is dead, and Santa Maria, who was their mother, and the saints, who were pieces of rotten wood, [but] the Pueblos’ own god, whom they obeyed, [had] never died.

— Recorded Pueblo declaration during the 1680 revolt, in Charles W. Hackett, ed., Revolt of the Pueblo Indians of New Mexico (1942)

Spanish survivors fled Santa Fe down the Rio Grande to El Paso, and for the next twelve years, no Spanish colonial government existed in New Mexico at all — a fact easy to state briefly and easy to underestimate. Twelve years is not a raid or a temporary setback. It is roughly the length of an entire cycle of colonial administration, undone.

Popé’s Fall, and the Limits of a Single Leader

The revolt’s success did not translate into stable Pueblo unity, and Popé himself did not remain in a position of authority for long. When traditional Pueblo religious practices failed to end the drought or stop continued raids by hostile groups, his standing suffered directly — the same evidentiary logic that had turned Pueblos away from Christianity now turned some against Popé’s leadership. Many Pueblos also resisted his more extreme demands, which included annulling marriages the Franciscans had sanctioned and rejecting new crops the Spanish had introduced. A revolt organized around restoring Pueblo religious and political autonomy did not automatically produce agreement about what that autonomy should look like in practice.

The Conceptual Payoff

Success and unity are not the same thing. The revolt that expelled Spain from New Mexico for twelve years was also a revolt whose own leader fell from power within that same window — a reminder that a successful uprising against a common enemy does not automatically resolve the disagreements a people have among themselves about what comes next.

Section 3: Reading Coerced Testimony

Whose Words Are These, Really?

Nearly everything historians know about Pueblo motives in 1680 comes from a specific and compromised source: declarations extracted from captured Pueblo people by Spanish interrogators in the years immediately following the revolt, later published by Charles Hackett as Revolt of the Pueblo Indians of New Mexico and Otermín’s Attempted Reconquest, 1680–1682. The three most frequently cited declarations — from Josephe, a Spanish-speaking Indian; Lucas, a Piro Indian; and Pedro Naranjo of the Queres nation — are not free first-person testimony. They are answers given under interrogation by a colonial power actively working to understand, and eventually reverse, its own military defeat.

This matters for how we read even a single phrase. When Pedro Naranjo describes Popé receiving instructions from supernatural figures who “emit fire from all the extremities of their bodies,” it is genuinely unclear how much of that specific framing reflects Naranjo’s own understanding, how much reflects a translator’s choices, and how much reflects Spanish interrogators actively listening for confirmation of exactly the kind of “diabolical” conspiracy their own theology expected to find. None of this means the declarations are worthless. It means they must be read as a negotiation between two parties with very different stakes in the story being told, not as a transparent window into Pueblo belief.

What Survives Anyway

Even filtered through coercion, certain details in the declarations resist easy dismissal precisely because they cut against what a cooperative captive would be expected to say to please an interrogator. Naranjo’s declaration explains that the revolt’s leadership promised abundant harvests, health, and leisure to those who followed the old ways — in other words, a positive religious vision, not merely a coerced conspiracy. Lucas’s brief declaration insists he knows only fragments and does not understand the languages involved, a limitation an interrogator seeking a clean confession would have little reason to invent on a captive’s behalf. These details suggest the record, however compromised, still carries real information — it simply cannot be read as if it were uncomplicated first-person Pueblo testimony.

Discussion prompt

Quick write (1–2 min): The Pueblo declarations were given under interrogation by the very Spanish authorities the revolt had just defeated. In one sentence, what should that fact make you more cautious about believing at face value — and what, if anything, can you still trust in the record?

The Conceptual Payoff

A coerced source is not a worthless source, but it is never a transparent one. Reading these declarations well means asking not just what they say, but who was asking the questions, what answer they were listening for, and which details in the record work against, rather than for, the interrogator’s expectations.

Section 4: Horse Networks Before and After 1680

What the Fleeing Spanish Left Behind

When Spanish colonists fled New Mexico in 1680, Pueblo communities seized substantial horse herds and redistributed animals through trade and raiding. Older accounts often treated this as the moment horses first escaped Spanish control and spread onto the Plains. A 2023 interdisciplinary study led by William Timothy Treal Taylor — combining archaeology, genetics, and Indigenous knowledge — demonstrates that horses were already moving through Indigenous exchange networks and had become integrated into communities of the central Plains and northern Rockies during the first half of the seventeenth century. The revolt therefore enlarged the number of horses under Indigenous control and accelerated networks that were already operating; it did not create Indigenous horse culture from nothing.

By the time Spain reestablished its colonial presence in the 1690s, New Mexico’s borders were ringed by increasingly mobile Native societies. Apache, Ute, and ancestral Comanche communities expanded horse raising, exchange, hunting, and raiding on their own terms. Spain’s 1680 defeat contributed materially to that transformation, but the newer evidence prevents attributing a continent-wide shift to a single revolt.

The Conceptual Payoff

The revolt’s consequences did not remain confined to New Mexico. By transferring large herds into Pueblo hands, the Spanish defeat accelerated Indigenous-controlled exchange and mobility across networks already carrying horses far beyond colonial settlements. The larger lesson remains Native agency, but the mechanism is diffusion through Indigenous networks before as well as after 1680, not a single colonial “escape” event.

Discussion prompt

Predict (1–2 min): The Spanish are gone from New Mexico, and additional horses now belong to Pueblo rebels and their neighbors. Before we say what happened next, predict how already-existing Indigenous trade, kinship, and raiding networks might change the pace and direction of horse dispersal.

Closing Synthesis

The Pueblo Revolt resists a story in which European colonization proceeds steadily, interrupted only by occasional, ultimately unsuccessful Native resistance. For twelve full years, the opposite was true: no Spanish colonial government existed anywhere in New Mexico, and the peoples whose religious practice the Spanish had prosecuted as sorcery had removed the Spanish entirely from a territory Spain had claimed for eight decades. That the revolt’s own leader lost authority within that same window, and that Spain eventually returned and reconquered the province by force, does not undo this fact. It complicates it — which is exactly what good history should do.

Almost everything we know about why the Pueblos revolted survives only because Spanish interrogators extracted it from captured participants after their side had already lost the war and was trying to understand how. That the record is compromised does not make it silent. Even filtered through coercion and translation, the declarations preserve a religious vision — abundant harvests, restored health, a world free of Franciscan supervision — that a Spanish interrogator had little reason to invent on a captive’s behalf. Reading history well sometimes means working with exactly this kind of imperfect source, rather than waiting for a clean one that does not exist.

This lecture’s furthest-reaching consequence, though, is not the twelve years of Pueblo independence or even the coerced record of why it happened. It is the expansion of Indigenous control over the horse. New research shows that Native horse networks predated 1680, but the revolt placed additional herds beyond Spanish control and accelerated exchanges already transforming the Great Plains and Great Basin. That corrected sequence strengthens rather than weakens the continental argument: Native communities, through their own networks and choices, determined how rapidly horses moved and what political possibilities mounted life created.

Open analytical question

If Indigenous horse networks could expand beyond European control both before and after the Pueblo Revolt, what does that suggest about how much of ‘early American history’ happened outside the boundaries — and beyond the documentary vision — of any European colony?

Suggested Readings for Instructor Preparation

William Timothy Treal Taylor et al., “Early Dispersal of Domestic Horses into the Great Plains and Northern Rockies” (Science, 2023)

The interdisciplinary correction behind Section 4: archaeological, genomic, isotopic, radiocarbon, and Indigenous-knowledge evidence places horses in Indigenous networks of the central Plains and northern Rockies during the first half of the seventeenth century.

Andrew L. Knaut, The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 (1995)

The standard scholarly narrative of the revolt’s causes and course; essential grounding for Sections 1 and 2.

Ramón Gutiérrez, When Jesus Came, the Corn Mothers Went Away (1991)

Examines religion, gender, and power in colonial New Mexico; useful for the holy-war framing in Section 1 and for understanding Popé’s contested authority in Section 2.

Charles W. Hackett, ed., Revolt of the Pueblo Indians of New Mexico and Otermín’s Attempted Reconquest, 1680–1682 (1942)

The primary-source collection containing the Josephe, Lucas, and Naranjo declarations used throughout Section 3; essential for instructors who want to read the full interrogation record rather than the excerpted lines.

David J. Weber, The Spanish Frontier in North America (1992)

Broader borderlands context for Spain’s position in New Mexico before and after the revolt; useful background for the reconquest material routed to independent study.

Pekka Hämäläinen, The Comanche Empire (2008)

A major account of Comanche power on the southern Plains. Read it together with the 2023 Taylor et al. study: the revolt increased Indigenous access to horses, but it should no longer be treated as the origin point of Native horse dispersal.

Daniel K. Richter, Facing East from Indian Country (2001)

Provides the continental, Native-centered framing this lecture series depends on throughout; directly supports the Closing Synthesis’s argument.

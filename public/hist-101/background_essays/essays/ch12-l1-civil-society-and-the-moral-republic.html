Essential question

What holds a society together when there is no king, no established church, and only a small national government?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain how church disestablishment, the expansion of white male suffrage, the Second Great Awakening, and the market revolution together produced an age of voluntary organizing.
  2. Describe the “Benevolent Empire” of national Bible, tract, Sunday school, missionary, and temperance societies, and explain how it worked.
  3. Analyze Lyman Beecher’s argument that voluntary societies could do the moral work once assigned to an established church.
  4. Summarize Alexis de Tocqueville’s analysis of American associations in Democracy in America, including the “art of association” and the danger of the “tyranny of the majority.”
  5. Define civil society and mediating structures, and evaluate both the democratic promise and the exclusions of antebellum associational life.

Introductory Framing

The lecture opens with a deceptively simple question: what holds a society together when there is no king? Europeans of the early nineteenth century had a ready answer for their own countries. A monarch, a titled aristocracy, and an established church gave society its shape. They set the moral rules, ran the charities, and told ordinary people where they stood. The young United States had thrown off the king, never had a hereditary aristocracy, and, state by state, was cutting its churches loose from public support. Many observers on both sides of the Atlantic expected such a society to fly apart.

It did not, and the lecture’s answer is that Americans built something in the space where king and church used to be. They formed voluntary associations by the thousands: Bible societies, tract societies, Sunday schools, missionary boards, temperance societies, mutual-aid societies, lyceums, and debating clubs. Through them, ordinary people raised money, printed literature, held meetings, elected officers, and argued about how the country should live. The lecture names this world civil society and introduces its most famous interpreter, the French visitor Alexis de Tocqueville, who concluded that the American habit of association was one of the secrets of democratic survival.

Historians read this world in several ways. Daniel Walker Howe stresses the evangelical energy that drove it. Nathan Hatch shows how revivalism democratized American religion itself. Paul E. Johnson and Mary P. Ryan connect reform to the anxieties of a new commercial middle class. Nancy Cott and Lori Ginzberg show how women used benevolent work as a training ground for public life. Historians of Black communities show that free African Americans built their own churches and societies as instruments of survival and resistance. This essay follows the lecture’s civic framing while keeping both sides of the story in view: associations taught democratic habits, and associations also drew lines around who belonged.

Key Terms: Civil Society, Voluntary Association, and Mediating Structures

Three terms carry this lecture. A voluntary association is a group that people choose to join, rather than one they are born into or ordered into, in order to pursue a shared purpose. A Bible society, a temperance society, or a fire company counts; a family or a government agency does not. In the early republic, associations usually had a constitution, elected officers, dues, regular meetings, and often a printed annual report. Running one was a small exercise in self-government.

Civil society is the whole sphere of such voluntary life, the space between the private household and the state, and in most definitions also distinct from the market. It includes churches, reform societies, mutual-aid groups, newspapers and print networks, and clubs of every kind. The term is old, with roots in classical and Enlightenment political thought, but the lecture uses it in its modern sense: the realm where citizens deliberate and act together without being commanded to.

Mediating structures, a phrase the sociologists Peter L. Berger and Richard John Neuhaus popularized in their 1977 essay To Empower People, are the institutions that stand between the lone individual and the large institutions of modern life, especially the state. Family, church, neighborhood, and voluntary association are their main examples. Berger and Neuhaus were writing about the 1970s, not the 1830s, but their idea helps us see what antebellum Americans were building.

Setting the Scene: Connecticut, 1818

In 1818 Connecticut adopted a new state constitution, and with it ended the special legal privileges of the Congregational churches that had been the colony’s official religion since the seventeenth century. Taxpayers would no longer be required to support the Congregational “standing order.” For the Reverend Lyman Beecher, then pastor in Litchfield and one of the standing order’s fiercest defenders, the change felt like a catastrophe. He had fought it for years, warning that without public support religion and morality would decay.

Beecher later remembered the defeat very differently. In his autobiography, compiled from his recollections and letters and published after his death, he called disestablishment the best thing that ever happened to the state of Connecticut. Cut off from tax support, he explained, the churches had been thrown back on their own resources, and the result was more energy, not less. Ministers and laypeople had to persuade, organize, and raise money. They built voluntary societies to do what the state had once guaranteed.

Beecher’s change of heart captures the lecture’s first point. Massachusetts, the last state to keep an established church, ended public support in 1833. Across the country, religion became a voluntary enterprise. Churches had to compete for members, and the skills they developed in that competition (fundraising, preaching to crowds, printing, organizing local chapters) spilled over into every kind of reform.

Primary source (paraphrase)

Looking back, Beecher judged that losing tax support had cut the churches loose from dependence on the state and thrown them back on their own resources and on God, and that ministers now exerted more influence through voluntary societies, missions, and revivals than they ever had through the old marks of clerical status.

— Paraphrased from Lyman Beecher, Autobiography, Correspondence, Etc., ed. Charles Beecher (1864–1865)

The Forces Behind the Age of Reform

The lecture identifies four forces that came together after about 1815.

1) Disestablishment

As Connecticut and Massachusetts show, the separation of church and state did not weaken American religion. It turned churches into voluntary associations themselves and taught believers to organize. Methodists and Baptists, who had never enjoyed state support, grew fastest. Methodist circuit riders and Baptist farmer-preachers carried religion into the backcountry with very little money and a great deal of organization.

2) Democratic expansion

Between roughly 1810 and the 1830s, most states dropped property requirements for voting, and the electorate of white men expanded sharply. That expansion came at a cost the lecture asks students to notice: several states that widened white suffrage narrowed or eliminated Black voting at the same time, and women could not vote anywhere. Still, more Americans than ever believed public life belonged to them. Associations gave people practice in the arts of democracy, and gave those shut out of the ballot box a way to act publicly anyway.

3) The Second Great Awakening

The wave of revivals historians call the Second Great Awakening ran from about 1800 into the 1840s. It began with outdoor camp meetings on the frontier, most famously at Cane Ridge, Kentucky, in August 1801, where thousands gathered for days of preaching, singing, and emotional conversions. It reached a climax in the 1820s and early 1830s in upstate New York, along the path of the Erie Canal, a region so often swept by revivals that it became known as the “burned-over district.” There the Presbyterian evangelist Charles Grandison Finney used what he called “new measures,” such as protracted meetings and the “anxious bench” for people on the verge of conversion, to bring whole towns to their knees. His revival in Rochester in 1830–1831 became legendary.

The theology mattered as much as the emotion. Older Calvinism had stressed God’s sovereign choice of who would be saved. The new revivalism stressed human choice: sinners could and must decide to turn to God. Some revivalists went further and preached a kind of perfectionism, the belief that converted Christians could live free from willful sin. If individuals could change themselves, the reasoning ran, then communities and nations could be changed too.

4) The Market Revolution

The canals, steamboats, and expanding commerce studied in Chapters 10 and 11 created new wealth and new problems. Young men and women left farms for towns and cities, away from family supervision. Workshops turned into factories, and masters no longer lived with their journeymen. Cities filled with strangers, and poverty became more visible. In A Shopkeeper’s Millennium (1978), Paul E. Johnson argues that Rochester’s businessmen embraced Finney’s revival in part because it promised to restore order among the workers they no longer supervised at home. Historians have debated how far to push that argument, but the link between economic change and reform anxiety is widely accepted. The market revolution also created a middle class with the time, money, and literacy to join societies, buy pamphlets, and attend meetings.

Analytical significance

The age of reform did not appear from nowhere. Disestablishment made religion voluntary, democratic expansion made public life feel open, revivalism supplied moral urgency, and the market revolution supplied both new problems and new resources. Together they produced an extraordinary burst of voluntary organizing.

From Revival to Reform

For converts of the Second Great Awakening, salvation was not the end of the story. A converted person was expected to show it through a changed life and through work to improve the world. Revival preaching thus fed directly into reform. Reformers tended to see social problems such as drunkenness, Sabbath-breaking, prostitution, poverty, and eventually slavery not as technical policy questions but as moral struggles between good and evil. Many also believed that the republic itself depended on a virtuous citizenry. A people that could not govern its own passions, they warned, could not govern a nation.

This moral framing gave reform tremendous energy. It also made compromise difficult. If your opponent is not merely mistaken but sinful, negotiation can look like surrender. The lecture asks students to hold both sides: moral conviction moved ordinary people to act, and it sometimes made reformers intolerant of those who disagreed, especially Catholics, immigrants, and working-class people whose customs they wanted to change.

The lecture also notes that evangelical Protestantism, though dominant, was not the only source of reform energy. Quakers had long opposed slavery and supported prison reform. Unitarians such as William Ellery Channing and the Transcendentalists around Ralph Waldo Emerson pursued improvement through reason and self-culture rather than revival. Utopian communities, from Robert Owen’s New Harmony to Brook Farm, imagined remaking society from the ground up. Free Black communities organized for their own advancement on their own terms.

Reflection: What happens when social change is framed as a moral mission? When does that framing empower people, and when might it limit empathy or tolerance for disagreement?

The Benevolent Empire

Between about 1815 and 1830, evangelical Protestants built a set of national organizations so large and interconnected that historians call them the Benevolent Empire. The core societies included the American Education Society (1815), which helped pay for the training of ministers; the American Bible Society (1816), which aimed to put a Bible in every American household; the American Sunday School Union (1824); the American Tract Society (1825), which printed millions of short religious pamphlets; the American Home Missionary Society (1826); and the American Society for the Promotion of Temperance, better known as the American Temperance Society (1826). Many shared officers and donors, and several held their annual meetings in New York during the same week each May, a ritual that showed off their combined strength.

These societies were pioneers of mass communication. The American Bible Society and the American Tract Society adopted steam-powered presses and stereotype printing in the 1820s and turned out printed matter on a scale few American businesses could match. They built networks of local auxiliaries, often run by women, that raised money and distributed literature door to door. In the 1830s the Bible Society even launched “general supply” campaigns aimed at reaching every family in the country. Historians such as David Paul Nord treat these campaigns as an early form of national mass media.

Bible House

The lecture’s image of Bible House shows where this empire eventually led. In 1853 the American Bible Society moved into a new headquarters at Astor Place in Manhattan, a six-story building filling an entire block between Third and Fourth Avenues. It housed printing presses, binderies, offices, and storerooms, and it was one of the largest buildings in the city at the time. Other religious and reform societies rented space there. A building like that made a point: voluntary benevolence was no longer a few pious neighbors meeting in a parlor, but an organized national enterprise.

What the Associations Did

The societies spread religious and moral literature, trained speakers, organized lectures, and circulated petitions. A good example is the campaign of the late 1820s against Sunday mail delivery. Evangelicals flooded Congress with petitions asking it to stop the post office from moving and opening mail on the Sabbath. They lost: in 1829 and 1830, reports written for Senator Richard M. Johnson of Kentucky rejected the request as an improper mixing of religion and government. But the campaign taught reformers how to mobilize nationally, and abolitionists soon used the same petition techniques. The Benevolent Empire turned local reform into a national moral movement, and it gave new leadership roles to women and, in separate organizations, to free Black Americans.

Analytical significance

The Benevolent Empire did with voluntary donations, print, and organization what European states and churches did with taxes and law. It is the clearest early example of American civil society operating on a national scale, and it supplied the methods that later movements, including abolition and women’s rights, would turn to more radical ends.

Lyman Beecher’s Moral Militia

Lyman Beecher (1775–1863) was the most important theorist of this new system. Born in New Haven, trained at Yale under Timothy Dwight, he served churches on Long Island, in Litchfield, and in Boston before moving west in 1832 to lead Lane Theological Seminary in Cincinnati. He was also the father of a remarkable family: Harriet Beecher Stowe, Catharine Beecher, and Henry Ward Beecher were among his children.

In 1812 Beecher preached a sermon, published in 1813 as A Reformation of Morals Practicable and Indispensable, urging Christians to form local societies to suppress vice and enforce the laws against drunkenness, profanity, and Sabbath-breaking. Such societies, he argued, would form a “disciplined moral militia” ready to act whenever public morals were threatened, giving courage to timid citizens and intimidating wrongdoers. The image is revealing. Beecher did not imagine reform as gentle charity. He imagined it as a kind of citizen army, organized, disciplined, and mobilized to defend the moral order the old establishment could no longer guarantee.

Beecher’s later career showed both the reach and the limits of that vision. His Six Sermons on Intemperance (1826) helped launch the temperance crusade discussed in Lecture 3. His A Plea for the West (1835) urged Protestants to send ministers, teachers, and schools to the Mississippi Valley before Catholics won it, and its anti-Catholic tone came just after a Boston crowd burned the Ursuline convent in Charlestown, Massachusetts, in August 1834, a few days after Beecher had preached against Catholicism in the city. Many historians do not hold him directly responsible for the riot, but his career shows how the same moral militia could protect some neighbors and threaten others.

Choosing the Lens: Why “Civil Society”?

At this point the lecture steps back from story to analysis. Which kind of history best explains antebellum reform? Social history gets close to the everyday lives of the people involved, but it can lose the big picture. Cultural history illuminates symbols and meanings, but it does not fully explain how people organized collective action. Political history, focused on parties and governments, misses most of the action, because most reform rose from churches, parlors, and lecture halls rather than from legislatures. To see the whole, historians often borrow a concept from political philosophy and sociology: civil society.

The idea has a long history. Ancient and early modern writers used versions of it to describe political community in general. Enlightenment thinkers such as John Locke, David Hume, Adam Ferguson, and Adam Smith used it to describe a society of free individuals bound by law, commerce, and sympathy. In the twentieth century, the German philosopher Jürgen Habermas wrote about the “public sphere” of debate in coffeehouses, newspapers, and clubs, and the political scientist Robert Putnam studied “social capital,” the networks of trust that make cooperation possible. In the American context, though, the most influential analysis came from a young Frenchman who visited in 1831.

Tocqueville and the Art of Association

Alexis de Tocqueville (1805–1859) came from an aristocratic Norman family that had suffered in the French Revolution; several relatives died on the guillotine, and his parents were imprisoned. In 1831, as a junior magistrate, he and his friend Gustave de Beaumont obtained permission from the French government to study American prisons, then famous among European reformers. They arrived in New York in May 1831 and spent about nine months traveling through the Northeast, the Great Lakes, Canada, the Ohio and Mississippi valleys, New Orleans, and Washington. Their official report, On the Penitentiary System in the United States and Its Application in France, appeared in 1833. Lecture 2 returns to it.

Tocqueville’s real subject, though, was democracy itself. He believed that the advance of social equality was the great fact of modern history and that France, too, would eventually become democratic. He wanted to know how Americans had made equality compatible with liberty and order. The result was Democracy in America, published in two volumes in 1835 and 1840.

A Nation of Joiners

Tocqueville was struck by how readily Americans formed associations for every imaginable purpose. In the second volume he wrote one of his most quoted passages:

Primary source

“Americans of all ages, all conditions, and all dispositions constantly form associations.”

— Alexis de Tocqueville, Democracy in America, vol. 2 (1840), pt. 2, ch. 5, trans. Henry Reeve

He went on to list associations religious and moral, serious and trivial, very general and very limited, enormous and tiny, for founding seminaries, building inns, raising churches, distributing books, and sending missionaries. Where in France one would see the government leading a new undertaking, and in England a great lord, he observed, in the United States one would surely find an association. He mentioned with some amusement the temperance societies whose members pledged to abstain from strong drink, an example that anticipates Lecture 3.

The “Mother Science”

Why did this matter so much to him? Tocqueville’s answer was that equality makes each individual weak. In an aristocracy, a powerful lord could gather dependents and get things done. In a democracy, every citizen is independent, but no one is strong enough alone to accomplish much. Unless people learn to combine voluntarily, they become isolated and powerless, and they will look to a central government to do everything for them. For that reason, he wrote, in democratic countries the knowledge of how to combine is the “mother of science,” and the progress of every other kind of knowledge depends on it. The deck’s translation renders this as the “mother science” of democracy.

Associations, in Tocqueville’s view, were schools of democracy. In them citizens learned to speak in public, run meetings, manage money, compromise, and trust one another. He saw a close link between civil associations and political ones: people who learned to cooperate on small local projects gained the skills and confidence to act together on larger public questions.

Associations Against the Majority

Tocqueville was not a simple admirer of American democracy. In his first volume he warned of the tyranny of the majority, the danger that in a society where the majority rules everything, public opinion could silence dissent more effectively than any king. He wrote that he knew of no country with less independence of mind and real freedom of discussion than America. Associations were one of the defenses against this danger. By banding together, minorities could make their views heard, protect themselves, and resist the pressure to conform.

Individualism and the Soft Despotism

Tocqueville also coined a warning about what he called individualism, a calm and considered feeling that disposes each citizen to withdraw into a small circle of family and friends and leave the larger society to itself. Carried far enough, he feared, individualism could produce a new kind of despotism: not a cruel tyrant but an immense, mild, protective government that kept citizens comfortable and dependent, managing their lives for them. Associations, local self-government, and religion were among the forces that kept this from happening in America. The lecture’s Pierian Spring section, discussed below, builds on this part of his argument.

Analytical significance

Tocqueville gives the lecture its answer to the opening question. In a society of equals, no lord or established church holds people together. Voluntary association does: it turns private conviction into collective action, trains citizens in self-government, and protects minorities against both the majority and an all-powerful state.

Who Belonged? The Limits and Uses of Civil Society

The lecture repeatedly asks students to consider who was welcomed into civil society and who was excluded. The answer complicates Tocqueville’s portrait.

Women

Women could not vote or hold office, and under the common law of coverture, married women had few independent property rights. Yet women were the backbone of the benevolent societies. They organized female auxiliaries to Bible and tract societies, ran Sunday schools, founded orphan asylums and relief societies, and raised a large share of the money. Historians such as Nancy Cott, Anne Boylan, and Lori Ginzberg show that this work gave women experience in running meetings, keeping accounts, petitioning legislatures, and speaking for public causes. Some of that experience later flowed into abolition and women’s rights. The same ideology that told women their place was in the home also gave them a claim to moral authority that justified public action on behalf of homes and families.

Free Black Americans

Free African Americans were often excluded from white benevolent societies or welcomed only on unequal terms, so they built their own. In Philadelphia, Richard Allen and Absalom Jones founded the Free African Society in 1787, a mutual-aid association, and Allen went on to found the African Methodist Episcopal Church, organized as a denomination in 1816. Black communities in Northern cities formed benevolent societies, literary societies, schools, and, beginning in 1830, a series of national conventions that discussed education, emigration, and civil rights. These institutions offered insurance against sickness and death, leadership training, and a public voice. Historians stress that for Black Americans, civil society was not only a school of democracy but a means of self-protection and resistance in a country that denied them citizenship.

Catholics, Immigrants, and Workers

The Benevolent Empire was overwhelmingly Protestant, and much of its literature treated Catholicism as a threat to the republic. Catholic immigrants responded by building their own parishes, schools, and charitable institutions. Working men formed trade societies and mutual-benefit associations, and sometimes resented middle-class reformers who wanted to close their taverns and regulate their Sundays. Civil society, in short, was plural and contested. It was not one community but many, and the associations of one group could be aimed at the habits of another.

Reflect: Civil society empowered people who joined it, but access was not equal. How did excluded groups use associations to build power of their own?

Why Civil Society Matters

The lecture’s answer to its opening question is that a thriving civil society holds a free society together, and that it does so in three ways. First, it creates social cohesion, binding people together through shared purposes in a period of migration and rapid change. Second, it cultivates democratic habits: running a meeting, debating respectfully, voting on decisions, and handling money are skills that have to be learned somewhere. Third, it enables reform. Almost every major movement of the era, from temperance to abolition to women’s rights, began in voluntary associations long before it changed any law.

Voluntary associations, the lecture concludes, were both the engine of reform and the classroom of democracy. The remaining lectures in this chapter follow that engine into prisons, asylums, schools, saloons, and the lives of children, and they ask whether the reform it produced always served the people it targeted.

Pierian Spring: Mediating Structures and Democratic Life

The deck’s independent-study section carries the argument into political theory and the present. It introduces Peter Berger and Richard John Neuhaus’s concept of mediating structures: family, church, neighborhood, and voluntary association, the institutions that stand between the individual and the state. Berger and Neuhaus argued that these institutions protect people from alienation, give private values a public expression, and keep the state from becoming the only source of meaning and help in people’s lives. When they weaken, the argument goes, individuals feel isolated and powerless and the state expands to fill the gap. This is a modern version of Tocqueville’s fear of soft despotism, and Robert Putnam’s Bowling Alone (2000) offered a related, data-driven argument that American civic participation declined in the late twentieth century.

The section closes with a case study on welfare. Before the twentieth century, churches, ethnic benevolent societies, Black fraternal orders, and charities ran orphanages, hospitals, relief funds, and burial societies. After the expansion of federal social programs, especially in the 1930s and the Great Society of the 1960s, government became the main provider of aid. The deck presents one interpretation of that shift, associated with Charles Murray’s Losing Ground (1984) and Marvin Olasky’s The Tragedy of American Compassion (1992): that government aid displaced community responsibility and weakened civil society.

Students should recognize this as one side of a live debate rather than a settled finding. Other scholars argue that nineteenth-century private charity reached only some of the needy, often on harsh or discriminatory terms, and was overwhelmed by industrial depressions; that federal programs reduced poverty among the elderly and children; and that government and voluntary groups have often worked as partners, with public money funding private agencies. Theda Skocpol, for example, has argued that many large American voluntary federations historically grew alongside government rather than in opposition to it. The deck’s own closing prompt asks the right question: where might government help where civil society failed, and where might government weaken what civil society once strengthened?

Conclusion: A Republic Held Together by Joining

Return to Lyman Beecher in 1818, convinced that Connecticut had lost its moral anchor when it cut the Congregational churches loose. Within a generation he and thousands like him had built a vast network of voluntary societies that printed Bibles by the million, organized temperance pledges, and sent missionaries west. A young French aristocrat traveling through the country in 1831 concluded that this habit of association was one of the great secrets of American democracy.

Both men were partly right. Associations did hold a kingless society together, and they did teach ordinary people the arts of self-government. But they were not neutral. They carried the moral vision of the people who founded them, mostly middle-class Protestants, and they often aimed to change the habits of others. Women and free Black Americans used associations to claim a public voice the law denied them. Catholics, immigrants, and workers sometimes experienced the same associations as moral policing. The lectures that follow ask what happened when reformers moved from persuading people to building institutions designed to change them.

Analytical significance

Main argument: in the absence of king, aristocracy, and established church, antebellum Americans built civil society, a dense web of voluntary associations that created cohesion, trained citizens, and launched reform. Its democratic promise was real, and so were its boundaries.

Interpretive Synthesis: Associations and Democracy

Between the 1810s and 1840s, disestablishment, the widening of white male suffrage, the Second Great Awakening, and the market revolution produced an age of voluntary organizing. Evangelical Protestants built the Benevolent Empire, national societies for Bibles, tracts, Sunday schools, missions, and temperance, and pioneered mass printing and national petition campaigns. Lyman Beecher theorized these societies as a moral militia replacing the lost establishment. Alexis de Tocqueville, visiting in 1831–1832, interpreted the American habit of association as the key to democratic survival in a society of equals and as a defense against both majority tyranny and an overbearing state.

The open question is how to weigh civil society’s two faces. Was it chiefly a school of democracy, or chiefly an instrument by which one group sought to shape the conduct of others? The evidence from this lecture supports a both-and answer. The same organizational skills served women organizing benevolent societies, Black Philadelphians organizing mutual aid, and nativists organizing against Catholics. Civil society made American democracy more participatory; it did not make it more equal by itself.

Instructor Notes on Accuracy and Teaching

Historical accuracy: The essay follows the deck’s order while qualifying several points: the full “moral militia” sentence and Beecher’s “best thing that ever happened” remark are paraphrased because their exact wording varies across editions; Tocqueville quotations are given in Henry Reeve’s translation and should be cited by translation; Tocqueville and Beaumont’s assignment was prisons, but their book on democracy ranged far beyond it; the expansion of white male suffrage coincided with Black disfranchisement in several states; and the welfare case study is presented as one contested interpretation.

Historiographical balance: The interpretation follows the lecture’s civic framing (Tocqueville, Howe) while giving weight to social-control readings (Johnson, Ryan), women’s history (Cott, Ginzberg, Boylan), and Black institution-building (Newman, Rael). It treats civil society as both a school of democracy and a field of conflict.

Narrative potential: Strong teachable scenes include the Cane Ridge camp meeting, Beecher mourning and then celebrating Connecticut’s disestablishment, Finney’s anxious bench in Rochester, the steam presses of the American Bible Society and the opening of Bible House, the Sunday mails petitions, Tocqueville and Beaumont stepping off the ship in New York in 1831, and Richard Allen founding the AME Church.

Historical-thinking spine: Context explains disestablishment and the democratic and economic changes of the early republic; Cause links revivalism and market anxiety to reform; Consequence appears in the national societies and the methods later movements adopted; Evidence can be modeled through Beecher’s sermons, society reports, and Tocqueville’s text. The open question of democracy versus social control keeps the analysis from collapsing into celebration or cynicism.

Bridging from Lecture 2

The last two lectures traced fear directed outward: holy war in New Mexico and total war in New England. This lecture asks how frontier warfare may have shaped a crisis that turned inward against New England’s own neighbors. Salem is usually taught as a story about superstition. Following historian Mary Beth Norton and the assigned film Witch Hunt, the lecture treats Wabanaki war, refugee trauma, and colonial failure as powerful contexts—then tests how much explanatory weight those contexts can bear alongside local feuds, gender expectations, religious belief, and a legal system that admitted unverifiable evidence.

Instructor note

This lecture is designed to pair with the assigned online video Witch Hunt, which students watch before class. The video carries the narrative — the afflicted girls, Tituba, the trials, the executions, and crucially the frontier-war connections through Mercy Lewis and George Burroughs. The live lecture therefore does NOT re-tell that story. It analyzes it: it states Norton’s thesis openly and then tests whether the evidence the students already watched actually supports it. Learning Objectives

  1. Explain Mary Beth Norton’s argument that the Salem witchcraft crisis was closely connected to the Wabanaki (Indian) wars on the Maine frontier, rather than an isolated outbreak of village superstition.
  2. Evaluate the specific evidence for that thesis — including the biographies of accusers like Mercy Lewis and accused figures like George Burroughs — and assess how strong the connection actually is.
  3. Evaluate the interpretive proposition that military frustration may have helped redirect fear toward alleged enemies within New England, while distinguishing a plausible interpretation from a documented psychological mechanism.
  4. Distinguish a monocausal explanation of Salem (‘it was really about the frontier war’) from a multicausal one (frontier war plus local feuds, gender, religious anxiety, and a failing legal standard), and weigh which the evidence better supports.
  5. Connect Salem to the series’ continental argument while explaining that frontier war partly shaped a crisis also produced by refugee trauma, political failure, local feuds, gender expectations, religious belief, and an evidentiary system that admitted unverifiable claims.

Section 1: The Claim — Salem Was a Frontier-War Story

Stating the Thesis Up Front

This lecture does not build to a surprise. Unlike a mystery, it states its central claim at the outset and then spends the rest of the hour testing how well that claim holds. The interpretation associated most closely with historian Mary Beth Norton, and dramatized throughout the assigned film, is that the Salem witchcraft crisis of 1692 cannot be understood without the Wabanaki wars raging on the Maine frontier at the same time. Several key accusers and one especially important accused man had specific, documented connections to that violence. Those connections make frontier war an essential context, but not a complete explanation.

This matters because the standard story of Salem, the one most students arrive already believing, treats the crisis as an isolated eruption of small-town superstition: repressed Puritans, hysterical girls, a self-contained village panic that burned itself out. That version is not exactly wrong — superstition, local feuds, and Puritan theology were all genuinely present — but Norton’s argument insists it is radically incomplete. Remove the frontier war from the picture, and the timing, the geography, and the specific people involved stop making sense.

The War Students Have Already Seen

By 1692, the Massachusetts Bay Colony was in the middle of a losing war against the Wabanaki peoples on its northern frontier. The film opens students directly into this: the town of York, Maine, in flames, fifty dead and a hundred taken captive; the earlier Falmouth massacre; a decade-long cycle of raids that had devastated English settlements across Maine and New Hampshire and sent a stream of traumatized refugees south into the older, denser towns of the colony — including Salem. The Puritans understood this war in explicitly cosmic terms: North America was the Devil’s territory, the Wabanaki were understood as his agents, and the war was not merely a military struggle but a spiritual siege.

Video tie-in

Point students back to the film’s opening sequence — York in flames, the Falmouth attack — and to the recurring framing that the Puritans saw the frontier itself as the Devil’s territory. They have already seen the war this lecture argues is central. The lecture’s job is to make them see it as cause, not backdrop.

The Conceptual Payoff

The strongest challenge to the ‘Salem was just village superstition’ story is not a new fact — it is a matter of timing and geography. A colony fighting a losing, terrifying war on its northern border erupted in witchcraft accusations at the exact moment that war was going worst. Norton’s claim is that this is not a coincidence.

Section 2: The Evidence For It — Mercy Lewis and George Burroughs

Mercy Lewis: The Accuser as War Refugee

One of the strongest pieces of evidence for Norton’s thesis is biographical, and the film builds much of its structure around it. Mercy Lewis, one of the most active and influential accusers, experienced two distinct episodes of frontier violence. As a small child, she survived the 1676 destruction of Falmouth; grandparents and other relatives were killed, while Mercy and her parents survived and were displaced. A later frontier attack killed her parents in 1689, when she was about sixteen. Now an adolescent orphan, she eventually entered the Putnam household in Salem Village as a servant and became a central voice in the accusations. News of York’s destruction in January 1692 then reached Salem near the moment the first fits began.

Read against this corrected chronology, Mercy Lewis’s visions take on a different character than mere adolescent performance. A young woman repeatedly exposed to frontier violence described torment by invisible agents, demands to sign the Devil’s book, and a diabolical conspiracy. Norton interprets that language as one way genuine trauma may have acquired a religious and legal vocabulary in Salem. The chronology strengthens the frontier-war interpretation without claiming direct access to Mercy’s inner psychology.

Video tie-in

The film makes Mercy Lewis a throughline. Clarify for students that it compresses two episodes: childhood survival and displacement at Falmouth in 1676, followed by the attack that killed her parents in 1689. Then ask how her testimony against Burroughs may echo repeated exposure to frontier violence.

George Burroughs: The Frontier Minister as Devil

If Mercy Lewis is the thesis’s strongest accuser, George Burroughs is its strongest accused. Burroughs was a Harvard-educated minister who had previously held the Salem Village pulpit and had survived Falmouth’s destruction alongside Mercy and her parents. By 1692 he remained closely associated with Maine’s frontier settlements. He was accused not merely of ordinary witchcraft but of leading the satanic conspiracy and of bewitching English soldiers fighting the Wabanaki. Cotton Mather later connected the Salem afflictions to Native enemies in Decennium Luctuosum, published in 1699. Because this was a retrospective reflection rather than a statement written during the crisis, it shows how Mather later interpreted the connection but cannot by itself prove what participants thought in 1692.

This is the detail that turns a suggestive coincidence into something much harder to dismiss. The colony was losing a war it understood as a spiritual siege. It then accused a frontier minister — a man physically associated with the war zone — of being Satan’s field general, responsible for the military defeats themselves. The witch hunt did not merely happen at the same time as the frontier war. In the case of Burroughs, the accusation was explicitly about the war: it named a supernatural traitor to explain why English forces kept losing to the Wabanaki.

Video tie-in

The film devotes significant time to Burroughs: his examination, the accusation that he bewitched frontier soldiers, and his execution after reciting the Lord’s Prayer. When pointing to Mather’s on-screen speculation about Native leaders and demons, identify it precisely as a later reflection in Decennium Luctuosum (1699), not as a statement composed during the 1692 afflictions.

Discussion prompt

Write one sentence (1–2 min): Of the two — Mercy Lewis the accuser, or George Burroughs the accused — which one do you think is stronger evidence that the frontier war shaped Salem, and why?

The Conceptual Payoff

The frontier-war thesis is not built on atmosphere alone; it is built on people. A key accuser experienced childhood displacement in 1676 and became an adolescent orphan through renewed frontier violence in 1689. A key victim was a frontier minister accused of bewitching soldiers fighting the actual war. These documented biographies make the connection difficult to dismiss, even though they do not make frontier war the crisis’s sole cause.

Section 3: Testing the Claim — How Far Does It Actually Reach?

The Honest Objection

A good historical claim invites testing rather than mere agreement, and the frontier-war thesis has real limits worth stating plainly to students. Not every accuser was a war refugee. Not every accused person had any connection to Maine. Much of what happened in Salem is explained better by other factors entirely: the vicious local feud between the Putnam faction and its rivals, which mapped onto a striking number of the accusations; the contractual fight over Reverend Parris’s salary; longstanding Puritan anxieties about women, sin, and social disorder; and a court willing to accept ‘spectral evidence’ — testimony that an invisible specter had done harm — which made accusations nearly impossible to disprove.

Video tie-in

The film is genuinely useful here because it shows the OTHER causes too — the Putnam family’s central role as serial accusers, the Parris salary dispute, the reliance on spectral evidence. Students can see with their own eyes that the frontier war is one thread in a rope, not the whole rope.

Monocausal Versus Multicausal

The analytical skill this lecture is really teaching is the difference between a monocausal and a multicausal explanation. A monocausal version of Norton’s thesis — ‘Salem was really just about the frontier war’ — overreaches, and the evidence does not support it. A multicausal version — ‘the frontier war was a powerful and badly underappreciated ingredient, interacting with local feuds, gender, religion, and a broken evidentiary standard’ — is far stronger, and is closer to what Norton actually argues. The point is not to replace the old ‘superstition’ story with a new single-cause story. It is to show that the frontier war belongs in the explanation at all — which the traditional account almost entirely omits.

Discussion prompt

Quick write (1–2 min): In one sentence — is it more accurate to say Salem was ‘caused by’ the frontier war, or ‘shaped by’ it? Defend your choice of verb.

The Conceptual Payoff

The goal is not to crown a single true cause of Salem and dismiss the rest. It is to test how much weight one factor — the frontier war — can actually bear, and to notice that a factor can be genuinely important without being the only thing that mattered. That distinction is the difference between a slogan and an explanation.

Section 4: Why Fear Turned Inward

An Interpretive Proposition: Fear Redirected?

If frontier war fed the Salem crisis, one question remains: why might that fear have taken the form of accusations against neighbors? Norton’s interpretation invites a plausible proposition. Wabanaki military successes exposed the colony’s weakness, while witchcraft prosecutions placed alleged internal enemies within reach of colonial courts. Military frustration may therefore have helped redirect fear inward. No source, however, documents a collective psychological decision to substitute neighbors for an enemy army. The proposition should be tested, not presented as fact.

This proposition connects Salem back to Lecture 1. Metacom’s War left New England colonists fearful of Native enemies and of what unrestricted violence had made of themselves. A later frontier war reactivated that dread. It is possible that accusations made an alleged internal enemy legally reachable when Wabanaki forces were not. Yet local feuds, gender expectations, religious belief, political instability, and spectral evidence were also necessary to turn fear into prosecutions.

Video tie-in

The film’s ending complicates the redirection-of-fear proposition. Elite opposition grew as accusations reached prominent people; court restructuring and rejection of spectral evidence then removed the legal and political conditions sustaining the prosecutions. Ask students which parts of the crisis’s ending are directly documented and which psychological claims remain interpretive.

The Conceptual Payoff

Fear does not always strike at its apparent source, and displaced fear is a powerful interpretive possibility. In Salem, frontier war helps explain the crisis’s emotional and political setting. Local conflict, religious and gendered assumptions, and a legal system admitting unverifiable evidence explain how accusations became executable cases. The strongest account keeps both context and mechanism in view.

Discussion prompt

Predict / connect (1–2 min): Think back to Lecture 1 — New England colonists feared that fighting ‘savages’ had made them savage. In one sentence, how is Salem a continuation of that same fear rather than a new one?

Closing Synthesis

Salem is the moment this lecture series turns fear inward. The first two lectures showed Native military and religious power forcing colonial societies into crisis from outside their settlements. Salem shows frontier war entering a more complicated causal field: refugee trauma, military failure, local conflict, gender and religious expectations, political instability, and a court willing to admit spectral evidence. Within that combination, New England hunted the Devil among its own neighbors and executed twenty people before authorities dismantled the machinery of prosecution.

The analytical heart of the lecture is not the thesis itself but the testing of it. Students arrive believing Salem was simply about superstition; they leave having weighed a serious historical argument that frontier war partly shaped the crisis. They also learn to distinguish a factor that genuinely belongs in an explanation from a slogan claiming to explain everything. The durable takeaway is that causes are usually plural, and the interesting question is rarely ‘what was THE cause?’ but ‘how much weight can this cause bear?’

Frontier war belongs in the explanation. That is the quiet radicalism of Norton’s argument and of the film built around it. Even Salem, often treated as an isolated eruption of superstition, was partly shaped by frontier war, refugee trauma, and political failure—alongside local feuds, gender expectations, religious belief, and a legal system that admitted unverifiable evidence. The continental context reached into Massachusetts meetinghouses and courtrooms without becoming the crisis’s single cause.

Open analytical question

If even the Salem witch trials—the seemingly most inward-looking episode in early New England—were partly shaped by war with Native peoples on the frontier, what other ‘internal’ colonial events might we misread by leaving continental and Native power out of the story?

Independent Study Slide Candidates — Do Not Lose

Because the assigned film Witch Hunt carries the Salem narrative, this lecture spends unusually little live time on story and unusually much on analysis. That inversion changes the Independent Study role: rather than housing overflow narrative the live hour couldn’t fit, the Independent Study slides here deepen the ANALYTICAL threads the live hour raises but cannot fully pursue. Live delivery carries three short 1–2 minute activities — the Lewis-vs-Burroughs evidence judgment, the ‘caused by vs. shaped by’ verb choice, and the connect-back to Lecture 1 — each at a genuine hinge, per the house rule. Target: 3–6 slides.

  1. Spectral evidence on trial — what ‘spectral evidence’ was, why it made accusations nearly impossible to disprove, and why throwing it out in late 1692 effectively ended the crisis. A self-contained lesson in how a broken evidentiary standard can manufacture a witch hunt regardless of the underlying fear.
  2. The Putnam feud in depth — mapping how many accusations traced back to the Putnam faction’s local grievances (land, the Parris salary, the old grudge against Burroughs). Lets students weigh the ‘local feud’ cause against the ‘frontier war’ cause using the film’s own evidence.
  3. How the crisis ended — Increase Mather’s warning against improper accusations, elite opposition, Governor Phips’s termination of the Court of Oyer and Terminer, the rejection of spectral evidence, and Ann Putnam Jr.’s later apology. This sequence documents the legal and political conditions that ended the prosecutions more firmly than it documents any collective need for a substitute enemy.

An optional fourth slide could present Norton’s scholarship directly — a short orientation to In the Devil’s Snare (2002) and how a historian builds a revisionist thesis — for students curious how the argument the film dramatizes was actually assembled from the trial records.

Suggested Readings for Instructor Preparation

Mary Beth Norton, In the Devil’s Snare: The Salem Witchcraft Crisis of 1692 (2002)

The lecture’s central source and the scholarly basis for the frontier-war thesis the film dramatizes. Essential for Sections 1, 2, and 4.

Emerson W. Baker, A Storm of Witchcraft: The Salem Trials and the American Experience (2014)

A broad, judicious synthesis of the many causes of Salem; especially useful for Section 3’s monocausal-versus-multicausal framing, as Baker weighs the frontier war alongside local, legal, and religious factors.

Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum, Salem Possessed: The Social Origins of Witchcraft (1974)

The classic account of the Putnam faction and Salem Village’s internal feuds; the strongest statement of the ‘local conflict’ explanation that Section 3 weighs against the frontier thesis.

Cotton Mather, Decennium Luctuosum (1699)

The source of Mather’s speculation linking the Salem afflictions to Native enemies. Because Decennium Luctuosum appeared in 1699, it is a later reflection rather than a statement composed during the 1692 crisis. It remains valuable evidence of Puritan wartime cosmology when used with that chronological caution.

Mary Beth Norton, “The Refugee’s Revenge” and related essays

Shorter treatments in which Norton lays out the frontier-refugee argument concisely; useful if assigning students a direct taste of the scholarship behind the film.

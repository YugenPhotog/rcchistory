Counterpower, nullification, and the repertoire of colonial resistance

Introductory Framing

Colonial assemblies could petition Parliament, publish resolves, and send agents to London, but they could not formally repeal an act of Parliament. The central question is therefore practical: how can people who lack recognized sovereign authority prevent a law from operating? The answer developed between 1765 and 1774 through a repertoire of resistance that joined constitutional argument to organized noncompliance, economic pressure, crowd action, publicity, and local enforcement.

The word nullification is useful if employed carefully. In the Stamp Act crisis, colonists did not obtain a judicial ruling invalidating the statute. They made implementation impossible. Stamp distributors resigned, courts and newspapers improvised ways to function, merchants adopted nonimportation, and communities refused cooperation. Parliament repealed the act only after the law had already become largely unenforceable. This sequence reveals a form of political power that existed outside formal office but was not simply spontaneous disorder.

Historians have interpreted this resistance from several angles. Edmund and Helen Morgan emphasized the constitutional crisis; Pauline Maier traced the development of legitimate resistance; Gary Nash and Benjamin Carp recovered urban crowds and artisans; T. H. Breen showed how consumer choices became political mobilization; Serena Zabin reconstructed the social world behind the Boston Massacre; and Jane Calvert's recent biography of John Dickinson restores civil disobedience and peaceful resistance to the story. The lecture should hold these dimensions together rather than choosing between ideas and action.

Argument Alone Could Not Stop a Stamp

The Constitutional Case

Patrick Henry's Virginia resolves, the Stamp Act Congress, Daniel Dulany's pamphlet, and colonial petitions articulated a broad claim: taxes required consent through representatives chosen by the taxed community. Colonists did not initially deny all parliamentary authority. Many distinguished internal taxation from external trade regulation or accepted a layered empire in which Parliament supervised commerce while colonial assemblies controlled internal revenue.

The British doctrine of virtual representation held that members of Parliament represented the interests of the entire empire, including people who did not vote. Colonists already accepted a local form of virtual representation because assemblies legislated for women, enslaved people, servants, and nonvoters. Their objection was geographical and constitutional. A propertyless Englishman might someday acquire a vote within the British electoral system; no colonist, regardless of wealth, could vote for a member of Parliament. Daniel Dulany made this asymmetry central to the colonial case.

Primary Evidence: The Stamp Act Congress

Primary source

That it is inseparably essential to the freedom of a people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes be imposed on them, but with their own consent, given personally, or by their representatives.

- Resolutions of the Stamp Act Congress, October 19, 1765

The resolution supplied a durable constitutional principle, but it did not remove stamped paper from customs houses or courts. Petitioning depended on Parliament's willingness to reconsider. The law's opponents therefore needed tactics that changed the cost of enforcement. This is the boundary between dissent and counterpower: dissent announces that authority is unjust; counterpower prevents authority from functioning.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Stamp Act crisis cannot be explained by ideas or riots alone. Constitutional argument defined the grievance and preserved claims of legitimacy, while organized noncooperation supplied the leverage that petitions lacked.

Nullification as an Administrative Collapse

Removing the Human Machinery of the Law

The Stamp Act depended on local distributors, customs officers, judges, lawyers, merchants, printers, and ordinary users of stamped paper. Resistance targeted these points of dependence. In Boston, an effigy of distributor Andrew Oliver appeared on the Liberty Tree in August 1765. A crowd destroyed the building believed intended as a stamp office and pressured Oliver to resign. Similar campaigns spread across the colonies. Except in Georgia, distributors resigned before the law took effect.

This was a strategically precise form of disruption. Parliament could enact a statute, but it could not instantly create a loyal local bureaucracy. If no one would accept the office, if juries would not convict, if merchants would not comply, and if communities protected resisters, the law existed on paper without operating in practice. The political accomplishment was not merely emotional protest. It was the systematic withdrawal of cooperation.

Institutional Improvisation

Nullification also required colonists to manage the consequences of defiance. Newspapers sometimes suspended publication and then resumed without stamps. Courts initially closed or hesitated, threatening commercial transactions and debt collection. Merchants and judges faced pressure to continue business while refusing the statute. Resistance therefore created an administrative problem for the colonists themselves: how to preserve social order while disabling imperial law.

Pauline Maier's work is especially useful here. She showed that colonists drew on an Anglo-American tradition in which resistance might be legitimate when government violated fundamental rights, but violence remained morally and politically dangerous. The movement sought to represent itself as the defense of law against unconstitutional innovation, not as hostility to all government.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

The Stamp Act was defeated because colonial society contained enough organized capacity to deprive the law of personnel, compliance, and legitimacy. Repeal followed practical nullification rather than causing it.

Crowds: Political Public or Coercive Mob?

The Two Boston Riots

The events of August 14 and August 26, 1765 illustrate why the category of crowd must be disaggregated. The first action targeted Andrew Oliver and the machinery of the Stamp Act. Many Bostonians viewed it as disciplined political enforcement. The second destroyed lieutenant governor Thomas Hutchinson's home and private papers. The scale of property destruction horrified many people who supported repeal. The same organizer, shoemaker Ebenezer McIntosh, was associated with both episodes, yet contemporaries assigned them different legitimacy.

Gary Nash and Benjamin Carp emphasize the social worlds that made crowd action possible. Artisans, laborers, sailors, fire companies, taverns, neighborhood networks, and annual rituals such as Pope's Day supplied organization before formal revolutionary committees did. Elite leaders sometimes encouraged popular pressure and then tried to distance themselves from violence. Crowds were neither puppets of gentlemen nor a unified democratic movement. They possessed their own leaders, grievances, and standards of justice.

The Boston Massacre and the Politics of Evidence

The stationing of troops in Boston created repeated conflict over jobs, space, insults, and authority. Serena Zabin's reconstruction of soldiers' families and neighborhood relations complicates the familiar image of two separate camps suddenly colliding on March 5, 1770. Soldiers and civilians lived in an entangled town. The massacre emerged from escalating local tensions, a ropewalk fight, street confrontation, and fear rather than from a planned British attack.

John Adams's defense of the soldiers allowed patriots to claim that resistance remained compatible with law. The acquittals and manslaughter verdicts also limited the Crown's ability to portray Boston as incapable of justice. Paul Revere's famous engraving, however, converted a confused confrontation into a visual narrative of deliberate military violence. Legal and propagandistic victories operated simultaneously.

Instructor note

Instructor note: Do not use crowd, mob, Sons of Liberty, artisans, and patriots as interchangeable categories. Membership and leadership overlapped, but motives and participants varied by event and place.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Crowd action mattered because it could enforce community judgments more quickly than formal institutions, but its legitimacy was always contested. The movement gained power by using coercion while insisting that its coercion defended a higher law.

Nonimportation and the Politicization of Everyday Life

Economic Pressure as Political Strategy

Merchants adopted nonimportation agreements to enlist British manufacturers and traders in the campaign for repeal. The strategy recognized that colonial petitions might be ignored, but economic distress could create metropolitan allies. Rockingham's ministry used merchant petitions to justify repeal of the Stamp Act without admitting that Parliament had yielded to American crowds.

The Townshend crisis made nonimportation broader and more demanding. Imports declined sharply during the height of the boycott, but they rebounded after partial repeal. The textbook's import table is valuable because it prevents a triumphalist conclusion. Boycotts could demonstrate solidarity and influence politics, but they were difficult to sustain. Merchants had unequal inventories and incentives, consumers desired British goods, and smugglers could profit from collective restraint.

Households, Women, and Consumption

Nonimportation transferred political responsibility into households. Women's decisions about tea, cloth, clothing, funerals, and household production became essential to the movement. Homespun carried symbolic power even when colonial production could not replace all imported textiles. The politics of consumption widened participation without granting women formal political rights.

T. H. Breen argues that a shared consumer culture helped inhabitants of different colonies imagine common interests. The claim should be used with qualification. Markets did not erase regional divisions, and political participation remained unequal. Yet the boycott made purchasing visible as a public act. Community surveillance turned private consumption into evidence of loyalty or betrayal.

Primary Evidence: A Merchant Agreement

Primary source

We, the underwritten, retailers of goods, do hereby promise and oblige ourselves not to buy any goods, wares, or merchandises ... that shall be shipped from Great Britain after the first day of January next unless the Stamp Act shall be repealed.

- New York Merchants Non-importation Agreement, November 1765

The agreement shows why boycott was more than abstention. It was a public promise whose effectiveness depended on reciprocal compliance. The political unit was not an isolated consumer but an association capable of monitoring members and punishing defectors through loss of reputation and trade.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

Nonimportation converted economic interdependence into political leverage. It also expanded the arena of politics from assemblies and pamphlets into shops, households, clothing, and social reputation.

Committees, Print, and the Creation of Counterpower

Communication and Escalation

Newspapers circulated resolves, rumors, symbols, and reports of resistance. The transmission of Patrick Henry's resolves is a classic example: printers published more radical versions than the Virginia House of Burgesses had formally adopted, creating the impression of a stronger intercolonial consensus. Print did not merely describe resistance. It standardized language, supplied models for imitation, and accelerated escalation.

Committees of correspondence transformed communication into organization. They gathered intelligence, circulated circular letters, coordinated responses, and encouraged towns to speak as political bodies. By 1772 and 1773, intercolonial networks could respond rapidly to the Gaspee affair, tea shipments, and imperial policy. The committees created continuity between crises that had previously risen and subsided.

From Voluntary Association to Enforcement

Resistance associations claimed to rest on voluntary consent, but they relied on sanctions. Importers could be publicly named, boycotted, pressured to apologize, or threatened. Tarring and feathering, though less common than popular memory suggests, dramatized the capacity of communities to punish perceived enemies outside ordinary legal procedures. This coercion is not incidental to the story. It explains both the movement's effectiveness and the fears of loyalists, moderates, and neutrals.

Jane Calvert's recent work on John Dickinson highlights a strand of disciplined, peaceful resistance rooted partly in Quaker practice. Dickinson urged coordinated noncompliance while warning against uncontrolled violence. His position helps demonstrate that colonists debated not only whether to resist but how resistance could remain morally legitimate and politically effective.

Analytical Significance

Analytical significance

By the early 1770s, resistance had become an organized system of communication, economic discipline, public judgment, and selective coercion. These practices did not yet constitute a new government, but they created the counterpower from which revolutionary government would later emerge.

Closing Synthesis

Colonists defeated imperial laws by attacking the gap between legislation and administration. Parliament could declare a tax, but enforcement required people, information, goods, courts, juries, and cooperation. The Stamp Act failed when distributors resigned and communities improvised around stamped paper. Nonimportation recruited British merchants while mobilizing colonial consumers. Crowds imposed immediate sanctions. Newspapers and committees made local action reproducible across provincial boundaries.

The historiography warns against a simple moral contrast between peaceful constitutionalists and irrational mobs. Edmund and Helen Morgan established the centrality of the constitutional dispute; Pauline Maier explained the inherited logic of resistance; Gary Nash and Benjamin Carp restored artisans and crowds; T. H. Breen illuminated consumer mobilization; Serena Zabin complicated the social world of military occupation; and Jane Calvert has recently emphasized John Dickinson's civil disobedience. The strongest interpretation treats ideas, organization, economic interest, and coercion as mutually dependent.

The unresolved problem is legitimacy. Patriots condemned taxation without consent yet often pressured neighbors to sign agreements, surrender goods, or conform publicly. Resistance worked because it could coerce, but it claimed moral authority because it portrayed that coercion as defensive and communal. At what point does organized noncompliance become a legitimate exercise of popular sovereignty, and who gets to decide when the community has spoken?

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Classic Interpretation

Edmund S. Morgan and Helen M. Morgan, The Stamp Act Crisis: Prologue to Revolution, 3rd ed. (1995; originally 1953).

Still the essential account of the Stamp Act controversy and the constitutional logic of repeal. Best used alongside later social histories that recover actors below the level of elite politics.

Essential Political Development

Pauline Maier, From Resistance to Revolution: Colonial Radicals and the Development of American Opposition to Britain, 1765-1776 (1972).

Traces how inherited ideas about legitimate resistance changed across successive crises. Maier is particularly useful for distinguishing defensive resistance from revolutionary claims of sovereignty.

Urban Social History

Gary B. Nash, The Urban Crucible: Social Change, Political Consciousness, and the Origins of the American Revolution (1979).

Connects social conflict and political mobilization in Boston, New York, and Philadelphia. It remains foundational for understanding crowds, artisans, and the urban settings of resistance.

Consumer Politics

T. H. Breen, The Marketplace of Revolution: How Consumer Politics Shaped American Independence (2004).

Explains how shared participation in Atlantic consumer markets made boycotts intelligible and helped colonists imagine common political interests. Useful for integrating women and households into the causal argument.

Crowds and Cities

Benjamin L. Carp, Rebels Rising: Cities and the American Revolution (2007).

A comparative study of urban revolutionary mobilization. Carp shows how the built environment, waterfront labor, taverns, neighborhoods, and local institutions shaped different patterns of resistance.

Recent Reassessment

Serena Zabin, The Boston Massacre: A Family History (2020).

Reconstructs the social relationships among soldiers, families, and civilians before March 1770. It corrects the image of two completely separate communities and strengthens discussion of contingency and propaganda.

Recent Scholarship

Jane E. Calvert, Penman of the Founding: A Biography of John Dickinson (2025).

Restores Dickinson's religious commitments, constitutional reasoning, and advocacy of peaceful civil disobedience. Particularly valuable for balancing crowd-centered accounts of resistance.

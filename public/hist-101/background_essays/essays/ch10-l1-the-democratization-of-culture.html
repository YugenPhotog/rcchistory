What happens when ordinary Americans gain direct access to print?

Introductory Framing

The lecture follows a clear arc. It begins with two key terms, popular culture and the democratization of culture, and a question that keeps the story honest: democratization for whom? It then sets the scene in 1815, when the United States was a rural republic with a highly literate white population. Next it introduces the idea of the public sphere, the space between private life and the government where strangers argue about public matters, and asks who was allowed in. Only then does it turn to the machinery that built that public: a federal post office that carried newspapers almost everywhere, and a print revolution of steam presses, machine-made paper, and advertising. The rest of the lecture follows that machinery into everyday life: penny newspapers that sold crime, scandal, and hoaxes to working people; cheap novels and sentimental fiction; mass-produced pictures; traveling lecturers on the lyceum stage; and party newspapers that turned elections into public shows. It ends with a painting of two men arguing over a newspaper in an oyster cellar, and with the question of who is not in the picture.

Historians approach this story from several directions. Jürgen Habermas gave us the idea of the public sphere, and Benedict Anderson showed how newspapers helped readers imagine themselves as a nation. Michael Schudson shows how the penny press invented “news” as a product sold to a mass audience. Richard R. John shows that the federal post office made a national conversation possible. Patricia Cline Cohen uses the Jewett case to show how crime became entertainment. Angela Ray examines the lyceum as a new kind of public. Jane Tompkins defends the sentimental novel against critics who saw it as a sign of cultural decline.

The resulting interpretation should avoid two easy stories. One is a simple tale of progress in which cheap print set everyone free. The other is a story of decline in which serious reading was cheapened by sensation. The evidence is more mixed. Cheap print widened the circle of people who could read, argue, and be heard. It also created new gatekeepers, such as editors, publishers, party managers, and moral reformers, and it left large groups of Americans outside the circle entirely.

Key Terms: Popular Culture and the Democratization of Culture

Two terms run through the whole chapter. Popular culture means culture produced for and consumed by ordinary people, not just elites. It is usually made commercially and sold to mass audiences, so it has to be affordable, accessible, and entertaining. Historians used to set it against “high culture,” the opera, fine art, and serious literature of the educated few, but in this period the boundary was blurring: as we will see, a philosopher like Emerson could draw a paying crowd in a small-town hall.

The democratization of culture means expanding access to culture beyond the traditional elite gatekeepers. Lower costs, new technologies such as the steam press, and new institutions such as the lyceum made that possible. Culture became market-based rather than patronage-based: instead of depending on a wealthy sponsor, a writer, editor, or lecturer now depended on selling to the public. Democratization in this sense is about access, not equality. That is why the lecture’s critical question matters from the start: who gained access, and who remained excluded?

Setting the Scene: 1815, a Cultural Turning Point

Picture a farm family in upstate New York in 1815. The War of 1812 has just ended, and a wave of national pride is sweeping the country. The family probably owns a Bible, an almanac, perhaps a schoolbook. A weekly newspaper arrives by post, shared among neighbors and read aloud in the evening. Most of what it prints is political news and items copied from other papers. City newspapers are expensive, sold mainly by annual subscription to merchants and lawyers.

Yet the foundations of something new were in place: postwar nationalism, rising literacy, growing towns and markets, and steam technology that would soon make printing far faster. Institutions that had served a small elite were beginning to open up. A painting from a few years later shows what that looked like. In John Lewis Krimmel’s The Village Politicians (c. 1819), ordinary villagers crowd a tavern. One man reads a newspaper aloud at the left, while the men at the table jab their fingers and argue over another paper. Krimmel, a German-born Philadelphia painter, adapted the scene from the Scottish artist David Wilkie’s 1806 painting of the same name, but the setting is American: political news has reached farmers and tradesmen, and they are talking it over in public.

The Public Sphere: A New Space Between Home and Government

The lecture’s central concept is the public sphere. Think of it as an imaginary town square. It is not your home, and it is not the government; it is the space in between, where private people come together, in person or through print, to discuss public matters such as elections, wars, and moral questions. Your family dinner table is private; Congress is the state. The public sphere sits between them, and the opinions formed there are ones the state has to reckon with. The German philosopher Jürgen Habermas described how coffeehouses, salons, and newspapers in eighteenth-century Europe created such a space for reasoned debate, and he warned that commercial mass media could later turn citizens into consumers. Keep that worry in mind for the penny press.

In the United States after 1815, the public sphere was created through communication: penny papers, novels, prints, lyceum lectures, and the partisan press. Those five media are also the map of this essay. In principle, anyone who could read and argue could join. The word “ideally” on the lecture slide is a warning that practice fell short.

How Print Creates a Public

How does a stack of newspapers create a public? Imagine a farmer in Ohio and a merchant in Boston who read about the same Senate debate in their local newspapers. They will never meet, but they now share knowledge and can argue about the same issue. Multiply that by millions of readers and you have “the public.” Benedict Anderson called nations “imagined communities” for exactly this reason: you will never meet most of your fellow citizens, yet reading the same news at the same moment makes you feel connected to them. Cheap newspapers and cheap lectures widened that circle, because a one-cent paper (instead of six cents) made taking part affordable for many more people.

Richard Caton Woodville’s painting War News from Mexico (1848) shows the public sphere in action. On the porch of a small-town hotel that doubles as the post office, a young man reads a fresh newspaper “extra” aloud while the men around him lean in to hear the latest from the war with Mexico. Now look at who is not on the porch. A woman listens from a window, and a Black man and a child sit at the bottom of the steps, close enough to hear but outside the circle of white male readers and voters. In one image you can see both how print gathered a public and who that public left at its edges.

Who Was Excluded?

The lecture sorts Americans by how much access they had to this public. White men were its primary participants: by the 1830s nearly all of them could vote, most could read, and many had some time and money for newspapers. Some middle-class women had a limited place as readers, writers, and members of reading circles and moral reform societies, but they could not vote and were criticized for speaking in public. Free African Americans were largely shut out of white institutions, so they built their own; Freedom’s Journal, the first African American–owned and –edited newspaper, appeared in New York in 1827. Enslaved people were barred by law from literacy in most Southern states. And the working poor faced barriers of time, cost, and limited schooling.

The sharpest limit was slavery. In 1829 David Walker, a free Black man in Boston, published his Appeal to the Coloured Citizens of the World, calling on enslaved people to resist, and copies were smuggled into Southern ports. Southern legislatures responded with laws against teaching enslaved people to read and write: Georgia in 1829, North Carolina in 1830, Virginia in 1831, Alabama in 1832, and South Carolina in 1834. After Nat Turner, a man who could read, led his rebellion in August 1831, these restrictions tightened further. Teaching an enslaved person to read could bring fines, jail, or whipping. Frederick Douglass, who learned to read in secret in Baltimore, understood exactly why.

Primary source

“From that moment, I understood the pathway from slavery to freedom.”

— Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave (1845), on overhearing why his enslaver forbade his reading lessons

Other barriers were quieter but real. A penny was cheap, but for the poorest families any regular expense mattered, and books remained costly. Print was strongest in cities and along canals and rail lines; isolated rural families saw far less of it. Immigrants arrived with different languages and levels of schooling; German speakers built their own presses, while many poor Irish immigrants of the 1840s had had little chance to learn to read. The Cherokee Nation published its own bilingual newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, from 1828. And women, central as readers and writers, could not vote in the elections the partisan press covered. Excluded groups did not simply wait. They built counterpublics: their own newspapers, churches, and societies.

Analytical significance

The public sphere was real: print let millions of strangers share news and argue about it. But access was structured by race, class, and gender. “Democratization” in this era was partial and contested, and excluded Americans answered by building publics of their own.

The Network Behind the Public: The Post Office

How could strangers in Ohio and Boston read the same news at all? The answer was a federal network. The Post Office Act of 1792 set very low postage for newspapers, 1 cent for up to 100 miles and 1.5 cents beyond, at a time when sending a letter cost 6 to 25 cents. It also let printers exchange papers with each other free. Small-town editors filled their pages with stories clipped from those exchange papers, so news printed in one town quickly spread to others, and news from Washington reached frontier towns. The lecture calls it the “information superhighway” of the early republic.

The network grew at astonishing speed: from 75 post offices in 1790 to 8,450 in 1830 and 18,417 in 1850. By 1840 post roads stretched more than 155,000 miles, and canals, steamboats, and later railroads carried the mail faster every year. By the late 1820s, historian Richard R. John notes, the United States had far more post offices per person than Britain or France. By the 1830s newspapers made up most of the weight of the mail but paid only a small share of its costs, so letter writers and the Treasury were in effect subsidizing the news. John argues that for most Americans the post office was the most important part of the federal government: the national public was built on government infrastructure, not just private enterprise.

Because it was a government network, though, the people who ran it could also decide what not to carry. In 1835 the American Anti-Slavery Society mailed large numbers of antislavery pamphlets into the South. In Charleston, a mob broke into the post office in late July and burned the mail in public, and Postmaster General Amos Kendall, a close ally of Andrew Jackson, declined to order local postmasters to deliver such material. The system that built a national reading public could be shut when the message threatened slavery.

Analytical significance

Cheap, reliable delivery of newspapers is the hidden foundation of everything else in this lecture. The penny press, the partisan press, and reform publishing all depended on a federal network that reached almost every town. That network was a deliberate public investment in an informed citizenry, and it could be closed to voices the powerful wanted silenced.

The Print Revolution: Scale and Impact

With the network in place, printing itself was transformed between the 1820s and the 1840s. A printer working a hand press pulled one sheet at a time, a couple hundred impressions an hour. Steam-powered cylinder presses multiplied that output, and in 1847 Richard Hoe patented a rotary “lightning” press that could print thousands of sheets an hour. Paper-making machines that produced long continuous rolls cut the cost of paper. Stereotyping (casting a solid metal plate from set type) made popular works cheap to reprint.

The economic change mattered as much as the machines. The old newspaper lived on subscriptions and on printing contracts from political allies. The new one would live on advertising. If a paper could sell its pages to advertisers, it could sell its copies cheaply, and the more copies it sold, the more advertisers would pay. Editors now had a reason to write for the largest possible audience. Print stopped being a luxury and became a commodity, something you bought, read, and threw away.

The numbers show the scale, though nineteenth-century counts were rough and should be treated as estimates. The country went from roughly 200 newspapers in 1800 to about 1,000 by 1835. When the 1840 census became the first to ask about literacy, it counted only about 9 percent of white adults over twenty as unable to read and write, roughly nine in ten literate and one of the highest rates in the world, though lower in the South. Common schools, Sunday schools, and the Protestant emphasis on reading scripture all helped. Book prices fell from a dollar and a half or more to as little as a quarter for cheap editions.

Mass media did not appear from nowhere. A literate white population, a federal postal network, faster presses, cheaper paper, and an advertising-based business model came together in the 1820s and 1830s. Machines made print possible at scale; advertising made it profitable.

News for a Penny: The Sun, the Herald, and the Spectacle of the Press

On September 3, 1833, a twenty-three-year-old printer named Benjamin Day put out the first issue of a small four-page paper called the Sun. It cost one cent, when most New York dailies cost six cents and were sold by yearly subscription. Day hired boys to buy bundles of papers and sell them on street corners, shouting the headlines. His motto was “It Shines for All.”

Primary source

“The object of this paper is to lay before the public, at a price within the means of every one, all the news of the day . . .”

— Benjamin H. Day, the Sun (New York), first issue, September 3, 1833

“All the news of the day” meant something new: police-court reports, fires, suicides, accidents, and human-interest stories about ordinary people. Apprentices, laborers, servants, and immigrants could afford it, and they bought it by the thousands. James Gordon Bennett launched the even livelier New York Herald in May 1835. Horace Greeley founded the New-York Tribune in 1841, a cheap paper with a more serious, reform-minded voice. Penny papers soon appeared in Boston, Philadelphia, and Baltimore. Selling on the street changed what got published: a paper bought one copy at a time had to grab a passerby every single day.

The Great Moon Hoax (1835)

In late August 1835 the Sun ran six articles that claimed to be reprinted from a Scottish science journal. They reported that the British astronomer Sir John Herschel, working at the Cape of Good Hope with an enormous new telescope, had seen forests, seas, bison-like animals, walking beavers, and winged, human-like creatures on the moon.

Primary source

“We scientifically denominated them the Vespertilio-homo, or man-bat.”

— “Great Astronomical Discoveries,” the Sun (New York), August 28, 1835

None of it was true. Herschel really was in South Africa, but the journal article was invented, almost certainly by Richard Adams Locke, a Sun reporter who later admitted it. Many readers believed the story at first, and other papers reprinted it. On August 28 the Sun boasted a circulation of 19,360, which it claimed beat the Times of London. Rival editors soon denounced the fake, but readers seemed to enjoy arguing over whether they had been fooled almost as much as the story itself.

The Murder of Helen Jewett (1836)

Less than a year later, the penny press found a darker story. Early on April 10, 1836, Rosina Townsend, who ran a brothel at 41 Thomas Street in lower Manhattan, discovered that Helen Jewett, a young woman in her house, had been killed with blows to the head and her bed set on fire. Police arrested Richard P. Robinson, a nineteen-year-old clerk and regular visitor. Bennett went to the scene himself and wrote a vivid, almost voyeuristic description of the body for the Herald. For weeks the papers fought over the case: the Herald suggested Robinson might be innocent; the Sun insisted he was guilty. In June a jury acquitted him, and many readers suspected that money and connections had bought his freedom. A private tragedy had become a public drama, and the papers that told it best sold the most copies.

Discussion prompt

Open discussion: What might the Moon Hoax and the Jewett coverage tell us about Americans’ relationship to truth and curiosity in a democracy? Where is the boundary between news and entertainment, and who gets to draw it?

Analytical significance

The penny press did more than report events; it staged them. Journalism shifted from information toward performance. Readers became a public that consumed the same stories at the same time, and that shared experience was genuinely democratic. It also rewarded sensation, blurred fact and fantasy, and turned real suffering into entertainment.

Novels, Story Papers, and Pictures: Books and Visual Print

The Book Revolution

Books got cheaper too. In New York, James and John Harper started a printing firm in 1817 that became Harper & Brothers in 1833 and one of the largest publishers in the country; in Philadelphia, Carey & Lea published many of the most popular American novels of the 1820s. Because the United States did not honor foreign copyrights, publishers could reprint British authors such as Walter Scott and Charles Dickens without paying them. Around 1839–1840, “mammoth” weekly story papers such as Brother Jonathan and the New World printed entire novels on huge sheets that sold for pennies, and serialization taught readers to wait eagerly for the next installment, much like a streaming series today. Books became affordable purchases rather than heirlooms.

American writers found audiences of their own. James Fenimore Cooper’s Leatherstocking Tales, beginning with The Pioneers (1823) and including The Last of the Mohicans (1826), followed the frontier scout Natty Bumppo, called Leatherstocking or Hawkeye, through the forests of New York. Picture a family gathered around a lamp on a winter evening while one of them reads a Cooper novel aloud. That was how many people read in this era: aloud and together, so a single book could thrill a whole household with ambushes, rescues, and wilderness chases. The excitement made Cooper America’s first best-selling novelist. It also turned the frontier into a national mythology about wilderness, conquest, and the supposed “vanishing” of Native peoples, a myth that hid ongoing removal. Reading became a form of leisure, self-improvement, and identity: Americans began to imagine themselves through the stories they read.

Sentimental Fiction and the “Scribbling Women”

The best-selling novels of the era were often written by women and read mainly by women. Catharine Maria Sedgwick’s Hope Leslie (1827) and Lydia Maria Child’s Hobomok (1824) were early examples. By mid-century, three authors led the field. Susan Warner’s The Wide, Wide World (1850), often called America’s first bestseller, follows young Ellen Montgomery as she loses her mother and learns to bear sorrow through faith. E.D.E.N. Southworth published dozens of novels, many first serialized in story papers; her most popular, The Hidden Hand (serialized in the New York Ledger in 1859), starred Capitola Black, a New York orphan who dresses as a boy to sell newspapers and survive the streets.

The biggest hit was Maria Susanna Cummins’s The Lamplighter (1854), which reportedly sold 40,000 copies in its first eight weeks. It opens with Gerty, a neglected, angry orphan in Boston. When her cruel guardian kills the kitten Gerty loves and throws the girl out into the cold, the kind old lamplighter, Trueman Flint, takes her in and raises her as his own. The novel follows Gerty as she grows through faith, self-control, and love. Books like these blended sentiment with moral teaching. They taught what the lecture calls emotional literacy: readers were expected to cry, to feel for the characters, and to come away morally improved, because feeling deeply was treated as a sign of virtue. When millions of women decided which books sold, female readers gained real cultural authority.

Not everyone approved. On January 19, 1855, Nathaniel Hawthorne, then serving as U.S. consul in Liverpool, answered his publisher William D. Ticknor, who had been urging him to come home and write again:

Primary source

“America is now wholly given over to a d——d mob of scribbling women, and I should have no chance of success while the public taste is occupied with their trash . . .”

— Nathaniel Hawthorne to William D. Ticknor, January 19, 1855

Hawthorne singled out the “innumerable editions” of The Lamplighter, while his own Scarlet Letter sold only a few thousand copies a year. The insult reveals envy as much as judgment; in his very next letter Hawthorne praised Fanny Fern’s novel Ruth Hall. Since the 1970s, scholars such as Nina Baym and Jane Tompkins have turned “scribbling women” into a neutral, even admiring label for these hugely successful authors. Hawthorne’s sneer reveals the stakes: when ordinary readers, many of them women, decided what sold, the old idea that an educated elite should define good literature came under pressure.

Lithography and the Democratization of Images

Pictures became cheap as well. Lithography, printing from an image drawn in grease on a flat stone, arrived in the United States around 1820 and spread quickly, because it did not require cutting into metal or wood. In 1834 a young New York printer named Nathaniel Currier went into the business. In January 1840 the steamboat Lexington burned on Long Island Sound, killing more than one hundred people; within days Currier issued a dramatic print of the disaster in an “extra” edition of the Sun, and it sold enormously. James Merritt Ives became his partner in 1857, and Currier & Ives went on to sell millions of inexpensive prints of disasters, horse races, farm scenes, and politicians. Political cartoons and, later, illustrated newspapers did the same for public life. Ordinary families could now hang pictures on their walls, and those images, of frontier, domesticity, and prosperity, shaped national myths. Americans could see idealized versions of themselves and their nation.

Analytical significance

Cheap books, story papers, and prints extended the democratization of culture from news to stories and images. Readers, especially women, gained new power over what succeeded. Critics like Hawthorne resented that power, and the argument over “taste” became part of the argument over democracy itself.

The Lyceum: Classroom or Stage?

In November 1826, in the small town of Millbury, Massachusetts, a lecturer named Josiah Holbrook organized his neighbors into a society for mutual education. He called it a lyceum, after Aristotle’s school in ancient Athens. Members met in the evening to hear talks on science, history, literature, reform, and practical subjects, to watch science demonstrations, and to debate. The goals were civic improvement and moral uplift: learning outside formal schools, for people who would never enroll in a college. The idea spread fast; Holbrook later claimed about 3,000 town lyceums by the mid-1830s, mostly in New England, New York, and the Midwest. By the antebellum years the lyceum had become a backbone of American public culture, the spoken-word partner of the print revolution. At first, local people lectured each other. Soon towns invited traveling speakers and charged admission, and some lecturers became celebrities.

The Headliner: Emerson and Transcendentalism

The most famous of them was Ralph Waldo Emerson (1803–1882). Born in Boston, Emerson followed his father into the ministry and became a Unitarian pastor. In 1832 he quit his pulpit because he could no longer in good conscience lead communion, and he found a new pulpit in the lecture hall. His little book Nature (1836) said every person could meet the divine directly in the natural world. In “The American Scholar” (1837), an address to Harvard’s Phi Beta Kappa society, he told young Americans to stop copying Europe and think for themselves; Oliver Wendell Holmes later called it America’s “intellectual Declaration of Independence.” His Divinity School Address (1838) told Harvard’s new ministers to trust their own souls over church tradition, and Harvard did not invite him back for nearly thirty years. “Self-Reliance” (1841) boiled his message down to two words: “Trust thyself.”

Emerson was the leading voice of Transcendentalism, a movement of young New England ministers and writers who felt their churches had grown cold and rule-bound. It grew out of liberal Unitarianism and was inspired by European Romantic writers and the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, who gave them the word “transcendental.” Their big claim was that truth does not come from a church, a tradition, or an expert; you find it yourself, through intuition, through nature, and inside your own soul. From 1836 they met as the Transcendental Club, and from 1840 to 1844 they published a magazine, The Dial, edited first by Margaret Fuller and then by Emerson. Some tried living the ideas: members of Brook Farm (1841–1847), near Boston, shared farm work and study, and Henry David Thoreau spent two years in a cabin he built at Walden Pond (1845–1847).

These ideas could have stayed in a few Concord parlors. The lyceum carried them to ordinary audiences. Emerson gave about 1,500 lectures between 1833 and 1881, touring small towns and city halls and traveling as far as California, and many of his famous essays began as talks to paying audiences. Philosophy became a public performance that tradesmen, teachers, and farmers could buy a ticket to hear.

The Crowd-Pleasers: Science, Pseudoscience, and Spectacle

If Emerson was the headliner, other acts were the crowd-pleasers. Alongside astronomy and moral philosophy, lyceum stages hosted phrenology, mesmerism, and electrical demonstrations. Phrenology was the claim, developed by the European doctors Franz Joseph Gall and Johann Spurzheim, that character could be read from the shape of the skull. Spurzheim lectured in Boston in 1832 and died there that November. The brothers Orson and Lorenzo Fowler later built a phrenology business in New York, reading heads for a fee, and phrenological charts such as The Symbolical Head (1842), which mapped “faculties” onto a human profile, hung in lecture halls and sold to the public. In 1836 the French lecturer Charles Poyen toured New England demonstrating mesmerism, also called animal magnetism, putting volunteers into trances on stage. Today we call these pseudosciences. To audiences in the 1830s, they looked like exciting new knowledge that anyone could judge, and their popularity reflected a hunger for self-knowledge and moral order in a fast-changing society. (Chapter 14, Lecture 3, “Gothic Science,” returns to phrenology and mesmerism, including phrenology’s role in scientific racism.)

Reformers on the Platform

The lecture platform also belonged to crusaders. John B. Gough, a down-and-out drunk, signed a temperance pledge in Worcester, Massachusetts, in October 1842 and turned his own story into a show; in his first year on the road, 1843, he spoke 386 times. Antislavery speakers were more controversial. In 1845 the Concord Lyceum fought over whether to invite the abolitionist Wendell Phillips back. When members voted yes, two curators resigned in protest, Emerson and Thoreau became curators, and Thoreau defended Phillips’s right to speak in a letter printed in The Liberator on March 28, 1845. That same year the New Bedford Lyceum barred Black people from membership, and Emerson and Charles Sumner refused to lecture there. By 1854 the Ohio Mechanics’ Institute in Cincinnati offered a whole lecture course called “American Slavery,” with speakers including Frederick Douglass and William Lloyd Garrison. These are the same temperance and abolition crusades that Lecture 2 traces to revival faith; the lyceum gave them a stage.

Classroom or Stage?

The lecture ends its lyceum section with a question meant to be argued: was the lyceum a democratic classroom or a performance stage? Did it elevate the public or merely entertain it? The case for a classroom is strong. Benjamin Silliman’s geology and chemistry lectures at Boston’s Lowell Institute (from 1840), Emerson’s ideas, and Douglass’s antislavery arguments reached farmers, clerks, and mechanics who would never attend college. The case for a stage is strong too. Lyceums ran on ticket sales, so lecturers had to entertain, which is why phrenology and mesmerism shared the bill with real science. One good synthesis is that the entertainment is what made the education democratic: people paid to be amazed and left having learned something. A good pushback is that audiences chose what they paid for, so the stage could reward the crowd-pleasing over the true; phrenology passed as science and later fed scientific racism.

[Instructor note: Holbrook’s figure of about 3,000 lyceums is his own estimate, and sources date it anywhere from 1834 to 1850. Treat it as a sign of rapid growth rather than a precise count. Emerson’s “The American Scholar” (1837) is sometimes called a lyceum lecture; it was a Phi Beta Kappa address at Harvard. The deck’s lyceum lecture-hall image is an AI-generated illustration.]

Discussion prompt

Think-Pair-Share: Was the lyceum a democratic classroom or a performance stage? Pick a side and back it with one example from this lecture: Emerson, phrenology, Gough, or the Concord fight over Wendell Phillips.

Analytical significance

The lyceum was both classroom and stage, and that was the point. It turned learning into a public event that ordinary people could pursue, debate, and judge. The same features that made it democratic, such as paying audiences, celebrity speakers, and a hunger for novelty, also blurred the line between education and entertainment, just as the penny press blurred news and spectacle.

Politics as Spectacle: The Partisan Press

Cheap print also transformed politics. As states dropped property requirements, nearly all white men could vote by the 1830s, and parties needed ways to reach them. Broadsides (one-sheet posters) and party newspapers were the answer. Andrew Jackson’s supporters built a national network of friendly papers, with the Washington Globe, founded in 1830 and edited by Francis Preston Blair, at its center. Amos Kendall, a former Kentucky editor in Jackson’s “Kitchen Cabinet,” helped shape that operation. The opposition Whigs built networks of their own.

Party papers did not pretend to be neutral. They printed speeches and platforms, but also insults, rumors, songs, and cartoons aimed at working-class readers. The same president could look like a hero or a tyrant depending on the paper: “General Jackson Slaying the Many Headed Monster” cast Jackson as a dragon-slayer fighting the Second Bank of the United States, while the opposition cartoon “King Andrew the First” showed him as a crowned tyrant trampling the Constitution. The election of 1840 showed how far this could go. When a Democratic paper mocked the Whig candidate, William Henry Harrison, as a man who would be content with a log cabin and a barrel of hard cider, the Whigs turned the insult into a brand. They staged rallies with log cabins, cider barrels, banners, and parades, and sang “Tippecanoe and Tyler Too.” Horace Greeley edited a Whig campaign paper called The Log Cabin. About 80 percent of eligible voters turned out, a record at the time, and Harrison, the son of a wealthy Virginia planter family, won as a man of the people. Political participation had become a form of popular entertainment.

Instructor note

[Instructor note: The lecture script pairs Amos Kendall with the Globe. Kendall helped launch and guide the paper, but Blair was its editor. Kendall became Postmaster General in 1835, which matters for the abolitionist mail controversy discussed in the post office section.]

Discussion prompt

Document analysis: Look at a political cartoon or broadside from the 1830s or 1840. What does it ask the viewer to feel? How much does it tell you about the candidates’ actual policies?

Analytical significance

The partisan press brought millions of ordinary white men into politics and made elections exciting public events. It also rewarded image over argument and deepened division. Wider participation and sharper partisanship arrived together, a paradox that has shaped American democracy ever since.

Background: Reform Print and the Struggle Over Taste

Instructor note

[Instructor note: The current Lecture 1 deck no longer has a slide on reform print; the American Tract Society and Lydia Maria Child do not appear on its slides. This section is kept as background because it links the penny press to Lecture 2’s Benevolent Empire and to the lecture’s exclusion theme. Use it if students ask how ministers responded to the penny papers, or skip it.]

To many ministers and reformers, penny papers full of murders looked like a moral danger. Their answer was not to stop printing but to print more, and better. The American Tract Society, founded in New York in 1825, bought steam-powered presses soon after it began and produced millions of short pamphlets on salvation, temperance, the Sabbath, and proper behavior. From 1841 it sent traveling agents called colporteurs into rural areas to sell or give away its books and pray with families. Historian David Paul Nord argues that such religious publishers were among the first true mass-media organizations in the country.

Women writers also used print to teach. Lydia Maria Child wrote The Frugal Housewife (1829), a best-selling guide to thrifty household management, and edited a children’s magazine, the Juvenile Miscellany. Catharine Maria Sedgwick wrote novels such as Home (1835) that modeled good family life. Both believed that reading could shape character, so reading the right things was a civic duty. Print could also carry reform into dangerous territory. In 1833 Child published An Appeal in Favor of That Class of Americans Called Africans, an attack on slavery and racial prejudice. Sales of her other books fell, the Boston Athenaeum withdrew her library privileges, and so many parents cancelled subscriptions that she gave up the Juvenile Miscellany in 1834.

Discussion prompt

Document analysis: Compare a Sun crime story with an American Tract Society tale. How do tone and vocabulary differ? What does each assume about its readers? Was the new mass press a tool of empowerment or of social control?

Conclusion: Who Is in the Booth?

The lecture closes with a painting rather than a summary. In Richard Caton Woodville’s Politics in an Oyster House (1848), two men sit in the curtained booth of a cheap oyster cellar. A young man with a penny paper in his hand counts off his political arguments on his fingers, while an older man listens, half amused. Students first answer three questions about the image, and only then see the lecture’s main argument.

The three questions add up to the whole lecture. What is the young man holding, and what is it doing to the conversation? A penny paper, and it is what starts the argument: he is reading from it and arguing back. Where are these men, and who are they? Two ordinary men in an ordinary place, not statesmen in a legislature. Print turned everyday places, the tavern in Krimmel’s painting, the post-office porch in War News from Mexico, and here the oyster cellar, into a debating public. Who is not in the booth? Women, Black Americans, and the very poor. The first two answers are the first half of the argument: cheap print gave ordinary people in ordinary places a shared set of news to argue about, which created a new public and a new American identity. The third answer is the second half: that public had walls, and access remained structured by race, class, and gender.

Analytical significance

Main argument: print didn’t just spread information; it created a new kind of public and a new kind of American identity. But democratization had limits: access remained structured by race, class, and gender.

Interpretive Synthesis: The People and the Printed Page

Between 1815 and 1850 the United States became a nation of readers. A literate white population, a federal postal network, steam presses, cheaper paper, and advertising combined to make print abundant, and that print created a public sphere in which strangers could share news and argue about it. Benjamin Day’s Sun proved that news could be sold to working people for a penny, and the Moon Hoax and the Jewett murder showed how quickly the new press could turn science and crime into shared public drama. Cheap books, sentimental novels, and lithographs carried the same change into fiction and images. Lyceums made learning a performance, from Emerson’s philosophy to phrenology and temperance confessions. Party newspapers made elections a spectacle that drew record numbers of voters.

So what happened when ordinary Americans gained direct access to print? They became a public: people who read the same stories at the same time, argued about them, and expected their tastes and opinions to matter. But the new culture also created new gatekeepers, such as editors chasing sales, party managers chasing votes, and moralists trying to police what the people should read and feel. And it was bounded by exclusion, most brutally in the South, where reading itself was made a crime for enslaved people. Next time, we will see how the emotional energy of the sentimental novel and the reform lecture overflowed into the revival tent of the Second Great Awakening.

The open question is whether the market for print expanded democracy or turned citizens into consumers, the worry Habermas raised. The penny press gave working people an audience but taught them to want sensation; the lyceum educated audiences but had to entertain them; the partisan press brought men to the polls, often with slogans rather than arguments. The evidence supports both readings. What it rules out is the idea that culture in this era was simply handed down from the top, or simply created from the bottom.

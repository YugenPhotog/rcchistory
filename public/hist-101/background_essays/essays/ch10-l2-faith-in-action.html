The Second Great Awakening and the remaking of American religion, reform, and family life

Essential question

What happened to American religion, reform, and family life when ordinary believers claimed the authority to speak for God?

LECTURE LEARNING OBJECTIVES

  1. Explain how camp meetings, itinerant preachers, and Arminian theology democratized religious authority in the Second Great Awakening.
  2. Analyze Charles Finney’s “new measures” and the Burned-over District as products of a society reshaped by the market revolution.
  3. Describe how women, African Americans, and new religious movements used revival energy to claim spiritual authority.
  4. Evaluate competing interpretations of the link between revival and reform, including Gordon Wood’s, Paul Johnson’s, and Gertrude Himmelfarb’s.
  5. Assess the Cult of Domesticity as a middle-class ideal, and explain how working-class and free Black women lived outside it.

Introductory Framing

Lecture 1 followed cheap print as it turned ordinary Americans into a reading public. Lecture 2 follows the same democratic energy into religion. Between roughly 1800 and 1850, waves of revival swept the United States, new denominations grew from nothing into giants, and entirely new faiths were founded on American soil. Historians call this the Second Great Awakening. Its defining feature was not any single doctrine. It was a shift in authority. Learned ministers, educated at Harvard, Yale, or Princeton and supported by taxes or wealthy congregations, lost ground to preachers with little schooling, to lay exhorters, to women who testified in prayer meetings, and to Black congregations that governed themselves.

That shift had consequences far beyond church walls. Revival preaching taught that individuals could choose salvation and that converted Christians had a duty to fight sin in the world. Those two ideas powered the great reform movements of the antebellum North: temperance, Sabbath observance, prison and asylum reform, abolition, and eventually women’s rights. Revivalism also reshaped the family. As the market revolution separated paid work from the household, middle-class Americans imagined the home as a moral sanctuary and the mother as its priest. That ideal, which historians call the Cult of Domesticity, gave women real moral authority and also confined them. It also described only a fraction of American women.

This essay follows the lecture’s path from the revival tent to the reform society to the parlor, and then beyond the parlor to the mill floor and the Black church. Throughout, it keeps one question in view: when religious authority spread to ordinary people, who gained a voice, who was disciplined, and who remained outside?

I. From Established Churches to a Religious Marketplace

The Revolution had unsettled American religion. The Church of England, established in several colonies, lost its status and much of its clergy. Virginia disestablished its church with the 1786 Statute for Religious Freedom, and the First Amendment barred a national church. A few New England states kept tax support for Congregational churches into the nineteenth century; Massachusetts was the last to end it, in 1833. Elite Americans flirted with Deism, a rational faith that saw God as a distant creator. Many clergy feared that the new republic was sliding into irreligion just as settlers poured west, beyond the reach of established churches.

Disestablishment turned out to be an opportunity. Without state support, churches had to compete for members, and the denominations best suited to competition grew fastest. In 1776, by the estimate of sociologists Roger Finke and Rodney Stark, Congregationalists, Episcopalians, and Presbyterians together claimed a majority of church adherents, and Methodists only about 2.5 percent. By 1850 Methodists made up about a third of all church adherents, the largest denomination in the country, and Baptists were second. The old colonial establishments had been overtaken by upstarts that met people where they lived.

Historian Nathan Hatch calls this the democratization of American Christianity. The winners spoke plainly, sang popular hymns, licensed preachers without seminary training, and told ordinary people that their religious experience counted as much as a minister’s learning. The losers were not irreligious; they were outcompeted. The religious marketplace, in other words, was another product of the same democratic culture that produced the penny press.

Analytical significance

The Second Great Awakening did not simply make Americans more religious. It changed who held religious authority, moving it from educated, often state-supported clergy toward popular preachers and ordinary believers.

II. Camp Meetings, Circuit Riders, and Arminian Theology

Cane Ridge and the Camp Meeting

In August 1801, at Cane Ridge in Bourbon County, Kentucky, a Presbyterian communion service hosted by minister Barton W. Stone grew into one of the largest gatherings the early republic had seen. Contemporary estimates ranged from about 10,000 to 25,000 people, astonishing figures for a state whose largest town held only a couple of thousand residents. Presbyterian, Methodist, and Baptist preachers spoke at once from stumps and wagons for nearly a week. Observers described worshipers who wept, shouted, fell to the ground, or shook uncontrollably with what they called “the jerks.” Critics saw disorder; participants saw the power of God made visible.

The camp meeting became a frontier institution, especially among Methodists. Families traveled long distances, camped for days, and joined a temporary community of prayer, preaching, and song. For isolated farm families, the meeting was also a rare social event. Its emotional style mattered: it treated feeling, not learning, as evidence of grace, and it let ordinary people, including women and enslaved people, testify in public.

The Circuit Rider

Methodist success rested on organization as much as emotion. Under Bishop Francis Asbury, Methodists divided the country into circuits. Young, often unmarried preachers rode those circuits on horseback, preaching almost daily in cabins, barns, and courthouses, and leaving behind small classes led by local laymen. The system was cheap, mobile, and expandable; it could follow settlers faster than any seminary-trained clergy could. Circuit riders were famously poorly paid and worn out early, and many died young. Their plain sermons, delivered in the idiom of their listeners, embodied the democratic style Hatch describes.

From Predestination to Choice

Underneath the style was a theological shift. Traditional Calvinism, the inheritance of New England Puritanism and Presbyterianism, taught predestination: God had already chosen the elect, and no human effort could change that choice. Methodists and many revivalists preached instead a theology associated with the Dutch theologian Jacobus Arminius: Christ died for all, grace is offered to everyone, and individuals can accept or reject it. Many Baptists remained formally Calvinist, and the lines were never neat, but the revival message increasingly sounded like an invitation to choose.

That message fit a society that celebrated self-made men and voluntary association. If salvation was a decision, then every person stood equal before God and responsible for his or her own soul. Religion became something you did, not only something done to you.

Think-Pair-Share: Why might emotional, accessible religion appeal more to farm families on the frontier than intellectual theology delivered by a learned minister? What would they gain, and what might they lose?

III. Finney and the Burned-over District

The Lawyer Who Argued for God

Charles Grandison Finney brought revival from the backwoods to the towns and cities of the North. Born in 1792 and trained as a lawyer in upstate New York, he experienced a dramatic conversion in 1821 and began preaching with a courtroom style: direct, logical, and personal, as if pleading a case before a jury of sinners. He rejected the idea that sinners must wait passively for God to act. His most famous lectures argued the opposite:

Primary source

A revival is not a miracle, nor dependent on a miracle, in any sense. It is a purely philosophical result of the right use of the constituted means.

— Charles G. Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835), Lecture I

In the same lecture Finney put it more bluntly still: “Religion is the work of man. It is something for man to do.” Revival, in his view, could be produced by the right methods. Those methods became known as the “new measures”: protracted meetings that ran night after night for weeks; the “anxious bench,” a front pew where those under conviction sat in full view of the congregation; praying for sinners by name; and allowing women to pray aloud in mixed meetings. Critics, including many Presbyterian and Congregational ministers, called the measures theatrical and manipulative. Lyman Beecher and others met with Finney at New Lebanon, New York, in 1827 to try to rein him in, without success.

Finney’s greatest triumph came in Rochester, New York, in the winter of 1830–31. Rochester was a boomtown created by the Erie Canal, full of merchants, master craftsmen, and journeymen. Over roughly six months, hundreds joined the town’s churches, and the revival rippled outward through the region. Finney later served as professor and then president of Oberlin College in Ohio, the first American college to admit both women and Black students on a regular basis, linking revival to education and reform.

Why Upstate New York?

Western New York saw so many revivals in the 1820s–40s that it earned the nickname “burned-over district”; Finney himself spoke of a “burnt district,” meaning a region where revival fires had passed through so often that little spiritual fuel remained. Whitney Cross’s classic study, The Burned-over District (1950), argued that the region’s intensity came not from frontier poverty but from rapid change: Yankee migrants from New England, the Erie Canal (completed 1825), fast commercial growth, and a population that was literate, mobile, and anxious. Upstate New York produced Mormonism, Millerism, Spiritualism, the Oneida Community, and the 1848 women’s rights convention at Seneca Falls.

Wood and Johnson Read Rochester

Two influential interpretations frame the lecture’s historiography. Paul E. Johnson’s A Shopkeeper’s Millennium (1978) studied Rochester closely. In the 1820s, he argued, the old system in which journeymen lived and drank in their masters’ households broke down. Workers moved into separate neighborhoods, and employers lost direct control over their behavior. Finney’s revival offered masters and merchants a solution: converted men internalized the discipline of sobriety, punctuality, and self-control. Converted employers then pressed temperance and Sabbath observance on their workers. In Johnson’s account, revival served the needs of an emerging capitalist middle class.

Gordon S. Wood, in The Radicalism of the American Revolution (1992) and Empire of Liberty (2009), reads the same period as a democratic explosion. The Revolution’s attack on hierarchy freed ordinary people to reject inherited authority in religion as in politics. Revival churches and voluntary societies were schools of self-government in which citizens learned to organize, persuade, and act. Where Johnson sees social control, Wood sees the making of virtuous republican citizens.

The two readings need not cancel out. Johnson explains why employers embraced revival; Wood explains why millions of ordinary people did so too. Mary P. Ryan’s Cradle of the Middle Class (1981), which studied nearby Oneida County, complicates both by showing that women, especially mothers, often led their families into the churches. Revival was a family strategy as much as a class strategy.

IV. Who Could Speak for God? Women, Black Churches, and New Faiths

Women in the Churches

Women made up a majority of church members across the North, often around two-thirds in New England congregations, and they were frequently the first converts in a household. Revival culture opened roles beyond the pew: women led prayer meetings, organized maternal associations and missionary societies, raised money, and in Finney’s meetings prayed aloud before mixed audiences. A few went further. Among Methodists and smaller sects, women such as Jarena Lee preached as itinerant exhorters, usually without formal license. Almost no denomination ordained women. The best-known exception came in 1853, when Antoinette Brown was ordained by a Congregational church in South Butler, New York. Phoebe Palmer, the influential Methodist holiness teacher, led famous Tuesday Meetings in New York and wrote widely, but she was never ordained. (The deck has been corrected on this point.)

Black Churches and Black Preachers

For African Americans, the democratization of religion meant independence. In Philadelphia, Richard Allen, Absalom Jones, and other Black Methodists walked out of St. George’s Methodist Church after white officials pulled Black worshipers from their knees during prayer for kneeling in the wrong section, an incident traditionally dated to 1787 and placed by some historians in the early 1790s. Allen founded Bethel Church in 1794, and in 1816 Bethel and other congregations formed the African Methodist Episcopal Church, the first independent Black denomination in the United States, with Allen as its first bishop. The AME Zion Church followed in New York in 1821. These churches were also schools, mutual-aid societies, and centers of antislavery organizing.

Jarena Lee tested the limits of that independence. She felt called to preach, and Allen at first refused her. Around 1819 he allowed her to exhort after she rose spontaneously during a service, and she went on to travel thousands of miles as a preacher. Her autobiography, The Life and Religious Experience of Jarena Lee (1836), is one of the earliest published autobiographies by an African American woman.

In the South, enslaved people built what Albert J. Raboteau, in Slave Religion (1978), called the “invisible institution”: secret worship in brush arbors and cabins, where enslaved Christians preached a God of deliverance rather than the obedience their enslavers’ ministers taught. (Chapter 9’s background essay on slave religion develops this story.) Revival culture, with its emphasis on direct experience of the Spirit, gave Black preachers and exhorters authority that no slaveholder had granted.

New Religious Movements

The democratic logic of revival also produced entirely new faiths. In 1830 Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon in upstate New York and organized the church that became the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Persecution drove the Mormons to Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in 1844; Brigham Young led the main body to the Great Salt Lake in 1847. William Miller, a Baptist farmer, calculated from biblical prophecy that Christ would return around 1843–44. Tens of thousands of Millerites prepared; when October 22, 1844 passed, they called it the Great Disappointment, and some regrouped into what became the Seventh-day Adventist Church. The Shakers, founded by Ann Lee, who arrived from England in 1774, practiced celibacy, communal property, and ecstatic dance. John Humphrey Noyes founded the Oneida Community in 1848 with radical ideas about property and marriage. Spiritualism, launched by the Fox sisters’ reported spirit rappings in Hydesville, New York, in 1848, promised conversation with the dead.

Some reformers also practiced “come-outerism,” withdrawing from churches they considered corrupted by slavery or worldliness. Taken together, these movements show the democratization of religion at its most radical: if anyone could read the Bible and receive revelation, then anyone could found a church.

Discussion prompt

Self-study: Why would celibacy (the Shakers) or the expectation of the world’s end (the Millerites) attract followers during an era of rapid economic growth and optimism? Consider the anxieties that rapid change can bring.

V. Providence and the Reform Impulse

A Providential Worldview

Most antebellum Protestants believed that God actively governed history, rewarding and punishing nations as well as individuals. Epidemics, fires, financial panics, and military victories were read as divine messages. When cholera struck in 1849, President Zachary Taylor proclaimed a national day of fasting, humiliation, and prayer for the first Friday in August. The idea that God had a special plan for the United States also fed expansionism; in 1845 an essay usually attributed to the editor John L. O’Sullivan spoke of “our manifest destiny to overspread the continent allotted by Providence.” A providential worldview could inspire humility, but it could also sanctify conquest.

From Saving Souls to Saving Society

Revivalists taught that a converted Christian could not be content with personal salvation. Drawing on the eighteenth-century theologian Samuel Hopkins, many embraced disinterested benevolence: true faith showed itself in selfless action for others. Some went further, toward perfectionism, the belief that Christians could overcome sin, and toward postmillennialism, the hope that reforming the world would prepare the way for Christ’s thousand-year reign. (The deck originally called this idea the “social gospel,” a term that properly belongs to a movement of the 1880s–1910s; it has been corrected.)

The result was a dense network of voluntary societies that critics and later historians called the Benevolent Empire: the American Bible Society (1816), the American Sunday School Union (1824), the American Tract Society (1825), the American Temperance Society (1826), and many more. They borrowed the tools of Lecture 1’s print revolution, flooding the country with Bibles, tracts, and pledges. Temperance was the largest movement. W. J. Rorabaugh’s The Alcoholic Republic (1979) estimates that around 1830 Americans aged fifteen and older drank about seven gallons of pure alcohol a year, roughly three times today’s level; by the mid-1840s consumption had fallen by more than half. Abolitionism drew on the same moral language. William Lloyd Garrison began The Liberator in 1831, and the American Anti-Slavery Society formed in 1833, defining slavery as a sin requiring immediate repentance.

Three Readings of Reform

Wood reads reform societies as democratic experiments in which citizens practiced self-government. Johnson reads them as instruments of middle-class social control, teaching workers sobriety and punctuality. Gertrude Himmelfarb, in The De-Moralization of Society (1995), offers a third view drawn mostly from Victorian Britain: the virtues reformers promoted, such as sobriety, thrift, and self-discipline, genuinely improved the lives of many poor families, and working people often embraced them for their own reasons. Students should test each interpretation against the evidence. Temperance could be coercive and condescending, and it could also protect wives and children from drunken violence. Both things were true.

Analytical significance

Revival created moral energy; the emerging middle class gave it organization and money. Whether that partnership expanded democracy, disciplined workers, or both depends on whose experience you center.

VI. The Middle Class and the Moral Household

A New Class

The market revolution created new kinds of work. Clerks, shopkeepers, small manufacturers, lawyers, and doctors earned salaries or profits rather than wages, worked with their heads more than their hands, and increasingly left home each morning for an office or shop. Stuart Blumin’s The Emergence of the Middle Class (1989) argues that this “middling” group defined itself through everyday practices: where it lived, how it furnished its parlors, what it read, and how it raised children. Irvin Wyllie’s The Self-Made Man in America (1954) traced the gospel of success that celebrated industry, sobriety, and thrift. Revival religion supplied the moral vocabulary.

From Republican Motherhood to the Cult of Domesticity

Historian Linda Kerber coined the term Republican Motherhood for the post-Revolutionary ideal that women served the nation by raising virtuous citizen sons. It justified better education for girls while keeping women out of politics. In the following generation, as paid work left the home, a new ideal took shape. Barbara Welter’s 1966 essay “The Cult of True Womanhood, 1820–1860” identified its four cardinal virtues as piety, purity, submissiveness, and domesticity. Nancy Cott’s The Bonds of Womanhood (1977) showed that the ideology of “separate spheres” both confined women and gave them a shared identity that could become a basis for activism.

The ideal was broadcast through print: advice manuals, sermons, and magazines such as Godey’s Lady’s Book, founded in 1830 and edited from 1837 by Sarah Josepha Hale. Catharine Beecher’s A Treatise on Domestic Economy (1841) treated housekeeping as a science and a moral calling. In this vision the home was a refuge from the competitive, corrupting market, and the mother was its spiritual guardian, leading family prayers, teaching scripture, and shaping her children’s consciences. Domestic novels carried the same message to a mass audience: Susan Warner’s The Wide, Wide World (1850) and Harriet Beecher Stowe’s Uncle Tom’s Cabin (1852) sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and Stowe turned the moral authority of the Christian mother into a weapon against slavery. Colleen McDannell’s The Christian Home in Victorian America (1986) shows how parlors filled with family Bibles, religious prints, and hymnals made the house itself into a kind of church.

Authority and Confinement

Was domesticity a cage or a platform? It was both. It denied women property rights in marriage, political rights, and most professions. Yet it also gave women recognized moral authority, and many used that authority to justify public action: benevolent societies, temperance, antislavery petitions, and eventually demands for rights. The Grimké sisters, Lucretia Mott, and Elizabeth Cady Stanton moved from moral reform to women’s rights. The 1848 Declaration of Sentiments at Seneca Falls grew directly out of that path. Ann Douglas (The Feminization of American Culture, 1977) and Jane Tompkins (Sensational Designs, 1985) debated whether sentimental, women-centered religious culture weakened or empowered American Protestantism, a debate that connects this lecture to Lecture 1’s discussion of sentimental fiction.

Discussion prompt

Document analysis: Read a short passage from Catharine Beecher or from Godey’s Lady’s Book. What powers does it claim for women? What does it deny them? Who could actually live the life it describes?

VII. Beyond Domesticity: Mill Women and Free Black Women

A Middle-Class Ideal

The Cult of Domesticity assumed a husband whose income could support the family, servants or at least time for household management, and the literacy to read advice books. Most American women had none of these. Enslaved women were denied control over their own families. Farm women did heavy productive labor. Urban working-class women, as Christine Stansell’s City of Women (1986) shows for New York, took in sewing, sold goods on the street, and worked as servants, and middle-class reformers often judged them as immoral for doing so.

The Lowell Mill Women

In the textile mills of Lowell, Massachusetts, thousands of young New England farm women worked twelve- to fourteen-hour days and lived in company boardinghouses under strict moral rules. Thomas Dublin’s Women at Work (1979) shows that many came for wages to help their families, pay for schooling, or gain independence before marriage. They also produced the Lowell Offering (1840–45), a literary magazine written by mill women. When the companies cut wages, the women resisted. About 800 turned out in 1834, and in 1836 about 1,500 struck against a rise in board charges. Harriet Hanson Robinson, then eleven, later recalled telling her fellow workers, “I don’t care what you do, I am going to turn out, whether any one else does or not.” In the 1840s Sarah Bagley and the Lowell Female Labor Reform Association petitioned the legislature for a ten-hour day. The mill women used the language of republican independence and Christian morality to defend their dignity, borrowing the culture’s ideals for their own ends.

Free Black Women

Free Black women in Northern cities were excluded from the middle-class ideal by racism as well as by economics; most had to work for wages. Yet they built churches, schools, literary societies, and antislavery organizations. Erica Armstrong Dunbar’s A Fragile Freedom (2008) traces their world in Philadelphia. Three figures from the deck show the range of their activism.

Maria W. Stewart, born free in Hartford, Connecticut, in 1803 and orphaned young, published a religious and political pamphlet, printed by William Lloyd Garrison’s press, in 1831, and gave a series of public speeches in Boston in 1832–33. She is often called the first American-born woman to lecture publicly to audiences of both men and women on political questions.

Primary source

O, ye daughters of Africa, awake! awake! arise! no longer sleep nor slumber, but distinguish yourselves.

— Maria W. Stewart, Religion and the Pure Principles of Morality (1831)

Criticism from within Boston’s Black community pushed her to give a farewell address in 1833, after which she moved to New York and worked as a teacher. Jarena Lee claimed the right to preach on the authority of her calling. Sarah Mapps Douglass, a Philadelphia teacher from a prominent Black family, helped found the interracial Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society in 1833, taught Black children for decades, headed the girls’ department of the Institute for Colored Youth from 1853, and later studied medicine and lectured on health and physiology to Black women. (The deck had called her a physician; it has been corrected.) These women used revival religion’s promise of spiritual equality to claim public voices that domestic ideology denied them.

Analytical significance

The Cult of Domesticity was a powerful ideal but not a description of most women’s lives. Mill women and free Black women adapted its moral language to claim dignity, wages, education, and freedom on their own terms.

Closing Synthesis

The Second Great Awakening democratized religious authority. Camp meetings, circuit riders, and Arminian preaching made faith a matter of personal choice and feeling. Finney turned revival into a method and brought it to the boomtowns of the market revolution. Women, Black Americans, and the founders of new faiths claimed the right to speak for God. Revival’s moral energy flowed into the Benevolent Empire and the great reform movements, and into a new middle-class ideal of the home as a sanctuary governed by a pious mother.

Historians still argue over what it all meant. Wood sees democratic citizens in the making; Johnson sees a middle class disciplining its workers; Himmelfarb sees virtues that helped the poor; Cott and Ryan see women building authority within limits. The strongest answer holds these views together. Revival expanded who could speak, while reformers also tried to control how others lived. And the ideal of the moral household was built on the labor and exclusion of women who could never live inside it. Lecture 3 turns from the church and the parlor to the theater, the minstrel stage, the prize ring, and Barnum’s museum, places where many of the same Americans went to be entertained and where reformers feared their moral project was being undone.

Suggested Reading for Deeper Preparation

Nathan O. Hatch, The Democratization of American Christianity (1989). The essential account of how popular preachers and upstart denominations overthrew the authority of the learned clergy.

Paul E. Johnson, A Shopkeeper’s Millennium: Society and Revivals in Rochester, New York, 1815–1837 (1978). A short, influential case study linking Finney’s revival to class relations in a canal boomtown.

Gordon S. Wood, Empire of Liberty: A History of the Early Republic, 1789–1815 (2009). Broad synthesis that reads revival and voluntary association as part of the Revolution’s democratic legacy.

Whitney R. Cross, The Burned-over District (1950). The classic regional study of upstate New York’s religious enthusiasm.

Mary P. Ryan, Cradle of the Middle Class: The Family in Oneida County, New York, 1790–1865 (1981). Shows women and families at the center of revival and middle-class formation.

Nancy F. Cott, The Bonds of Womanhood: “Woman’s Sphere” in New England, 1780–1835 (1977). How separate-spheres ideology both limited women and created the basis for collective action.

Barbara Welter, “The Cult of True Womanhood, 1820–1860,” American Quarterly 18 (1966). The article that named the ideal; still useful for its prescriptive sources.

Linda K. Kerber, Women of the Republic (1980). Origin of the concept of Republican Motherhood.

Thomas Dublin, Women at Work (1979); Christine Stansell, City of Women (1986). Working women in Lowell and New York, and how they lived outside the domestic ideal.

Erica Armstrong Dunbar, A Fragile Freedom: African American Women and Emancipation in the Antebellum City (2008). Free Black women’s activism, faith, and community in Philadelphia.

Albert J. Raboteau, Slave Religion: The “Invisible Institution” in the Antebellum South (1978). Foundational study of enslaved people’s Christianity.

Gertrude Himmelfarb, The De-Moralization of Society (1995). A conservative defense of Victorian virtues; best read alongside Johnson as a deliberate counterpoint.

2. Textbook baseline

The assigned chapter already carries unusually broad survey responsibility. It supplies the 1776 New York crisis, Trenton and Princeton, the 1777 campaigns, French intervention, the global and southern wars, Loyalist and Native choices, Black freedom-seeking, women and religion, state constitutions, the Articles of Confederation, the Northwest Ordinance, Shays’s Rebellion, Philadelphia, ratification, and the Bill of Rights. Its review and critical-thinking questions ask students to explain French intervention, British defeat in the South, social transformation, state constitutionalism, the 1780s crisis, and whether the Constitution fulfilled or restrained the Revolution.

Lecture therefore should not retell campaign chronology or reproduce the textbook’s constitutional survey. Its proper contribution is to organize that breadth around four historical problems: why battlefield success did not restore British authority; how unequal actors made revolutionary claims enforceable; how popular sovereignty became an institution; and why Americans disagreed over whether liberty was endangered by excessive or insufficient governmental power.

One textbook formulation deserves caution rather than direct repetition: fixed thirds for Patriot, Loyalist, and neutral allegiance flatten a fluid and coercive civil war. The essays already correct this by treating allegiance as situational, local, and reversible.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

War as political restoration, civil conflict, and global coalition

The newest major synthesis reinforces, rather than overturns, Essay 1. The Cambridge History’s battlefield, civil-war, alliance, and global-war chapters reject a nation-centered march toward victory. They emphasize subordinate actors, contingency, disease and environment, local coercion, multiple overlapping wars, and the expansion of the conflict across the Caribbean, Africa, Europe, and South Asia. Essay 1 already combines military survival, local allegiance, British political constraints, and coalition warfare; its structure remains current.

Recent synthesis: Cambridge History of the American Revolution, vol. 2.

Campaign contingency: Woody Holton, ‘The New Battlefield History’.

Multiple war frames: Nicolas Bell-Romero, ‘The Civil War’.

Global scale: Stephen Conway, ‘Global Warfare’.

The strongest narrative sequence remains December 1776: Washington writes that the game may be up, crosses the Delaware, restores a possibility rather than conquering territory, and preserves enough military and political credibility for Saratoga and alliance. The 1778 treaty then supplies evidence that France guaranteed American independence while pursuing its own strategic interests.

Primary source: George Washington to Lund Washington, 10–17 December 1776.

Primary source: Treaty of Alliance with France, 6 February 1778.

Claims, coercion, Black freedom, and Native sovereignty

Essay 2’s claims-versus-power framework is the chapter’s strongest interpretive achievement. Current scholarship continues to treat independence as a contested process shaped by extralegal action, print, popular loyalism, enslaved flight, gendered labor, and Indigenous calculation. Scholarship on Black women’s fugitivity supplies the one warranted addition: flight was frequently family-centered and gendered, not merely a male military response.

Current framing of independence: Michelle Orihel, ‘Independence’.

Black women’s fugitivity: Karen Cook Bell, Running from Bondage.

The Book of Negroes is the most important primary-source discovery for this audit. It records about three thousand Black refugees leaving New York for Nova Scotia, while the case of Betty—returned to Thomas Smith after he asserted ownership—shows why official recognition and evidence were decisive. The document can carry a lecture scene without converting British policy into a simple emancipation story.

Primary source: Nova Scotia Archives, Book of Negroes.

Native claims require a different analytical category. The existing essay correctly distinguishes rights within the United States from a nation’s right not to be ruled by the United States. Calloway, Taylor, and Blackhawk confirm that Native wartime alliances and postwar diplomacy were efforts to preserve collective sovereignty; the Treaty of Paris did not make British cession equivalent to Native consent.

Native community histories: Colin G. Calloway, The American Revolution in Indian Country.

Postwar borderland sovereignty: Alan Taylor, The Divided Ground.

Continental synthesis: Ned Blackhawk, The Rediscovery of America.

States, rights, and the contingency of union

The newest scholarship strengthens Essay 3’s rejection of teleology. Van Gosse emphasizes that the United States began as a contingent union of sovereign states and that rights remained fluid, state-specific, and increasingly structured by race. The essay already teaches Pennsylvania and Massachusetts as competing diagnoses of political danger, treats popular sovereignty as a procedural achievement, and presents the Articles as an intelligible republican design rather than an inexplicable failure.

Recent historiography: Van Gosse, ‘States and Rights’.

The Constitutional Design Workshop is particularly strong because it makes students diagnose a danger, choose institutions, stress-test them, revise, and only then compare Pennsylvania and Massachusetts. The primary sources support comparison without turning the session into abstract civics.

Primary source: Pennsylvania Constitution of 1776.

Primary source: Massachusetts Constitution of 1780.

Primary source: Articles of Confederation.

Primary source: Northwest Ordinance of 1787.

Postwar political economies and the constitutional bargain

Essay 4 is already current enough to include Hannah Farber’s 2026 argument that the founding era contained multiple competing political economies rather than a single conflict between two fixed economic programs. It accurately treats public credit, taxation, debtor relief, commerce, treaty enforcement, and regional interests as competing diagnoses of crisis; distinguishes Shays’s Rebellion from the prior nationalist movement; and places slavery and territorial power inside rather than outside the constitutional settlement.

Newest scholarship: Hannah Farber, ‘Political Economies’.

The strongest evidence pairing is not Constitution-as-answer but competing diagnoses. A Shays petition or court-closing record can establish rural grievance; Federalist No. 10 explains the fear of faction; Brutus I explains consolidation and distance; the Constitution shows the powers granted; and the Bill of Rights shows how losing objections reshaped the settlement.

Primary-source context: Massachusetts Historical Society, Shays’ Rebellion document discussion.

Primary source: United States Constitution transcript.

Primary source: Federalist No. 10.

Primary source: Brutus I.

Primary source: Bill of Rights transcript.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

Verdict: PASS

The roadmap complements the textbook and creates a coherent intellectual arc: survival makes independence possible; independence multiplies claims; claims require legitimate institutions; institutions reopen the problem of power. Each question is worth lecture time, none is excessively abstract once carried by its narrative and source architecture, and the chapter moves naturally among cause, context, consequence, comparison, and evidence without quotas.

Lecture 1’s engine is Washington’s December 1776 crisis and the reversal from near-collapse to coalition war. Lecture 2’s engine is flight toward British lines and the institutional decision that could honor or revoke a promise. Lecture 3’s engine is the student design-and-stress-test workshop, followed by the Pennsylvania/Massachusetts comparison. Lecture 4’s engine is the Springfield confrontation and the Federalist/Anti-Federalist battle over where tyranny was most likely to arise.

The roadmap also respects assignment division of labor. Lecture 4 should model competing diagnoses and source evaluation, but the graded Convention and Ratification analysis should remain the place where students sustain the documentary argument. No roadmap adjustment is approved or needed.

6. Individual professor-background essay audits

Essay 1 — Britain Kept Winning. Why Did the Revolution Not End?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy and causation: the essay correctly separates battlefield victory, occupation, destruction of military capacity, and political restoration; treats British conciliation as intelligible; avoids fixed allegiance categories; and makes French naval and imperial rivalry indispensable without erasing American survival. Historiography: the 2026 Cambridge synthesis confirms its multiscalar and contingent interpretation. Primary sources and narrative: Washington’s December letter, the Delaware sequence, the alliance treaty, and Yorktown’s coalition mechanics provide abundant material. Historical thinking arises naturally through Cause, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. No substantive change is warranted.

Essay 2 — Liberty Was Declared. Who Could Claim It?

Verdict: PASS WITH LIMITED SUBSTANTIVE ADDITIONS — COMPLETED AND LOCKED

Accuracy and balance: the essay already distinguishes Black claims to freedom within or beyond the polity from Native claims to collective sovereignty; treats British and Patriot routes to freedom without romanticizing either; and integrates northern emancipation, southern persistence, racial exclusion, women, loyalists, and religion without a quota structure. Substantive changes applied in place: added one paragraph using Karen Cook Bell’s scholarship to specify the family and gender dimensions of flight and the Book of Negroes case of Betty to show how property claims and official evidence could defeat a military promise; added live links to both sources. Narrative: Betty’s attempted evacuation is the strongest new human scene. Historical thinking: Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. The authoritative Drive file retains its original ID.

Essay 3 — The King Was Gone. Who Had the Right to Rule?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy and context: the essay correctly distinguishes constituent from constituted power, avoids equating popular sovereignty with universal participation, compares Pennsylvania and Massachusetts as coherent diagnoses of danger, and treats the Articles and Northwest Ordinance as achievements carrying structural limits and an Indigenous contradiction. Historiography: Van Gosse’s 2026 state-centered synthesis reinforces its contingent union framework. Narrative and pedagogy: the design workshop supplies a genuine unfolding problem rather than abstract institutional taxonomy. Historical thinking: Comparison, Context, Cause, and Consequence. No substantive change is warranted.

Essay 4 — What Could Destroy the Republic: Too Much Power or Too Little?

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy and causation: the essay rejects the automatic critical-period narrative, treats Shays’s Rebellion as both coercive resistance and a continuation of revolutionary repertoires, shows that nationalism predated the uprising, explains the Convention’s break with the Articles, and places slavery and territorial capacity inside the bargain. Historiography: the reading apparatus already includes the newest 2026 work on competing political economies and balances governmental-incapacity, democratic-conflict, economic-interest, state-building, and ratification interpretations. Narrative and evidence: Springfield, Federalist No. 10, Brutus I, ratification promises, and the Bill of Rights provide a strong sequence. Historical thinking: Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. No substantive change is warranted.

8. Downstream issues to remember — do not implement in this audit

  • Reveal decks: preserve the narrative engines above; keep textbook chronology compressed; include exactly two designated in-class historical-thinking activities per formal lecture; mark optional Want to Go Deeper material as non-assessed under v1.2.
  • Lecture 2 deck and presenter notes / Study Buddy: integrate Bell’s family-centered fugitivity and Betty’s case; avoid presenting British policy as reliable general emancipation.
  • Lecture 3 deck and Historian’s Notebook: keep the workshop’s diagnose → design → stress-test → revise sequence; preserve the distinction between constituent power and ordinary government.
  • Lecture 4 materials: distinguish Shays’s Rebellion as catalyst and symbol from the earlier nationalist movement; teach Federalist and Anti-Federalist diagnoses before asking for judgment.
  • Think Like a Historian practice: use comparisons such as battlefield victory versus political control, rights within a polity versus sovereignty outside it, Pennsylvania versus Massachusetts, and Federalist versus Anti-Federalist risk.
  • Major assignment: do not let lecture pre-answer the Constitutional Convention and Ratification primary-source analysis; use lectures to equip students to evaluate the documents.
  • Exam materials: avoid fixed thirds for allegiance; do not assess material appearing only in Want to Go Deeper; ensure every constitutional or source-evaluation task has been modeled upstream.

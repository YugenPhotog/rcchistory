1. Governing standards and audit scope

This audit follows the current HIST 101 Y1 Canonical Course Production Protocol v1.1 and its addendum. Research precedes scholarly understanding, pedagogical judgment, and presentation form. The textbook is a baseline rather than a ceiling. The threshold for revision is substantive: consequential accuracy, historiography, causation, context, interpretive balance, primary-source depth, narrative opportunity, and historical thinking. Minor wording, citation style, and publication-level polish are not grounds for reopening an internal professor-preparation essay.

The assigned textbook chapter was read once as a whole. The standalone roadmap and four background essays were then read individually. The coursework plan was consulted to establish the major assignment. Downstream decks, notebooks, practice, assignments, and assessments were not audited or changed.

2. Textbook baseline

Chapter 4 gives students the breadth of provincial America from roughly 1720 to 1763. It covers expansion and Anglicization; household autonomy and women’s legal position; regional development in the Old South, mid-Atlantic, backcountry, and New England; immigration, print, Enlightenment, consumer culture, Georgia, and the Great Awakening; assemblies, governors, and Court/Country political language; renewed imperial warfare; Native and enslaved opportunities within those wars; and the Seven Years’ War through the Peace of Paris.

The review questions emphasize those seven section-level themes. The critical-thinking questions ask students to confront the slaveholding language of liberty and to compare explanations for the postwar imperial rupture. The reading therefore already carries chronology, institutions, broad social change, and the war’s campaign sequence. Lecture needs to add people, dilemmas, promises, Native and Black political choices, disputed evidence, contingency, and multicausal explanation rather than repeat the survey.

One textbook compression should be corrected orally: Christopher Gist—not George Washington—was the Ohio Company’s hired surveyor. Washington’s connection ran through family and elite networks, and he went west in 1753 as Governor Dinwiddie’s emissary. The existing roadmap and Essay 2 already handle this accurately.

4. Fresh chapter-wide research reconnaissance

Freedom southward: Fort Mose, Stono, and conditional sanctuary

The strongest scholarship holds two truths together: Spanish Florida offered a real legal route out of British slavery, and Spain organized that freedom through Catholic conversion, military service, and imperial security. Fort Mose is therefore neither a cynical fiction nor a modern liberal refuge. Menéndez’s repeated movement between enslavement, militia command, emancipation, capture, and return supplies the human narrative. Thornton’s Kongo interpretation remains valuable when stated as an argument from Atlantic context and fragmentary colonial evidence rather than certainty about every rebel.

Foundational monograph: Jane Landers, Black Society in Spanish Florida.

Stono’s African and evidentiary dimensions: John K. Thornton, ‘African Dimensions of the Stono Rebellion’.

Archaeological evidence: Florida Museum, Fort Mose historical archaeology.

The Ohio Country as a Native political world

Recent continental and Indigenous-centered scholarship reinforces the essay’s central claim: the Ohio Valley was not empty space between France and Britain. Multiethnic towns used kinship, trade, migration, and diplomacy to widen political options. The French label ‘republics’ described unwelcome independence, not a European constitutional form. Haudenosaunee claims, Ohio residents’ sovereignty, French forts, and British land companies overlapped without becoming equivalent. The essay’s distinction between British ‘liberty’ and Native demands for land, trade, sovereignty, and kept obligations is exactly the right interpretive safeguard.

Shawnee mobility and political formation: Stephen Warren, The Worlds the Shawnees Made.

Negotiated imperial framework: Richard White, The Middle Ground.

Women, agriculture, and Ohio prosperity: Susan Sleeper-Smith, Indigenous Prosperity and American Conquest.

Current continental synthesis: Kathleen DuVal, Native Nations.

1755: coalition failure, battlefield contingency, and removal

The best military history rejects the old caricature of Braddock as simply incompetent. Road building and logistics were serious achievements; diplomatic failure deprived the expedition of allies and intelligence; surprise, terrain, command breakdown, French leadership, and disciplined Native tactics decided the battle. The reported Shingas exchange remains powerful precisely because it is layered evidence preserved through Charles Stuart’s later captivity narrative, not a verified transcript.

The Acadian deportation belongs beside, but not inside, the Braddock story. Recent work extends the chronology of eliminationist thinking before 1755 and clarifies how later labels—deportation, expulsion, ethnic cleansing, genocide—carry different descriptive, legal, memorial, and moral work. The essay already preserves the necessary distinctions among Native sovereignty, Acadian conquered status, oath politics, confiscation, settler replacement, and a decade-long diaspora.

Coalition and battlefield reconstruction: David L. Preston, Braddock’s Defeat.

Diaspora history: Christopher Hodson, The Acadian Diaspora.

Current terminological analysis: Richard LeBlanc, ‘A Terminological History of the Acadian Tragedy’.

Current settler-colonial analysis: Richard LeBlanc, ‘Settler Colonialism and the Elimination of the Acadian’.

Victory, global war, and the postwar political economy

The 2024 Oxford Handbook strengthens the global frame without displacing the chapter’s North American problem: Britain’s victory rested on naval power, continental subsidies, parliamentary credit, provincial manpower, Native diplomacy, and negotiated incentives across distinct but linked theaters. The decisive postwar mistake was to confuse a European diplomatic transfer with conquest of Native nations and to treat wartime cooperation as proof of metropolitan command.

New work on public credit and war markets materially strengthens the transition from Pitt’s bargains to postwar crisis. Merchant contracting, provincial credit, incomplete reimbursement, and austerity damaged colonial confidence in the British fiscal state. This complements rather than replaces the established explanation centered on debt, the standing army, western policy, taxation, ideology, and contingent decisions after 1763.

Current global synthesis: The Oxford Handbook of the Seven Years’ War.

Native sovereignty after 1763: Susan Sleeper-Smith, ‘Native America after 1763’.

Public credit and wartime markets: Donovan Fifield, ‘Our Own and Our Country’s Ruin’.

5. Four-lecture roadmap audit

Verdict: PASS

The roadmap complements the textbook rather than duplicating it. Its questions are worth lecture time; its arc moves coherently from conditional Black freedom, to a Native-controlled interior, to the related but non-equivalent crises of 1755, to the bargains and divergent meanings of British victory. Each lecture has a viable narrative engine and a clear student-scale question. Indigenous, African, Acadian, provincial, imperial, and military actors are structurally integrated where the history requires them, not added as quotas.

The Great Awakening assignment carries material the lectures need not duplicate. The roadmap’s short launch preserves accountability and curiosity without turning the assignment into a fifth lecture. Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence recur naturally across the chapter. The fresh research reinforces the roadmap’s corrected claims and interpretive arc; no substantive adjustment is warranted. The authoritative roadmap was therefore preserved unchanged.

6. Individual professor-background essay audits

Essay 1 — The Town Called Mose

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the sanctuary decrees, two Fort Mose phases, Menéndez chronology, Stono, Negro Act, war, and 1763 evacuation are carefully stated. Historiography: Landers, Thornton, Wood, Morgan, and recent Atlantic scholarship supply a strong foundation; fresh research confirms the interpretation. Primary sources and archaeology reveal conditional freedom, imperial strategy, and evidentiary gaps. Balance and context: the essay holds refuge and garrison, agency and constraint, Kongo context and uncertainty together. Narrative engine: Menéndez’s repeated reversals and the southward road to freedom. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Essay 2 — The Republics

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: Ohio Company/Gist, Washington’s role, Hendrik identities, Covenant Chain, Albany, and overlapping claims are responsibly distinguished. Historiography: White is used with appropriate limits and supplemented by Warren, Witgen, Shannon, Hinderaker, and McConnell. Primary sources expose Native warnings, British land ambition, visual diplomacy, and competing explanations for colonial disunion. Balance and context: kinship is treated as effective political infrastructure without romanticizing it, and Native sovereignty is not translated into British liberty. Narrative engine: French officials naming independent towns ‘republics,’ followed by Hendrik’s declaration that the chain had broken. Natural Five Moves: Comparison, Context, Cause, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Essay 3 — No Savage Should Inherit the Land

Verdict: LOCK AS-IS

Accuracy: the essay correctly identifies the Shingas quotation’s retrospective provenance, explains Braddock’s logistics and battlefield defeat without caricature, and gives defensible Acadian chronology and scale. Historiography: Preston’s coalition account, diaspora scholarship, and both recent LeBlanc articles are real and accurately represented. Primary sources make memory, land, allegiance, and removal visible while requiring source criticism. Balance and context: diplomatic failure is neither ignored nor allowed to explain every battlefield event; Braddock and Acadia are compared without being collapsed. Narrative engine: the reported land exchange, the Monongahela reversal, and families scattered after Beauséjour. Natural Five Moves: Cause, Comparison, Context, Consequence, and Evidence. Substantive change: none.

Essay 4 — The Victory That Broke the Empire

Verdict: PASS WITH LIMITED SUBSTANTIVE ADDITIONS — COMPLETED AND LOCKED

Accuracy and evidence: two missing dates weakened otherwise precise primary-source framing. Barré’s ‘Sons of Liberty’ speech is now dated 6 February 1765, and Pitt’s mobilization circular is now dated 30 December 1757. Historiography: Anderson, Calloway, Dowd, Blackhawk, DuVal, the 2024 Oxford Handbook, and Fifield’s 2025 article are accurately characterized. Balance and context: Loudoun, Pitt, Easton, global war, Native sovereignty, Fort Mose, the War of 1763–1766, debt, and taxation form a genuinely plural causal chain. Narrative engine: Wolfe and Barré’s opposed judgments, the Easton bargain, and four readings of the Peace of Paris. Natural Five Moves: all five.

Substantive changes applied in place to the authoritative Google Doc: restored the two verified dates as native date elements and added clickable stable links to every web-accessible work in the existing suggested-reading apparatus. No other prose, structure, or interpretation was changed. Connector readback confirmed both date elements, all thirteen links, the intended document, and the intended tab.

8. Narrative opportunities by lecture

Lecture 1 — The Town Called Mose. Draw one line south from Charleston to St. Augustine, then follow Francisco Menéndez through enslavement, militia command, emancipation, capture, re-enslavement, and return. The road makes freedom’s direction and conditions visible.

Lecture 2 — The Republics. Begin with the French insult ‘republics,’ reconstruct a village held together by kinship, then let Hendrik announce that the Covenant Chain is broken. The lecture becomes a story of a political system empires misunderstood.

Lecture 3 — No Savage Should Inherit the Land. Put Shingas’s reported words on screen with their provenance, then move from diplomatic refusal to the compressed British column at the Monongahela and to Acadian families placed aboard ships. The remembered quotation becomes evidence to test, not a transcript to trust.

Lecture 4 — The Victory That Broke the Empire. Open with Wolfe’s contempt and Barré’s praise, explain Loudoun and Pitt as two ways to mobilize an empire, mark the Easton bargain, and let the Peace of Paris split into four incompatible meanings. Victory itself becomes the reversal.

9. Downstream issues to remember — do not implement in this audit

Reveal lecture decks and presenter notes / Study Buddy: preserve the Gist correction; label the Shingas quotation as a later reported recollection; describe the Easton treaty’s parties, promises, and disputed scope precisely; use ‘claimed sovereignty’ rather than ‘owned’ or ‘conquered’ for Britain’s 1763 territorial settlement; and carry the corrected Barré and Pitt dates.

Historian’s Notebook and Think Like a Historian practice: preserve the distinction between British liberty and Native sovereignty, land, trade, and obligations. Make evidence work visible through Thornton’s Stono argument, the Hendrik portrait, the Stuart/Shingas source chain, Easton’s minutes, and competing postwar memories.

Chapter assignment: retain The Great Awakening as the single major primary-source analysis. The two-minute lecture launch should pose the authority question without resolving it. Do not create a second major assignment from Washington’s first war or duplicate revival content in lecture.

Exam materials: avoid the textbook’s Washington-as-Ohio-Company-surveyor compression; do not treat Fort Mose as unconditional freedom, Ohio towns as empty imperial space, Braddock’s defeat as simple European stupidity, Acadian removal as a single-day event, or the Peace of Paris as conquest of Native nations. Test plural causation rather than inevitability in the road from 1763 to Revolution.

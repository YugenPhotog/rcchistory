Before You Read

Lu Xun (1881-1936), the pen name of Zhou Shuren, is often called the founder of modern Chinese literature. As a young man he went to Japan to study medicine, and from 1904 he attended a medical college in the city of Sendai. In 1918 he published his first short story, “Diary of a Madman,” in New Youth, the Beijing magazine at the center of the New Culture Movement. In 1922 he gathered his stories into his first collection, Call to Arms, and wrote this preface to explain how he became a writer at all. Ebrey tells the same story in brief on p. 422; notice that she calls what Lu Xun saw a “newsreel,” while Lu Xun’s own words describe projected slides.

About this translation

This is a new English translation made directly from the Chinese for this course. The mark [...] shows where part of the preface has been left out. Words in square brackets are short explanations added by the translators, not Lu Xun’s own words.

How to cite the reading

Use the paragraph number in the purple badge, for example: (Preface, 1.4), (Preface, 1.6), or (Preface, 2.6). Part 1 paragraphs begin with 1, and Part 2 paragraphs begin with 2.

Reading a difficult primary source

Part 1 describes a scene of public punishment and Lu Xun’s harsh judgment of his own people. Read it as his argument, written years later for a purpose, not as a neutral report. Description is not always endorsement.

Key Terms

TermWorking definition
SendaiA city in northern Japan. Lu Xun studied at its medical college from 1904 until he left for Tokyo in 1906.
Russo-Japanese War (1904-1905)A war between Japan and Russia over control of Manchuria and Korea. Much of it was fought on Chinese soil while the Qing government stayed officially neutral. Japan’s victory shocked the world.
Lantern slideA glass slide projected onto a screen by a “magic lantern,” a lamp-and-lens projector. In the early 1900s classrooms used them to show still pictures, much as teachers use slideshows today.
New Youth (Xin qingnian)A magazine founded by Chen Duxiu in 1915 (Ebrey, p. 420). It attacked Confucian tradition, praised science and democracy, and soon printed everything in vernacular Chinese.
Qian Xuantong (“Jin Xinyi”)A Beijing University scholar and one of the editors of New Youth. In the preface Lu Xun calls him by a joking nickname, Jin Xinyi.
Iron houseThe image at the center of this preface (Preface, 2.6). Part of your job is to decide what it stands for.

Selected Readings

Read both parts in order. Part 1 explains why Lu Xun gave up medicine; Part 2, set more than ten years later, explains why he finally began to write fiction. Notice how the same question, whether waking people up can help them, runs through both.

1 The Lantern Slide

Just before this passage, Lu Xun describes his father’s long illness and death under the care of traditional doctors, and his years at a new-style school in Nanjing, where he read that Japan’s modernization had begun largely with Western medicine. Part 1 opens as he enrolls at a medical school in Japan. Watch for the moment his plans change, and for the reason he gives.

Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922), Part 1 | “Not So Important After All”

1.1

Because of this naive knowledge, I later found myself enrolled at a medical college in a country town in Japan [Sendai, in northern Japan, 1904]. My dream was a lovely one. After graduating I would come home and relieve the suffering of patients who, like my father, had been harmed by bad doctoring. In time of war I would serve as an army doctor. And at the same time I would strengthen my countrymen’s faith in reform.

1.2

I have no idea how much the teaching of microbiology has advanced since then, but in those days we were shown projected slides of what microbes looked like. So whenever a section of the lecture ended before the hour was up, the instructor would show the students pictures of scenery or current events to use up the extra time.

1.3

This was during the Russo-Japanese War [1904-1905, fought largely on Chinese soil in Manchuria], so naturally there were quite a few war pictures, and in that lecture hall I often had to go along with my classmates as they clapped and cheered.

1.4

Then one time, on one of the slides, I suddenly came face to face with a crowd of Chinese people, whom I had not seen in a long while. One man was tied up in the middle, with many others standing around him. They were all strong and sturdy in build, yet their faces showed a numb indifference.

1.5

According to the explanation, the man tied up had spied for the Russian army and was about to have his head cut off by the Japanese as a public example, and the people around him had come to enjoy the grand spectacle.

1.6

Before that school year was over, I had left for Tokyo, because after that day I felt that medicine was not so important after all. The people of an ignorant and weak nation, however healthy and sturdy their bodies, can only serve as the raw material for pointless public spectacles, or as the onlookers who watch them. However many of them die of illness, there is no need to count it a great misfortune.

1.7

So our first task was to change their spirit, and at the time I believed that the best way to change the spirit was, of course, through literature and the arts. And so I decided to start a literary movement. [...]

2 The Iron House

More than ten years later, Lu Xun was working as a Ministry of Education official in Beijing, living alone and keeping away from public life. Part 2 begins in his rooms at a provincial guild hall, where an old friend from New Youth comes to visit. Watch how the conversation turns on the word “hope.”

Lu Xun, Preface to Call to Arms (1922), Part 2 | “An Iron House with No Windows”

2.1

[...] For many years I lived in those rooms [at a guild hall in Beijing], copying inscriptions from ancient stone tablets. Visitors were rare, and in the old inscriptions I met with no “problems” and no “isms” [a jab at the political arguments of the day]. My life was quietly slipping away, and that was all I wanted.

2.2

The only one who came by now and then to talk was an old friend, Jin Xinyi [Lu Xun’s nickname for Qian Xuantong, an editor of New Youth]. He would set his big leather briefcase on my broken table, take off his long gown, and sit down across from me, his heart still seeming to pound, because he was afraid of dogs.

2.3

“What’s the use of copying these?” he asked one night, leafing through my copies of the inscriptions with a scholar’s curiosity.

2.4

“No use at all.”“Then what’s the point of copying them?”“No point at all.”“I think you could write something...”

2.5

I understood what he meant. He and his friends were running New Youth [the magazine founded by Chen Duxiu in 1915 that launched the New Culture Movement], but at that time it seemed that nobody had come forward to agree with them, and nobody had even bothered to oppose them. I guessed they must be feeling lonely. But what I said was:

2.6

“Imagine an iron house with no windows at all, one that is almost impossible to break open. Inside are many people sound asleep, and soon they will all suffocate. But they will pass from sleep into death without ever feeling the sorrow of dying. Now suppose you start shouting and wake up a few of the lighter sleepers, so that this unlucky few must suffer the agony of a death that nothing can prevent. Do you really think you are doing them a favor?”

2.7

“But once a few people are awake, you can’t say there is no hope at all of destroying the iron house.”

2.8

It was true. I had my own convictions, but hope was something I could not wipe out, because hope lies in the future. I could never use my proof that there was none to defeat his claim that there might be some. So in the end I agreed to write something too. That was my first story, “Diary of a Madman.”

2.9

After that, once I had started, I could not stop. Every so often I would write something that looked more or less like a short story, to keep my friends happy, and over time there came to be more than a dozen of them.
